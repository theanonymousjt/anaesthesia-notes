## 1.1. IDEAL INTRAVENOUS ANESTHETIC PROPERTIES







**The ideal intravenous anesthetic agent would possess both optimal pharmacological and physicochemical properties.**

#### 1.1.1.1. PHARMACOLOGICAL PROPERTIES

* **Hypnosis and amnesia** with rapid onset (time of one arm-brain circulation) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Rapid metabolism** to inactive metabolites [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Minimal cardiovascular and respiratory depression** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **No histamine release or hypersensitivity reactions** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Nontoxic, nonmutagenic, noncarcinogenic** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **No untoward neurological effects** (seizures, myoclonus, antianalgesia, neurotoxicity) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Additional beneficial effects:** analgesia, antiemetic, neuroprotection, cardioprotection [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Pharmacokinetic-based models** to guide accurate dosing [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Ability to continuously monitor delivery** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Analgesic at sub-anesthetic concentrations** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **No emetic effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **No excitation or emergence phenomena** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **No interaction with other agents** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Safe following inadvertent intra-arterial injection** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

#### 1.1.1.2. PHYSICOCHEMICAL PROPERTIES

* **Water-soluble** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Stable formulation, nonpyrogenic** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Nonirritating:** painless on intravenous injection [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Small volume needed** for induction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Inexpensive** to prepare and formulate [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Antimicrobial preparation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Long shelf-life at room temperature** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Minimal environmental impact** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

💡 No single agent currently meets all these criteria; propofol has become the "gold standard" but still has limitations including pain on injection, hypotension, and respiratory depression.

**Bottom Line:** The ideal intravenous anesthetic would achieve rapid, pleasant induction with minimal side effects, predictable recovery, and be easy to administer safely—a target for future drug development as no current agent fulfills all criteria.

## 1.2. BASIC PHARMACOKINETIC PRINCIPLES







**Intravenous anesthetics exhibit complex pharmacokinetic profiles that affect their clinical utility.**

* **Rapid onset** of IV anesthetics due to:
  * Rapid distribution to higher perfused, vessel-rich tissues [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Lipophilicity allowing quick crossing of the blood-brain barrier [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Slight delay between blood concentration and brain effect (hysteresis) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Single bolus effects** terminate primarily via:
  * Redistribution of anesthetic from brain to less perfused tissues (muscle, fat) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * This explains the short duration of clinical effect despite longer elimination half-lives [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Continuous infusion considerations:**
  * Initial bolus establishes desired blood concentration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Redistribution becomes less important as peripheral tissues equilibrate with blood [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Infusion rates typically decrease over time to maintain stable blood concentration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 1.2.2. TRIEXPONENTIAL DECLINE AND THREE-COMPARTMENT MODEL

* **Plasma concentration curve after bolus** follows triexponential decline with three phases:
  1. **Rapid distribution phase:** Drug quickly distributes from plasma to peripheral tissues [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  2. **Slow distribution phase:** Continued distribution concurrent with return from rapid distribution tissue [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  3. **Terminal (elimination) phase:** Drug is removed from the body [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

* **Three-compartment model** explains IV anesthetic behavior:
  * **Central compartment (CA1):** Blood [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Peripheral compartments (CA2, CA3):** One rapid and one slow [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Drug behavior:** Distribution to peripheral compartments and elimination (G1) balanced by infusion rate (r[T]) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Over time:** Peripheral compartments accumulate drug and contribute more to plasma levels [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 1.2.3. CONTEXT-SENSITIVE HALF-TIME [PART A/B]

* **Definition:** Time to achieve a 50% reduction in concentration after stopping a continuous infusion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Significance:** Demonstrates the influence of distributive processes in governing drug disposition [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Clinical relevance:** Explains why some drugs (e.g., thiopental) have prolonged effects with long infusions while others (e.g., propofol) recover more predictably [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Comparison to elimination half-time (t½):**
  * Elimination half-time works for single-compartment models or single administrations [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Context-sensitive half-time accounts for multiple compartments and continuous administration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

![Context-sensitive half-time of intravenous anesthetics](IV_IV_Untitled_2.png_b39c1f.png_dd1edc.png)

* **Remifentanil example:** Exhibits context-sensitive half-time that is essentially independent of infusion duration (approximately 3 minutes) due to metabolism by nonspecific plasma esterases [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

![Time to 50% decrease in drug concentration after variable-length infusions](IV_IV_Untitled_3.png_37e56e.png_bfb89e.png)

💡 **Context-sensitive half-time varies significantly between drugs:**

* Remifentanil: ~5 mins (regardless of infusion duration) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Propofol: ~40 mins (after 8-hour infusion) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Alfentanil: ~50 mins (after 8-hour infusion) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Thiopental: ~150 mins (after 8-hour infusion) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Fentanyl: ~250 mins (after 8-hour infusion) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

⚠️ The longer the infusion duration, the more drug accumulates in peripheral compartments, resulting in longer context-sensitive half-times for most agents.

**Bottom Line:** The three-compartment model and context-sensitive half-time concept are essential for understanding the clinical behavior of IV anesthetics, especially during continuous infusions, and explain why some drugs (like remifentanil and propofol) are better suited for infusion than others (like thiopental).

## 1.3. PHARMACODYNAMIC PRINCIPLES







### 1.3.2. GENERAL MECHANISMS OF ACTION

**Intravenous anesthetics primarily act through several main receptor pathways:**

* **GABA receptors** (most common target) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * GABA is the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter in CNS
  * Activation increases chloride conductance, causing hyperpolarization of neurons and inhibiting nerve transmission
  * **Propofol and Barbiturates:** Decrease rate of dissociation of GABA from its receptor, increasing duration of chloride channel opening
  * **Benzodiazepines:** Facilitate attachment of GABA to its binding site, increasing frequency of chloride channel openings
* **NMDA receptors** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * NMDA receptors are glutamate, glycine, and D-serine activated excitatory ion channels
  * **Ketamine:** Acts as an uncompetitive antagonist
* **Alpha-2 receptors** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * **Dexmedetomidine:** Alpha-2 agonist that inhibits norepinephrine release via:
	1. CNS inhibition via the locus coeruleus (primary site for brain release of NE) in brain stem
	2. Analgesia via decreased substance P release at the dorsal horn of the spinal cord

### 1.3.3. RECEPTOR TARGETS

**Each IV anesthetic has a distinctive receptor activity profile:**

![IV Agents Receptor Activity](IV_IV_IV_Agents_Receptor_Activity.001.jpeg_a90aba.jpeg_91c996.jpeg)

**Systemic effects beyond the brain:**
* All hypnotic agents affect other major organ systems besides the brain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Dose-dependent respiratory depression (exception: Ketamine) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Hypotension and cardiac depression (Etomidate causes least cardiac depression) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

⚠️ Profound hemodynamic effects are more pronounced with hypovolemia due to higher drug concentration achieved within central compartment [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

⚠️ Large hemodynamic depressant effects can be seen in elderly patients and those with pre-existing cardiovascular disease [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

💡 These patients often exhibit decreased dose requirements due to both pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic factors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 1.3.4. ANATOMICAL SITES OF ACTION

**General anesthetics affect multiple sites in the central nervous system:**

* **Primary anatomical targets** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * **Thalamus:** Most likely primary target based on auditory and sensory evoked potential data
  * **Limbic system:** Associated with memory effects
  * **Certain cortical areas:** Contribute to loss of consciousness
  * **Spinal cord:** Especially important for halogenated volatile anesthetics
* **Site specificity varies by agent** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Halogenated volatile anesthetics appear to have greater influence on the spinal cord than intravenous agents
  * Different agents may preferentially affect different brain regions

### 1.3.5. MOLECULAR THEORIES OF ANESTHETIC ACTION

**Multiple theories explain how anesthetics produce their clinical effects:**

* **Lipid solubility correlation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Meyer-Overton correlation: Linear relationship between lipid solubility and anesthetic potency
  * Originally suggested non-specific mechanism based on physicochemical properties
  * Limitation: Some anesthetics (e.g., ketamine) are extreme outliers
  * Limitation: Stereoisomers (e.g., R-etomidate vs. S-etomidate) have identical lipid solubility but only R-etomidate has anesthetic properties
* **Membrane theories** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * **Membrane expansion:** Anesthetics penetrate lipid bilayer, altering molecular arrangement of phospholipids
  * **Critical volume hypothesis:** Volume expansion of membrane disrupts function of membrane-spanning ionic channels
  * Limitation: Temperature increases membrane thickness similarly without enhancing anesthesia
  * **Annular lipid perturbation:** Anesthetics disrupt specific lipids surrounding ion channels
* **Protein binding theories** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Current consensus: Anesthetics bind to specific protein sites
  * The correlation between potency and lipid solubility reflects the lipophilic nature of the binding sites
  * Ligand-gated ion channels are more sensitive to anesthetics than voltage-gated channels
  * GABAA, glycine, neuronal nicotinic, and NMDA channels are key targets
* **GABAA receptor** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Pentameric family of ligand-gated ion-channel receptors
  * Has binding sites for GABA on α subunits
  * Modulatory sites at α/γ interface for benzodiazepines
  * Modulatory sites on β subunit for etomidate, barbiturates, propofol, and volatile agents
  * Stereospecificity of etomidate action supports protein-based mechanisms

**Bottom Line:** IV anesthetics primarily act through GABAergic pathways (except ketamine and dexmedetomidine), with variable effects on other receptors that explain their differing side effect profiles. All agents affect multiple organ systems, with effects that are dose-dependent and more pronounced in elderly or compromised patients. Modern theories suggest specific protein binding rather than non-specific lipid interactions as the primary mechanism.

# 2. INTRAVENOUS ANESTHETIC AGENTS

## 2.1. PROPOFOL [PART A/B]







![Propofol Structure](IV_IV_Untitled_4.png_3d3591.png_f8eeea.png)

**Propofol (2,6-diisopropylphenol) is the most widely used intravenous anesthetic agent with a favorable pharmacokinetic profile enabling versatile clinical applications.**

### 2.1.2. CHEMICAL PROPERTIES AND FORMULATION

* **Chemical structure:** Phenol derivative (2,6-diisopropylphenol) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Formulation evolution:**
  * Initially formulated in Cremophor EL in 1970s, withdrawn due to anaphylactic reactions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Current formulation consists of [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
	* 1% propofol (1% or 2% available) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
	* 10% soybean oil
	* 2.25% glycerol
	* 1.2% egg phospholipid emulsifier (egg yolk lecithin - relevant for allergenicity)
	* EDTA (added in 1990s as antimicrobial agent)
  * Appears as milky white emulsion, stable at room temperature [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * 💡 Patients with egg allergies typically react to egg white proteins, not egg yolk phospholipids, making propofol generally safe for those with egg allergies [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Weak organic acid with pKa of 11, almost entirely unionized at physiological pH [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Addition of medium chain triglycerides has been used in some formulations to reduce free propofol and incidence of pain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 2.1.3. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* **Primary mechanism:** Positive allosteric modulator at GABA-A receptor [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Enhances GABA inhibitory pathways, increasing chloride channel opening duration
* **Secondary mechanisms:** Also affects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Alpha-adrenergic receptors
  * NMDA receptors
  * Central cholinergic transmission
* **Target sites:** Interneurons within cerebral cortex, brain stem, and thalamus that are critical in arousal [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Dose-dependent effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Low doses → sedation
  * Intermediate doses → paradoxical excitation (disinhibition, unpredictable movement, broken speech)
  * Higher doses → loss of consciousness, apnea, muscle relaxation, loss of brainstem reflexes

![Propofol's mechanism of action in the CNS](IV_IV_Untitled_5.png_d6dc18.png_2d0169.png)

### 2.1.4. PHARMACOKINETICS

* **Metabolism:**
  * Primarily hepatic metabolism to inactive, water-soluble metabolites [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Metabolites excreted by kidneys; minimal (<3%) unmetabolized propofol excreted in urine and feces [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Clearance rate: 20-30 mL/kg/min (approximately 1.5 L/min), exceeding average hepatic blood flow (15 mL/kg/min) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Significant extrahepatic metabolism (kidneys and lungs account for up to 30% of metabolism) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * 💡 Liver and kidney disease have minimal effect on propofol pharmacokinetics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Detailed metabolic pathway** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
	* Approximately 70% conjugated to propofol glucuronide
	* 30% undergoes Phase 1 reaction to 4-hydroxypropofol within CYP2B6 (and CYP2C9)
	* Further conjugation to form 4-hydroxypropofol-sulphate, 4-hydroxypropofol-glucuronide and 1-hydroxypropofol-glucuronide
	* All conjugated forms excreted in urine with <1% unchanged propofol
	* No major metabolites have hypnotic activity
* **Distribution and elimination:**
  * **Two-compartment model:** Initial distribution half-life 2-4 minutes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Three-compartment model:**
	* Initial distribution half-life 1-8 minutes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Secondary slow distribution half-life 30-70 minutes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Elimination half-life 2-24 hours [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] (5-12 hours) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * **Context-sensitive half-time:** Remains ≤40 minutes for infusions up to 8 hours [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Volume of distribution:** 4 L/kg body weight [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] (largest of all induction agents) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * **Protein binding:** 98% [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] (extensively bound to plasma proteins, mainly albumin, and erythrocytes with only 1-2% free in plasma) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * **pKa:** 11 (weak organic acid), almost entirely unionized at physiological pH [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Hepatic extraction:** Very efficient with blood extraction ratio of 90% (reductions in hepatic blood flow lead to reductions in metabolism) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 2.1.5. PHARMACODYNAMICS [PART A/B]

#### 2.1.5.1. CENTRAL NERVOUS SYSTEM EFFECTS

* **EEG effects are dose-dependent:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Lower sedative doses → increased beta-wave activity
  * Induction doses → EEG resembles deep non-REM sleep (decreased beta-wave activity, increased alpha and delta activity)
  * Higher doses (plasma concentration ~8 μg/mL) → burst suppression
  * Further increase → isoelectric EEG
* **Neuroprotective effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Decreases cerebral metabolic oxygen consumption (CMRO₂)
  * Decreases intracranial pressure (ICP)
  * Decreases cerebral blood flow (CBF)
  * Cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) may decrease due to effect on systemic blood pressure
  * Antioxidant properties and free radical scavenging
  * Attenuation of excitotoxic glutamate pathways
  * Anti-inflammatory effects (e.g., decreasing TNF-alpha)
* **Anticonvulsant effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Generally considered anticonvulsant at induction doses reaching burst suppression
  * Used successfully to treat status epilepticus
  * 💡 May shorten seizure duration in Etc procedures
  * ⚠️ Rare case reports of grand mal seizures exist
  * Excitatory effects have been associated with propofol in up to 10% of patients but probably do not represent true cortical seizure activity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Addiction potential:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Increased incidence of propofol abuse over last 10 years, highest in anesthesia providers
  * 18% of U.S. academic institutions reported propofol abuse or diversion in last decade
  * Associated with feelings of well-being during emergence
  * DEA proposed classifying propofol as controlled substance in 2010
  * Currently only fospropofol is on scheduled substance list
* **Reversal potential:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Loss of consciousness can be partially reversed by central cholinomimetic physostigmine
  * Activation of central cholinergic pathways leads to arousal

#### 2.1.5.2. CARDIOVASCULAR EFFECTS

* **Hemodynamic effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Dose-dependent and more significant after induction than during infusion
  * Characteristic drop in systolic and diastolic blood pressure without expected increase in heart rate
  * Decreased cardiac output, stroke volume, and systemic vascular resistance (SVR)
  * Decreased sympathetic activity causing indirect arterial vasodilation and venodilation
  * Direct effects on smooth muscle and myocardium affecting intracellular calcium
  * Inhibition of baroreceptor response leading to diminished reflex increase in heart rate
  * Potential suppression of supraventricular tachycardia
  * Associated with bradycardia, especially if administered with an opioid [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

#### 2.1.5.3. RESPIRATORY EFFECTS

* **Respiratory depression:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Dose-dependent effects
  * Apnea common with higher induction doses
  * Maintenance doses → diminished tidal volumes and increased respiratory rate
  * Blunted response to hypoxia (direct effect on chemoreceptors)
  * Decreased respiratory response to hypercarbia
* **Airway effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Potent bronchodilator (direct effects on intracellular calcium homeostasis)
  * Depression of upper airway reflexes (facilitates LMA placement or endoscopy without muscle relaxants)
  * Rare to observe cough or laryngospasm compared to other agents [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 2.1.6. CLINICAL USES

* **Induction of anesthesia:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Induction dose in healthy adults: 1-2.5 mg/kg
  * Loss of consciousness typically achieved at blood concentration ~3 μg/kg
  * **Dose adjustments:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Elderly patients: Decreased dose due to increased sensitivity, decreased cardiac output and clearance
	* Children: Increased dose due to larger volume of distribution and quicker clearance
	* Morbidly obese patients: Calculate using lean body weight
	* Chronic alcohol abuse: Increased dose requirement
	* Cardiovascular disease: Reduced dose due to exaggerated hemodynamic response
* **Maintenance of anesthesia:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Infusion rates: 100-200 μg/kg/min
  * Total Intravenous Anesthesia (TIVA)
	* Either propofol alone or in combination with opioids
	* Suitable for all surgery types
	* Advantage: Prevention of PONV
* **PONV prevention:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Antiemetic properties achieved even at subhypnotic concentrations
  * Infusions as low as 10 μg/kg/min and blood concentrations as low as 350 ng/mL significantly decrease PONV
  * May possess antiemetic properties through antagonism of dopamine D2 receptor [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Special circumstances:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Anesthesia for malignant hyperthermia susceptible patients (not a trigger)
  * When inhalational anesthetics are difficult to administer (shared airway, e.g., rigid bronchoscopy)
  * Office-based anesthesia where anesthesia machine may not be available
* **Sedation:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Typical infusion doses: 25-75 μg/kg/min
  * Used for:
	* Minor procedures
	* Outpatient surgery
	* Off-site anesthetics
	* ICU sedation of mechanically ventilated patients

### 2.1.7. SIDE EFFECTS AND COMPLICATIONS

#### 2.1.7.1. PAIN ON INJECTION

* **Incidence:** 60-70% of patients when administered in peripheral vein without additives [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Mitigation strategies:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Most effective technique:** Pretreatment with lidocaine plus venous occlusion with tourniquet (modified Bier block)
  * **Most effective site:** Using antecubital vein instead of hand veins
  * **Other effective methods:**
	* Addition of lidocaine to propofol (2 mL of 1% lidocaine in 18 mL propofol)
	* Pretreatment with lidocaine without tourniquet
	* Pretreatment with opioids, ketamine, or NSAIDs
	* Diluting the emulsion
	* Changing the lipid solvent
  * Injection into small veins is painful but may be reduced if lidocaine is mixed with propofol or if the affected limb is raised to facilitate venous drainage [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

#### 2.1.7.2. PROPOFOL INFUSION SYNDROME (PRISE)

* **Description:** Rare but potentially deadly side effect first described in children in 1990s, later in adults [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Risk factors:** Associated with doses >4 mg/kg/h for >48 hours [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Clinical features:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Unexplained metabolic acidosis
  * Hyperkalemia
  * Hyperlipidemia
  * Rhabdomyolysis
  * Hepatomegaly
  * Renal failure
  * ECG changes, arrhythmias, cardiac failure
* **Pathophysiology:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Possibly mitochondrial toxicity and uncoupling of intracellular respiration chain
  * Alternative hypothesis: inhibition of fatty acid oxidation
  * Animal and human models demonstrate interference with oxidative phosphorylation, mitochondrial energy production
  * Energy mismatch between demand and utilization leads to muscle necrosis
* **Clinical Presentation:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Most common presentation is new-onset metabolic acidosis and cardiac dysfunction
  * ECG changes similar to Brugada syndrome typically precede cardiac dysfunction
  * Direct muscle necrosis with creatine kinase (CK) levels >10,000 units/L may occur
* **Risk Factors:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Typically occurs after 3 days of propofol-based sedation
  * Higher risk in patients with sepsis, burns, trauma, pancreatitis, neurological injuries
  * Contraindicated in patients 16 years and younger for ICU sedation
* **Prevention:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Propofol sedation should not exceed 4 mg/kg/hr for more than 48 hours
  * Regular monitoring of CK, lactate, and triglyceride levels
* **Mortality:** Approximately 50% despite interventions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* ⚠️ FDA altered recommended maximum propofol infusion dose to 4 mg/kg/h in 2006 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 2.1.7.3. OTHER ADVERSE EFFECTS

* **Green urine:** Reported with prolonged infusions due to extrahepatic metabolism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] (may also turn hair green) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Hypotension:** Due to decreased SVR, venodilation, and direct myocardial depression [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Respiratory depression and apnea:** Dose-dependent effect requiring careful titration [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Allergic reactions:**
  * Initial concerns about egg allergy contraindication [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * A thorough analysis of large patient cohorts showed propofol is generally safe in patients allergic to egg, soy, and peanut
  * For children allergic to egg, more evidence is required to use propofol with complete confidence
  * No persuasive evidence that propofol is unsafe in those allergic to soy or peanut
* **Intra-arterial injection:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Unlike thiopental, propofol does not appear to cause adverse effects when given intra-arterially
  * Onset of anesthesia is delayed with intra-arterial administration
* **Subcutaneous injection:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Caution required with peripheral IV cannula placement
  * Deep subcutaneous injection is not especially painful and may be recognized only when induction fails
  * Can cause erythema, pain, and swelling over 24 hours (dose-dependent)
* **Bacterial growth:** Formulation supports microbial growth; requires strict aseptic technique and labeling of expiration times (typically 12 hours) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Propofol should be administered within 6 hours of opening the ampule [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 2.1.8. TOXICITY AND ENVIRONMENTAL IMPACT

* **Environmental considerations:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * No direct global warming potential (unlike inhaled agents)
  * Environmental footprint relates to production, transport, and electricity used for syringe pumps
  * Global warming potential four orders of magnitude less than inhaled agents
  * "Moderate persistence" in environment with toxicity to aquatic organisms, but overall low risk
  * Unused propofol should be disposed of in containers for incineration, not discarded down sinks

**Bottom Line:** Propofol offers rapid onset, smooth induction, quick emergence, and antiemetic properties, making it versatile for induction, maintenance, and sedation. However, it requires vigilant monitoring for hemodynamic depression, respiratory effects, and pain on injection. Long-term high-dose infusions risk propofol infusion syndrome, necessitating strict dosing limits and monitoring.

## 2.2. ETOMIDATE [PART A/B]







![Etomidate Structure](IV_IV_Untitled_6.png_c4a74c.png_475a38.png)

**Etomidate is a hemodynamically stable induction agent that preserves cardiovascular function but causes adrenocortical suppression.**

### 2.2.2. CHEMICAL PROPERTIES AND FORMULATION

* **Chemistry:** Imidazole derivative (ethyl 3-[(1R)-1-phenylethel)]-imidazole-5-carboxylate) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Introduction:** Entered anesthetic practice in 1972 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Formulation:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Dissolved in propylene glycol (can cause pain on injection, thrombophlebitis)
  * Not stable in neutral pH solutions
  * Clear colourless solution (200 µg/mL or 2 mg/mL)
  * Prepared as 0.2% solution at pH 4.1 with 35% v/v propylene glycol to improve stability and reduce irritant properties [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Lipid formulation now also available [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Continues to be used infrequently in UK but withdrawn in North America and Australia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Single stereoisomer: R isomer is 5x more potent than S

### 2.2.3. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* **Primary mechanism:** GABA-A receptor agonist [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Effect:** Binds as an agonist to the GABA-A receptor, enhancing inhibitory influence on the brain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 2.2.4. PHARMACOKINETICS

* **Onset and offset:** Quick onset ("vein to brain"), fast redistribution [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Distribution:** Follows three-compartment kinetic model [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Metabolism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Hepatic (and plasma) metabolism by ester hydrolysis to inactive metabolite
  * Excretion predominantly by kidney (~80%) and bile (~20%)
  * Highly protein bound (~75%)
* **Pharmacokinetic parameters:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Initial distribution half-life: 2.7 min
  * Redistribution half-life: 29 min
  * Elimination half-life: 2.9-5.3 h
  * Volume of distribution: 2.5-4.5 L/kg
* **Additional details:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Actions terminated by rapid distribution into tissues
  * Elimination depends on hepatic metabolism and renal excretion
  * Non-specific hepatic esterases and possibly plasma cholinesterase hydrolyze etomidate to ethyl alcohol and carboxylic acid metabolite
  * May inhibit plasma cholinesterase

### 2.2.5. PHARMACODYNAMICS [PART A/B]

#### 2.2.5.1. CENTRAL NERVOUS SYSTEM EFFECTS

* **Cerebral effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Potent vasoconstrictor that reduces cerebral blood flow (CBF)
  * Decreases intracranial pressure (ICP)
  * Decreases cerebral metabolic oxygen consumption (CMRO₂)
  * Cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) maintained or increased due to minimal effect on mean arterial pressure
* **EEG effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Can achieve burst suppression unlike benzodiazepines
  * Often associated with epileptogenic activity (excitatory spikes) on EEG despite neurodepressant properties
  * May cause observable seizure-like, myoclonic movements following induction
* **Evoked potentials:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Increases latency intervals on somatosensory-evoked potentials (SSEPs)
  * Paradoxically increases amplitude of SSEPs (unlike other anesthetics)
* **Seizure threshold:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Proconvulsant (lowers seizure threshold)
  * Used for electroconvulsive therapy (Etc)

#### 2.2.5.2. CARDIOVASCULAR EFFECTS

* **Hemodynamic stability:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Minimal effect on mean arterial pressure (MAP)
  * Minimal effect on pulmonary artery (PA) pressure, PA wedge pressure, central venous pressure
  * Maintains stroke volume, cardiac index, systemic vascular resistance (SVR), pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR)
  * ⚠️ Primary feature that makes it valuable for cardiac, trauma, and hypovolemic patients
  * Of the commonly used IV anesthetics, produces the least cardiovascular disturbance [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Peripheral vascular resistance may fall slightly but less than with other induction agents [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Myocardial oxygen supply, contractility, and blood pressure remain largely unchanged [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

#### 2.2.5.3. RESPIRATORY EFFECTS

* **Airway reflexes:** Mixed data regarding effects on airway reflexes - generally considered to depress reflexes less than propofol [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Pulmonary vasculature:** Relaxes smooth musculature of pulmonary vasculature similar to propofol [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 2.2.6. CLINICAL USES

* **Primary indication:** Hemodynamically stable induction in cardiovascularly compromised patients [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Induction dose: 0.2-0.3 mg/kg [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Common uses:
	* Cardiac patients requiring anesthesia
	* Trauma patients who are hemodynamically unstable or hypovolemic
	* Any patient requiring hemodynamic stability during induction
* **Other uses:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * MAC sedation (due to preservation of airway reflexes)
  * Etc (due to proconvulsant properties)

### 2.2.7. SIDE EFFECTS AND COMPLICATIONS

#### 2.2.7.1. ADRENOCORTICAL SUPPRESSION

* **Mechanism:** Inhibits activity of 11-beta-hydroxylase enzyme, preventing conversion of cholesterol to cortisol [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Duration:** Even a single induction dose can transiently suppress adrenocortical axis for 4-8 hours; more pronounced with infusions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Clinical significance:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Mixed data regarding adverse outcomes following bolus of etomidate
  * Increased mortality reported when infused for sedation in ICU, particularly in septic patients
  * Most practitioners avoid repeat doses or continuous infusions
  * Some suggest pretreatment with dexamethasone to mitigate effect
* **Clinical implications:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Associated with increased mortality when used as infusion to sedate septic patients in ICU
  * Single doses influence adrenocortical function but probably have little clinical significance in otherwise fit patients
  * Limited use in elective patients; paradoxically, etomidate has best cardiovascular profile in unwell patients in whom consequences of steroid inhibition are likely most detrimental

#### 2.2.7.2. OTHER SIDE EFFECTS

* **Pain on injection:** Due to propylene glycol solvent, can be decreased by prior injection of lidocaine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] (occurs in up to 25% of patients) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Myoclonus:** 30-60% incidence with induction (disinhibitory effects on parts of nervous system controlling extrapyramidal motor activity) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV):** Increased incidence compared to other induction agents [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Thrombophlebitis:** Due to propylene glycol solvent [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Hypersensitivity reactions:** Less common with etomidate than other agents; histamine release is rare [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Porphyria:** May precipitate a porphyric crisis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

**Bottom Line:** Etomidate provides hemodynamically stable induction, making it valuable for cardiovascularly compromised patients, but its use is limited by adrenocortical suppression (even after single dose), increased PONV, and myoclonic movements on induction.

## 2.3. BARBITURATES [PART A/B]







![Barbiturates Structure](IV_IV_Untitled_7.png_2ed935.png_84453d.png)

**Barbiturates, particularly thiopental, are historical intravenous anesthetics with rapid onset but prolonged recovery after infusions.**

### 2.3.2. CHEMICAL STRUCTURE AND CLASSIFICATION

* **History:** Thiopental introduced in 1934, was "gold standard" for 50 years; U.S. production ceased in 2011 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Chemical classes:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Oxybarbiturates:** Oxygen at position 2 (e.g., methohexital)
  * **Thiobarbiturates:** Sulfur at position 2 (e.g., thiopental, thiamylal)
* **Structure:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Pyrimidine center
  * Produced as racemic mixtures despite unequal potency between stereoisomers
  * Methohexital has two chiral centers and four potential stereoisomers
* **Basic chemistry:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * All barbiturates derived from barbituric acid (condensation product of urea and malonic acid)
  * When oxygen is exchanged for sulfur at C2 position, oxybarbiturates become thiobarbiturates
  * Not readily soluble in water at neutral pH
  * Solubility depends on transformation from keto to enol form (tautomerism), occurring most readily in alkaline solutions
  * Thiobarbiturates are very lipid-soluble, highly protein-bound, and completely metabolized in liver
  * Oxybarbiturates are less lipid-soluble, less protein-bound, and some are excreted almost entirely unchanged

### 2.3.3. PRESENTATION AND PREPARATION

* **Formulation:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Highly alkaline solutions allowing formation of water-soluble salts
  * ⚠️ Addition to acidic solutions (e.g., vecuronium) leads to precipitation
  * Limited storage after reconstitution (thiobarbiturates up to 2 weeks, methohexital up to 6 weeks in alkaline solution)
  * Thiopental: 500mg yellow powder mixed with sodium carbonate, stored under nitrogen, reconstituted to 2.5% solution with pH 10.5
  * **Additives purpose:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
	* Sodium carbonate (6% by weight) reacts with water to create alkaline solution (pH 10.5) favoring water-soluble enol form
	* Nitrogen replaces air to prevent carbon dioxide from creating a less alkaline solution
	* 2.5% solution stable for many days and bacteriostatic due to alkaline pH

### 2.3.4. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* **Primary mechanism:** Positive allosteric modulator of GABA-A receptor [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Decreases the rate of dissociation of GABA from its receptor
  * Increases duration of chloride channel opening
* **Secondary mechanisms:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Inhibition of central excitatory pathways (glutamate via NMDA receptors)
  * Inhibition of acetylcholine pathways

### 2.3.5. PHARMACOKINETICS

* **Metabolism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Primary hepatic metabolism yielding water-soluble inactive metabolites
  * Excretion in urine and bile
  * Oxidation to hydroxyl derivatives is most common metabolic pathway
* **Thiopental specifics:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Elimination half-life: 12 hours
  * Clearance rate: 3 mL/kg/min (10-fold longer than propofol)
  * After extended infusion, exhibits zero-order kinetics
  * Very long context-sensitive half-time (poor choice for continuous infusion)
* **Methohexital specifics:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Elimination half-life: 4 hours (shorter than thiopental)
  * Clearance rate: 11 mL/kg/min (more efficient hepatic extraction)
* **Distribution:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Highly lipid soluble (60% unionized at physiological pH)
  * pKa 7.6
  * 70-80% protein bound
  * Rapid redistribution (half-life 8 minutes)
  * Undergoes keto-enol tautomerization to facilitate crossing blood-brain barrier
  * After single induction dose, termination of action due to redistribution
  * Initial Vd reduced in elderly due to 10-15% reduction in total body water
* **Protein binding and distribution:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Predominantly bound by plasma proteins (80%)
  * Only 20% free and unbound in plasma
  * With pKa of 7.6, 60% of free form is unionized (12% of total drug in plasma immediately available)
  * Despite limited free fraction, rapid onset due to high lipid solubility and large brain cardiac output
  * Dynamic equilibrium exists between protein-bound and free drug
  * Critically ill patients (acidotic with reduced plasma protein binding) require smaller doses
  * NSAIDs may reduce available protein binding sites and increase free drug fraction

### 2.3.6. PHARMACODYNAMICS [PART A/B]

#### 2.3.6.1. CENTRAL NERVOUS SYSTEM EFFECTS

* **EEG effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Dose-dependent progression
  * Low doses → high-frequency, low-amplitude EEG pattern
  * Higher doses → burst suppression and isoelectric EEG
* **Anticonvulsant properties:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Thiopental generally considered anticonvulsant, used for status epilepticus
  * At smaller concentrations may have proconvulsant properties
  * Methohexital has significant proconvulsant effects in epilepsy patients (often used for Etc)
* **CMRO₂ and cerebral effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Dose-dependent decrease in CMRO₂ (up to 50% decrease with isoelectric EEG)
  * Increased cerebral vascular resistance
  * Decreased cerebral blood flow and ICP
  * CPP often unchanged or improved (decrease in MAP typically less than decrease in ICP)
* **Neuroprotective effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Multiple mechanisms beyond CMRO₂ reduction
  * Anticonvulsant properties
  * Improved blood flow to ischemic brain areas (reverse steal effect)
  * Free-radical scavenging
  * Attenuation of excitatory neurotransmitter release
  * Membrane stabilization
  * More effective for focal and incomplete ischemia than global injury
* **Analgesia:** When used in very low doses, it is antanalgesic [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

#### 2.3.6.2. CARDIOVASCULAR EFFECTS

* **Hemodynamic effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Decreased MAP and cardiac output
  * Primary mechanism: reduction of venous vascular tone with peripheral pooling and decreased venous return
  * Negative inotropic effects (direct myocardial depression)
	* Direct: altering intracellular calcium homeostasis
	* Indirect: diminishing sympathetic tone
  * Baroreceptor-mediated heart rate increase may be impaired but still typically present (10-30% increase)
  * ⚠️ Effects more pronounced in patients with cardiovascular disease or hypovolemia
* **Methohexital comparison:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Smaller decrease in MAP
  * Larger increase in heart rate than thiopental

#### 2.3.6.3. RESPIRATORY EFFECTS

* **Ventilatory effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Dose-dependent respiratory depression
  * Central apnea at deeper stages of anesthesia
  * Typical induction dose causes apnea after 1-1.5 minutes
  * Ventilatory response to CO₂ returns to baseline in approximately 6 minutes
  * Shorter time to recovery from respiratory depression compared to propofol
* **Airway effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Less bronchodilation than propofol or inhalational anesthetics
  * Case reports of laryngospasm and bronchospasm after induction
  * Airway reflexes generally more preserved compared to propofol

#### 2.3.6.4. OTHER SYSTEM EFFECTS

* **Renal effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Urine output may fall due to increased antidiuretic hormone release secondary to CNS depression
  * Decreased cardiac output also contributes to reduced urine output

### 2.3.7. CLINICAL USES

* **Induction of anesthesia:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Thiopental induction dose: 3-5 mg/kg in adults, 5-6 mg/kg in children, 6-8 mg/kg in infants
  * Light anesthesia in 15-30 seconds, deeper stages in 30-40 seconds, lasting approximately 1 minute
  * Dose modification factors:
	* Premedication may decrease required dose by 50%
	* Nitrous oxide may decrease plasma concentration needed by 67%
	* Elderly patients require lower doses
	* Obese patients require lower doses on a per kilogram basis (use lean body mass)
	* Dose reductions needed with liver disease, heart failure, shock, severe anemia
* **Methohexital uses:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Induction dose: 1-2 mg/kg IV
  * Rectal administration (25 mg/kg) as pediatric sedative
  * Maintenance of sedation/anesthesia (50-150 μg/kg/min infusion)
  * Etc (favorable for seizure induction)
* **Neuroprotection:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Thiopental-induced burst suppression used for neurosurgery and after head trauma
  * Efficacy for preventing long-term brain injury questioned
  * More effective for focal ischemia during cardiopulmonary bypass and carotid surgery
* **Status epilepticus:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Thiopental occasionally used for status epilepticus
  * At sufficient plasma concentrations (maintained by continuous infusion) produces isoelectric EEG
  * Confirms maximal reduction of cerebral oxygen requirements
  * Inotropic support may be required to maintain adequate cerebral perfusion
* **Other routes:** Thiopental has previously been used rectally, although it has slow onset via this route [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 2.3.8. SIDE EFFECTS AND COMPLICATIONS

* **Intra-arterial injection:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Inadvertent arterial administration can cause crystal formation, vasospasm, thrombosis, pain, and possible tissue necrosis
  * **Mechanism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
	* When 2.5% thiopental at pH 10.5 is injected into arterial blood (pH 7.4), tautomeric equilibrium shifts toward keto form
	* Results in less water-soluble solution and precipitation of thiopental crystals
	* Crystals become wedged into small blood vessels leading to ischemia and pain
  * **Emergency management:**
	* High-volume saline dilution
	* Arterial administration of papaverine (40mg) to dilate artery
	* Consider sympathetic blockade with regional anesthesia
	* Heparinization
  * **Prevention:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
	* Thiopental does not precipitate when injected intravenously as it is continually diluted by more venous blood
	* Perivascular injection is painful and may cause serious tissue necrosis if large doses extravasate
* **Other complications:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Pain: Thiopental generally not irritating; methohexital causes more pain on injection
  * Porphyria: Contraindicated in patients with acute intermittent porphyria (exacerbates symptoms via effects on hepatic enzymes)
  * Anaphylaxis: Occurs in approximately 1 in 20,000 administrations
  * Hemodynamic instability in compromised patients
  * **Additional drugs contraindicated in porphyria:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
	* Other barbiturates, etomidate, halothane, cocaine
	* Lidocaine and prilocaine (bupivacaine is safe)
	* Clonidine, metoclopramide, hyoscine
	* Diclofenac, ranitidine

### 2.3.9. STRUCTURE-ACTIVITY RELATIONSHIPS

* **Key pharmacological determinants:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Substitution at C5 determines hypnotic potency and anticonvulsant activity
	* Phenyl group in phenobarbital is anticonvulsive whereas methohexital is not
  * Substitution at C2 increases lipid solubility
	* Oxygen in oxybarbiturates vs. sulfur in thiobarbiturates - thiobarbiturates have greater lipid solubility
	* Thiopental and thiamylal have greater potency, more rapid onset, shorter duration than pentobarbital

**Bottom Line:** Barbiturates (particularly thiopental) provide rapid induction with neuroprotective effects but are limited by prolonged context-sensitive half-time making them unsuitable for infusions. Their hemodynamic and respiratory depressant effects require caution in compromised patients, and intra-arterial injection demands immediate intervention to prevent tissue necrosis.

## 2.4. KETAMINE [PART A/B]







![Ketamine Structure](IV_IV_Untitled_8.png_4190e6.png_ab0261.png)

**Ketamine is a dissociative anesthetic with unique properties including analgesia, maintenance of respiratory drive, and cardiovascular stimulation.**

### 2.4.2. CHEMICAL PROPERTIES AND FORMULATION

* **History:** Discovered in search for a phencyclidine (PCP) derivative with fewer psychomimetic effects, described as causing "dissociative anesthesia" in 1965 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Chemistry:** Phencyclidine derivative [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Enantiomers:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Exists as a racemic mixture of S and R enantiomers
  * S(+) enantiomer is 3-4 times more potent than R enantiomer
  * S enantiomer has shorter duration of action and is cleared more rapidly
  * S(+) ketamine produces minimal bronchodilation compared to racemic mixture
* **Formulation:** Available as clear colorless solution of 10, 50, or 100 mg/mL [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] (soluble in water forming acidic solution, pH 3.5-5.5) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Presentation:** Racemic mixture or single S(+) enantiomer [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 2.4.3. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* **Primary mechanism:** NMDA receptor antagonist [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Binds to phencyclidine (PCP) binding site within NMDA channel
  * Decreases channel opening time
  * Antagonism more profound if channel previously opened by glutamate
  * Decreases "wind up" (amplification of repeated stimulation), an elementary form of CNS sensitization
* **Secondary mechanisms:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Binds to mu, delta, and kappa opioid receptors (but not primary analgesic mechanism)
  * Stimulates CNS noradrenergic neurons and inhibits catecholamine uptake
  * Affects CNS cholinergic neurons (anticholinergic physostigmine can antagonize hypnotic effects)
  * Inhibits the HCN (hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide-gated) channel
  * Inhibits nicotinic acetylcholine receptors
  * Inhibits L-type calcium channels
* **Neurofunctional activity:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Primarily binds to NMDA receptors on inhibitory interneurons in cortex, limbic system, and hippocampus
  * Produces uncoordinated increases in neuronal activity with active EEG pattern
  * At spinal level, blocks NMDA glutamate-mediated nociceptive signals from peripheral afferents

### 2.4.4. PHARMACOKINETICS

* **Routes of administration:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Intravenous (93% bioavailability)
  * Intramuscular (93% bioavailability)
  * Transnasal (25-50% bioavailability)
  * Rectal or oral (16% bioavailability)
* **Distribution and onset:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * High lipid solubility, low protein binding (20%)
  * Rapid uptake in brain
  * Onset after IV administration: 30-60 seconds
  * Duration after IV administration: 10-15 minutes
  * pKa 7.5
  * Central effects evident 90 seconds after IV dose (different from other IV anesthetics - does not induce anesthesia in one arm-brain circulation time) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Metabolism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Hepatic metabolism by cytochrome P-450 enzymes (CYP 3A4 > CYP 2C9 > CYP 2B6)
  * Extensive hepatic uptake (hepatic extraction ratio of 0.9)
  * Primary metabolite: norketamine (1/3 as potent as ketamine)
  * Eliminated by renal excretion
  * Demethylated to active metabolite norketamine, further metabolized to inactive glucuronide metabolites [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Elimination pattern:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Bior triexponential pattern after IV bolus
  * Alpha-elimination phase (redistribution from CNS): 11 minutes
  * Beta-elimination phase: 2.5 hours
  * Following IV dose, plasma concentration falls in bi-exponential fashion due to initial distribution across lipid membranes followed by slower hepatic metabolism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 2.4.5. PHARMACODYNAMICS [PART A/B]

#### 2.4.5.1. CENTRAL NERVOUS SYSTEM EFFECTS

* **Dissociative state:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Patients unconscious with eyes open, maintain spontaneous respiration
  * Do not react to painful or noxious stimuli
  * EEG shows depression of thalamocortical pathways with concomitant activation of limbic system
  * Despite increased epileptiform activity, no clinical evidence of seizure or spread to cortical areas
* **Consciousness effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Low doses → sedation
  * Higher doses → dissociative amnestic state
  * Functional MRI studies show dose-dependent reduction in cerebral activation pathways (thalamus to insula to cingulate to prefrontal cortex)
  * Blocks signals from spinoreticular pathway, depresses activity of medial thalamic nuclei and medial reticular formation
* **Cerebral effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Traditionally described as increasing cerebral metabolic rate (CMRO₂)
  * Increases cerebral blood flow
  * Historically thought to increase intracranial pressure (ICP)
  * **Recent evidence:** ICP typically remains normal with ketamine administration in neurosurgical patients with controlled ventilation
  * May have neuroprotective properties
* **EEG effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Associated with epileptiform activity on EEG - mostly theta waves and some delta waves
  * These waveforms not seen in cortex, making ketamine unlikely to precipitate seizures
* **Emergence phenomena:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Vivid and unpleasant dreams, hallucinations, and delirium
  * Reduced by concurrent use of benzodiazepines or opioids
  * S(+) ketamine produces less intense but no less frequent emergence phenomena
  * Less common in young and elderly patients and those left to recover undisturbed
* **Other CNS effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Muscle tone is increased with possible jerking movements of limbs
  * Psychomimetic effects (hallucinations, out-of-body experiences) can be frightening
  * Lateral gaze nystagmus common

#### 2.4.5.2. CARDIOVASCULAR EFFECTS

* **Hemodynamic effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Increases heart rate and blood pressure
  * Mechanism: activation of sympathetic nervous system (catecholamine release)
  * Increases systemic vascular resistance
  * Makes it suitable for hemodynamically unstable patients
  * **Direct myocardial depression:** Present but usually masked by sympathetic stimulation
  * ⚠️ Can cause cardiovascular collapse in patients with depleted catecholamines
  * Unlike other induction agents, produces sympathetic nervous system stimulation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Increases circulating levels of adrenaline and noradrenaline [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Increases heart rate, cardiac output, blood pressure, and myocardial oxygen requirements [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Does not appear to precipitate arrhythmias [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * S(+) ketamine produces less direct cardiac depression in vitro compared to R(-) ketamine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Racemic ketamine blocks ATP-sensitive potassium channels (mechanism of ischemic preconditioning), but S(+) ketamine does not (advantage for patients with ischemic heart disease) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

#### 2.4.5.3. RESPIRATORY EFFECTS

* **Ventilatory effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Maintains spontaneous respiration
  * Minimal respiratory depression
  * Hypoxia can occur but easily treated with supplemental oxygen
  * Preserves airway reflexes
  * Respiratory rate may be increased, laryngeal reflexes relatively preserved [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Patent airway often but not always maintained; increased jaw muscle tone may precipitate airway obstruction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Bronchial effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Bronchodilator (especially racemic mixture)
  * Secondary agent for management of severe bronchospasm or status asthmaticus
  * Increases salivation that may result in laryngospasm (consider glycopyrrolate administration)
  * Useful for patients with asthma [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

#### 2.4.5.4. OTHER EFFECTS

* **Ocular effects:** Increases intraocular pressure due to increased ocular blood flow and tensing of extraocular muscles [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **GI effects:** Emetogenic [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] (higher incidence of nausea and vomiting than with propofol or thiopental) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Salivation:** Stimulates salivary glands (consider glycopyrrolate coadministration) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] (requires anticholinergic premedication) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Long-term use:** "Ketamine bladder" associated with recreational long-term use [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] (non-prescription, high-dose use associated with severe interstitial cystitis that may require cystectomy) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 2.4.6. CLINICAL USES

#### 2.4.6.1. ANESTHESIA

* **Induction:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * IV induction dose: 1-2 mg/kg
  * IM induction dose: 4-10 mg/kg (useful for uncooperative patients)
  * Onset 1 minute, duration 10-20 minutes
  * Particularly valuable for hemodynamically unstable patients
  * Advantage over etomidate in unstable patients: no adrenal suppression
* **Limitations:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * May be unsuitable for some cardiac patients (critical coronary artery disease) due to increased myocardial oxygen demand
  * Increased pulmonary vascular resistance may make it unsuitable for severe right heart dysfunction

#### 2.4.6.2. SEDATION

* **Procedural sedation:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Burn patients during wound care (analgesia with maintained spontaneous respiration and airway reflexes)
  * Pediatric patients for painful procedures (e.g., fracture reduction in emergency department)
  * Can be administered via IM injection for uncooperative patients
  * Intravenous doses of 0.2-0.5 mg/kg to facilitate positioning of patients with fractures before regional anesthesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Also used via oral and rectal routes for sedation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

#### 2.4.6.3. ANALGESIA

* **Acute pain management:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Subanesthetic doses provide significant analgesia
  * Reduces opiate requirements in postoperative patients
  * Most effective when given as bolus before surgical incision followed by postoperative infusion
  * Particularly useful in patients requiring high doses of opiates (chronic opiate therapy, history of opiate abuse)
* **Mechanism of analgesia:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Prevention of hyperalgesia
  * Decreased CNS sensitization associated with acute pain
  * Not direct analgesia but rather prevention of amplified pain responses

#### 2.4.6.4. CHRONIC PAIN MANAGEMENT

* **Complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS):** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Decreases pain scores and opiate consumption
  * Effects limited in duration after administration
  * Optimal regimen still being determined
* **Cancer pain:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Decreases but does not eliminate opiate requirements
  * Limited by CNS side effects and lack of reliable oral administration
* **Intranasal administration:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Significantly lowers intensity of breakthrough pain in chronic pain patients
  * Decreases pain scores in patients with chronic neuropathic pain
  * Few serious side effects reported
  * Combined with intranasal midazolam for postoperative pain management
* **Alternative administration routes:** Intrathecal and epidural routes used for analgesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

#### 2.4.6.5. TREATMENT OF DEPRESSION [PART B/C]

* **Rapid antidepressant effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Decreased depression symptoms and suicidal ideation within 1 hour of administration
  * Duration may last from days to approximately 2 weeks following single injection
  * Excitement due to variable results with current treatments (slow onset, poor efficacy)
  * Nasal spray of S(+) ketamine approved by FDA for treatment of resistant depression [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Administration protocol:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Typically infused IV over 40 minutes at subanesthetic dose of 0.5 mg/kg
  * Optimal regimen still being determined
* **Mechanism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Unclear if related to NMDA antagonism
  * Memantine (another NMDA antagonist) failed to improve depressive symptoms
  * May be related to effects on other CNS receptors (dopamine, adrenergic)

### 2.4.7. SIDE EFFECTS AND COMPLICATIONS

* **Psychogenic reactions:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Hallucinations and out-of-body experiences (can be frightening)
  * Can be reduced with co-administration of benzodiazepines
  * Lateral gaze nystagmus common
* **Cardiovascular:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Increased heart rate and blood pressure (usually beneficial but can be problematic in some patients)
  * ⚠️ Direct myocardial depression may cause cardiovascular collapse in catecholamine-depleted patients
* **Respiratory:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Generally maintains spontaneous respiration
  * Hypoxia possible but responsive to supplemental oxygen
  * Increased salivation may lead to laryngospasm
* **Emergence reactions:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Unpleasant emergence phenomena and delirium - secondary to ketamine induced depression of inferior colliculus and medial geniculate nucleus
  * More common in adults (females, >15y, >2mg/kg, hx of personality disorder/frequent dreaming) than children
  * Reduced by premedication with benzodiazepines and pre-emption

**Bottom Line:** Ketamine provides dissociative anesthesia with maintained respiratory drive, cardiovascular stimulation, and profound analgesia, making it valuable for hemodynamically unstable patients and painful procedures. Its unique profile includes potential uses for treatment-resistant depression and chronic pain, though psychomimetic side effects limit widespread application.

## 2.5. DEXMEDETOMIDINE [PART A/B]







**Dexmedetomidine is a selective alpha-2 adrenergic agonist that provides sedation with minimal respiratory depression and preserves patient arousability.**

### 2.5.2. CHEMICAL PROPERTIES

* **Structure:** (S)-4-[1-(2,2-Dimethylphenyl)ethyl]-3-H-imidazole [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Introduction:** Introduced into clinical practice in 1999 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Receptor selectivity:** Alpha-2-adrenergic agonist with 7-8 times greater affinity for alpha-2 receptor than clonidine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 2.5.3. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* **Primary mechanism:** Alpha-2 adrenergic receptor agonist [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Sites of action:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Locus coeruleus:** Activates sleep centers in the brain, inhibiting norepinephrine release
  * **Spinal cord:** Provides analgesia at spinal cord level
* **EEG pattern:** Resembles natural non-REM sleep [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Clinical manifestation:** Patients sedated but easily arousable, able to follow commands with minimal respiratory depression [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

![Dexmedetomidine's mechanism of action in the CNS](IV_image-1.png_50deae.png)

### 2.5.4. PHARMACOKINETICS

* **Distribution:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Rapid distribution phase with half-life of approximately 6 minutes
  * Terminal elimination half-life of approximately 2 hours
  * Steady-state volume of distribution approximately 118 L
  * Linear pharmacokinetics in dose range of 0.2-0.7 μg/kg/h for infusions up to 24 hours
* **Protein binding:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Average 94% bound to plasma proteins
  * Binding significantly decreased in subjects with hepatic impairment
* **Metabolism and elimination:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Almost complete biotransformation (minimal unchanged dexmedetomidine in urine/feces)
  * Metabolism via direct glucuronidation and cytochrome P450 pathways
  * Clearance approximately 39 L/h
  * Lower clearance in patients with hepatic impairment
  * Pharmacokinetics not significantly different with severe renal impairment
  * Context sensitive half time increases with prolonged duration
	  * Therefore for short infusions (<2h) - stop 30-40min before
	  * For longer infusions (>4h) - stop 1-2h before due to tissue accumulation

### 2.5.5. PHARMACODYNAMICS [PART A/B]

* **Sedation characteristics:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Produces sedation that mimics natural sleep
  * Patients remain arousable and can follow commands even when sedated
  * Minimal respiratory depression
* **Cardiovascular effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Bradycardia (expected for alpha-2 agonist)
  * Hypotension
  * Compared to placebo, causes 7 mmHg decrease in systolic blood pressure
  * Decreases mean heart rate by 1-8 beats per minute
* **Analgesic properties:** Found to have analgesic effects, can reduce opiate requirements [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 2.5.6. CLINICAL USES

* **ICU sedation:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Provides sedation for mechanically ventilated patients
  * Patients require less rescue sedation with midazolam or morphine versus placebo
  * Maintenance of spontaneous respiration allows extubation while still receiving dexmedetomidine
  * Useful for neurosurgical ICU patients (enables neurologic examinations during sedation)
  * Analgesic properties reduce opiate requirements
  * ⚠️ Systematic review found no clear sedation superiority compared to midazolam
* **Procedural sedation:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Sedation during awake fiberoptic intubation
  * MAC sedation alone or in combination with other medications
  * **Administration:** 0.5-1 μg/kg loading dose over 15 minutes, followed by 0.3-0.7 μg/kg/h infusion
  * Compared to propofol:
	* Less hypotension and better pain scores in PACU
	* Longer onset of sedation
	* More prolonged sedation and hypotension in PACU
	* Inferior to propofol for upper endoscopy procedures in some studies
* **Pediatric applications (off-label):** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Intranasal administration (1 μg/kg) for preoperative anxiolysis
	* Onset 25 minutes, duration 85 minutes
	* Superior to oral midazolam for sedation but limited by long onset time
  * Airway procedures (rigid bronchoscopy) due to preserved spontaneous respiration
  * Adjunct to TIVA during posterior spine fusion surgery (lowers propofol and sevoflurane requirements)
  * Minimal effect on somatosensory-evoked potentials (SSEPs) and motor-evoked potentials (MEPs)
* **Prevention of emergence delirium:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Meta-analysis: alpha-2 agonists decrease emergence delirium via oral, IV, or caudal routes
  * Dexmedetomidine (0.5-1 μg/kg) decreases emergence delirium from 47% to 2.8%
  * Slightly prolongs emergence time
* **Regional anesthesia adjuvant:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Prolongs motor and sensory blocks when administered intrathecally with local anesthetic
  * Perineural injection as part of brachial plexus block extends duration (not statistically significant)
  * Further studies needed to define potential benefit

### 2.5.7. SIDE EFFECTS AND COMPLICATIONS

* **Bradycardia:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Reported in up to 40% of patients
  * Can be managed with atropine, ephedrine, or volume administration
  * ⚠️ Case reports of severe bradycardia and cardiac arrest (though unlikely as sole causative agent)
  * More common after loading dose; omitting loading dose decreases incidence
* **Hypotension:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Expected with alpha-2-adrenergic agonist
  * In healthy volunteers after 1 μg/kg bolus, blood pressure decreased 23% from baseline
  * Infusions of 0.2-0.7 μg/kg/h associated with larger decreases compared to placebo
  * Returns to baseline within 6 hours of treatment cessation without rebound
* **Overdose reports:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Two patients received infusions of 2 or 4 μg/kg/h (instead of 0.2-0.4 μg/kg/h): excess sedation but minimal hemodynamic effect
  * 3-year-old child received accidental 9 μg/kg bolus: decreased heart rate, blood pressure, and oxygen saturation; managed with oxygen, fluid, epinephrine; recovered after 7 hours
* **Other side effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Dry mouth
  * Nausea
  * ⚠️ Use with caution in patients who cannot tolerate bradycardia or receiving medications causing bradycardia

**Bottom Line:** Dexmedetomidine provides unique sedation that mimics natural sleep, preserves respiratory drive and patient arousability, making it valuable for ICU sedation, awake procedures, and reduction of emergence delirium. Its primary limitations are bradycardia, hypotension, and slower onset compared to other sedatives.

## 2.6. BENZODIAZEPINES [PART A/B]







**Benzodiazepines, particularly midazolam, are versatile anesthetic adjuncts providing anxiolysis, amnesia, sedation, and hypnosis with favorable safety profiles.**

### 2.6.2. CHEMICAL STRUCTURE AND CLASSIFICATION

* **Structure:** Benzene ring and 7-member diazepine ring [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Midazolam: Has an imidazole ring contributing to water solubility at low pH
  * Diazepam and lorazepam: Insoluble in water, parenteral preparations contain propylene glycol (can cause venous irritation)

![Midazolam Structure](IV_image-2.png_3a70e2.png)

* **Common parenteral agents:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Midazolam (most widely used in perioperative period)
  * Lorazepam
  * Diazepam
* **Formulation characteristics:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Midazolam: Water-soluble, undergoes conformational change in bloodstream becoming more lipophilic; manufactured as acidic formulation (may cause mild irritation)
  * Diazepam and lorazepam: Require propylene glycol (causes vein irritation); alternative lipid emulsion formulations available with decreased bioavailability

### 2.6.3. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* **Primary mechanism:** Positive allosteric modulator at GABA-A receptor [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Binds to specific receptor sites on GABA-A receptor complex
  * Augments GABA-A receptor/chloride channel coupling
  * Increases frequency of chloride channel opening (unlike barbiturates/propofol that increase duration)
  * Resulting hyperpolarization leads to neural inhibition
* **"Ceiling effect":** Enhanced affinity of GABA-A receptor for GABA molecule and decreased unbinding produces dose-dependent CNS depression with ceiling effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * 20% receptor occupancy → anxiolysis
  * 30-50% receptor occupancy → sedation
* **Receptor distribution:** High density of GABA-A receptors in cerebral cortex explains sedative/hypnotic, anxiolytic, and amnestic properties [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 2.6.4. PHARMACOKINETICS

* **General characteristics:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Highly protein bound and highly lipophilic
  * High protein binding produces smaller free fraction available to cross blood-brain barrier
  * High lipophilicity results in larger volume of distribution and rapid onset
  * Peak effect of IV midazolam within 2-3 minutes
* **Metabolism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Primarily hepatic cytochrome P450 system (oxidation and glucuronic conjugation)
  * Metabolized products renally excreted
  * Some drugs (diazepam) have pharmacologically active metabolites with long elimination half-lives
  * Cytochrome P450 inhibitors can prolong benzodiazepine duration
* **Midazolam specific parameters:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Volume of distribution: 1-3.1 L/kg after single bolus
  * Elimination half-life: 1.8-2.6 hours (mean ~3 hours)
  * Total clearance: 6.4-11 mL/kg/min
  * Active metabolite (1-hydroxymidazolam) contributes minimally to clinical effects (~20% of midazolam's potency)
  * Termination of effect primarily by redistribution, not metabolism
  * Context-sensitive half-time: Short, allowing for continuous infusion (unlike other benzodiazepines)
* **Route-specific considerations:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * IM diazepam: Painful and unreliably absorbed
  * IM midazolam/lorazepam: Well absorbed, peak levels at 30 and 90 min respectively
* **Comparative pharmacokinetics:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

| Drug | Duration | Metabolites | Hepatic Clearance |
|------|----------|-------------|-------------------|
| Midazolam | Short | 1-hydroxymidazolam (mild CNS depressant) | High |
| Diazepam | Intermediate | Oxazepam and desmethyldiazepam | Slow |
| Lorazepam | Long | Inactive | Intermediate |

### 2.6.5. PHARMACODYNAMICS [PART A/B]

* **CNS effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Sedation/hypnosis (dose-dependent)
  * Anxiolysis
  * Anterograde amnesia
  * Decreased CMRO₂ and cerebral blood flow with maintained CO₂ responsiveness
  * Minimal effect on ICP
  * Cannot achieve isoelectric EEG or burst suppression (ceiling effect)
  * Limited neuroprotective effect (though activation of GABA-A receptors may inhibit neuronal death in brain ischemia)
  * Anticonvulsant properties (first-line therapy for seizures)
  * Muscle relaxation at supratherapeutic doses (spinal cord-mediated)
* **Respiratory effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Decreased upper airway reflexes
  * Depressed central respiratory drive
  * Minimal effect at premedication doses
  * ⚠️ Significant respiratory depression with comorbidities and/or concurrent opioid use
* **Cardiovascular effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Decreased SVR and blood pressure at induction doses
  * Minimal impact due to preservation of homeostatic reflexes
  * More pronounced effect in hypovolemic patients
  * Generally considered hemodynamically stable induction agents
* **Reversal:** Flumazenil is a specific antagonist [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Very short acting (45-90 minutes following 1-3 mg dose)
  * May see re-sedation as benzodiazepine is eliminated more slowly
  * ⚠️ Observation required! Midazolam has elimination half-life of 2-3h compared to flumazenil

### 2.6.6. CLINICAL USES

* **Premedication:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Anxiolysis
  * Anterograde amnesia
  * Midazolam doses:
	* IV/IM: 0.02-0.04 mg/kg (typically 1-2 mg)
	* PO: 0.25-1 mg/kg (popular in pediatric premedication)
	* Intranasal: 0.2-0.3 mg/kg
	* Buccal: 0.07 mg/kg
	* Sublingual: 0.1 mg/kg
* **Induction of anesthesia:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Hypnosis/amnesia/sedation
  * Midazolam dose: 0.1-0.2 mg/kg IV
* **Maintenance of anesthesia:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Hypnosis/amnesia
  * Midazolam infusion: 0.25-1 μg/kg/min (in conjunction with volatile anesthetics)
* **ICU sedation:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Meta-analysis: Midazolam and propofol provide similar quality sedation
  * Extubation time and recovery time shorter with propofol
  * Hemodynamic complications for either regimen usually not clinically significant
* **Anticonvulsant:** First-line therapy in management of seizures [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 2.6.7. SIDE EFFECTS AND COMPLICATIONS

* **Respiratory depression:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * More significant with higher doses, comorbidities, or concurrent opioids
  * Usually minimal at standard premedication doses
* **Hemodynamic effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Hypotension (more pronounced in hypovolemic patients)
  * Generally milder than with propofol or barbiturates
* **Paradoxical reactions:** Agitation, confusion, aggressive behavior (especially in elderly and children) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Venous complications:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Pain or thrombophlebitis following IV injection, especially with diazepam
  * Related to propylene glycol solvent
  * Midazolam may cause burning (acidic formulation)
* **Allergic reactions:** Extremely rare [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 2.6.8. REMIMAZOLAM [PART A/B] [EMERGING]

* **Structure and pharmacology:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Similar structure and onset to midazolam
  * Binds as agonist to GABA-A receptor
* **Unique properties:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Rapid offset following first-order kinetics at recommended doses
  * Metabolism by tissue esterases (similar to remifentanil)
  * No drug accumulation
  * Organ-independent metabolism
  * Reversible with flumazenil
* **Clinical applications:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Currently being developed for procedural sedation (e.g., colonoscopy)
  * Potential for ICU sedation (valuable for patients with end-organ dysfunction)
  * Advantages: Avoids Prise, limits accumulation leading to prolonged sedation
  * Combines hypnotic/amnestic effects of midazolam with metabolism similar to remifentanil
* **Comparative efficacy:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Meta-analysis vs. midazolam for procedural sedation showed:
	* Improved procedure success rate
	* Reduced need for rescue medication
	* Decreased recovery time
	* Better cognitive recovery
	* Similar adverse event profile
  * Compared to propofol for general anesthesia:
	* Slightly longer time to loss of consciousness
	* Lower incidence of hypotension and vasopressor requirements

**Bottom Line:** Benzodiazepines, particularly midazolam, provide reliable anxiolysis, amnesia, and sedation with favorable safety profiles and specific reversal capability. Their ceiling effect limits respiratory depression but also restricts utility for deep anesthesia. Newer agents like remimazolam combine benzodiazepine effects with rapid, predictable offset similar to remifentanil.

## 2.7. NEW INTRAVENOUS ANESTHETICS [PART A/B] [EMERGING]







**New intravenous anesthetics are being developed to achieve rapid onset, predictable offset, and minimal side effects regardless of administration duration.**

### 2.7.2. PROPOFOL FORMULATIONS

* **Limitations of current propofol formulation:** 1 or 2% lipid-water emultion containing 10% soybean oil, 2.25% glycerol, 1.2% egg phosphatide [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

  * Anaphylaxis risk
  * Sepsis risk from microbial contamination

* **Development approaches:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Reduce percentage of lipid in emulsion
	  * i.e. (Ampofol - 5% soybean oil means less bacterial growth but more painful)
  * Eliminate lipid entirely
  * Create prodrug formulations
* **Challenge:** Modifying formulation may alter well-established pharmacokinetics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 2.7.2.1. FOSPROPOFOL

* **Description:** Water-soluble prodrug of propofol [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Advantages:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Less pain on injection
  * Less hyperlipidemia
  * Reduced bacteremia risk
  * Water-based solution
* **Limitations:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Delayed onset time
  * Longer elimination time
  * Still associated with hypotension and respiratory depression (though possibly less frequent)
  * Increased pruritus and paresthesias
  * Currently not approved in the US (reasons may include delayed onset, slower recovery, significant perineal paresthesia and pruritus)

### 2.7.3. CYCLOPROPYL-METHOXYCARBONYL METOMIDATE (CPMM/AB700)

* **Description:** Etomidate analog showing promise in animal studies [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Advantages over etomidate:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Less adrenal suppression
  * Less potent with shorter duration of action
* **Potential applications:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Bolus or infusion for sedation and general anesthesia
  * Clinical profile more consistent with propofol than etomidate
  * Demonstrated less drug accumulation and more rapid recovery after prolonged infusion compared to propofol

### 2.7.4. THRX-918661/AZD-3043

* **Description:** Metabolically labile, positive allosteric modulator of GABA-A receptor [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Properties:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Water-soluble
  * Rapidly hydrolyzed by blood and tissue esterases to inactive carboxylate metabolite
* **Development status:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Phase I clinical trial showed no serious adverse events and rapid recovery after infusion
  * Further studies needed to evaluate potential for rapid recovery regardless of anesthesia duration

### 2.7.5. COMPUTER-ASSISTED DELIVERY SYSTEMS

* **SEDASYS:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * FDA-approved in 2013: Computer-assisted patient-controlled sedation system for propofol administration during colonoscopies/endoscopies
  * Monitoring: ECG, pulse oximetry, NIBP, end-tidal CO₂
  * Sedation assessment via automated patient responsiveness monitoring
  * Algorithmic adjustment of propofol infusion rate based on parameters
  * Early study (1,000 ASA 1-3 patients): Fewer desaturations, improved patient/physician satisfaction
  * Potential health care cost savings by removing anesthesiologist
  * Safety concerns:
	* Limited ability to provide deeper planes of anesthesia when needed
	* Skepticism from anesthesiologists about patient-controlled system

**Bottom Line:** Development of new IV anesthetics focuses on achieving rapid onset, predictable recovery regardless of infusion duration, and reduced side effects. While promising agents include metabolically labile compounds and improved propofol formulations, none have yet displaced current agents in clinical practice.

# 3. TOTAL INTRAVENOUS ANESTHESIA (TIVA)





## 3.1. PRINCIPLES OF TIVA [PART A/B]







### 3.1.2. DEFINITION AND COMPONENTS

**Total intravenous anesthesia involves inducing and maintaining general anesthesia using only intravenous agents to achieve hypnosis, analgesia, and muscle relaxation without volatile anesthetics.**

* **Core components:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Usually a combination of a sedative-hypnotic and an analgesic
  * Classic combination: propofol and remifentanil
  * Can be delivered either manually using formulae or by target-controlled infusions
* **Historical perspective:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Transition from bolus to infusion techniques
  * Introduction of shorter-acting agents (midazolam, propofol, remifentanil)
  * Development of variable-rate infusion pumps
  * Integration with depth of anesthesia monitors
  * Evolution to closed-loop automated delivery systems
* **Pharmacokinetic considerations:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Initial bolus establishes desired blood concentration
  * Maintenance infusion compensates for redistribution and elimination
  * Infusion rate typically decreases over time as peripheral compartments equilibrate
  * Understanding of context-sensitive half-time critical for emergence planning

### 3.1.3. ADVANTAGES AND DISADVANTAGES OF TIVA

**TIVA offers specific benefits but also presents unique challenges compared to inhalational anesthesia.**

#### 3.1.3.1. ADVANTAGES

* **Avoids adverse effects of volatile anesthetic agents:** `[LITFL:1]`
  * Prevention of nausea and vomiting
  * Eliminates environmental pollution
  * Avoids unwanted increased cerebral blood flow
  * No contribution to global warming potential
  * Avoids malignant hyperthermia triggers
* **Clinical benefits:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] `[LITFL:1]`
  * Reduced postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV)
  * Smoother emergence and extubation
  * Suitable for patients with known susceptibility to malignant hyperthermia
  * Facilitates procedures where volatile delivery is impractical (bronchoscopy, tracheostomy)
  * Enables precise control over anesthetic depth in specific procedures

#### 3.1.3.2. DISADVANTAGES

* **Pharmacological limitations:** `[LITFL:1]`
  * **Metabolic dependence:** All drugs must be metabolized (no pulmonary elimination pathway)
  * **Variable plasma concentration:** Individual pharmacokinetic variability affects drug concentrations
  * **No direct concentration monitoring:** Unlike end-tidal volatile monitoring, actual drug levels cannot be measured
* **Clinical challenges:** `[LITFL:1]` [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Potential increased likelihood of awareness:**
	* Likely related to poor application of technique rather than inherent technique problems
	* Mostly occurs due to disconnection of infusion without adequate depth monitoring
	* Absence of equivalent to end-tidal gas monitoring for depth assessment
  * **Equipment dependency:** Relies on functioning IV access and infusion pumps
  * **Technical complexity:** Requires understanding of pharmacokinetic models and TCI principles

### 3.1.4. IDEAL TIVA AGENT

**The ideal TIVA agent would possess optimal physical and patient-centered characteristics to minimize disadvantages.**

* **Physical characteristics:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Cheap and easy to manufacture
  * No reconstitution needed
  * Stable to store and transport
  * Inert in standard anesthetic vials, syringes, and giving sets
  * Compatible with other medications
* **Patient-centered characteristics:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Painless on administration
  * Rapid and predictable onset and offset
  * Predictable, reproducible plasma concentrations and clinical effect using given pharmacokinetic model
  * Minimal and rapid metabolism with no active metabolites
  * Depression of airway reflexes
  * Minimal cardiovascular compromise

**Bottom Line:** TIVA offers significant advantages including avoidance of volatile agent side effects and environmental benefits, but presents challenges with awareness risk due to inability to directly monitor drug concentrations and dependence on functioning IV access. The ideal agent would combine rapid, predictable pharmacokinetics with minimal side effects.

## 3.2. TIVA ADMINISTRATION TECHNIQUES







### 3.2.2. TARGET-CONTROLLED INFUSION (TCI) [PART B/C]

**Target-controlled infusion uses microprocessor-driven syringe pumps with pre-programmed pharmacokinetic models to achieve and maintain desired drug concentrations.**

* **Definition:** Syringe driver with pre-programmed microprocessor using three-compartment pharmacokinetic model for specific drugs [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]] `[LITFL:1]`
  * Use of pharmacokinetic models (typically combined with microprocessor-controlled infusion pumps) to achieve a target concentration of drug in a particular body compartment
* **System characteristics:** `[LITFL:1]`
  * **Open-loop systems:** Effects of drug are not measured (unlike with end-tidal gas monitoring), which introduces vulnerability that can lead to awareness
  * **Comparison to inhalational anesthetics:** Inhalational anesthetics use closed-loop monitoring with end-tidal drug monitoring
* **BET Principle:** TCI systems follow the **Bolus, Elimination, Transfer** principle `[LITFL:1]`
  * **Bolus (B):** Loading dose is given to saturate the volume of distribution to achieve target concentration
  * **Elimination (E):** Infusion rate compensates for drug elimination from the body
  * **Transfer (T):** Infusion rate compensates for drug distribution between compartments
* **Target adjustments:** `[LITFL:1]`
  * **For higher concentration:**
	* Small bolus is given to rapidly achieve new target
	* Infusion rate is increased to maintain higher level
  * **For lower concentration:**
	* Infusion is paused until desired level is reached
	* Infusion rate restarts at a lower rate to maintain new target
* **Pharmacokinetic models:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Propofol models:**
	* Marsh model
	* Schneider model
	* Eleveld model (performs better at extremes of age and BMI)
  * **Remifentanil model:** Minto model
* **Target types:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] `[LITFL:1]`
  * **Plasma target:** Traditional approach with Marsh model
	* Will not approximate effect site concentration until steady state is reached
	* Therefore target should be increased during induction so effect site concentration will rise more quickly
	* Should be adjusted to the level of the surgical stimulus
  * **Effect site (brain) target:** Traditional approach with Schneider model
	* Over-pressure occurs automatically, so there is no requirement to increase target during induction
	* More directly reflects the site of drug action
  * Modern machines allow either targeting method for both models
* **Functional aspects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Drug concentration and patient parameters (weight, age, height) programmed into driver
  * Algorithm determines infusion rate to achieve target concentration
  * ⚠️ No actual measurement of plasma/effect site concentrations (estimated by algorithm)
  * When target is changed:
	* Higher target → pump delivers bolus to rapidly establish new setpoint
	* Lower target → pump temporarily stops infusion before restarting at lower rate
* **Clinical markers:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Patients typically lose consciousness at effect site concentration of 2.5-3 μg/ml with propofol alone
  * With remifentanil, loss of consciousness at 2-2.5 μg/ml propofol
  * **Decrement time:** Time estimated by algorithm for blood concentration to drop to 1.2 μg/ml (approximate awakening threshold)

![TCI Target Comparison](IV_IV_TCI_Comparison.001.jpeg_0ee6e4.jpeg_43d416.jpeg)

* **Model comparisons:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] `[LITFL:1]`
  * **Development background:** Computer controlled models, both derived on very small groups of patients (18 and 24 respectively)
  * **Temporal differences:** Models differ mostly in the first 10 minutes after induction, and progressively converge over time
  * **Initial behavior:** The initial behavior of the model is key in deciding which model to apply to any particular patient
  * Marsh model:
	* Larger, weight-dependent central compartment
	* Generally delivers more propofol as initial bolus
	* Programs up to 150kg only
	* **Targeting characteristics:** `[LITFL:1]`
	  * Typically target plasma concentration, but can target effect site
	  * Effect site targeting is usually done with the modified Marsh model, due to the large bolus dosing given by the standard Marsh model
	* **Required variables:** Total body weight (TBW)
  * Schneider model:
	* Calculates lean body mass using James equation (underestimates BMI above 40)
	* Would give too much propofol in morbidly obese patients
	* **Targeting characteristics:** `[LITFL:1]`
	  * Typically effect site, but can target plasma concentration
	  * Plasma targeting gives inconsistent results, as the fixed size of V₁ means any increase in desired plasma concentration results in the same size bolus being given, irrespective of patient parameters
	* **Required variables:** Total body weight, height, age
  * Minto model:
	* Also uses James equation
	* Would give too little remifentanil in morbidly obese patients
  * Eleveld model:
	* Better performance at extremes of age and BMI
	* Generally results in quicker wake up than Marsh
* **Technical limitations:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Accuracy depends on accuracy of pharmacokinetic model
  * Pharmacokinetic variability between patients means actual plasma concentration may differ from target
  * FDA cited lack of precision as unacceptable risk (not approved in US)
  * ⚠️ Despite FDA concerns, extensive use outside US with good safety record

### 3.2.4. MANUAL TIVA ADMINISTRATION

**Manual TIVA uses calculated infusion rates based on established models to achieve and maintain anesthesia without TCI technology.**

* **Bristol model:** Most commonly used manual approach [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] `[LITFL:1]`
  * **Historical significance:** First pharmacokinetic model for propofol
  * **Based on:** Three-compartment model of healthy patients
  * **Assumptions:** `[LITFL:1]`
	* Premedication with temazepam
	* Fentanyl 3 μg/kg on induction
	* Inhaled nitrous oxide (N₂O)
	* Target plasma concentration of 3 μg/ml
  * Aims to achieve target plasma concentration of 3 μg/ml within 2 minutes
  * Assumes premedication with temazepam followed by induction with 3 μg/kg fentanyl
  * Protocol:
	* 1 mg/kg propofol bolus at start, then
	* 10 mg/kg/h infusion for 10 minutes, then
	* 8 mg/kg/h infusion for next 10 minutes, then
	* 6 mg/kg/h infusion thereafter
* **Limitations:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Generally administers more propofol than TCI models
  * Results in more hemodynamic instability
  * Produces slower wake up
  * Less precise than TCI

**Bottom Line:** Target-controlled infusion represents the most sophisticated approach to TIVA delivery, using pharmacokinetic models to estimate and achieve desired drug concentrations. Manual techniques are available but typically result in higher drug administration, more hemodynamic instability, and slower emergence.

## 3.3. CONTEXT-SENSITIVE HALF-TIME IN TIVA [PART A/B]







**Context-sensitive half-time (the time required for plasma concentration to decrease by 50% after stopping an infusion) profoundly influences drug selection and recovery from TIVA.**

* **Definition:** Time taken for concentration of drug in plasma to drop to half of previous steady-state concentration after discontinuing infusion [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Context:** Refers to duration of infusion
  * Differs from elimination half-life which applies to single-dose administration
* **Physiologic basis:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * During continuous infusion, drug redistributes from blood into other compartments (brain, muscle, fat)
  * Compartments become saturated when steady-state is reached
  * When infusion stops, plasma concentration drops as drug is cleared
  * Drug redistributes from compartments back into plasma, slowing the decrease in plasma concentration
  * Longer infusions result in more drug accumulation in peripheral compartments
  * More drug redistribution back to plasma means longer time to clear from body
* **Comparison between anesthetic agents:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

| Drug | Context-Sensitive Half-Time After 8-Hour Infusion |
|------|---------------------------------------------------|
| Remifentanil | 5 minutes |
| Propofol | 40 minutes |
| Alfentanil | 50 minutes |
| Thiopental | 150 minutes |
| Fentanyl | 250 minutes |

* **Infusion duration effect:** Context-sensitive half-time increases with infusion duration for most agents [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

| Infusion Length (Hours) | Propofol (minutes) | Alfentanil (minutes) | Fentanyl (minutes) |
|-------------------------|--------------------|-----------------------|---------------------|
| 2 | 20 | 40 | 40 |
| 6 | 30 | 50 | 240 |
| 9 | 50 | 80 | 300 |

* **Clinical implications:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Remifentanil exhibits context-sensitive half-time essentially independent of infusion duration
  * This unique property makes remifentanil ideal for prolonged TIVA
  * Propofol's relatively short context-sensitive half-time (compared to thiopental) makes it suitable for continuous infusion
  * Drugs with long context-sensitive half-times (thiopental, fentanyl) are poor choices for prolonged TIVA

**Bottom Line:** Understanding context-sensitive half-time is crucial for TIVA practice as it predicts recovery profiles after infusion discontinuation. Remifentanil and propofol have favorable profiles making them optimal choices for TIVA, while agents like thiopental and fentanyl have prolonged context-sensitive half-times limiting their utility for extended infusions.

## 3.4. PROPOFOL-REMIFENTANIL COMBINATION







**The propofol-remifentanil combination represents the most common TIVA approach due to their complementary properties and pharmacokinetic synergy.**

### 3.4.2. SYNERGISTIC EFFECTS

* **Pharmacodynamic synergy:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Synergistic effect means smaller doses of both drugs required to achieve adequate depth of anesthesia
  * Represented by the black line (equal clinical effect) on isobologram

![Propofol-Remifentanil Isobologram](IV_IV_Prop_Remi_Isobologram.001.jpeg_af3260.jpeg_f26556.jpeg)

* **Complementary properties:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Together very effective at obtunding laryngeal reflexes for intubation
  * Remifentanil provides potent analgesia while propofol provides hypnosis
  * Both have relatively rapid onset and predictable offset

### 3.4.3. PRACTICAL ADMINISTRATION APPROACHES

* **Timing considerations:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Propofol lasts longer than remifentanil
  * If switched off together, propofol will be the limiting factor determining wake-up time
* **Starting options:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Simultaneous start:** Both infusions started together, especially if both programmed to use effect site target
  * **Remifentanil first:** If started first and allowed to equilibrate, less propofol needed for induction
	* ⚠️ Risk of apnoea; patient needs to be reminded to breathe
	* Technique often used in awake tracheal intubation
* **Target concentrations:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Remifentanil:**
	* Induction: 4-8 ng/ml
	* Maintenance: 3-6 ng/ml
	* End of surgery: 1-2 ng/ml (facilitates smooth extubation)
	* ⚠️ Minto model gives initial bolus dose 3x higher if effect site target used versus plasma target
* **Post-operative considerations:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Remifentanil provides negligible post-operative analgesia due to rapid metabolism
  * Requires alternative analgesic options (regional anesthesia, longer-acting opioids)
  * Hyperalgesia possible after rapid withdrawal of high-dose remifentanil infusions
	* Thought due to down-regulation of μ receptors
	* Prevented by ensuring longer-acting opioid is given at least 30 minutes before stopping remifentanil

**Bottom Line:** The propofol-remifentanil combination offers excellent synergy for TIVA with complementary hypnotic and analgesic properties. The extremely short context-sensitive half-time of remifentanil provides precise control over analgesia, though requiring careful planning for post-operative pain management due to its rapid offset.

## 3.5. SAFETY CONSIDERATIONS AND RISKS [PART B/C]







**TIVA presents unique safety challenges, primarily related to the risk of accidental awareness due to inability to measure drug concentrations directly.**

* **Open-loop system vulnerabilities:** `[LITFL:1]` [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * **TCI systems are open-loop:** Effects of drug are not measured (unlike with end-tidal gas monitoring)
  * **Comparison to volatile anesthetics:** Inhalational anesthetics provide closed-loop monitoring through end-tidal concentration measurement
  * **Awareness vulnerability:** This introduces a vulnerability that can lead to awareness, as actual drug effect cannot be directly confirmed
* **Primary risks:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Accidental awareness:
	* Difficult to measure depth of anesthesia (no equivalent to end-tidal agent monitoring)
	* Cannula dislodgement, especially if hidden under drapes
  * Equipment failure
* **Safety features in TIVA pumps:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Pump calculates TCI targets using two separate circuits, delivers drug only if calculations match
  * Should be plugged into mains power and serviced within last 12 months
  * Anti-syphon valves
  * High and low pressure alarms
* **Clinical safety practices:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Calibrating target concentration by noting plasma concentration at which patient loses consciousness
	* Can also note loss of response to voice and jaw thrust
  * ⚠️ Neuromuscular blockade should not be given until no response to jaw thrust
  * Co-administration of benzodiazepine to reduce awareness risk
  * Use of depth of anesthesia monitoring (e.g., BIS)
  * Having a backup plan if pump or cannula fails
  * Maintaining TIVA cannula visibility where possible with fluids running to demonstrate patency
  * ⚠️ Not labeling remifentanil syringe until drug has been drawn up (to prevent mix-ups)
* **Emergency management:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * If TIVA pump fails mid-operation:
	* Cannot simply restart as algorithm "forgets" previous administration
	* Options include:
	  * Switch to volatile anesthetics (may cause significant hemodynamic effects)
	  * Switch to manual TIVA using rate from prior to failure, or using Bristol model

**Bottom Line:** TIVA safety depends on proper equipment functioning, vigilant monitoring, and preventive strategies to detect or address IV access problems, awareness risk, and equipment failure. Unlike inhalational anesthesia, direct measurement of drug concentrations is not possible, making sound safety practices essential.

## 3.6. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS AND BENEFITS







### 3.6.2. INDICATIONS [PART B/C]

**TIVA offers specific advantages for selected patient populations and clinical scenarios.**

* **Patient-specific indications:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Known risk of malignant hyperthermia
  * History of PONV
  * Long QT syndrome
  * Neuromuscular disease where paralysis ideally avoided
* **Procedure-specific indications:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Need for "tubeless" surgery where volatiles are impractical (e.g., bronchoscopy, tracheostomy)
  * Anticipated difficult intubation
  * Need for smooth extubation
  * Neurosurgery
  * Surgery requiring neuromuscular monitoring
  * Intraoperative wake-up but maintaining analgesia (e.g., awake craniotomy)

### 3.6.3. BENEFITS

**TIVA offers several advantages over inhalational anesthesia in specific contexts.**

* **Recovery benefits:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Reduced PONV
  * Smoother wake-up without laryngospasm
* **Avoidance of volatile agent issues:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * No fluoride ion production
  * Avoids possible link to cancer recurrence (particularly in breast cancer with sevoflurane)
  * Avoids malignant hyperthermia triggers
* **Logistical advantages:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Easier in cases with difficult volatile delivery:
	* Bronchoscopy
	* Tracheostomy
	* Off-site locations without anesthesia machines

**Bottom Line:** TIVA provides specific advantages for patients at risk for MH, PONV, or requiring procedures where volatile delivery is challenging. It offers smoother emergence and may be preferable for neurosurgical procedures requiring neurophysiological monitoring or procedures requiring intraoperative wake-up tests.

## 3.7. SPECIAL POPULATIONS







### 3.7.2. MORBIDLY OBESE PATIENTS

**TIVA can be used effectively in morbidly obese patients with appropriate adjustments to dosing and models.**

* **Model limitations:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * TCI models not specifically validated for morbid obesity but can be used
  * Servin's formula can be used to calculate input mass
* **Model-specific considerations:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Marsh model only programs up to 150kg
  * Schneider and Minto models calculate lean body mass using James equation, which underestimates BMI above 40
	* For complex algorithmic reasons, Schneider would give too much propofol and Minto would give too little remifentanil
* **Alternative approaches:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Eleveld model may perform better at extremes of BMI
  * Generally results in quicker wake-up than Marsh model

### 3.7.3. PROPOFOL-RELATED INFUSION SYNDROME RISK [PART B/C]

**Prolonged high-dose propofol infusions can rarely cause a potentially fatal metabolic syndrome.**

* **Description:** Syndrome associated with prolonged high-dose propofol infusions [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Most commonly an issue in pediatric practice
  * Thought to be caused by metabolic dysfunction and mitochondrial uncoupling
  * Results from abnormally high levels of fat in the blood
* **Diagnostic features:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Metabolic acidosis and cardiovascular dysfunction, plus at least one of:
	* Rhabdomyolysis
	* Hypertriglyceridemia
	* Renal failure
* **Prevention:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Avoid prolonged high-dose propofol infusions, particularly in children
  * Monitor for early signs of metabolic disturbance
  * Consider alternative sedatives for long-term use

**Bottom Line:** TIVA can be safely used in special populations including morbidly obese patients with appropriate modifications to dosing models. Vigilance for rare but serious complications like propofol infusion syndrome is essential, particularly with prolonged high-dose infusions.

## 4. MALIGNANT HYPERTHERMIA CRISIS MANAGEMENT
<!-- EPA-2:K3:crisis_management EPA-2:EQ2:mh_recognition EPA-2:S3:emergency_response -->

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|Part A: 2.2]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2: Sedation and General Anaesthesia|Part B: C2]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Patient Care|Part C: PC]]

**Malignant hyperthermia (MH) is a potentially fatal pharmacogenetic disorder of skeletal muscle triggered by volatile anesthetics and succinylcholine, requiring immediate recognition and treatment with dantrolene.**

### 4.1. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND GENETICS

#### 4.1.1. MECHANISM

**MH results from uncontrolled calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum** [StatPearls]:

* **Triggering event**: Volatile anesthetic or succinylcholine exposure in genetically susceptible individual
* **Cellular mechanism**: Defective ryanodine receptor (RYR1) allows uncontrolled Ca²⁺ release from SR
* **Consequences of sustained muscle contraction**:
  * ATP depletion (sustained contraction requires energy)
  * Increased oxygen consumption
  * Increased CO₂ production
  * Excessive heat generation
  * Rhabdomyolysis with release of K⁺, CK, myoglobin

#### 4.1.2. GENETIC BASIS

**MH is inherited as autosomal dominant with variable penetrance**:

| Gene | Protein | Frequency |
|------|---------|-----------|
| **RYR1** | Ryanodine receptor type 1 | Most common (~70%) |
| **CACNA1S** | L-type calcium channel α-subunit | ~1% |
| **STAC3** | Signal transduction adaptor | Rare |

**Associated conditions**:
* Central core disease
* King-Denborough syndrome
* Multiminicore myopathy

⚠️ **Safety Warning**: A previous uneventful general anesthetic does NOT exclude MH susceptibility. Each exposure to triggering agents carries risk in susceptible individuals.

### 4.2. TRIGGERING AGENTS

#### 4.2.1. KNOWN TRIGGERS (MUST AVOID IN MH-SUSCEPTIBLE PATIENTS)

**All volatile anesthetics and succinylcholine are triggering agents** [MHAUS]:

* **Volatile anesthetics**: Desflurane, sevoflurane, isoflurane, enflurane, halothane
* **Depolarizing muscle relaxant**: Succinylcholine

**Key points**:
* Triggering is dose-dependent but minimum triggering dose unknown
* Modern agents (desflurane, sevoflurane) can cause delayed reactions (hours into anesthesia)
* Combined trigger exposure (volatile + succinylcholine) may cause more severe reactions

#### 4.2.2. SAFE DRUGS (NON-TRIGGERING)

**All of the following are safe in MH-susceptible patients**:

| Drug Class | Examples |
|------------|----------|
| **IV anesthetics** | Propofol, ketamine, etomidate, barbiturates |
| **Opioids** | All opioids safe |
| **Benzodiazepines** | Midazolam, diazepam, lorazepam |
| **Non-depolarizing NMBs** | Rocuronium, vecuronium, cisatracurium, atracurium |
| **Local anesthetics** | All local anesthetics safe (amides and esters) |
| **Inhaled gases** | Nitrous oxide, xenon |
| **Other** | Dexmedetomidine, sugammadex |

### 4.3. CLINICAL PRESENTATION

#### 4.3.1. EARLY SIGNS

**Unexplained rising ETCO₂ is often the earliest sign** [MHAUS]:

| Sign | Characteristics |
|------|-----------------|
| **Elevated ETCO₂** | Earliest sign; rises despite adequate ventilation |
| **Tachycardia** | Often unexplained by surgical stimulus |
| **Masseter spasm** | Especially after succinylcholine (MMR - masseter muscle rigidity) |
| **Tachypnea** | In spontaneously breathing patients |

💡 **Clinical Pearl**: Increased ETCO₂ is the earliest sign when not preceded by masseter spasm. Earlier diagnosis reduces incidence of severe metabolic acidosis and complications.

#### 4.3.2. LATE SIGNS

**Progressive signs indicate fulminant MH crisis**:

* **Hyperthermia**: May rise 1-2°C every 5 minutes (late sign)
* **Generalized muscle rigidity**: Indicates sustained contraction
* **Metabolic acidosis**: Base deficit >-8
* **Respiratory acidosis**: Despite increasing minute ventilation
* **Hyperkalemia**: From rhabdomyolysis
* **Elevated CK**: May exceed 20,000 IU/L
* **Myoglobinuria**: Dark urine; risk of renal failure
* **Cardiac dysrhythmias**: VT, VF (from hyperkalemia and acidosis)
* **DIC**: In severe cases

#### 4.3.3. CLINICAL GRADING SCALE (CGS)

**Six categories are assessed to determine MH likelihood** [Larach et al.]:

1. Muscle rigidity
2. Rhabdomyolysis
3. Respiratory acidosis
4. Temperature increase
5. Cardiac involvement
6. Family history

**Interpretation**: Raw score correlates with ranks 1-6 (1 = unlikely; 6 = almost certain)

### 4.4. TREATMENT PROTOCOL (MHAUS GUIDELINES)

#### 4.4.1. IMMEDIATE ACTIONS

**The MHAUS emergency protocol must be initiated immediately upon suspicion** [MHAUS]:

| Priority | Action | Details |
|----------|--------|---------|
| **1** | **STOP TRIGGERS** | Discontinue volatiles AND succinylcholine immediately |
| **2** | **CALL FOR HELP** | MHAUS Hotline: 1-800-644-9737 (US) |
| **3** | **HYPERVENTILATE** | 100% O₂ at 10 L/min; flush volatiles |
| **4** | **GIVE DANTROLENE** | 2.5 mg/kg IV rapidly (see dosing below) |
| **5** | **NOTIFY SURGEON** | Halt or expedite surgery as appropriate |

**If surgery must continue**: Maintain anesthesia with IV non-triggering agents (propofol, opioids, benzodiazepines, non-depolarizing NMBs)

**Activated charcoal filters**: Can reduce volatile concentration to <5 ppm within 3 minutes if available

#### 4.4.2. DANTROLENE ADMINISTRATION

**Dantrolene is the ONLY specific treatment for MH** [MHAUS]:

**Mechanism**: Inhibits calcium release from SR via RYR1 antagonism

**Dosing**:
* **Initial dose**: 2.5 mg/kg IV rapidly through large-bore IV
* **Repeat**: Every 5-10 minutes until response (↓ETCO₂, ↓rigidity, ↓HR)
* **Maximum**: >10 mg/kg may be required; if no response, consider alternative diagnosis

**Formulations and Reconstitution**:

| Product | Vial Content | Reconstitution | Vials per 70 kg patient |
|---------|--------------|----------------|------------------------|
| **Dantrium®/Revonto®** | 20 mg | 60 mL sterile water | ~9 vials for 2.5 mg/kg |
| **Ryanodex®** | 250 mg | 5 mL sterile water | ~1 vial for 2.5 mg/kg |

**Stocking requirements (MHAUS)**:
* Dantrium/Revonto: 36 vials
* Ryanodex: 3 vials

⚠️ **Critical timing**: Medications must be available within 10 minutes. Complication rate increases 1.6x for every 30-minute delay. Delay >50 minutes = 100% complication rate.

#### 4.4.3. SUPPORTIVE MANAGEMENT

**Hyperkalemia Treatment (K⁺ >5.9 mEq/L or ECG changes)**:

| Treatment | Dose |
|-----------|------|
| **Calcium chloride** | 10 mg/kg IV (max 2000 mg) |
| **Calcium gluconate** | 10-50 mg/kg IV (max 3000 mg) |
| **Sodium bicarbonate** | 1-2 mEq/kg IV |
| **Glucose + Insulin** | Adult: 10 units regular insulin + 50 mL D50<br>Pediatric: 0.1 units/kg + 2 mL/kg D25 |

**Acidosis Management**:
* Sodium bicarbonate 1-2 mEq/kg for base excess >-8
* Continue hyperventilation

**Temperature Management**:
* Cool if core temperature >39°C (or rapidly rising)
* Methods: Cold IV fluids, ice packs, cold lavage, cooling blankets
* **Stop cooling when temperature <38°C** (avoid hypothermia)

**Dysrhythmia Management**:
* Standard ACLS medications
* ⚠️ **AVOID calcium channel blockers** (verapamil, diltiazem) - interaction with dantrolene causes hyperkalemia and cardiovascular collapse

**Renal Protection**:
* Target urine output >1 mL/kg/hr
* Sodium bicarbonate infusion 1 mEq/kg/hr to alkalinize urine if myoglobinuria present

### 4.5. MONITORING AND POST-CRISIS CARE

#### 4.5.1. MONITORING PARAMETERS

**Essential monitoring during MH crisis**:

| Parameter | Purpose |
|-----------|---------|
| Core temperature | Track hyperthermia |
| ETCO₂ | Earliest response indicator |
| Arterial/central venous access | Blood gases, drug administration |
| Urine output | Renal protection |
| Serial blood gases | Acidosis monitoring |
| Potassium | Hyperkalemia risk |
| CK | Rhabdomyolysis progression |
| Coagulation studies | DIC surveillance |

#### 4.5.2. POST-CRISIS MANAGEMENT

**Transfer to ICU for minimum 24 hours monitoring** [MHAUS]:

**Continued dantrolene therapy**:
* 1 mg/kg IV every 4-6 hours for at least 24 hours
* Can extend interval to 8-12 hours if stable

**Discontinuation criteria**:
- [ ] Metabolic stability for 24 hours
- [ ] Core temperature <38°C
- [ ] CK decreasing
- [ ] No ongoing myoglobinuria
- [ ] Muscle rigidity resolved

#### 4.5.3. RECRUDESCENCE

**MH can recur after initial successful treatment** [MHAUS]:

* **Incidence**: ~25% of treated patients
* **Timing**: 50% within 9 hours; 80% within 16 hours
* **Signs**: Muscle rigidity, rising CK, respiratory acidosis, hyperthermia
* **Management**: Resume dantrolene boluses; continue supportive care

### 4.6. MH-SUSCEPTIBLE PATIENT MANAGEMENT

#### 4.6.1. ANESTHESIA MACHINE PREPARATION

**Goal: Deliver <5 ppm of any volatile anesthetic** [BJA Education]:

**Standard preparation**:
* Remove/disable vaporizers
* Replace all circuit components (breathing circuit, bellows, CO₂ absorbent)
* Flush with high-flow O₂ (10 L/min) for ≥20 minutes

**Alternative**: Use activated charcoal filters
* Can achieve <5 ppm within 3 minutes
* Replace after 1 hour of use

#### 4.6.2. SAFE ANESTHETIC TECHNIQUE

**TIVA with non-triggering agents is the standard approach**:

* **Induction**: Propofol, fentanyl, non-depolarizing NMB
* **Maintenance**: Propofol infusion ± remifentanil (TIVA)
* **Muscle relaxation**: Rocuronium (reversible with sugammadex)
* **Analgesia**: Regional techniques, opioids, non-opioid adjuncts

⚠️ **NO prophylactic dantrolene**: Not recommended as it causes significant muscle weakness and is unnecessary if triggers avoided

**Have dantrolene immediately available** (within 10 minutes)

### 4.7. DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS

**Conditions that may mimic MH**:

| Condition | Distinguishing Features |
|-----------|------------------------|
| Neuroleptic malignant syndrome | Dopamine antagonist exposure; days to develop |
| Serotonin syndrome | Serotonergic drug exposure; hyperreflexia, clonus |
| Thyroid storm | History of thyrotoxicosis; eye signs |
| Pheochromocytoma | Episodic; hypertension prominent |
| Sepsis | Source of infection; hypotension |
| Heat stroke | Environmental exposure; CNS dysfunction |
| Iatrogenic overheating | Equipment malfunction (Bair Hugger) |
| Soda lime reaction | Check CO₂ absorbent color |

**Bottom Line:** Malignant hyperthermia is a medical emergency requiring immediate recognition (rising ETCO₂, tachycardia, rigidity) and treatment with dantrolene 2.5 mg/kg IV, repeated until response. Stop all triggering agents, hyperventilate with 100% O₂, and provide supportive care for hyperkalemia, acidosis, and hyperthermia. Avoid calcium channel blockers. Post-crisis monitoring in ICU for 24 hours is essential due to recrudescence risk.

[Sources: MHAUS Guidelines 2024; StatPearls; EMHG Guidelines 2024; BJA Education]

# 5. ICU SEDATION PROTOCOLS
**PADIS Guidelines 2018 and KSCCM 2021**

## 5.1. SEDATION ASSESSMENT
**Standardised tools for measuring sedation depth in critically ill patients.**

### 5.1.1. RICHMOND AGITATION-SEDATION SCALE (RASS)
**The most widely validated sedation assessment tool in ICU.**

| Score | Term | Description | Assessment Method |
|-------|------|-------------|-------------------|
| **+4** | Combative | Violent, immediate danger to staff | Observe |
| **+3** | Very agitated | Pulls at/removes tubes, aggressive | Observe |
| **+2** | Agitated | Frequent non-purposeful movements, fights ventilator | Observe |
| **+1** | Restless | Anxious, apprehensive, movements not aggressive | Observe |
| **0** | Alert & calm | Baseline | Observe |
| **-1** | Drowsy | Not fully alert, sustained awakening to voice (>10 sec) | Verbal stimulus |
| **-2** | Light sedation | Briefly awakens to voice (eye opening <10 sec) | Verbal stimulus |
| **-3** | Moderate sedation | Movement/eye-opening to voice (no eye contact) | Verbal stimulus |
| **-4** | Deep sedation | No response to voice, movement to physical stimulus | Physical stimulus |
| **-5** | Unrousable | No response to voice or physical stimulation | Physical stimulus |

* **Target sedation level**: Light sedation (RASS -1 to -2) for most patients
* **Assessment frequency**: At least every 4 hours; more often if titrating
* 🔑 **HY**: RASS and SAS (Sedation-Agitation Scale) are the most valid and reliable sedation scales

### 5.1.2. SEDATION-AGITATION SCALE (SAS)
**Alternative sedation scoring system.**

| Score | Level | Description |
|-------|-------|-------------|
| 7 | Dangerous agitation | Pulling at ETT, thrashing, striking staff |
| 6 | Very agitated | Requires restraint, frequent verbal reminding |
| 5 | Agitated | Anxious/mildly agitated, attempts to sit, calms to verbal |
| 4 | Calm and cooperative | Calm, awakens easily, follows commands |
| 3 | Sedated | Difficult to arouse, awakens to verbal/gentle shaking |
| 2 | Very sedated | Arouses to physical stimuli but doesn't communicate |
| 1 | Unrousable | Minimal/no response to noxious stimuli |

**Bottom Line:** Regular sedation assessment using validated scales (RASS or SAS) is essential for ICU sedation management, with light sedation (RASS -1 to -2) targeted for most mechanically ventilated patients.

## 5.2. DELIRIUM ASSESSMENT
**Systematic screening for ICU delirium using validated tools.**

### 5.2.1. CAM-ICU (CONFUSION ASSESSMENT METHOD FOR ICU)
**Four-feature diagnostic tool for ICU delirium.**

* **Prerequisite**: Patient must have RASS ≥-3 (cannot assess if RASS -4 or -5)

#### FEATURE 1: Acute Onset or Fluctuating Course
* Baseline mental status change from pre-admission OR
* Fluctuation in mental status in past 24 hours (RASS variation)
* If YES → proceed to Feature 2

#### FEATURE 2: Inattention
* **ASE Letters Test**: Read 10 letters (SAVEAHAART), patient squeezes hand on "A"
* **Error**: Squeeze on non-A OR no squeeze on A
* **Positive**: >2 errors out of 10
* If positive → proceed to Features 3 and 4

#### FEATURE 3: Disorganized Thinking
* Ask 4 yes/no questions (e.g., "Will a stone float on water?")
* Ask to perform simple command (e.g., "Hold up this many fingers")
* **Positive**: Combined >1 error

#### FEATURE 4: Altered Level of Consciousness
* RASS score other than 0 (either agitated or sedated)

**Positive CAM-ICU** = Features 1 AND 2, PLUS (Feature 3 OR 4)

* **Sensitivity**: 93-100%
* **Specificity**: 89-100%
* **Assessment frequency**: At least twice daily
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: CAM-ICU takes <2 minutes to perform once trained

### 5.2.2. ICDSC (INTENSIVE CARE DELIRIUM SCREENING CHECKLIST)
**Alternative 8-item delirium screening tool.**

| Item | Feature |
|------|---------|
| 1 | Altered level of consciousness |
| 2 | Inattention |
| 3 | Disorientation |
| 4 | Hallucination, delusion, psychosis |
| 5 | Psychomotor agitation or retardation |
| 6 | Inappropriate speech or mood |
| 7 | Sleep/wake cycle disturbance |
| 8 | Symptom fluctuation |

* **Scoring**: 1 point per feature present
* **Positive**: Score ≥4
* **Sensitivity**: 99%; **Specificity**: 64%

### 5.2.3. DELIRIUM SUBTYPES

| Type | Characteristics | RASS | Prognosis |
|------|-----------------|------|-----------|
| **Hyperactive** | Agitation, restlessness, combativeness | +1 to +4 | Better recognition |
| **Hypoactive** | Decreased responsiveness, withdrawal, lethargy | -1 to -3 | Often missed; worse outcome |
| **Mixed** | Fluctuates between hyper- and hypoactive | Variable | Most common subtype |

* ⚠️ **Hypoactive delirium** is frequently missed and associated with worse outcomes

**Bottom Line:** Delirium screening using CAM-ICU or ICDSC should occur at least twice daily for all ICU patients; hypoactive delirium is commonly missed and carries poorer prognosis than hyperactive forms.

## 5.3. SEDATIVE AGENT SELECTION
**Evidence-based selection of ICU sedation agents based on PADIS guidelines.**

### 5.3.1. PROPOFOL
**First-line agent for short-term sedation in ICU.**

* **Mechanism**: GABA-A receptor potentiation
* **Advantages**:
	* Rapid onset and offset
	* Allows frequent neurological assessment
	* Preferred for patients requiring rapid awakening
	* Titratable
* **Dosing**:
	* Infusion: 5-50 mcg/kg/min (up to 200 mcg/kg/min in some patients)
	* No loading dose for ICU sedation
* **Limitations**:
	* Hypotension (dose-dependent)
	* Respiratory depression
	* Propofol Infusion Syndrome (PRIS) with prolonged high-dose use

#### PROPOFOL INFUSION SYNDROME (PRIS)
* **Definition**: Rare but potentially fatal complication of propofol infusion
* **Risk factors**:
	* Dose >4 mg/kg/hr (67 mcg/kg/min)
	* Duration >48 hours
	* Catecholamine infusion
	* Critical illness
* **Features**: Metabolic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalemia, cardiac failure, lipemic plasma
* **Prevention**: Monitor lactate, CK; avoid >4 mg/kg/hr for >48 hours
* ⚠️ **PRIS is often fatal; early recognition is critical**

### 5.3.2. DEXMEDETOMIDINE
**Alpha-2 agonist preferred for light sedation and delirium reduction.**

* **Mechanism**: Central alpha-2 agonist in locus coeruleus
* **Advantages**:
	* "Cooperative sedation" - easily rousable
	* Minimal respiratory depression
	* Reduces delirium incidence vs benzodiazepines
	* Analgesic properties (opioid-sparing)
	* Anxiolytic without amnesia
* **Dosing**:
	* Infusion: 0.2-1.4 mcg/kg/hr (typically 0.4-0.7 mcg/kg/hr)
	* Loading dose: Generally avoided in ICU (causes hypotension, bradycardia)
* **Limitations**:
	* Suitable only for RASS 0 to -3 (not deep sedation)
	* Bradycardia (heart block risk)
	* Hypotension
	* Cost
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: Dexmedetomidine mimics natural sleep pathways

### 5.3.3. BENZODIAZEPINES
**Reserved for specific indications only.**

* **Agents**: Midazolam, lorazepam
* **Mechanism**: GABA-A receptor potentiation
* **Current recommendation**: NOT first-line for ICU sedation
* **Specific indications**:
	* Alcohol withdrawal
	* Seizure management
	* Procedural sedation
	* Deep sedation when absolutely required
* **Limitations**:
	* Prolonged mechanical ventilation
	* Increased ICU length of stay
	* Increased delirium risk (independent predictor)
	* Accumulation with prolonged use (especially lorazepam)
* ⚠️ **PADIS 2018**: Recommend propofol or dexmedetomidine OVER benzodiazepines
* **Duration limit**: Restrict midazolam to <48-72 hours when used

### 5.3.4. SEDATIVE COMPARISON

| Parameter | Propofol | Dexmedetomidine | Midazolam |
|-----------|----------|-----------------|-----------|
| Onset | Rapid (1-2 min) | 15-30 min | 2-5 min |
| Offset | Rapid | Intermediate | Prolonged |
| Delirium risk | Lower | Lowest | Highest |
| Respiratory depression | Yes | Minimal | Yes |
| Hemodynamic effects | Hypotension | Bradycardia, hypotension | Hypotension |
| Deep sedation | Yes | No (RASS ≥-3 only) | Yes |
| Recommended use | First-line | First-line (light) | Limited |

**Bottom Line:** Propofol or dexmedetomidine are preferred over benzodiazepines for ICU sedation; dexmedetomidine offers the advantage of reduced delirium and minimal respiratory depression, while benzodiazepines should be reserved for alcohol withdrawal, seizures, or when deeper sedation is absolutely required.

## 5.4. SEDATION STRATEGIES
**Evidence-based approaches to optimise sedation delivery.**

### 5.4.1. LIGHT VS DEEP SEDATION
**Light sedation is the preferred target for most ICU patients.**

* **Target**: RASS -1 to -2 (or -2 to +1 in some protocols)
* **Benefits of light sedation**:
	* Shorter weaning time from mechanical ventilation
	* Reduced tracheostomy frequency
	* Shorter ICU length of stay
	* Does NOT increase unplanned self-extubation rates
	* Allows better neurological assessment
* **When deep sedation may be required**:
	* ARDS with refractory hypoxemia
	* Neuromuscular blockade
	* Status epilepticus
	* Severe agitation uncontrolled by other measures
	* Specific procedures

### 5.4.2. DAILY SEDATION INTERRUPTION (DSI)
**Also known as "Sedation Holiday" or Spontaneous Awakening Trial (SAT).**

* **Protocol**: Stop sedative infusion(s) daily until patient awakens (RASS 0 to +1)
* **Benefits**:
	* Reduces total sedative consumption
	* Shorter duration of mechanical ventilation
	* Shorter ICU length of stay
	* Allows neurological assessment
* **Safety screen before SAT**:
	* No neuromuscular blockade
	* Not receiving deep sedation for specific indication
	* No uncontrolled seizures
	* No severe agitation
	* No active myocardial ischemia
* **Restart criteria**: Resume sedation at 50% of prior dose if significant agitation

### 5.4.3. NURSE-PROTOCOLIZED SEDATION
**Sedation titration according to predefined protocols.**

* **Principle**: Nurses adjust sedation to maintain RASS target
* **Evidence**: Reduces sedation depth and duration
* **Combined approach**: Protocol + DSI offers best outcomes
* 🏥 **Clinical Practice**: Most units combine protocolized sedation with DSI

### 5.4.4. ABCDEF BUNDLE
**Multicomponent care bundle for ICU liberation.**

| Letter | Component | Key Elements |
|--------|-----------|--------------|
| **A** | Assess, prevent, manage pain | Regular pain assessment; analgesia-first approach |
| **B** | Both SAT and SBT | Coordinate awakening and breathing trials |
| **C** | Choice of analgesia and sedation | Light sedation target; avoid benzodiazepines |
| **D** | Delirium assessment and management | CAM-ICU twice daily; non-pharmacologic interventions |
| **E** | Early mobility and exercise | Progressive mobilisation; PT/OT involvement |
| **F** | Family engagement | Family presence; shared decision-making |

* **Evidence**: Significantly reduces delirium, shortens MV duration, reduces mortality
* **Implementation**: Coordinate SAT with SBT (SAT before SBT)
* 🔑 **HY**: The ABCDEF bundle is strongly recommended for all ICU patients

**Bottom Line:** Light sedation targeting RASS -1 to -2 with daily sedation interruption, nurse-protocolized titration, and implementation of the ABCDEF bundle represents best practice for ICU sedation management.

## 5.5. DELIRIUM PREVENTION AND TREATMENT
**Evidence-based strategies for managing ICU delirium.**

### 5.5.1. RISK FACTORS FOR ICU DELIRIUM

| Modifiable | Non-Modifiable |
|------------|----------------|
| Benzodiazepine use | Advanced age |
| Blood transfusions | Dementia/cognitive impairment |
| Immobility | Prior coma |
| Pain | Emergency surgery |
| Sleep disruption | Trauma |
| Sensory deprivation | High APACHE/ASA score |

### 5.5.2. NON-PHARMACOLOGIC PREVENTION
**First-line approach for delirium prevention (PADIS).**

* **Orientation strategies**: Clocks, calendars, familiar objects
* **Sleep promotion**: Reduce nighttime noise, minimise interruptions, cluster care
* **Sensory aids**: Ensure glasses and hearing aids available
* **Early mobilisation**: Progressive mobility as tolerated
* **Family involvement**: Familiar faces, communication
* **Avoid deliriogenic medications**: Anticholinergics, benzodiazepines
* **Ensure adequate pain control**: Using opioid-sparing approaches where possible

### 5.5.3. PHARMACOLOGIC PREVENTION
**NOT recommended for routine delirium prevention.**

* ⚠️ **PADIS 2018**: Do NOT use haloperidol, atypical antipsychotics, statins, or ketamine for delirium prevention

### 5.5.4. PHARMACOLOGIC TREATMENT

* **First-line**: Non-pharmacologic interventions
* **Antipsychotics**:
	* NOT recommended for routine use
	* Consider short-term, low-dose only for severe agitation causing distress or safety risk
	* Atypical antipsychotics preferred over haloperidol (fewer EPS)
	* **Haloperidol dose if used**: 0.5-1 mg IV q2-4h PRN (max 20 mg/day)
	* ⚠️ Monitor QTc (risk of torsades de pointes)
* **Dexmedetomidine**:
	* Consider if agitation precludes weaning/extubation
	* May reduce hyperactive delirium duration
	* Alternative to antipsychotics in agitated delirium

**Bottom Line:** Non-pharmacologic interventions are the cornerstone of delirium prevention and management; routine pharmacologic prevention is not recommended, and antipsychotics should be reserved for severe agitation causing distress or safety risks.

## 5.6. SEDATION WEANING AND WITHDRAWAL
**Strategies for discontinuing sedation and managing withdrawal.**

### 5.6.1. WEANING PROTOCOLS

* **Daily Sedation Interruption**: Standard approach
* **Systematic dose reduction**:
	* Initial reduction: 20-40% of dose
	* Subsequent: 10% every 12-24 hours based on response
	* Monitor for withdrawal signs

### 5.6.2. SEDATION WITHDRAWAL SYNDROMES
**Recognising and managing withdrawal from prolonged sedation.**

* **Risk factors**:
	* Infusion >7 days
	* High-dose therapy
	* Abrupt discontinuation
* **Signs of withdrawal**:
	* Agitation, anxiety
	* Tremor
	* Sweating, tachycardia
	* Hypertension
	* Nausea, vomiting
	* Seizures (benzodiazepine withdrawal)

| Agent | Withdrawal Features | Management |
|-------|---------------------|------------|
| Benzodiazepines | Seizures, autonomic instability | Slow taper; consider clonazepam |
| Opioids | Diarrhea, piloerection, yawning | Slow taper; consider methadone |
| Propofol | Less common; rebound agitation | Usually not significant |

### 5.6.3. ADJUNCTS FOR WITHDRAWAL MANAGEMENT

* **Clonidine**: Alpha-2 agonist; reduces sympathetic hyperactivity
* **Dexmedetomidine**: Alternative to clonidine; sedative properties
* **Methadone**: For opioid withdrawal (long half-life)

**Bottom Line:** Prolonged sedation (>7 days) carries risk of withdrawal syndrome; gradual dose reduction and monitoring for withdrawal signs are essential, with adjunctive agents like clonidine or dexmedetomidine available for management.

[Sources: PADIS Guidelines 2018; KSCCM Guidelines 2021; BJA Education; ICU Liberation Bundle SCCM]

## 6.1. PRINCIPLES OF DRUG TRANSFER TO BREAST MILK

### 6.1.1. MECHANISMS OF DRUG TRANSFER

**Drug transfer to breast milk occurs primarily by passive diffusion from maternal plasma, with concentration dependent on several pharmacokinetic properties:**

* **Lipid solubility** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|i]]
  * Highly lipophilic drugs penetrate breast milk more readily
  * Most anaesthetic agents are lipophilic but have short redistribution half-lives
* **Protein binding** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|i]]
  * Only unbound (free) drug can cross into milk
  * Less protein-bound medications transfer more easily
  * Propofol (98% protein bound) has very limited transfer
* **Molecular weight** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|i]]
  * Lower molecular weight (<500 Da) correlates with greater penetration
  * Large molecules (e.g., NMBAs, sugammadex) have minimal transfer
* **pKa and ionization** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|i]]
  * Breast milk is slightly more acidic than plasma (pH ~7.0 vs 7.4)
  * Weakly basic drugs (higher pKa) may become "ion trapped" in milk
  * Most anaesthetics have rapid redistribution despite this effect
* **Maternal plasma concentration** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|i]]
  * Drug concentration in maternal blood proportionally affects milk levels
  * As drugs redistribute from plasma, milk levels decline in parallel

### 6.1.2. RELATIVE INFANT DOSE (RID)

**The Relative Infant Dose (RID) is the key safety metric for breastfeeding drug compatibility:**

* **Definition:** Percentage of the weight-adjusted maternal dose that the infant receives via breast milk
* **Formula:** RID = (Infant dose via milk / Maternal dose per kg) x 100
* **Safety threshold:** RID <10% is generally considered safe for the infant [Source: ASA Statement 2019]
* **Anaesthetic drugs:** Nearly all anaesthetic drugs have RID values significantly <10%

| Drug Class | Typical RID | Safety Assessment |
|------------|-------------|-------------------|
| Propofol | <1% | Very safe |
| Fentanyl | ~1% | Very safe |
| Morphine | ~9% | Safe with monitoring |
| Local anaesthetics | <0.3% | Very safe |
| Volatile agents | Negligible | Very safe |
| Ondansetron | ~3.7% | Very safe |

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The 10% RID threshold provides a wide safety margin. Even morphine at ~9% RID has an excellent safety record when used appropriately.

## 6.2. INTRAVENOUS INDUCTION AGENTS

### 6.2.1. PROPOFOL

**Propofol is safe for breastfeeding mothers and no interruption of breastfeeding is required.**

* **Transfer characteristics:**
  * Breast milk concentrations negligible 2 hours post-delivery
  * High protein binding (98%) limits free drug available for transfer
  * Low oral bioavailability in infants limits systemic absorption
  * Rapid hepatic metabolism to inactive metabolites
* **Evidence:** Study of 21 patients receiving 2.5 mg/kg induction dose for elective cesarean section showed negligible breast milk concentrations at 2 hours [Source: PMC Review 2015]
* **Recommendation:** Breastfeeding can resume as soon as the mother has recovered sufficiently from general anaesthesia to nurse; discarding milk is unnecessary [Source: AoA Guideline 2020]

### 6.2.2. ETOMIDATE

**Etomidate is compatible with breastfeeding.**

* **Transfer characteristics:**
  * Milk-to-plasma ratio of 1.2 at 30 minutes following 0.3 mg/kg dose
  * 75% decrease in milk concentration at 2 hours
  * Undetectable at 4 hours post-administration
* **Recommendation:** Safe to breastfeed once mother is alert

### 6.2.3. KETAMINE

**Ketamine has insufficient human data but is presumed compatible based on pharmacological properties.**

* **Transfer characteristics:**
  * No human breastfeeding studies available
  * High lipophilicity suggests potential for transfer
  * Rapid redistribution and metabolism likely limit infant exposure
* **Recommendation:** Proceed with caution; breastfeed once mother is fully alert

### 6.2.4. THIOPENTAL

**Thiopental is compatible with breastfeeding.**

* **Transfer characteristics:**
  * Large, polar molecule in sodium salt form
  * Limited transfer expected
  * Historical use without reported adverse effects in breastfed infants
* **Recommendation:** Safe to breastfeed once mother is alert

## 6.3. OPIOIDS

### 6.3.1. SAFE OPIOIDS

#### 6.3.1.1. FENTANYL

**Fentanyl is safe and recommended for breastfeeding mothers requiring opioid analgesia.**

* **Transfer characteristics:**
  * RID ~1% (well below 10% threshold)
  * After 100 mcg epidural bolus: undetectable in breast milk at time of first postoperative analgesic request
  * After 100 mcg IV: <0.1% of administered dose measured in breast milk
  * Low oral bioavailability (~30%) further reduces infant exposure
* **Recommendation:** Resume breastfeeding when mother is alert; no need to discard milk [Source: ASA Statement 2019]

#### 6.3.1.2. REMIFENTANIL

**Remifentanil is particularly well-suited for breastfeeding mothers due to its ultra-short half-life.**

* **Transfer characteristics:**
  * Ultra-short context-sensitive half-life (~3 minutes)
  * Rapidly metabolized by nonspecific plasma esterases
  * Small studies showed no untoward respiratory effects in infants breastfed 1.5-5 hours post-anaesthesia
* **Recommendation:** Ideal choice for procedures in breastfeeding mothers; resume breastfeeding when alert

#### 6.3.1.3. ALFENTANIL

**Alfentanil is compatible with breastfeeding.**

* **Transfer characteristics:** Similar profile to fentanyl
* **Recommendation:** Safe to breastfeed when mother is alert

#### 6.3.1.4. MORPHINE

**Morphine is the recommended opioid of choice if strong postoperative analgesia is required in breastfeeding women.**

* **Transfer characteristics:**
  * RID ~9% (approaches but remains below 10% threshold)
  * Infants may receive 0.8-12% of maternal oral dose
  * Peak concentration 82 ng/mL in breast milk 30 minutes after 4 mg epidural bolus
  * Half-life in breast milk approximately 180 minutes
  * Low oral bioavailability in infants (~20%) provides additional safety margin
* **Clinical considerations:**
  * Single doses unlikely to cause adverse effects
  * For repeated doses: monitor infant for sedation, poor feeding, respiratory depression
  * Sedation occurs in 50% of infants at milk concentrations of 125 ng/mL
* **Recommendation:** Recommended as opioid of choice when strong analgesia required; monitor infant during repeated dosing [Source: AoA Guideline 2020]

### 6.3.2. OPIOIDS TO USE WITH CAUTION

#### 6.3.2.1. HYDROMORPHONE

**Hydromorphone should be used with discretion in breastfeeding mothers.**

* **Transfer characteristics:**
  * Low milk transfer (~2.2 mcg/day after intranasal 2 mg)
  * Half-life in breast milk approximately 10.5 hours (longer than morphine)
  * High potency means small amounts could have significant effect
* **Recommendation:** Use with caution; consider morphine as alternative; monitor infant closely

### 6.3.3. OPIOIDS TO AVOID

⚠️ **Safety Warning:** The following opioids should NOT be used in breastfeeding mothers:

#### 6.3.3.1. CODEINE

**Codeine is CONTRAINDICATED in breastfeeding mothers (FDA warning).**

* **Mechanism of concern:**
  * Prodrug metabolized to morphine by CYP2D6
  * "Ultrarapid metabolizer" mothers (1-7% of population depending on ethnicity) produce supratherapeutic morphine levels
  * CYP2D6 polymorphisms are unpredictable without genetic testing
* **Case reports:** Infant deaths reported from respiratory depression when breastfed by ultrarapid metabolizer mothers
* **Recommendation:** AVOID - use alternative opioids [Source: FDA Advisory; ASA Statement 2019]

#### 6.3.3.2. TRAMADOL

**Tramadol is CONTRAINDICATED in breastfeeding mothers (FDA warning).**

* **Mechanism of concern:**
  * Also metabolized by CYP2D6 to active metabolite O-desmethyltramadol
  * Same pharmacogenetic variability concerns as codeine
  * Risk of neonatal opioid overdose in slow-metabolizer neonate breastfed by ultrarapid metabolizer mother
* **Recommendation:** AVOID - use alternative opioids [Source: FDA Advisory; ASA Statement 2019]

#### 6.3.3.3. MEPERIDINE (PETHIDINE)

**Meperidine should be AVOIDED in breastfeeding mothers.**

* **Mechanism of concern:**
  * Active metabolite normeperidine has 65-hour half-life (vs 13-hour parent compound)
  * Wide range of levels secreted in breast milk
  * Accumulation risk with repeated dosing
* **Case reports:** Reports of cyanosis, bradycardia, and apnea in breastfed infants
* **Recommendation:** AVOID - theoretical higher risk of neonatal respiratory sedation; use morphine or fentanyl instead [Source: PMC Review 2015]

## 6.4. BENZODIAZEPINES

### 6.4.1. MIDAZOLAM

**Midazolam is compatible with breastfeeding.**

* **Transfer characteristics:**
  * 24-hour breast milk collection contained only 0.004% (0.08 mcg) of maternal 2 mg dose
  * Rapid metabolism and short half-life limit accumulation
* **Expert guidance:**
  * Two expert panels advocate waiting 4 hours after single IV dose (e.g., for endoscopy) before nursing
  * However, most guidelines state no interruption needed once mother is alert
* **Recommendation:** Safe to breastfeed; consider brief wait (2-4 hours) after procedural sedation if mother remains drowsy

### 6.4.2. OTHER BENZODIAZEPINES

* **Lorazepam:** Short half-life preferred; compatible with breastfeeding
* **Diazepam:** Use with caution due to longer half-life and potential for infant accumulation

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Short-acting benzodiazepines (midazolam, lorazepam) are preferred over long-acting agents (diazepam) in breastfeeding mothers.

## 6.5. LOCAL ANAESTHETICS

**All commonly used local anaesthetics are safe during breastfeeding.**

### 6.5.1. LIDOCAINE AND BUPIVACAINE

* **Transfer characteristics:**
  * RID <0.3% for local anaesthetics
  * Large, polarized molecules do not easily cross lactating ducts
  * Milk-to-plasma ratios consistent with minimal transfer
  * APGAR scores and 24-hour clinical exams normal in study populations
* **Regional anaesthesia considerations:**
  * Epidural/spinal doses produce minimal serum concentrations
  * After fentanyl 100 mcg + bupivacaine epidural for cesarean: negligible breast milk drug levels
* **Recommendation:** Cessation of breastfeeding is unwarranted for any local anaesthetic technique [Source: PMC Review 2015]

### 6.5.2. ROPIVACAINE

* **Transfer characteristics:** Similar safety profile to bupivacaine
* **Recommendation:** Safe for breastfeeding

## 6.6. NEUROMUSCULAR BLOCKING AGENTS

**All neuromuscular blocking agents are considered safe during breastfeeding.**

* **Transfer characteristics:**
  * Large molecular weight limits transfer
  * Quaternary ammonium compounds - highly polar
  * Poor oral bioavailability means any ingested drug is not absorbed
  * Do not cross blood-brain barrier
* **Specific agents:**
  * **Suxamethonium:** Safe - large molecule, low lipid solubility
  * **Rocuronium:** Safe - quaternary ammonium compound
  * **Vecuronium:** Safe - similar to rocuronium
  * **Atracurium:** Safe - similar to rocuronium
* **Recommendation:** No human breastfeeding studies, but presumed safe based on pharmacological properties; no need to interrupt breastfeeding [Source: AoA Guideline 2020]

## 6.7. REVERSAL AGENTS

### 6.7.1. ANTICHOLINESTERASES

**Neostigmine and glycopyrrolate are safe during breastfeeding.**

* **Transfer characteristics:**
  * Quaternary ammonium compounds
  * Do not penetrate blood-brain barrier
  * Poor oral bioavailability
* **Recommendation:** Safe to breastfeed

### 6.7.2. SUGAMMADEX

**Sugammadex is compatible with breastfeeding but warrants judicious use in the early postpartum period.**

* **Transfer characteristics:**
  * Very large molecule (2002 Da)
  * Highly polar - limited placental transfer and excretion into breast milk expected
  * Poor oral bioavailability
* **Special considerations:**
  * Early postpartum period: large gaps between mammary alveolar cells may enhance protein delivery to milk
  * Immature neonatal metabolism and renal function may delay clearance
  * SOAP recommends judicious use until more data available
* **Safety record:** No reported cases of infant harm attributed to sugammadex in breast milk
* **Recommendation:** Likely safe; resume normal breastfeeding routine once recovered from general anaesthesia; consider informing patient that effects on lactation are not fully known [Source: LactMed; SOAP Statement]

## 6.8. VOLATILE ANAESTHETICS

**All volatile anaesthetics are safe during breastfeeding.**

* **Agents:** Sevoflurane, isoflurane, desflurane, nitrous oxide, halothane
* **Transfer characteristics:**
  * Rapidly exhaled after discontinuation
  * Poor oral bioavailability
  * No concern for infant exposure via breast milk
* **Recommendation:** Resume breastfeeding without concern once mother is alert [Source: AoA Guideline 2020]

## 6.9. ANTIEMETICS

### 6.9.1. ONDANSETRON

**Ondansetron is safe during breastfeeding.**

* **Transfer characteristics:**
  * RID 3.7% of weight-adjusted 4 mg maternal dose (well below 10% threshold)
  * Frequently used for nausea during/after cesarean section
  * Use during cesarean does not affect onset of breastfeeding
  * No adverse infant effects reported
* **Additional note:** Ondansetron is labelled for use in infants as young as 1 month
* **Recommendation:** Safe to use; no interruption of breastfeeding required [Source: LactMed]

### 6.9.2. METOCLOPRAMIDE

**Metoclopramide is compatible with breastfeeding for short-term use.**

* **Transfer characteristics:**
  * RID expected to be <10% in most cases
  * Variable degree of transfer documented
* **Precautions:**
  * Monitor infant for sedation, GI disturbances, difficulty feeding, irritability
  * Monitor for extrapyramidal symptoms (tremor, jerky movements)
  * Maximum dose: 30 mg daily for maximum 5 days
  * Avoid in patients with history of depression
* **Recommendation:** Compatible for short-term use with monitoring [Source: LactMed]

### 6.9.3. DEXAMETHASONE

**Dexamethasone is safe during breastfeeding.**

* Single perioperative doses have low transfer to breast milk
* **Recommendation:** Safe to breastfeed

## 6.10. NON-OPIOID ANALGESICS

### 6.10.1. FIRST-LINE ANALGESICS

**Non-opioid analgesics are the preferred first-line agents for postoperative pain in breastfeeding mothers.**

| Drug | Status | Notes |
|------|--------|-------|
| **Paracetamol** | SAFE - First line | Excellent safety profile |
| **Ibuprofen** | SAFE - First line | Half-life ~1.8 hours; levels negligible if feed 2-3 hours after dose |
| **Diclofenac** | SAFE | Low transfer to milk |
| **Naproxen** | SAFE | Low transfer to milk |
| **Ketorolac** | SAFE | Acceptable option |
| **Celecoxib** | SAFE | Low transfer to milk |

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Prioritizing non-opioid analgesia reduces both maternal sedation and any theoretical risk to the breastfed infant.

## 6.11. TIMING AND PRACTICAL RECOMMENDATIONS

### 6.11.1. WHEN TO RESUME BREASTFEEDING

**Key Recommendation (Association of Anaesthetists 2020):**
> "Breastfeeding is acceptable to continue after anaesthesia and should be supported as soon as the woman is alert and able to feed, without the need to discard breast milk."

**Practical guidance:**
* Resume breastfeeding once awake, stable, and alert
* Return to baseline mentation and strength indicates sedating medications have redistributed
* **General rule:** "If you are awake enough to hold your breastfed child, you are awake enough to breastfeed"
* **Pre-operative:** Encourage pumping or breastfeeding just prior to the procedure

### 6.11.2. "PUMP AND DUMP" - EVIDENCE-BASED POSITION

⚠️ **Safety Warning:** The advice to "pump and dump" breast milk after surgery is **OUTDATED** and NOT routinely recommended.

**Evidence-based position:**
* Pumping and dumping is rarely required
* Most anaesthesia medications reach negligible concentrations in breast milk within hours
* As mother regains consciousness, sedating medications redistribute from plasma and milk to adipose tissue and muscle
* The outdated "pump and dump" recommendation predates scientific data on drug accumulation

**When discarding milk MAY be considered:**
* Mother received genuinely contraindicated medication (codeine, tramadol, meperidine)
* Radioactive contrast agents requiring specific wait times
* Chemotherapy agents

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** "Sleep and keep" (rest then breastfeed when alert) is the evidence-based approach, not "pump and dump."

### 6.11.3. SPECIAL POPULATIONS

#### 6.11.3.1. NEONATES <6 WEEKS OLD

**Extra caution required with opioids and benzodiazepines in mothers of young neonates:**

* Use corrected gestational age for premature infants
* Monitor for signs of abnormal drowsiness
* Watch for respiratory depression, especially if mother is also sedated
* Shorter duration opioid exposure preferred

#### 6.11.3.2. OPIOID-SPARING STRATEGIES

**Regional anaesthesia and multimodal analgesia have specific benefits for breastfeeding women:**

* Minimizes systemic drug exposure and transfer to milk
* Maintains maternal ability to care for infant
* Reduces sedation that interferes with breastfeeding
* Day surgery preferred when possible to avoid disrupting routines
* **Remember:** Pain itself interferes with successful breastfeeding - adequate analgesia is important

### 6.11.4. PRE-OPERATIVE ASSESSMENT

**All women with an infant <2 years should routinely be asked if they are breastfeeding during pre-operative assessment.**

**Counselling points:**
* Reassure that breastfeeding can continue after anaesthesia
* Address any "pump and dump" misconceptions
* Discuss drug selection if specific concerns exist
* Plan for infant care while mother is sedated/recovering

### 6.12.2. KEY TAKE-HOME POINTS

1. **Nearly all anaesthetic drugs are compatible with breastfeeding**
2. **RID <10% = generally safe** - most anaesthetics are well below this threshold
3. **Resume breastfeeding when alert enough to hold infant** - no arbitrary wait times needed
4. **"Pump and dump" is NOT routinely necessary** - this is outdated advice
5. **AVOID:** Codeine, tramadol, meperidine/pethidine
6. **Extra caution:** Multiple doses of opioids/benzodiazepines in first 6 weeks; monitor infant for sedation
7. **Regional anaesthesia preferred** - reduces systemic drug exposure
8. **Pre-operative counselling** - ask all women with infants <2 years if breastfeeding

**Bottom Line:** The vast majority of anaesthetic drugs are safe during breastfeeding, and mothers should be encouraged to resume breastfeeding as soon as they are awake, alert, and able to safely hold their infant. The outdated practice of "pumping and dumping" is not evidence-based and should be discouraged except in rare circumstances involving genuinely contraindicated medications.
