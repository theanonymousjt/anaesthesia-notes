# 1. BLOOD GROUPS AND COMPATIBILITY TESTING

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Immunohematology and Blood Groups|Part A]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Clinical Management of Transfusion|Part B]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Patient Safety|Part C]]

## 1.2. ABO BLOOD GROUP SYSTEM

* The main grouping system is the ABO system
* All patients' RBCs have a disaccharide 'core' antigen, called the H antigen
* Blood group distributions in the UK:
  * Group O: 44-47% (universal donor - no antigens, has anti-A and anti-B antibodies)
  * Group A: 42% (can receive A and O but will react to B and AB blood)
  * Group B: 8-10% (can receive B and O but will react to A and AB blood)
  * Group AB: 3-4% (universal recipient, can receive all groups)
* Groups are determined by which antigens are present on RBCs:
  * Group O: These patients' RBCs only express the H antigen (fucose residue); 'O' signifies that no other sugars are added
  * Group A: Has A RBC antigen - an additional carbohydrate group (N-acetylgalactosamine) is bound to the H antigen, making a trisaccharide
  * Group B: Has B RBC antigen - a different carbohydrate group (d-galactose) is bound to the H antigen, making a different trisaccharide
  * Group AB: Has both A and B RBC antigens - these patients' RBCs express both A and B antigens
* ABO blood group antigens are oligosaccharide chains that project from the red cell surface:
  * Attached to proteins and lipids that lie in the red cell membrane
  * The ABO gene encodes a glycosyltransferase that catalyzes the final step in the synthesis of the chain, which has three common alleles: A, B and O
  * The O allele encodes an inactive enzyme, leaving the ABO antigen precursor (called the H antigen) unmodified
  * The A and B alleles encode enzymes that differ by four amino acids and hence attach different sugars to the end of the chain
* Almost all individuals not having A or B antigen "naturally" produce antibodies, mainly immunoglobulin (Ig) M, against those missing antigens within the first year of life
* Antibody development is thought to be an immune response to environmental antigens that have a similar chemical structure to the ABO antigens:
  * Blood group O: develop anti-A and anti-B antibodies
  * Blood group A: develop anti-B antibodies only
  * Blood group B: develop anti-A antibodies only
  * Blood group AB: do not develop anti-A or anti-B antibodies
* The anti-A and anti-B antibodies produced are immunoglobulin M (IgM) which cannot cross the placenta
* Individuals are tolerant to their own ABO antigens, but do not suppress B-cell clones producing antibodies against ABO antigens that they do not carry themselves

## 1.3. RHESUS (RH) BLOOD GROUP SYSTEM

* The second most commonly used blood group system
* Named after the Rhesus monkey, the animal whose blood was used in the discovery of the Rh system
* These are IgG antibodies on the surface of the RBC and are either present (Rh +) or absent (Rh -)
* Determined by two loci on chromosome 1
* Approximately 46 Rh group red cell antigens have been discovered to date
* The most important Rh antigens are: D, C, c, E and e
* By far the most important Rh antigen is D - this is the antigen present when patients are referred to as being Rhesus positive, Rh factor positive or RhD positive
* The RhD antigen is a large (30‑kDa) cell membrane protein that is thought to be a subunit of an ammonia transport protein
* D Rhesus antigen positive individuals are considered Rh-positive
* Approximately 85% of the UK population are rhesus positive, 15% negative
* In contrast to the ABO groups, Rh-negative patients usually develop antibodies against the D antigen only after an Rh-positive transfusion or with pregnancy (Rh-negative mother delivering an Rh-positive baby)
* The 15% of the population who are genetically RhD antigen negative do not naturally develop RhD antibodies
* Anti-RhD antibodies are only acquired on exposure to foreign RBCs carrying the RhD antigen
* The probability of developing anti-D antibodies after a single exposure to the Rh antigen is 50% to 70%
* Clinically, exposure to RhD antigen may occur due to:
  * Incompatible blood transfusion: Transfusion of RhD-positive blood into an RhD-negative patient will trigger anti-RhD antibody production
  * Foetal–maternal haemorrhage: If the blood of an RhD-positive foetus and RhD-negative mother mix (e.g., following childbirth, abortion, trauma or placental abruption), the maternal immune system will be exposed to RhD antigen and will develop anti-RhD antibodies
* Unfortunately, RhD antibodies are usually IgG and are therefore able to cross the placenta
* An example is seen when a rhesus positive baby to a rhesus positive mother triggers antibody production in the mother's blood:
  * This isn't an issue for that baby, but future rhesus positive pregnancies are susceptible to attack from the newly formed antibodies
  * Causes hemolytic disease of the newborn (HDN), hence rhesus negative mothers are offered Anti-D immunoglobulin, to destroy any fetal cells in the maternal blood and prevent antibodies from forming
* ⚠️ An RhD-negative female who may subsequently become pregnant should never be transfused with RhD-positive blood

### 1.3.1. RH DISEASE (HEMOLYTIC DISEASE OF THE NEWBORN)

* Rh disease affects RhD-negative mothers with RhD-positive offspring
* When an RhD-negative mother is exposed to RhD-positive foetal blood (e.g. following trauma), her immune system produces anti-RhD IgG
* Maternal anti-RhD IgG is then able to cross the placenta and attack the RBCs of an RhD-positive foetus, either for the same or a subsequent pregnancy
* The foetus develops a severe anaemia in utero and, if the foetus survives to birth, Rh disease (haemolytic disease of the newborn)
* RhD-negative mothers are given anti-RhD immunoglobulin once or twice during pregnancy and at delivery to prevent maternal sensitisation to RhD
* The parenteral anti-RhD IgG binds any foetal RBCs that pass into the maternal circulation
* HDN can also be caused by other alloantibodies against red cell antigens, usually after previous pregnancies or transfusions:
  * Rhc, RhC, RhE, Rhe, and the Kell, Kidd and Duffy antigen systems
* HDN can also occur if there is fetomaternal ABO incompatibility, most commonly seen in a group O mother with a group A fetus:
  * Generally less severely affected by ABO incompatibility than by RhD, Rhc or Kell antigen mismatch, and the incompatibility is often picked up coincidentally after birth
  * ABO Abs are generally IgM class Ab; Group A and Group B predominantly IgM; Serum from O contains antiA antiB and AntiA-B which is IgG predominantly

## 1.4. OTHER BLOOD GROUP SYSTEMS

* Other red cell antigen systems include:
  * Lewis, P, Ii, MNS
  * Kidd, Kell, Duffy, Lutheran
  * Xg, Sid, Cartright, YK, Ss, and Chido Rodgers
* May be responsible for less severe reactions or after multiple transfusions
* With some exceptions (Kell, Kidd, Duffy, and Ss), alloantibodies against these antigens rarely cause serious hemolytic reactions

## 1.5. TYPE AND SCREEN VS TYPE AND CROSSMATCH

* RBCs must be ABO compatible to avoid intravascular hemolysis, and RhD-negative patients should receive D-negative RBCs to avoid anti-D alloimmunization
* Rh, Kell, Kidd, Duffy, and some other non-ABO antibodies can also hemolyze transfused RBCs
* In emergencies, uncross-matched group O RBCs can be given, albeit with the risk of non-ABO antibody incompatibility
* Group AB is the universal donor plasma and avoids transfusing anti-A or anti-B versus the patient's RBCs
* Reduction of transfusion reaction risk
	* ABO-compatible blood - 99.4%
	* ABO & Rh compatible blood - 99.8%
	* ABO & Rh compatible blood + negative antibody screen - 99.94%
	* ABO & Rh compatible blood + negative antibody screen + Coomb's test - 99.95%

### 1.5.1. TYPE AND SCREEN (TAKES 30-120 MIN, LASTS 72 HR)

* **Type:** Test ABO-Rh antigens on RBC
  * Recipient RBCs tested with anti-A&B and anti-D (Rh) antibodies
* **Screen:** Indirect Coombs' test to assess for antibodies (not currently attached to RBC) in recipient's serum
  * Recipient serum mixed with RBCs of known antigens
  * No agglutination = negative screen
  * If antibody screen is positive: the serum is tested further
* Antigens not represented in screen cells are unlikely to cause clinically significant hemolytic reactions - can be safely transfused with negative Type-specific blood if negative screen
* Risk of transfusion reaction with negative antibody screen is approximately 0.2%
* 💡 In the situation of negative antibody screen without crossmatch, the incidence of serious hemolytic reaction with ABOand Rh-compatible transfusion is less than 1:10,000

### 1.5.2. TYPE AND CROSSMATCH (IF T&S NEGATIVE, TAKES 30-60MIN)

* Crossmatch is a compatibility test between donor and recipient blood
* Types of crossmatch:
  * **Major crossmatch**: The recipient's serum is mixed with donor RBCs – this is a test of compatibility between the recipient's antibodies and donor antigens. If donor RBCs clump together, the blood is incompatible
  * **Minor crossmatch**: The recipient's RBCs are mixed with the donor's plasma. Minor crossmatch is no longer routinely performed because RBCs are now transfused as packed cells and thus contain an insignificant amount of donor plasma
* **Immediate phase (5min)**
  * Checks against ABO typing errors & incompatibilities caused by naturally occurring antibodies to the MN, P, and Lewis systems
* **Incubation phase (45min)**
  * Immediate phase reaction products incubated to detect incomplete antibodies to the Rh system that do not cause agglutination in the first phase
* **Indirect Antiglobulin test**
  * Antiglobulin serum added to products of first two tests to look for incomplete recipient antibodies to Rh, Kell, Duffy, and Kidd
* Use when it is very likely you will transfuse (this actually reserves blood products)

## 1.6. UNIVERSAL DONORS AND RECIPIENTS

* The universal donor is a blood group O, RhD-negative patient
  * Group O RBCs only express the core H antigen, so are safe for donation to recipients with anti-A or anti-B antibodies
  * RhD-negative RBCs are safe to transfuse in recipients with anti-RhD antibodies
  * In an emergency situation where there is insufficient time to establish the blood type of the recipient (e.g. trauma, ruptured aortic aneurysm, obstetric haemorrhage), it is considered safe to transfuse with O negative blood
  * However, there may still be minor blood group antigen–antibody incompatibility reactions; a full crossmatch is required to ensure blood is fully compatible
* The universal recipient is more of academic than clinical interest
  * Blood group AB, RhD-positive patients can receive donor blood irrespective of its ABO and Rh status
  * Their plasma has neither anti-A nor anti-B antibodies, because their RBCs contain both antigens
  * Similarly, their plasma does not contain anti-RhD antibodies, because their RBCs express the RhD antigen
  * It is important to note that the minor blood group antigens are not necessarily compatible; a crossmatch is still required

**Bottom Line:** Blood groups determine transfusion compatibility, with ABO and Rh being the most critical systems. Type and screen testing checks for recipient blood type and antibodies, while crossmatching directly tests compatibility between donor units and recipient serum, necessary when transfusion is likely. The immune system naturally develops antibodies against non-self ABO antigens from environmental exposure, while Rh antibodies develop only after exposure to Rh-positive blood.

## 2.2. PROCESS OF BLOOD COLLECTION AND PROCESSING

* Venesection of a unit of whole blood or collection of a specific component by apheresis (centrifugation of blood as it is drawn into a machine, returns blood to donor)
* Each donation screened for:
  * HBV, HCV, HIV, HTLV nucleic acid and antibodies
  * Platelet concentrates screened for bacterial contamination
  * Testing for Trypanosoma cruzi (Chagas' disease) is necessary in areas of South America and the USA where infection is prevalent
  * Tests for West Nile virus have been required in the USA
* Components for use in specific patient groups are prepared from hepatitis E virus-negative donors in the UK
* Plasma donated in the UK is not used at present for producing pooled plasma derivatives in view of concerns about transmission of variant Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease

## 2.3. PACKED RED BLOOD CELLS

### 2.3.1. DEFINITION, USE, & STORAGE

* Single donor; volume 250-300 ml with Hct ~60-70%
* 1 unit pRBCs: increases adult Hgb ~1 g/dl or Hct ~3%
  * 10 ml/kg pRBC increases Hct 10%
* Always run in with bag of NS or normosol on blood pump
* Solutions not compatible with pRBC:
  * ⚠️ LR (theoretical clot formation due to calcium)
  * ⚠️ D5W, hypotonic solutions (RBC hemolysis)
* Stored at 4˚C in:
  - Based on minimum acceptable survival rate of at least 70% of RBCs should be alive after 24h
  * CPD (lasts 21 days)
  * CPDA (lasts 35 days)
  * Adsol (lasts 42 days)
* Run through a warmer:
  * Slow rates: Ranger
  * Fast rates: Belmont or Level 1
* CPDA - RBC preservation solutions:
  * Citrate (anticoagulant): metabolized by liver to bicarb; at high transfusion rates, excess citrate binds to calcium (resulting in hypocalcemia)
  * Phosphate (buffer)
  * Dextrose (energy source)
  * Adenine (precursor to ATP synthesis)
* Despite preservatives, several metabolic changes occur during RBC storage:
  * 2,3–Diphosphoglycerate (2,3–DPG) is depleted in the first 2 weeks
  * Shifts the oxygen dissociation curve to the left, increasing hemoglobin binding of oxygen (although this reverses after transfusion)
  * Some RBCs become spherical, reducing their lifespan
  * K+ concentration rises to 30 mmol/L by 28 days' storage
  * ATP and 2,3-DPG levels fall. Following transfusion, it takes 24 h for 2,3-DPG concentrations to return to normal. Transfused blood therefore has a higher O2-binding affinity than native blood and thus O2 offloading to the tissues is impaired
- **Storage lesions** of blood (temporal changes in stored blood)
	- Loss of functional platelets after 24-36h storage at 4C
	- Loss of some coagulation factors - "labile factors"
		- FVIII - decrease to 50% by 24h, 6% after 21 days
		- FV - decrease to 50% after 14 days
		- Decrease in 2,3 DPG
		- Small amount of haemolysis
		- Formation of microaggregates
		- Biochemical changes: increased K+, decreased pH (acidosis)
### 2.3.2. INDICATIONS AND TRANSFUSION THRESHOLDS

![The ASA's most recent publication on the Practice Guidelines for Perioperative Blood Management defines restrictive practices as "hemoglobin criteria for transfusion less than 8 g/dL and hematocrit values less than 25%."](TRANSFUSION_TRANSFUSION_image_1.png_b784ee.png_0fd7c6.png)

* ASA Practice Guidelines for Perioperative Blood Management defines restrictive practices as "hemoglobin criteria for transfusion less than 8 g/dL and hematocrit values less than 25%"
* Hb <7 in young, healthy patients
* Usually unnecessary when Hb >10
* Hb 6-10 - decision depends on:
  * Ongoing indications of organ ischemia
  * Potential for ongoing blood loss
  * Volume status
  * Risk factors for complications of inadequate O2 - i.e., MI
* Transfusion thresholds for guiding RBC transfusion - Cochrane review findings:
  * Restrictive transfusion strategies did not increase or decrease the risk of 30-day mortality compared with liberal transfusion strategies (RR 0.99, 95% CI 0.86 to 1.15; 31 studies, 16,729 participants; I2 = 30%; moderate-quality evidence)
  * High-quality evidence shows that the liberal transfusion threshold did not affect the risk of infection (pneumonia, wound infection, or bacteremia)
  * By clinical group analysis:
	* 7g/dl threshold shown to have similar safety profiles to higher thresholds in critical care trials in acute gastrointestinal bleeding
	* 7.5g/dl was the threshold for patients undergoing cardiac surgery in the largest trials that showed similar outcomes
	* In orthopedic surgery, it is 8g/dl having similar risk profile to higher transfusion thresholds (not possible to conclude 7 is as efficacious as 8)
	* Other subgroups like vascular and hematological malignancies had insufficient evidence
	* For patients with underlying cardiovascular disease, the analysis does provide some evidence that a restrictive strategy might be appropriate – REALITY trial. Yet, pooled analysis revealed a high-risk ratio with wide confidence interval suggestive of significant benefit for more liberal transfusion policies. Ongoing larger trials have the potential to provide additional evidence
  * 💡 The implication is that given there is no evidence of additional benefit of red blood cell (RBC) transfusion at higher hemoglobin concentration thresholds (9.0 g/dL to 10.0 g/dL), and that blood for transfusion is a costly and scarce biological resource with finite risks, a restrictive transfusion trigger policy (7.0 g/dL to 8.0 g/dL) could be widely adopted
  * ⚠️ There is still little evidence to support a clear recommendation for hemoglobin thresholds in patients at high risk of secondary injury and end-organ dysfunction in the setting of acute neurologic injury or traumatic brain injury (TBI) as often these patients are excluded from studies with restrictive transfusion practices
	* The Hb at which anemia induces detrimental patient outcomes for those with acute neurologic injury remains unclear
	* Evidence indicates that both hemoglobin levels <9 g/dL and RBC transfusion are independently predictive of poor outcome in patients with stroke, cerebral hemorrhage, or TBI

## 2.4. FRESH FROZEN PLASMA

### 2.4.1. DEFINITION, USE AND STORAGE

* Modern preparations of plasma now include:
  * Fresh frozen within 8 hours of phlebotomy (FFP)
  * Plasma frozen within 24 hours of collection (PF24)
  * "Thawed plasma," which includes units of frozen plasma that have been thawed for more than 24 hours yet remain usable for up to 4 additional days
* Both maintain normal levels of stable factors and at least 70% normal levels of labile factors such as FVIII and FV
* Prior to use, each unit must be thawed at 30° to 37°C, but can be stored at 4° ± 2°C for up to 24 hours as thawed FFP or 5 days as thawed plasma
* Contains all clotting factors, fibrinogen, plasma proteins (particularly albumin), electrolytes, physiologic anticoagulants (protein C, protein S, antithrombin), and added anticoagulants (citrate)
* 10-20ml/kg will raise factors by 20-30% (2.5-4.5 units for 70kg patient). Each unit of FFP generally increases the level of each clotting factor by 2-3% in adults
* Goal is to achieve 30% of normal coagulation factor concentration. Repeat dosing is guided by serial diagnostic coagulation tests such as the PT, INR, aPTT, and thromboelastography
* The most recent update to the guidelines from the pan-European task force for Advanced Bleeding Care in Trauma recommend using fixed ratios of plasma:RBC of at least 1:2 for resuscitation of massive hemorrhage only until goal-directed PBM strategies can be prescribed based on coagulation profile assay results
* Use ABO-compatible; Rh-incompatible is OK
  * AB blood type is the universal donor
* Stored frozen (-18C) up to 1yr; takes 30min to thaw; use within 24hrs of thawing
* ⚠️ During preparation and distribution, care must be taken to ensure ABO compatibility for plasma transfusion to avoid transfusion of donor anti-A and anti-B antibodies that may lead to hemolysis

### 2.4.2. INDICATIONS

* Correction of acquired multifactor deficiencies with clinical evidence of bleeding or in anticipation of major surgery or an invasive procedure with PT or aPTT >1.5 times the control, or INR >2.0:
  * Liver dysfunction with clinical signs of bleeding
  * DIC with clinical signs of bleeding
  * Reversal of vitamin K antagonists (warfarin) when PCCs are not available
  * Heparin resistance secondary to AT deficiency when AT concentrate is not available
  * Treatment of thrombotic microangiopathies (thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura, HELLP syndrome, or hemolytic uremic syndrome)
* (Massive transfusion) Replacement of multifactor deficiencies with clinical bleeding after transfusion of more than one blood volume (>70 mL/kg) when PT, INR, or aPTT cannot be obtained immediately
* Urgent reversal of warfarin (or can use Prothrombin complex concentrate)
* Correction of inherited factor deficiencies when there is no specific factor concentrate (e.g., factor V) and when the PT or aPTT is >1.5 times the mean control or INR >2.0
* Treatment of hereditary angioedema when C1–esterase inhibitor is not available
* 💡 FFP has an INR of 1.6-1.8, hence think about whether it really makes sense to "correct" your particular patient's INR before giving

## 2.5. PLATELETS

### 2.5.1. DEFINITION, USE AND STORAGE

* Average dose of platelets is one concentrate from apheresis donation, or a random donor pool of four to six concentrates from whole-blood donations (Platelet concentrate)
* Platelet Concentrate (PC):
  * Platelets from one donated unit, vol = 50-70 ml; plt ~5,000-10,000
  * "6-pack" = 6 pooled PCs from different donors (rarely used anymore)
* Apheresis Unit:
  * Platelets from a single donor; vol = 200-400 ml; plt ~30-50,000
  * Document as 250ml (no exact number written on unit)
* Can give ABO-incompatible platelets, Rh tested only
  * ⚠️ However, contain a small amount of RBCs so Rh sensitization can occur for some
* Platelets are either pooled from the whole blood of multiple donors or collected from one donor by a process called apheresis
* Platelet concentrates are ultraviolet irradiated to reduce the risk of transmission of infectious diseases
* Transfused platelets generally survive only 1 to 7 days following transfusion
* Stored at room temperature for ≤5 days
* Platelets are stored at room temperature (20-24C) as they quickly become nonfunctional at 4°C
* Hang separately (on blood pump with NS) – Do not run through fluid warmer, Level 1, or Belmont (heating can injure the platelets but studies have challenged this theory)
- pH 6.2-7.8
	- Undergoes transformation at low or high pH
	- Produces lactic acid and CO2 lower pH progressively following storage
	- Hence stored in bags made with special plastic to allow gas exchange (O2 and CO2)
		- Promotes aerobic metabolism to minimize lactic acid production
		- Allows CO2 loss
### 2.5.2. INDICATIONS (ASA GUIDELINES)

![image.png](TRANSFUSION_TRANSFUSION_image_4.png_da402e.png_9f8b44.png)

* Rarely when plt > 100K unless there is suspicion of platelet dysfunction (e.g., CPB, plt inhibitors, renal dysfunction)
* Regardless of the platelet count, if bleeding is out of proportion to the level of thrombocytopenia, qualitative deficiency should be investigated and treated
* For stable patients with severe thrombocytopenia, transfusion can be held until counts fall below 10,000/μL, in the absence of signs or symptoms of bleeding
* **In general, as prophylaxis when plt < 50K and undergoing major surgery/procedure (spontaneous bleed at < 10K)**
* For specific procedures with higher bleeding risk:
	* >30,000/uL required for safe vaginal delivery
	* >50,000/uL required for operative vaginal/caesarean delivery
  * Epidural catheter placement or removal should be supported with platelets >80,000/μL
  * Eye/CNS surgery require > 100,000/μL
  * Critical blood loss w hemorrhagic shock, poly-trauma, intracerebral hemorrhage, TBI >100,000/μL
* For minor surgery/procedures, where bleeding risk is low enough to tolerate more restrictive transfusion thresholds:
  * Lumbar puncture may be performed if the platelet count is >40,000/μL
  * Central venous catheters can be placed when platelets exceed 20,000/μL
* Symptomatic bleeding that is not life-threatening may be managed conservatively with platelet goals of >30,000/μL, but any patient with significant ongoing blood loss should have a higher transfusion trigger —in excess of 50,000/μL
* Platelets can be transfused without crossmatch, but the platelet count rises more if they are ABO-type specific

## 2.6. CRYOPRECIPITATE

### 2.6.1. DEFINITION, USE, & STORAGE

* Fraction of plasma that precipitates when FFP is thawed
* FFP is frozen to –70°C then thawed and centrifuged and the precipitate collected
* Small concentrates from each FFP unit are typically combined for a single adult dose from five pools and refrozen at –18°C for up to 12 months
* Contains Factors I (fibrinogen), VIII, XIII and vWF
  * 1 unit contains ~5X more fibrinogen than 1 unit FFP
  * Low-volume alternative for fibrinogen replacement, especially during acquired hypofibrinogenemia states such as DIC and massive hemorrhage
* Cryoprecipitate is rich in factors VIII, XIII, fibrinogen and von Willebrand factor and is used in clotting factor deficiencies and hypofibrinogenaemia
* Contains 200-250mg of Fibrinogen/unit: 10 units = 2g
  * 10 units will raise a 70kg patient's fibrinogen by 70mg/dL (60-100)
* 0.1u/kg raises fibrinogen by 100mg/dL
  * Once thawed must be stored at room temp and given w/in 6hrs
  * Can be stored -18-20 degrees celsius for 1yr
* Not screened for ABO incompatibility d/t limited Ab concentration
* 💡 Fibrinogen concentrates exist - used frequently around the world as a safe, low-volume alternative to cryoprecipitate, but are only approved by the FDA for the treatment of bleeding patients with congenital hypofibrinogenemia

### 2.6.2. INDICATIONS (ASA GUIDELINES)

![image.png](TRANSFUSION_TRANSFUSION_image_5.png_a0e2d6.png_376098.png)

* Consensus from various guidelines recommends conservative goals with fibrinogen targets of only 50 to 80 mg/dL in nonbleeding patients, and the use of cryoprecipitate to achieve fibrinogen concentrations of at least 150 mg/dL in the setting of acute major bleeding
* Rarely when fibrinogen >150 mg/dl - unless it is obstetric hemorrhage at the time of delivery / DIC / Massive hemorrhage and transfusion
* When fibrinogen <100 mg/dl (some guidelines 50-80) with microvascular bleeding
* Bleeding patients with von Willebrand Disease / Hemophilia A
* Congenital fibrinogen deficiency

## 2.7. PROTHROMBIN COMPLEX CONCENTRATE

### 2.7.1. UNACTIVATED PCC

* 4 Factor - Kcentra - contains inactive form of F2, 7, 9, 10 (also contains heparin)
* 3 factor - Profilnine - contains inactive form of F2, 9, 10; contains little or no factor VII (does not contain heparin)
* Freeze-dried factor IX concentrate, which also contains a small amount of factors II, VII and X, is used to replace factor IX in patients with factor IX deficiency (Christmas disease)

### 2.7.2. ACTIVATED PCC (APCC)

* 4 Factor - FEIBA - F2, 7, 9, 10 - of these only F7 is the mostly activated form (does not contain heparin)

### 2.7.3. USES

* Warfarin reversal in major bleeds
* Oral factor Xa inhibitors - apixaban, edoxaban, rivaroxaban - (although typically uses andexanet alfa)

## 2.8. PLASMA DERIVATIVES

* Types include:
  * Albumin, immunoglobulins, clotting factors, C1 esterase inhibitors and α1-antitrypsin
* Processing:
  * Albumin are pasteurized at 60C for sterility
  * Immunoglobulin undergo robust pathogen inactivation process i.e., nanofiltration and fractionation
* Factor concentrates include:
  * VIII, IX, X, XIII, vWF, fibrinogen, XI, PCC, AT concentrates
  * Freeze-dried factor VIII concentrate, which also contains a small amount of fibrinogen, is used to replace factor VIII in patients with haemophilia A
* Others:
  * IVIG
	* Used in GBS, ITP; anti rhesus D Ig; Anti zoster Ig
	* Risk of renal dysfunction - use no or low sucrose version
  * Human albumin
	* 5% used as a colloid resuscitation but no more effective and more expensive than crystalloid
	* 20% used for hypoproteinemic edema in nephrotic syndrome, ascites in CLD
	  * Hyperoncotic

## 2.9. RBC AND PLATELET SUBSTITUTES

* O2 carrying substitutes are undergoing research: genetically engineered recombinant Hb or "HemeAct" - Hb bonded to albumin proteins
* Several hemoglobin solutions have been made from pools of human or bovine hemoglobin, or from recombinant hemoglobin, all of which were chemically modified to facilitate extracellular O2 offloading
* ⚠️ The potentially toxic effects of free hemoglobin, including nephrotoxicity and both systemic and pulmonary hypertension, remain a significant obstacle to ongoing product development

## 2.10. EMERGING BLOOD PRODUCTS

* Low titer group O whole blood (LTOWB)
  * Provides RBCs, plasma, platelets in one product
  * Store at 1-6C up to 14-21 days
* Cold-stored platelets (CSPs)
  * Store at 1-6C for up to 10-14 days

## 2.11. BLOOD SUBSTITUTES

* There are a number of alternative O2-carrying solutions (blood substitutes) currently undergoing clinical trials, which can be classified as:
  * **Hb-based O2 carriers**: Pure Hb from RBCs cannot be used as it causes renal tubular damage. Instead, Hb must be cross-linked, polymerised or encapsulated
  * **Perfluorocarbon-based O2 carriers**: These man-made chemicals are able to dissolve significant quantities of gases, including O2 and CO2. However, perfluorocarbons are hydrophobic and therefore must be prepared as an emulsion to mix with blood. They do not have the same complex dissociation kinetics as Hb
* Potential advantages of blood substitutes over human blood:
  * Blood transfusion is expensive and associated with a number of immune and non-immune complications
  * Human blood can only be stored for 30 days and must be refrigerated. Blood substitutes have a much greater shelf life and can be stored at ambient temperature, which makes them an attractive prospect in military trauma medicine
  * Alternative O2-carrying solutions are likely to be acceptable to Jehovah's Witnesses

**Bottom Line:** Blood components have specific storage requirements, clinical indications, and dosing considerations. Understanding these factors is crucial for effective and safe transfusion therapy while minimizing waste of these valuable resources. Each component has unique properties that make it appropriate for specific clinical situations, and proper storage is essential to maintain product efficacy.

# 3. PHYSIOLOGICAL CONSIDERATIONS IN TRANSFUSION

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Oxygen Transport Physiology|Part A]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Clinical Application in Perioperative Management|Part B]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Patient Safety|Part C]]

## 3.2. IMPORTANT EQUATIONS

* Estimated blood volumes (ml/kg):
  * Preemie - 100ml
  * Term - 90ml
  * <1y - 80ml
  * 1-6y - 75ml
  * Male - 70ml
  * Female - 65ml
  * Obese - <60ml
* Arterial O2 contents:
  * CaO2 = O2-Hb + Dissolved O2 = (Hb x 1.36 x SaO2/100) + (PaO2 x 0.003) = (15 x 1.36 x 100%) + (100 x 0.003) ≈ 20 cc O2/dl (normal)
* Allowable blood loss:
  * ABL = ((Hct at start - Hct allowed at end) x EBV)/Hct at start
* Volume to transfuse:
  * Volume = ((Hct desired - Hct current) x EBV)/Hct of transfused blood

## 3.3. PHYSIOLOGIC COMPENSATION FOR ANEMIA

* DO2 (O2 delivery to tissue) is dependent on CO, regional blood flow and CaO2 (blood's O2-carrying capacity)
* The clinical justification for RBC transfusion assumes that increasing the hemoglobin will improve oxygen-carrying capacity and therefore avoid tissue hypoxia
* However, given the ability of the body to compensate for anemia, it is unclear whether increasing hemoglobin in a stable anemic patient improves tissue oxygenation
* There are several compensatory mechanisms for anemia, most notably increased CO, altered microcirculatory blood flow, and improved tissue oxygen extraction from hemoglobin
* These physiologic changes, together with the detrimental impact of RBC storage limit the therapeutic effects of transfusion

![image.png](TRANSFUSION_TRANSFUSION_image_2.png_2f3058.png_4b90f7.png)

### 3.3.1. INCREASED CARDIAC OUTPUT

* Anemia + Hypoxia > SNS > HR increases
* Anemia (or isovolemic hemodilution - i.e., crystalloid replacement in acute blood loss) > reduced blood viscosity > reduces shear stress in microvasculature > increases flow and venous return > increases preload > increases stroke volume
* Tissue acidosis > arteriolar vasodilation > reduces afterload

### 3.3.2. ALTERED MICROCIRCULATORY BLOOD FLOW

* Decreased blood viscosity > lower shear force in capillary bed > improves microcirculation
* Tissue acidosis / hypoxia > local release of NO > dilation of arterioles + arteriovenous shunting and recruitment of new circulatory beds + angiogenesis (increasing overall microcirculatory blood volume) > increasing blood flow to ischemic tissue

### 3.3.3. INCREASED TISSUE O2 EXTRACTION

* Right shift of O2-dissociation curve due to increased 2,3-DPG in RBCs + acidosis > decreased affinity of O2 for Hb > improved tissue O2 extraction ratio

**Bottom Line:** Physiological compensation for anemia involves multiple mechanisms including increased cardiac output, altered microcirculation, and enhanced oxygen extraction. These compensatory mechanisms must be considered when evaluating the need for transfusion in stable patients, as they can often maintain adequate tissue oxygenation despite moderate anemia.

# 4. SPECIAL NEEDS OF PATIENTS IN TRANSFUSION

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Immunohematology Principles|Part A]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Special Clinical Situations|Part B]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Patient Safety|Part C]]

## 4.2. SPECIAL BLOOD PRODUCT REQUIREMENTS

### 4.2.1. IRRADIATED PRODUCTS

* Indications:
  * BMT (GVHD)
  * Cellular immunodeficiency
  * Donated by first or second degree relative
  * ⚠️ Not needed for solid organ transplant
* Gammaor X-ray irradiation of blood components before their administration prevents lymphocyte proliferation
* Those at risk of TA GVHD, who must receive irradiated blood components:
  * Congenital T-cell immunodeficiencies
  * Hodgkin lymphoma
  * Aplastic anemia receiving immunosuppressive therapy with antithymocyte globulin (ATG)
  * Recipients of hematopoietic stem cell transplants or of blood from a family member
  * Neonates who have received an intrauterine transfusion
  * T-lymphocyte-suppressing drugs, such as fludarabine and other purine analogues

### 4.2.2. LEUKOREDUCED PRODUCTS

* Indications:
  * Chronically transfused pts
  * CMV seronegative pts and at-risk (HIV, transplant)
  * Post-transplant pts
  * Previous febrile non-hemolytic rxn
  * Immunosuppressed
  * Reducing FNHTR
  * HLA-alloimmunization
* ⚠️ Leucoreduction only reduce by 10^3 (10^9 to 10^6/l); GVHD at 10^4/l, leucoreduction does not prevent TAGVHD
- Achieved through bedside filters or prestorage depletion (avoids accumulation of cytokines from donor WBC while in storage)
- Other unconfirmed benefits
	- Shortened hospitalization
	- Decreased postop mortality in cardiac surgery
	- Decreased postop infection
	- Prevention of transfusion-related increase in tumour recurrence
### 4.2.3. WASHED PRODUCTS

* Indications:
  * IgA deficiency
  * Complement dependent autoimmune hemolytic anemia
  * Continued allergic rxn despite pretreatment with antihistamines
  * 💡 Only 1 in 100 patients with IgA deficiency and anti-IgA antibodies develop anaphylaxis

## 4.3. JEHOVAH'S WITNESS CONSIDERATIONS

* Religious belief background:
  * Passages such as Genesis 9, verse 4 ("But you shall not eat flesh with its life, that is, its blood") and Leviticus 17, verse 10 ("If any one of the house of Israel…eats any blood, I will … cut him off from among his people") lead to a proscription against receiving blood product transfusions
  * Followers believe that once blood has left the body it should not be consumed in any way
  * Receipt of a blood transfusion is believed to cause irreversible death to a member's soul without hope of eternal life
* Management approach:
  * Clear conversation; Jehovah's Witnesses are fully aware of how their belief system affects medical management with routine life-sustaining treatments
  * If elective - preoperative anemia optimization - ESAs, IV Iron, B12/Folate, antifibrinolytics, vit K, factor concentrates, desmopressin should be considered
  * Cell salvage is accepted by some Jehovah's Witnesses
  * Alternative O2-carrying solutions (blood substitutes) are likely to be acceptable to Jehovah's Witnesses

## 4.4. PATIENTS WITH MULTIPLE ANTIBODIES OR RARE BLOOD TYPES

* Autologous blood transfusion is indicated for use in patients in whom it would be difficult to find compatible blood products due to multiple antibodies or rare blood types, and those who refuse to receive allogenic transfusion
* Acute normovolemic hemodilution can be considered in patients with multiple antibodies or rare blood type or those who refuse allogenic transfusion or stored blood component therapy (i.e. Jehovah's witness)

**Bottom Line:** Special populations may require modified blood products such as irradiated, leukoreduced, or washed components. Understanding each patient's specific needs, including religious beliefs like those of Jehovah's Witnesses, is essential for providing appropriate care while respecting patient autonomy.

# 5. TRANSFUSION PROCEDURES

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Scientific Basis of Compatibility Testing|Part A]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Clinical Implementation of Safe Transfusion|Part B]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Safety Systems and Error Prevention|Part C]]

## 5.2. PRETRANSFUSION TESTING

* GXM (Group and Crossmatch):
  * Patient's RBC tested to determine the ABO and RhD type
  * Patient's serum tested against an array of red cells expressing the most important antigens to detect any red cell antibodies
	* Any antibody detected can be identified by further testing
	* Red cell units that lack the corresponding antigen can be selected
  * The patient's sample can be held in the laboratory for up to a week, so that the hospital blood bank can quickly prepare compatible blood without the need for a further patient sample
  * Conventional cross-matching consists of the group and antibody screen, followed by direct confirmation of the compatibility of individual units of red cells with the patient's serum
  * Full cross-matching takes about 45 minutes if no red cell antibodies are present, but may require hours if a patient has multiple antibodies
  * If ABO and RhD groups identified and confirmed on 2 separate occasions and antibody screen negative → can be 'electronically issued' without need for GXM

## 5.3. BEDSIDE PROCEDURE FOR SAFE TRANSFUSION

![](TRANSFUSION_TRANSFUSION_Untitled.png_3a4b08.png_966f08.png)

* ⚠️ Proper identification procedures:
  * Check patient identity using two unique identifiers
  * Check blood product matches patient
  * Check compatibility with previous records
  * Verify expiration date of product
* Monitoring requirements:
  * Vital signs before starting transfusion
  * Stay with patient for first 15 minutes of transfusion
  * Monitor vital signs at regular intervals during transfusion
  * Document completion of transfusion

## 5.4. CONSENT FOR ELECTIVE BLOOD TRANSFUSION

![Consent form](TRANSFUSION_TRANSFUSION_Untitled_1.png_0e9f97.png_264ff8.png)

* Signed consent to be obtained from:
  * Pts >21y of age
  * Parent/legal guardian/responsible adult for pts <21y of age
* Following can give consent even if <21 y:
  * NS <21
  * Female pts <21, undergoing TOP
* Consent can be obtained on a case by case basis for:
  * Married pts <21y
  * Underaged parent of a paediatric pt <21y
* If pt/parent unable to give consent because of illness:
  * 2 drs (MO and Reg/above) can certify blood transfusion is in their best interests
  * ⚠️ Any pts of sound mind can refuse blood transfusion for personal, valid, religious reason
	* But 2 consultants can counter sign

## 5.5. POST-TRANSFUSION PROTOCOL

In case of suspected transfusion reaction, perform:

1. General: FBC, PBF, RP, Repeat GXM, DIC screen
2. Hemolysis test: DCT, Retics, Haptoglobin
3. LFT: bilirubin, LDH
4. Urine: Hemoglobinuria
5. Cultures: blood/urine culture
6. Action:
   * Return entire implicated blood unit/drop set to blood bank
   * Provide details of blood unit/transfusion reaction, hx to blood bank on request form for 'ix of a suspected transfusion reaction'
   * Any remaining blood units, if any, should not be transfused and should be returned to blood bank

**Bottom Line:** Safe transfusion procedures require meticulous attention to proper testing, identification, monitoring, and documentation. Obtaining informed consent and knowing how to manage suspected transfusion reactions are essential components of transfusion safety protocols.

## 6.3. FEBRILE NONHEMOLYTIC TRANSFUSION REACTION

* Classically present within 4h of transfusion

![Recipient alloimmunization to HLAs from donor WBCs and the release of leukocyte-derived cytokines accumulated in the donor product during storage. As a result, the risk of febrile reactions increases with repetitive transfusions.](TRANSFUSION_TRANSFUSION_Untitled_3.png_36a5b2.png_f34dc4.png)

* Recipient alloimmunization to HLAs from donor WBCs and the release of leukocyte-derived cytokines accumulated in the donor product during storage
* Risk increases with repetitive transfusions
* Recipient antibodies react with antigens on the small number of donor leukocytes contained within donor blood
* The antibody–antigen complex activates complement and cytokines are released
* Febrile reactions were previously common, but have become much rarer since the introduction of universal leukodepletion
* Clinical presentation:
  * Fever (temperature rise ≥1°C)
  * Chills, rigors
  * Headache
  * Nausea, vomiting
* Management:
  * Supportive care
  * Antipyretics
  * Prevention with leukoreduction for future transfusions
* Febrile reactions are usually mild, but it is important to note that fever is also an early symptom of the life-threatening immediate haemolytic transfusion reaction

## 6.4. RED CELL INCOMPATIBILITY

### 6.4.1. ACUTE HEMOLYTIC TRANSFUSION REACTION

![](TRANSFUSION_TRANSFUSION_Untitled_5.png_aeed18.png_6feac1.png)

* ABO and rhesus incompatibility
* A haemolytic transfusion reaction will occur if the recipient's plasma contains antibodies that are reactive against the donor's RBC antigens
* The recipient's antibodies coat the donor RBCs: the antibody–antigen complex activates complement, leading to haemolysis of donor RBCs
* Occurs when incompatible ABO group are transfused (especially if a group O recipient is transfused with group A, B or AB red cells)
* The recipient's IgM anti-A, anti-B or anti-AB binds to the transfused red cells → full complement pathway → intravascular hemolysis
* Pathophysiology:
  * C3a and C5a → cytokines release (TNF, IL1, IL8) → degranulation of mast cells w release of vasoactive mediators → increase vascular permeability and hypotension → shock and renal failure; platelet aggregation, lung peribronchial edema and smooth muscle contraction
  * 20–30% of ABO-incompatible transfusions cause some degree of morbidity
  * 5–10% cause or contribute to a patient's death
  * The main reason for this relatively low morbidity is the lack of potency of ABO antibodies in group A or B subjects; even if the recipient is group O, those who are very young or very old usually have weaker antibodies that do not lead to the activation of large amounts of complement

![](TRANSFUSION_TRANSFUSION_Untitled_6.png_3fde97.png_667245.png)

* Clinical presentation:
  * Fever, chills
  * Back/flank pain
  * Chest tightness, dyspnea
  * Hypotension, tachycardia
  * Hemoglobinuria (dark/red urine)
  * Bleeding (DIC)
* Management:
  * Stop transfusion immediately
  * Maintain IV access
  * Support blood pressure with fluids, vasopressors if needed
  * Maintain alkaline urine output - bicarb, mannitol, furosemide, crystalloid
  * Monitor for and treat DIC
  * The diagnosis is confirmed with laboratory findings of elevated lactate dehydrogenase, low haptoglobin, bilirubin increases, a positive direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test, and hematuria

![](TRANSFUSION_TRANSFUSION_Untitled_7.png_ef411e.png_c58244.png)

![](TRANSFUSION_TRANSFUSION_Untitled_8.png_db6d6c.png_0851aa.png)

### 6.4.2. RHESUS D, HEMOLYTIC DISEASE OF NEWBORN

* RhD-negative individuals do not normally produce substantial amounts of anti-RhD antibodies
* However, if RhD-positive red cells enter the circulation of an RhD-negative person → IgG antibodies
* Occurs during pregnancy if the mother is exposed to fetal cells via fetomaternal hemorrhage, or following transfusion
* If a woman is so sensitized → during a subsequent pregnancy anti-RhD antibodies can cross the placenta:
  * If the fetus is RhD-positive → hemolysis with severe fetal anemia and hyperbilirubinemia → severe neurological damage or death due to hemolytic disease of the newborn (HDN)
  * Administration of anti-RhD Ig (anti-D) perinatally prevents this
* ⚠️ Therefore, an RhD-negative female who may subsequently become pregnant should never be transfused with RhD-positive blood
* HDN can also be caused by other alloantibodies against red cell antigens, usually after previous pregnancies or transfusions:
  * Rhc, RhC, RhE, Rhe, and the Kell, Kidd and Duffy antigen systems
* HDN can also occur if there is fetomaternal ABO incompatibility, most commonly seen in a group O mother with a group A fetus:
  * Generally less severely affected by ABO incompatibility than by RhD, Rhc or Kell antigen mismatch, and the incompatibility is often picked up coincidentally after birth
  * ABO Abs are generally IgM class Ab; Group A and Group B predominantly IgM; Serum from O contains antiA antiB and AntiA-B which is IgG predominantly

![](TRANSFUSION_TRANSFUSION_Untitled_9.png_136cc4.png_912c90.png)

### 6.4.3. DELAYED HEMOLYTIC TRANSFUSION REACTION

* Associated with alloantibodies to minor RBC antigens, Kidd, or Rh; one-third have clinical reaction
* Minor RhD antigens and the minor blood group systems may cause a delayed haemolytic transfusion reaction, occurring 7–21 days following transfusion
* Delayed transfusion reactions are difficult to prevent. Following a prior exposure to a blood antigen, patients develop a low titre of antibody – too low for laboratory detection. When incompatible blood is transfused, a secondary immune response occurs: it takes time for new IgG antibodies to be produced, leading to a delay before haemolysis is evident
* Symptoms of DHTRs are much milder than AHTRs and rarely result in major morbidity or mortality because the hemolysis occurs extravascularly in the reticuloendothelial system, liver, and spleen
* Typically occurs 3-10 days after transfusion
* Clinical presentation:
  * Mild fever
  * Mild jaundice
  * Decreased hemoglobin
  * Positive direct Coombs test
* Management:
  * Generally supportive
  * Document antibodies to prevent future reactions

![](TRANSFUSION_TRANSFUSION_Untitled_10.png_e5e982.png_a4e640.png)

## 6.5. TRANSFUSION-ASSOCIATED CIRCULATORY OVERLOAD (TACO)

![](TRANSFUSION_TRANSFUSION_Untitled_11.png_eb97a4.png_a002f3.png)

* Not immune mediated
* In 2018 the CDC updated their definition to require at least three of the following findings:
  * Evidence of acute respiratory distress within 6 hours of transfusion
  * Increased central venous pressure
  * Increased brain natriuretic peptide
  * Clinical signs of volume overload
  * Radiographic evidence of bilateral acute pulmonary edema
* Rapid transfusion can lead to left ventricular failure in susceptible patients (those with heart or renal failure, the elderly, those with hypoalbuminaemia)
* Transfusions in these patients are often accompanied by the administration of furosemide
* The resulting dyspnoea may be confused with TRALI
* Treatment: Diuretic therapy and afterload reduction
* Prevention: slowing rate of transfusion, reducing volume

## 6.6. IMMUNOLOGICAL COMPLICATIONS

### 6.6.1. TRANSFUSION-RELATED IMMUNOMODULATION (TRIM)

* Rare but serious
* Multifactorial mechanisms for immunomodulation after allogenic blood transfusion, implicating transfused WBCs, donor plasma HLA class 1 peptides, cytokines, and immune mediators released during blood product storage leading to an increase in CD4 cells, a decrease in the overall CD4/CD8 T cell ratio, and inhibition of natural killer cells and lymphocytes
* Several experts have also proposed a "two-insult" model for TRIM:
  * First insult: precondition that "primes" both the immune system and vascular endothelium (e.g., trauma, surgery, or acute illness > active neutrophils adhere to vascular endothelial cells > NP hypersensitive to blood-borne immune mediators
  * Second insult: transfused blood products contain WBCs with HLA class I antigens, as well as soluble immune response modifiers in the form of cytokines, complement factors, and the breakdown products of lipid membranes
  * Result: decrease in T cell responsiveness and inhibition of monocyte and lymphocyte function
* 💡 Not related to duration of pRBC storage - ABLE, TRANSFUSE, RECESS, TOTAL trial

### 6.6.2. ALLOIMMUNIZATION

* Alloimmunization: induction of immune response to allogenic antigen exposure
* Occasionally occurs in pregnancy; majority is from transfusion of blood products with immunogenic antigens on RBC surface
* Alloimmunization prompts an amnestic IgG-mediated humoral immunity to foreign proteins > no RBC destruction until second antigen exposure > complement activation + delayed extravascular hemolysis
* Especially evident in those who require frequent platelet transfusions - as platelets have a lot of human platelet agents (HPA1 to 15)
* Using single-donor apheresis units may limit exposure to HPA and HLA; preemptive matching of RBC to ABO + nonABO blood groups can reduce risk of alloimmunization

### 6.6.3. TRANSFUSION-ASSOCIATED DYSPNEA

* Relatively new diagnosis
* Mild respiratory symptoms that develop within 24h of blood product administration - tachypnea, mild hypoxemia, SOB
* Diagnosis of exclusion
* Self-limiting, minimal supportive care with supplemental O2
* Risk can be mitigated by slowing rate of transfusion

### 6.6.4. TRANSFUSION-RELATED ACUTE LUNG INJURY (TRALI)

* National Healthcare Safety Network Hemovigilance Module established consistent criteria for clear diagnosis of TRALI:
  * Acute onset dyspnea within 6 hours of transfusion
  * Hypoxemia (PaO2/FiO2 ≤300 mmHg or oxygen saturation ≤90% on room air)
  * Bilateral infiltrates on chest radiography
  * No evidence of left atrial hypertension
* The clinical picture involves low-pressure noncardiogenic pulmonary edema secondary to neutrophil activation and sequestration in the lungs > endothelial damage and capillary leakage. Once the antibodies are transfused into the recipient, they complex with native WBC antigens on the surface of monocytes (HLA class II), endothelial cells (HLA class I), and neutrophils (HNAs and HLA class I)
* TRALI is thought to occur through both immune and non-immune mechanisms
  * In immune TRALI, antibodies within the donor blood attack the recipient's tissues
  * Non-immune TRALI is thought to be due to neutrophil activation following release of reactive lipid products from the donor RBC membranes
* Whatever the mechanism, TRALI causes a spectrum of lung disease indistinguishable from acute respiratory distress syndrome
* Other proposed mechanism is the "Two-Hit Model" by Silliman

![](TRANSFUSION_TRANSFUSION_Untitled_13.png_36ca22.png_109b16.png)

![](TRANSFUSION_TRANSFUSION_Untitled_14.png_0a1069.png_7e60e0.png)

* Management of TRALI:
  * Supportive to limit lung injury (lung protective ventilation) and optimize oxygenation
  * Maximize PEEP
  * Give judicious fluids and support haemodynamically with vasopressors (if hypotensive) Avoid volume overload
  * Low TV lung protective strategies during MV
  * No steroids, no antibiotics
* Prevention:
  * Use only male donors
  * Prestorage LR and the use of solvent detergent-treated plasma products

### 6.6.5. TRANSFUSION-ASSOCIATED GRAFT VS HOST DISEASE (TAGVHD)

* Incidence is decreasing secondary to preventative γ irradiation and LR
* Rare but mortality is more than 90%
* When there is sharing of a human leucocyte antigen (HLA) haplotype between donor and recipient → allows transfused lymphocytes to engraft, proliferate and recognise the recipient as foreign, resulting in acute GVHD
* Cytotoxic activity of donor T lymphocytes becoming sensitised to their new host (seen as foreign)
* In immunocompromised patients, T-lymphocytes within the donor blood launch an immune response against the recipient's tissues
* Following the introduction of universal leukodepletion, this has become a rare but very serious complication of blood transfusion. Certain high-risk immunocompromised patients therefore require irradiated blood

![](TRANSFUSION_TRANSFUSION_Untitled_15.png_3070bc.png_a9da4a.png)

![](TRANSFUSION_TRANSFUSION_Untitled_16.png_0f6691.png_689ff6.png)

* Acute (in the first 100d) -1/3 of pts:
  * Skin: rashes
  * Liver: jaundice
  * Gut: diarrhea
  * Mild to lethal in severity
  * Prevention:
	* HLA matching
	* Immunosuppressant drugs: methotresxate, ciclosporin, alemtuzumab, ATG
* Chronic (arise independently or follow acute GVHD):
  * Resembles CTD
  * Rx: glucocorticoids, prolonged immunosuppression (ie. ciclosporin)
  * Increased infection risk
  * Has GVD effect and lower relapse of underlying malignancy
* Prevention:
  * Gammaor X-ray irradiation of blood components before their administration to prevent lymphocyte proliferation
  * ⚠️ Leucoreduction only reduce by 10^3 (10^9 to 10^6/l); GVHD at 10^4/l, leucoreduction does not prevent TAGVHD
* Those at risk of TA GVHD, who must receive irradiated blood components:
  * Congenital T-cell immunodeficiencies
  * Hodgkin lymphoma
  * Aplastic anemia receiving immunosuppressive therapy with antithymocyte globulin (ATG)
  * Recipients of hematopoietic stem cell transplants or of blood from a family member
  * Neonates who have received an intrauterine transfusion
  * T-lymphocyte-suppressing drugs, such as fludarabine and other purine analogues

### 6.6.6. POST-TRANSFUSION PURPURA

* Severe thrombocytopenia with purpura 5-10 days posttransfusion
* Rare, high morbidity and mortality
* Plt count can fall <10,000/L or <25% of pretransfusion levels
* Most pts are female (previously pregnant) w plt specific alloantibodies such as anti-HPA-1a > destruction of transfused and autologous platelets
* Treatment: IVIG, plasmapharesis to remove Ab

### 6.6.7. ALLERGIC REACTIONS

* For mild allergic reactions:
  * Pathophysiology is unclear but likely involves an immune response to recipient WBC antigens or transfused plasma proteins
  * Prophylaxis with antihistamine may not have any benefit
  * Treat supportively when symptoms arise and reduce rate of transmission; else wash cellular products
* Foreign protein in the donor blood cross-reacts with recipient IgE
* Allergic reactions are usually mild, causing urticaria and pruritus

## 6.7. TRANSFUSION-TRANSMITTED INFECTION

* Based on the reported incidence of infection in donors for the American Red Cross Blood Services. These residual risks are likely overestimates, since not all transfusions of known infected blood products result in recipient infection. The estimates assume 100% infectivity with even one infectious unit of viral particles per blood bag. It also presumes that the collection and storage process is harmless to viral reproduction. Furthermore, viral infectivity will vary depending on the acute phase of infection when an immunocompetent recipient could resist transmission or the chronic phase of infection when antibodies are present and reduce infectivity
* By 2013 in the UK, the estimated chance that a unit of blood from a 'safe' donor might transmit one of the viruses for which blood is tested was:
  * 1 in 6.6 million units for HIV-1
  * 1 in 51.5 million for HCV
  * 1 in 2.6 million for HBV
* Variant CJD is a human prion disease linked to bovine spongiform encephalitis
  * The risk of a recipient acquiring the agent of vCJD from a transfusion is uncertain, but of 16 recipients of blood from donors who later developed the disease, 3 have died with clinical vCJD and 1 other had postmortem immunohistological features of infection
* Bacterial contamination is extremely rare - can result in severe bacteremia and sepsis
* Septicaemia from bacterial contamination of blood products can lead to septicaemia. Platelets are at a particular risk because they are stored at room temperature, which allows bacteria to replicate
* ⚠️ Still, avoid non-essential transfusion

## 6.8. ANAPHYLAXIS AND ANAPHYLACTOID REACTIONS

* For anaphylaxis/anaphylactoid (most major allergic responses have no evidence of T1 hypersensitivity IgE antibodies):
  * Recipient - typically IgA deficient with IgA antibodies or have anti-haptoglobulin or anti-C4 antibodies
  * Wash blood products prior to transfusion or use products from IgA deficient donors

## 6.9. METABOLIC DERANGEMENTS

* HyperK, Citrate toxicity, Hypothermia
* HyperK:
  * As storage time for blood products increases, the cellular components leak potassium and metabolize glucose into lactate, resulting in hyperkalemia and/or acidemia in the transfused product
  * Acidosis is quickly cleared by physiologic buffers, as citrate preservative in blood products is metabolized to bicarbonate; therefore, ongoing acidosis in patients undergoing massive transfusion is likely secondary to tissue hypoxia and not to transfusion of acidemic blood products
  * ⚠️ Hyperkalemia results from high-volume transfusion, especially when infusion rates exceed 100 to 150 mL/hr
  * Packed RBCs can contain over 7 mEq/dL of potassium depending on the storage age and can produce consequences of hyperkalemia
* Citrate:
  * Anticoagulant
  * Metabolized by liver
  * Rapid administration can accumulate and chelate calcium > hypocalcemia > weakness, tetany, arrhythmias, myocardial dysfunction, acquired coagulopathy

## 6.10. IRON OVERLOAD

* pRBCs carry iron as a result of hemolysis during storage and transfusion
* Each pRBC have 200-250mg of iron
* Increased mortality if have >20u of pRBCs > organ iron deposition > generation of harmful intracellular free radicals > cellular dysfunction + organ failure
* Increased iron availability for microorganisms increases risk of recurrent infections
* Treatment: chelation therapy - deferoxamine or deferasirox
* The body does not have a disposal mechanism for excess iron; only the absorption of iron is regulated
* Haemosiderosis (iron overload) can occur in patients who require frequent blood transfusions, such as those with thalassaemia or sickle cell disease
* Excess iron deposits in the liver, heart and endocrine organs, resulting in organ damage

## 6.11. OTHER COMPLICATIONS

* Air embolism

**Bottom Line:** Transfusion reactions range from mild to life-threatening, with immediate recognition and appropriate management being critical. Understanding the pathophysiology, prevention strategies, and treatment approaches for each type of reaction is essential for patient safety. The most serious reactions include acute hemolytic reactions, TRALI, and TA-GVHD.

## 7.2. MASSIVE TRANSFUSION PROTOCOL

![4 pRBC, 4 FFP, 4 PLC. 2nd round has cryo](TRANSFUSION_TRANSFUSION_Untitled.png_c01023.png_29d5e1.png)

* Definitions of massive bleeding/transfusion (indication for MTP):
  * > 4u RBC in 1h
  * > 10u RBC or 50% blood loss in <3h
  * 150ml blood loss/min
  * > 2.5L over 4H
  * > 1 blood volume in 24h
* Massive transfusion is the transfusion of a greater volume of stored blood than a patient's circulating volume in a 24‑h period or transfusion of more than half a patient's circulating volume in a 4‑h period (note: there are many other similar definitions)

## 7.3. BLOOD SELECTION FOR UNKNOWN PATIENT BLOOD GROUP

* Red Cells:
  * Blood group O Rhesus Negative pRBC for Caucasian & Indian Female patients of child-bearing age or younger
  * Blood group O Rhesus Positive pRBC for all other patients
* FFP:
  * Blood group AB Rhesus Positive FFP for all patients
* Platelets & Cryoprecipitate:
  * Platelets will usually be Group O, but cryoprecipitate can be of any ABO group. ABO compatibility is not essential for platelet and cryoprecipitate transfusion
  * The cryoprecipitate will be either pre-pooled (preferred option – prepooled cryoprecipitate is available and stored at each restructured hospital blood bank), or prepared and provided direct from 24-Hour Cross-match Lab, BSG). - given from 2nd MTP onwards
* ⚠️ Patients who are already known to be RH negative & who have positive antibodies should be excluded from this MTP protocol:
  * For MTP patients of known RH Negative Blood Group, the team doctor should use the 1st two units of Emergency O-ve Blood if necessary, (already available) but also contact the BSG MO/Team urgently for advice
  * For patients with known requirement for rare blood due to clinically significant red cell allo-antibodies, the team doctor should contact the BSG MO/team immediately to seek advice. (PLEASE NOTE: NOT ALL ANTIBODIES ARE CLINICALLY SIGNIFICANT. IF IN DOUBT-PLEASE CHECK WITH BSG TEAM)

## 7.4. CONSTITUENTS OF MTP

* 💡 Volume wise PCT and FFP is 1:1, the 1 unit of pooled platelets is very small like 30-40ml but made from 4u of blood - not to get confused
	* 1u FFP ~250ml contains 0.8g fibrinogen (total 3.2g in 4u)

## 7.7. SUPPORTIVE MEASURES

1. Avoid Hypothermia (keep T>35C: eg. with IV warming device, Bair Hugger, Ambient Temperature Control, etc ), Acidosis (keep pH> 7.1) and Hypocalcaemia
2. Constant monitoring of FBC, coagulation profile (PT, aPTT, serum fibrinogen), with a aim to further correct any coagulation abnormalities on lab result beyond MTP replacement of hemostatic factors:
   * Keep PT/PTT < 1.5x reference value
   * Serum fibrinogen > 1.5 g/L or 150 mg/dL
   * Platelets >50x10^9/L
   * Additional transfusions of cryoprecipitate should be considered if fibrinogen < 1.5 g/dl
   * In cases of major obstetric haemorrhage, fibrinogen replacement should be considered when serum fibrinogen falls below 2 g/L
3. All labs are sent STAT while MTP is in progress. Laboratory values and amount of products administered should all be tracked
4. ⚠️ Strict compliance with product/recipient identification procedures is mandatory, regardless of time pressures
5. The MTP leader or designee keeps the BB informed of changing needs or location
6. Patients are to be transfused with type specific and cross matched blood whenever possible

## 7.9. COMPLICATIONS OF MASSIVE TRANSFUSION

1. **Hypothermia**
   * Blood products are stored cold!
   * This worsens coagulopathy and is why you need to run blood through a warming device
   * Transfusion of multiple units of refrigerated packed cells leads to a fall in core body temperature through conduction
   * Hypothermia is associated with coagulopathy, cardiac arrhythmias, reduced tissue oxygenation (as the oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve is shifted to the left) and a worse overall outcome
   * The risk of hypothermia can be reduced by warming all transfused blood and fluids, as well as active warming of the patient; for example, by using a forced-air warmer

2. **Coagulopathy**
   * Dilutional thrombocytopenia - Platelet count likely <100,000 after ~10 units pRBCs
   * Dilutional coagulopathies - Decreased Factors V & VIII ("labile factors") in stored blood
   * Multiple transfusions of packed RBCs along with crystalloid administration leads to dilution of plasma constituents
   * Of particular importance are clotting factors and platelets because haemostasis will become impossible, leading to further haemorrhage and a requirement for more blood products
   * Aggressive and pre-emptive replacement of clotting factors and platelets with FFP, cryoprecipitate and platelet concentrate is required

3. **Acid-Base Abnormalities**
   * At 21 days, stored blood has pH <7.0, due mostly to CO2 production, which can be rapidly eliminated with respiration
   * Acidosis more commonly occurs due to decreased tissue perfusion
   * A unit of packed RBCs has a lower pH (around 6.8) than plasma, mainly as a result of lactate accumulation due to the anaerobic metabolism of erythrocytes during storage
   * Massive transfusion may therefore worsen acidosis, though the additional lactate is usually rapidly cleared by the liver

4. **Citrate toxicity**
   * Citrate is in CPDA storage solution as a Ca2+ chelator
   * Rapid transfusion (>65cc/min in a healthy adult with healthy liver) can cause an acute hypocalcemia
   * Citrate also binds magnesium causing hypomagnesemia
   * Clinically important hypocalcemia, causing cardiac depression, will not occur in most normal patients unless the transfusion rate exceeds 1 unit every 5 min, and intravenous calcium salts should rarely be required in the absence of measured hypocalcemia
   * Packed cells contain only a small amount of the anticoagulant citrate; FFP and platelet concentrate contain much higher citrate concentrations
   * The role of citrate is the chelation of Ca2+, preventing the coagulation of stored blood products
   * In massive transfusion, there is sufficient intravascular citrate to cause a severe hypocalcaemia
   * Without Ca2+ replacement, this can lead to hypotension and electrocardiogram changes (bradycardia, flat ST segments, prolonged QT interval)
   * Hypocalcaemia should be treated with 10 mL of 10% calcium chloride

5. **Hyperkalemia**
   * K+ moves out of pRBCs during storage
   * If EKG changes occur, stop transfusion and treat hyperkalemia
   * As discussed above, when RBCs are stored, K+ seeps out of the erythrocytes
   * When blood is transfused, K+ tends to diffuse back into the RBCs, but this process is hindered if the patient is acidotic or hypothermic, leading to hyperkalaemia

6. **Impaired O2-Delivery Capacity**
   * 2,3-DPG decreases in stored blood, causing a left-shifted O2-Hb dissociation curve rendering Hgb to hold on to & not release as much oxygen at target sites

**Bottom Line:** Massive transfusion protocols provide a standardized approach to replacement of blood products in patients with severe hemorrhage. Key elements include fixed ratios of blood components, measures to prevent hypothermia, acidosis, and hypocalcemia, along with ongoing laboratory monitoring to guide therapy. Understanding and preventing complications specific to massive transfusion is essential for optimal patient outcomes.

## 8.2. PERIOPERATIVE ANEMIA MANAGEMENT

* Perioperative iron supplementation:
  * PO Iron takes 2-4 weeks to be effective, a/w poor bioavailability and AE (limiting patient compliance)
  * IV Iron effective for short term when surgery is more urent
* Perioperative EPO:
  * FDA approved for treatment of anaemia in oncology patients with chemotherapy-induced anaemia and CKD
  * Used to optimize patients with preoperative anemia undergoing surgery with significant risk of transfusion and patients undergoing preoperative autologous donation (PAD)
  * General routine use not recommended for now - increased VTE risk
  * Indicated in conjunction with iron supplementation in anemic patients with hemoglobin levels <13.0 g/dL anticipating major orthopedic surgery

## 8.3. AUTOLOGOUS BLOOD TRANSFUSION

* Initially developed because of rising risk of transfusion-transmitted viral infections in 1980s - but now allogenic blood components have very low infectious risk
* Given the general acceptance for restrictive transfusion practices, the increased wastage of PAD units (up to 45%), diminishes the utility and cost-effectiveness of its routine use, especially since intraoperative blood salvage (IOBS) is clinically efficacious
  * ⚠️ Yet, avoiding wastage is not an appropriate reason to transfuse autologous unit - i.e. overtransfusion to "use up" stored autologous blood
* Indicated for use in patients in whom it would be difficult to find compatible blood products due to multiple antibodies or rare blood types, and those who refuse to receive allogenic transfusion
* Avoid if the scheduled procedure is at risk of postponement because autologous donated units may become outdated if surgery does not proceed on time
* Composed of 3 separate processes:
* Autologous blood transfusion is where blood is taken from a patient and reinfused back into the same patient when required (e.g. intraoperative cell salvage, preoperative autologous blood donation, acute normovolaemic haemodilution)

### 8.3.1. PREOPERATIVE AUTOLOGOUS BLOOD DONATION

* Collected 4-5 weeks prior
* Eliminates risk of transfusion-transmitted infection and alloimmunization
* But at risk for clerical error, TACO, bacterial infection, metabolic derangements and TRIM
* With iron supplementation (for iron deficient) and erythropoietin therapy, at least 3 or 4 units can usually be collected prior to operation
* Eligible to donate a unit as long as hematocrit at least 34% or Hb at least 11 - rarely able to donate >4u (but if start earlier, can donate more)
* Min of 72h between donations to ensure plasma volume has returned to normal
* Despite the ability to donate up until 72hs before surgery, it is not recommended to undergo donation within 28 days of planned surgery as PAD rapidly loses its effectiveness when donation occurs in proximity to surgery and patients present with preoperative anemia

### 8.3.2. ACUTE NORMOVOLEMIC HEMODILUTION (ANH)

* ANH is the process of extracting multiple units of blood (predetermined volume) immediately before surgical incision while maintaining euvolemia with crystalloid or colloid supplementation (healthy patients will compensate with lower O2 carrying capacity of blood by increasing CO)
* Premised on if concentration of red cells is decreased, total red cell loss is reduced when large amounts of blood are shed; because intravascular volume is maintained, cardiac output remains normal
* Blood is stored in a CPD bag at room temp (up to 6h)
* Target Hb nadir 25%-30% >> Volume to be removed = EBV × [(Hcti – Hctt)/Hctave]
  * EBV is the estimated blood volume; Hcti, the starting hematocrit; Hctt, the target low hematocrit; Hctave, the average of Hct
* Eliminates the infectious and alloimmunization risks of allogenic transfusion, as well as the immunomodulatory risks of blood storage
* Most comparative studies demonstrated ANH to be inferior to high-efficiency IOBS and protocolized PAD for non-cardiac surgery
* Not recommended for routine use
* Considered in patients w multiple antibodies or rare blood type or those who refuse allogenic transfusion or stored blood component therapy (i.e. Jehovah's witness)
* Most effective in patients with high preoperative hemoglobin levels, minimal cardiovascular comorbidities that allow intraoperative anemia, and a surgical risk for large volume blood loss

## 8.4. CELL SALVAGE

* Cell salvage is a method of autologous blood transfusion: a patient's own blood is collected, processed, stored and retransfused
* It is an important method of reducing the requirement for allogenic transfusion, with all its associated risks
* Cell salvage is usually performed intraoperatively, where spilt blood is collected from the surgical field
* This blood is then processed and returned to the patient
* The steps involved in the processing of blood depend on the particular cell salvage equipment – for example, RBCs may be centrifuged, washed and resuspended in saline

### 8.4.1. INTRAOPERATIVE BLOOD CELL SALVAGE (IOBS)

* Efficient, cost-effective, and safe
* Shed blood is aspirated intraoperatively into a reservoir and mixed with heparin
  * Uses a double-lumen suction catheter with one port for aspiration from the surgical field and the other for the addition of an anticoagulant solution, usually heparin or citrate
* After a sufficient amount of blood is collected, the red cells are concentrated and washed to remove debris and anticoagulant and then suspended in saline ready for reinfusion
  * Newer, simpler systems allow reinfusion of shed blood without centrifugation
  * By filtering through leukodepletion filter + irradiation, no concerns for use in cancer surgery or contamination by urine and amniotic fluid
* May be stored at 4°C for up to 6 hours with careful patient and product identification
* To be used effectively, this technique requires blood losses greater than 1000 to 1500 mL
* IOBS yields a hematocrit ranging from 55% to 70%
  * Yield affected by volume of blood processed, length of time blood remains on wound, rate and precision of suctioning (increased turbulence causes RBC damage by shear stress)
* Evidence shows that salvaged blood has better O2 carrying capacity and tissue oxygenation than stored blood, secondary to retention of red cell's biconcave disc shape and increased levels of 2,3–DPG and ATP
* Contraindications to blood salvage and reinfusion include septic contamination of the wound and perhaps malignancy. But does not exacerbate alloimmunization (fetal RBC mixing w mother RBC)
* Indicated for patients with low preoperative hemoglobin who cannot tolerate PAD or ANH, those unwilling to consent to allogenic transfusion, and patients with pre-existing bleeding risks or multiple alloantibodies
* Risks include nonimmunogenic hemolysis, fever, and contamination with various substances such as topical anticoagulants, urine, amniotic fluid, or bacteria

### 8.4.2. ADVANTAGES AND DISADVANTAGES OF CELL SALVAGE

#### 8.4.2.1. ADVANTAGES

* Cell salvage is more cost-effective than autologous transfusion
* It does not have the infective and immune risks of autologous transfusion
* Cell salvage is accepted by some Jehovah's Witnesses

#### 8.4.2.2. DISADVANTAGES

* Electrolyte abnormalities may occur, particularly in massive transfusion
* Clotting factors are lost when blood is processed; a dilutional coagulopathy may occur
* In obstetric haemorrhage, there is a risk of precipitating an amniotic fluid embolus, as amniotic fluid mixed with the salvaged RBCs may not be completely removed by the washing process and subsequently be reinfused. However, cell salvage is now considered safe if used in combination with a leukocyte depletion filter
* In cancer surgery, there is a risk that malignant cells suctioned along with spilt blood from the surgical site may not be completely removed by the washing process and be reinfused. Again, a number of studies have shown that the use of a leukocyte depletion filter reduces this risk significantly

### 8.4.3. POSTOPERATIVE BLOOD SALVAGE (POBS)

* Collection and reinfusion of blood shed into surgical wound drains in the immediate postoperative period
* Recovered blood product types:
  * Washed - centrifuged, washed, resuspended
	* Removes inflammatory mediators, activated clotting factors, fibrin, fibrin split products, products of haemolysis (i.e. from thorax and mediastinal surgeries) that can cause renal damage, lung injury or coagulopathy
  * Unwashed - filtered before reinfusion > resultant hematocrit of unwashed POBS ranges from 20% to 30% and should not be expected to increase the patient's hemoglobin level
	* Aid in avoidance of dilutional anemia associated with the fluid resuscitation for postoperative bleeding
* Overall efficacy and cost-effectiveness remain unclear
* Contraindicated for patients with preexisting hemoglobinopathies such as sickle cell disease and thalassemia. Avoid if blood has been contaminated with microbials or drugs not indicated for systemic use (chlorhex)

## 8.5. SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS FOR JEHOVAH'S WITNESSES

* Passages such as Genesis 9, verse 4 ("But you shall not eat flesh with its life, that is, its blood") and Leviticus 17, verse 10 ("If any one of the house of Israel…eats any blood, I will … cut him off from among his people") lead to a proscription against receiving blood product transfusions. Followers believe that once blood has left the body it should not be consumed in any way. Receipt of a blood transfusion is believed to cause irreversible death to a member's soul without hope of eternal life
* Clear conversation; Jehovah's Witnesses are fully aware of how their belief system affects medical management with routine life-sustaining treatments
* If elective - preoperative anaemia optimization - ESAs, IV Iron, B12/Folate, antifibrinolytics, vit K, factor concentrates, desmopressin should be considered
* Cell salvage is accepted by some Jehovah's Witnesses
* Alternative O2-carrying solutions (blood substitutes) are likely to be acceptable to Jehovah's Witnesses

**Bottom Line:** Perioperative blood conservation strategies include preoperative anemia management, autologous blood donation, acute normovolemic hemodilution, and intraoperative/postoperative blood salvage. Each technique has specific indications, contraindications, and efficacy considerations. These strategies are especially important for patients who refuse allogeneic transfusion or have rare blood types or multiple antibodies.
