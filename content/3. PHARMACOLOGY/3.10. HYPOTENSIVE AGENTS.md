# 1. INTRODUCTION TO HYPOTENSIVE AGENTS 












## 1.1. PHYSIOLOGICAL BASIS OF HYPERTENSION AND BLOOD PRESSURE CONTROL

* **Vascular aging affects blood pressure regulation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * As patients age, their vasculature becomes less compliant [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * When a pulse wave is generated by ventricular contraction, it propagates through the arterial system and is reflected back at branch points of the aorta [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * In younger patients, the reflected wave tends to augment diastole, improving diastolic pressure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * In older patients, the reflected wave arrives sooner due to noncompliant vasculature during late systole [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
	* Causes increased cardiac workload [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Results in decreased diastolic pressure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Older patients typically develop increased systolic pressure and decreased diastolic pressure, creating a widened pulse pressure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Widened pulse pressures (difference between systolic and diastolic) are associated with [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
	* Increased incidence of postoperative kidney dysfunction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Increased risk of cerebral events in patients undergoing coronary bypass surgery [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

## 1.2. CLINICAL INDICATIONS FOR HYPOTENSIVE THERAPY

* **Hypertensive emergencies** require immediate intervention [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Defined as blood pressure >180/120 mm Hg with signs of organ injury (e.g., encephalopathy) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Treatment goals:
	* Mean arterial pressure should be reduced gradually to prevent organ hypoperfusion (except in acute aortic dissection) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* Target initial reduction: 20% decrease in mean arterial pressure or a diastolic blood pressure of 100–110 mm Hg [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

**Enhanced Evidence-Based Perioperative Hypertension Management:**

**Risk Stratification by Surgery Type:**
* **Noncardiac surgery:** In absence of concomitant cardiovascular disease or hypertensive end-organ damage (e.g., LV hypertrophy or renal dysfunction), isolated hypertension does not significantly increase perioperative risk
* **Meta-analysis findings:** Over 30 observational studies found no clinically significant association between hypertension (systolic <180 mm Hg and diastolic <110 mm Hg) and perioperative complications
* **⚠️ Severe hypertension threshold:** Limited data exists for blood pressures above 180/110 mm Hg - clinical judgment required

**High-Risk Surgical Contexts:**
* **Cardiovascular procedures:** Systolic hypertension (>140 mm Hg), systolic hypervariability (>140 and/or <80 mm Hg), and pulse pressure hypertension (>80 mm Hg) are significant risk factors for:
  * Perioperative death
  * Stroke
  * LV dysfunction
  * Renal failure
* **Vascular procedures:** Perioperative hypertension significant risk factor for neurologic deficit in both carotid endarterectomy and carotid stenting
* **Pheochromocytoma resection:** Progressive reduction in perioperative cardiovascular complications attributed to contemporary perioperative hypertension management

**Clinical Considerations:**
* **LV hypertrophy impact:** Adds significant additional perioperative cardiovascular risk in noncardiac surgery - typically results from long-standing systemic hypertension
* **White coat hypertension:** Very common on day of surgery even in patients with no previous hypertension history due to anxiety - confers no additional perioperative cardiovascular risk
* **Management approach:** Randomized trial of 989 patients with well-controlled baseline hypertension presenting with diastolic BP >110 mm Hg showed no outcome difference between immediate surgery vs. delayed surgery with further medical control

**Evidence-Based Treatment Thresholds:**
* **Proceed with surgery:** Most patients with systolic <180 mm Hg and diastolic <110 mm Hg, especially if no end-organ damage
* **Consider delay:** Systolic ≥180 mm Hg or diastolic ≥110 mm Hg, particularly with evidence of end-organ complications
* **Emergency surgery:** Weigh surgical urgency against cardiovascular risk - may proceed with enhanced monitoring regardless of blood pressure

**Bottom Line:** Understanding age-related vascular changes helps explain hypertension patterns in older patients. Treatment of hypertensive emergencies requires careful balance to avoid organ hypoperfusion while adequately reducing dangerous blood pressure levels.

# 2. NITROVASODILATORS 












## 2.1. COMMON MECHANISM OF ACTION

* Organic nitrates (e.g., nitroglycerin) and NO donors (e.g., sodium nitroprusside) release NO through different mechanisms [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Organic nitrates: Enzymatic sulfhydryl group reduction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * NO donors: Spontaneous mechanism that does not require metabolism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Common pathway after NO release** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * NO stimulates guanylyl cyclase within vascular smooth muscle cells [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Guanylyl cyclase converts GTP to cyclic guanosine 3',5'-monophosphate (cGMP) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * cGMP activates protein kinase G [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Protein kinase G dephosphorylates myosin light chains, causing relaxation of vascular smooth muscle [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * NO also stimulates reuptake of Ca²⁺ into the endoplasmic reticulum by activating its Ca²⁺-ATPase through a cGMP-independent mechanism, decreasing intracellular Ca²⁺ and causing relaxation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Causes K⁺ efflux from cell by opening K⁺ channels, resulting in cellular hyperpolarization which closes voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels, leading to relaxation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

## 2.2. CLINICAL EFFECTS AND APPLICATIONS 

* **Primary hemodynamic effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]], [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Arterial and venodilation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Venodilation reduces preload, resulting in reduced LV and RV end-diastolic volume, pressure, and wall stress [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Arterial dilation reduces systemic and pulmonary arterial pressures, decreasing LV and RV end-systolic wall stress [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * These effects decrease myocardial O₂ demand and increase supply of O₂ by dilating epicardial coronary arteries [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Reduced LV end-diastolic pressure and coronary vasodilation enhance subendocardial perfusion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Clinical applications** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Effective in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * No evidence of efficacy in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

## 2.3. TOLERANCE AND ADVERSE EFFECTS 

* **Tolerance development** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Cardiovascular effects inevitably decline with prolonged use [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Mechanisms of tolerance:
	* Some patients may be relatively resistant in the presence of oxidative stress because superoxide anions scavenge NO, cause reversible oxidation of guanylate cyclase, and inhibit aldehyde dehydrogenase [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Progressive attenuation of hemodynamic responses may develop from sympathetic nervous system and renin–angiotensin–aldosterone axis activation ("pseudo-tolerance") [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* This can lead to rebound hypertension when nitrovasodilators are abruptly discontinued [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Inhibition of guanylate cyclase activity is most likely responsible for true tolerance to organic nitrates [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Management of tolerance:
	* A daily "drug holiday" can reverse this effect in patients requiring prolonged treatment [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Administration of N-acetylcysteine (a sulfhydryl donor) may also be effective [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Adverse effects with prolonged use** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Methemoglobinemia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Interference with platelet aggregation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Heparin resistance [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * ⚠️ Use with caution in patients receiving PDE V inhibitors (sildenafil) because NO-induced vasodilation is enhanced, which can lead to profound hypotension, acute myocardial infarction, and death [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

## 2.4. SODIUM NITROPRUSSIDE 









**Potent, short-acting direct NO donor vasodilator for hypertensive emergencies and controlled hypotension.**

- Exists as a lyophilized reddish-brown powder
- Reconstitute with dextrose > straw colored solution with pH 4.5
- Sensitive to sunlight > need opaque syringe/aluminium foil wrapped
- Will turn dark brown or blue
- Run as infusion
### 2.4.1. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* **Short-acting direct NO donor** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* Extremely rapid onset of action (1–2 min) and fleeting duration of action allow precise titration of arterial blood pressure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* After IV injection, sodium nitroprusside enters red blood cells [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* It receives an electron from the iron (Fe²⁺) of oxyhemoglobin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* This nonenzymatic electron transfer results in an unstable nitroprusside radical and methemoglobin (Hgb Fe³⁺) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* The former moiety spontaneously decomposes into five cyanide ions and the active nitroso (NO) group [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* Sodium nitroprusside vasodilates arteries and veins by the production of nitric oxide (NO) which activates guanylate cyclase [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

### 2.4.2. METABOLISM AND TOXICITY   

* **Cyanide metabolism** has three possible pathways [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * (1) Binding to methemoglobin to form cyanmethemoglobin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * (2) Undergoing a reaction in the liver and kidney catalyzed by the enzyme rhodanase (thiosulfate + cyanide → thiocyanate) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * (3) Binding to tissue cytochrome oxidase, which interferes with normal oxygen utilization leading to cyanide toxicity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* The metabolism is complex - within the red blood cell, SNP reacts with oxyhemoglobin to form NO, five CN⁻ ions and methemoglobin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* The methemoglobin may combine with CN⁻ to form cyanomethemoglobin (non-toxic) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* CN⁻ escaping from the red blood cell is converted in the liver and kidney by rhodanase with a sulfhydryl group to form thiocyanate (SCN) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Red blood cells contain thiocyanate oxidase, which can convert SCN back to CN⁻, but most SCN is excreted in the urine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* CN⁻ can also combine with hydroxycobalamin (vitamin B12) to form cyanocobalamin, which forms a non-toxic store of CN⁻ that can be excreted in urine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

![Untitled](HYPOTENSIVE_HYPOTENSIVE_Untitled_1.png_6523a3.png_7ff8d2.png)

* **Cyanide toxicity** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]], [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]:
  * Characterized by metabolic acidosis, cardiac arrhythmias, and increased venous oxygen content (due to inability to utilize oxygen) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * An early sign is acute resistance to the hypotensive effects of increasing doses of sodium nitroprusside (tachyphylaxis) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * More likely if cumulative daily dose is greater than 500 mcg/kg or administered at rates greater than 2 mcg/kg/min for more than a few hours [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Free CN⁻ can bind cytochrome oxidase and impair aerobic metabolism, leading to metabolic acidosis and increased mixed venous oxygen saturation as tissues become unable to utilize oxygen [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] . > mixed venous O2 saturation should increase
  * ⚠️ **Treatment of cyanide toxicity** [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]:
	* 100% O₂ administration [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].
	* Sodium thiosulfate (150 mg/kg over 15 min) to provide additional sulfhydryl groups to facilitate the conversion of CN⁻ to SCN [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]. 
	* 3% sodium nitrite (5 mg/kg over 5 min), which oxidizes hemoglobin to methemoglobin [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]. - oxidizer
		* A methemoglobin concentration of 10% to 20% is the aim of nitrite administration [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].
		* ⚠️ Use nitrite with caution in patients experiencing cyanide toxicity secondary to combustion, as carboxyhemoglobin from CO poisoning might be present [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].
	* Hydroxocobalamin (Vit B12) combines with cyanide to form cyanocobalamin (vitamin B12) and can be administered to treat cyanide poisoning [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].
	* Dicobalt edetate, which chelates CN⁻ ions, may also be used [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]] .
* **Thiocyanate toxicity** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Accumulation of large amounts of thiocyanate (e.g., in patients with kidney failure) may result in a milder toxic reaction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Symptoms include thyroid dysfunction, muscle weakness, nausea, hypoxia, and acute toxic psychosis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * SCN has an elimination half-life of 2 days but this may increase to 7 days in the presence of renal impairment [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Methemoglobinemia treatment** [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]:
  * Excessive doses of sodium nitroprusside or sodium nitrite causing methemoglobinemia can be treated with methylene blue (1–2 mg/kg of a 1% solution over 5 min), which reduces methemoglobin to hemoglobin [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].

### 2.4.3. CLINICAL USES AND DOSING

* **Primary indications** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Potent and reliable first-line agent for hypertensive emergencies [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * May also be used in cardiogenic shock as a potent arterial vasodilator (improves forward flow) and decreases preload and LV filling pressures [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Dosing** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Diluted to a concentration of 100 mcg/mL and administered as a continuous intravenous infusion (0.25–5 mcg/kg/min) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * 💡 Requires protection from light to prevent degradation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Presented as red-brown lyophilized (freeze-dried) powder that is reconstituted with 5% dextrose [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Usually administered as a 0.005-0.02% (50-200 μg/ml) intravenous infusion, the dose of 0.5-6 μg·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹ being titrated to effect [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .

### 2.4.4. SYSTEM-SPECIFIC EFFECTS  

* **Cardiovascular effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]], [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Reduced preload and afterload [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Cardiac output is usually unchanged in normal patients [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * May increase cardiac output in patients with congestive heart failure, mitral regurgitation, or aortic regurgitation due to afterload reduction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Arterial vasodilation reduces the systemic vascular resistance and leads to a drop in blood pressure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Venous vasodilation increases the venous capacitance and reduces preload [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Cardiac output is maintained by a reflex tachycardia, but for patients with heart failure, the reduction in pre and afterload will increase cardiac output with no increase in heart rate [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * ⚠️ Despite decreased myocardial O₂ requirements initially, reflex-mediated responses to falling arterial BP can cause tachycardia and increased myocardial contractility, increasing myocardial O₂ demand (detrimental when AMI is present) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * ⚠️ Dilation of coronary arterioles may result in an intracoronary steal of blood flow away from ischemic areas that are already maximally dilated - **contraindicated in patients with AMI** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **CNS effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Dilates cerebral vessels [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Abolishes cerebral autoregulation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Maintains or increases cerebral blood flow unless MAP is markedly reduced [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * ⚠️ Increased cerebral blood volume may increase intracranial pressure, especially in patients with reduced intracranial compliance (e.g., brain tumors) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * This intracranial hypertension can be minimized by slow administration and institution of hypocapnia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Intracranial pressure is increased due to cerebral vasodilation and increased cerebral blood flow, however, cerebral autoregulation is maintained well below the normal limits during SNP infusion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Pulmonary effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Dilates pulmonary vasculature, reducing pulmonary artery pressure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * May decrease perfusion of normally ventilated alveoli, increasing physiological dead space [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Prevents normal vasoconstrictive response of pulmonary vasculature to hypoxia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * These effects cause V/Q mismatch, increase venous admixture, and decrease arterial oxygenation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * SNP may inhibit pulmonary hypoxic vasoconstriction and lead to increased shunt. Supplemental oxygen may help [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Other effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Decreased arterial BP leads to increased renin and catecholamine release [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Renin release can be inhibited with β-blockers [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Kidney function is fairly well maintained during sodium nitroprusside infusion, despite moderate drops in arterial blood pressure and renal perfusion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Some patients develop tachyphylaxis, the exact mechanism of which is unclear [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

**Bottom Line:** Sodium nitroprusside is a potent, fast-acting vasodilator that allows precise blood pressure control, but requires careful monitoring for metabolic acidosis and venous O₂ content due to the risk of cyanide toxicity, especially with prolonged infusions or higher doses.

## 2.5. NITROGLYCERIN (GLYCERYL TRINITRATE) 









**Organic nitrate that primarily causes venodilation, used for myocardial ischemia, hypertension, and heart failure.**

- Exists in many formulations: IV, transdermal, sublingual, spray, tablet
- Requires hepatic reductase
### 2.5.1. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* Similar to sodium nitroprusside: metabolism to nitric oxide, which activates guanylyl cyclase, leading to increased cGMP, decreased intracellular calcium, and vascular smooth muscle relaxation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* Glyceryl trinitrate (GTN) vasodilates veins by the production of nitric oxide [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* This activates the enzyme guanylate cyclase leading to increased levels of intracellular cyclic GMP [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Although Ca²⁺ influx into vascular smooth muscle is inhibited, its uptake into smooth endoplasmic reticulum is enhanced so that cytoplasmic levels fall, resulting in vasodilation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

### 2.5.2. CARDIOVASCULAR EFFECTS  

* **Hemodynamic effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Predominantly causes venodilation over arterial dilation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * At lower doses:
	* Causes venodilation without significant decrease in systemic vascular resistance [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Reduces preload, LVEDT / Wall tension / O2 demand, arterial pressure, and cardiac output [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Reduces pulmonary arterial pressures and vascular resistance [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* However, it also causes coronary vasodilation increasing myocardial oxygenation and increases contractility (moves curve up and left).
  * At higher doses:
	* Causes arterial dilation, reducing LV afterload [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Produces more pronounced decrease in arterial pressure with greater reflex tachycardia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* ⚠️ Overshoot hypotension and tachycardia may occur in hypovolemia, poorly controlled essential hypertension, and pregnancy-induced hypertension [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * In contrast to SNP and despite a similar mechanism of action, GTN produces vasodilation predominantly in the capacitance vessels (veins), although arteries are dilated to some extent [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Myocardial effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]], [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Improves myocardial oxygen supply-demand balance through several mechanisms [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
	* Pooling of blood in large-capacitance vessels reduces effective circulating blood volume and preload [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Decreased ventricular end-diastolic pressure reduces myocardial oxygen demand and increases endocardial perfusion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Afterload reduction from arteriolar dilation decreases end-systolic pressure and oxygen demand [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Direct coronary vasodilation redistributes coronary blood flow to ischemic areas of the subendocardium [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* May relieve coronary artery spasm [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Dilates arterial conduits (e.g., internal mammary artery and radial artery used in CABG) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Produces a reduction in preload, venous return, ventricular end-diastolic pressure and wall tension. This in turn leads to a reduction in oxygen demand and increased coronary blood flow to subendocardial regions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * ⚠️ Fall in diastolic pressure may lower coronary perfusion pressure and actually decrease myocardial oxygen supply [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Use with caution in hypovolemic patients to avoid severe hypotension compromising coronary perfusion pressure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Other effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Similar CNS effects as sodium nitroprusside; also causes headache from raised intracranial pressure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]], [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Relaxes bronchial smooth muscles [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Relaxes sphincter of Oddi as well as esophagus [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .
  * An increase in intracranial pressure and headache resulting from cerebral vasodilation may occur but is often only problematic at the start of treatment [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

### 2.5.3. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS

* **Primary uses** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Relieves myocardial ischemia, hypertension, and ventricular failure [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Treatment of angina [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Used after cardiac surgery [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * For spastic disorders of the esophagus [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * 💡 Obstetrical applications: 50–100 mcg boluses can be an effective (though transient) uterine relaxant for certain procedures when the placenta is still present (e.g., retained placenta, uterine inversion, uterine tetany, breech extraction, external version of the second twin) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Used in the treatment and prophylaxis of angina, in left ventricular failure associated with myocardial infarction and following cardiac surgery. It has also been used in the control of intra-operative blood pressure and for oesophageal spasm [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .

### 2.5.4. DOSING AND ADMINISTRATION 

* **Routes of administration** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Intravenous: Diluted to a concentration of 100 mcg/mL and administered as a continuous infusion (0.5–5 mcg/kg/min) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Sublingual: Spray 400μg or 300μg sublingual tablets, peak effect in 4 minutes [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Transdermal: Sustained release for 24 hours [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * 💡 Requires specific giving sets as GTN absorbs into PVC [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * ⚠️ Remove transdermal patches before DC cardioversion as these can explode if heated [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Formulations** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * Aerosol spray delivering 400 μg per metered dose and tablets containing 300–600 μg, both used sublingually, as required [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .
  * Modified-release tablets containing 1–5 mg for buccal administration (placed between the upper lip and gum), used at a maximum dose of 5 mg tds [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .
  * Modified-release tablets (2.6–10 mg) to be swallowed, used at a maximum dose of 12.8 mg tds [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .
  * The transdermal patch preparation releases 5–15 mg/24 hours, and should be resited at a different location on the chest [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .
  * Clear colorless solution for injection contains 1–5 mg/ml and should be diluted to a 0.01% (100 μg/ml) solution before administration by an infusion pump, used at 10–200 μg/min [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .

### 2.5.5. METABOLISM AND COMPARATIVE EFFECTS 

* **Metabolism** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Undergoes rapid reductive hydrolysis in the liver and blood by glutathione-organic nitrate reductase [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * One metabolic product is nitrite, which can convert hemoglobin to methemoglobin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Significant methemoglobinemia is rare [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Rapidly absorbed from sublingual mucosa and the GI tract, although the latter is subject to extensive first-pass hepatic metabolism resulting in an oral bioavailability of less than 5% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Sublingual effects are seen within 3 minutes and last for 30–60 minutes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Hepatic nitrate reductase is responsible for the metabolism of GTN to glycerol dinitrate and nitrite (NO₂⁻) in a process that requires tissue thiols (R-SH) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Nitrite is then converted to NO, which confers its mechanism of action [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Under certain conditions nitrite may convert oxyhemoglobin to methemoglobin by oxidation of the ferrous ion (Fe²⁺) to the ferric ion (Fe³⁺) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Tolerance** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]], [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Can develop tolerance rapidly (within two days), requiring a drug-free period each day [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Tolerance develops within 48 hours and may be due to depletion of sulfhydryl groups within vascular smooth muscle [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .
  * A daily drug-free period of a few hours prevents tolerance [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .
  * It has been suggested that infusion of acetylcysteine (providing sulfhydryl groups) may prevent tolerance [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .
* **Comparative effects** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Heart rate is unchanged or minimally increased compared to other vasodilators [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Rebound hypertension is less likely after discontinuation of nitroglycerin than following discontinuation of sodium nitroprusside [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

## 2.6. ISOSORBIDE PREPARATIONS

* **Isosorbide dinitrate (ISDN)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Prepared with lactose and mannitol to reduce the risk of explosion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Well absorbed from the gut and is subject to extensive first-pass metabolism in the liver to isosorbide 2-mononitrate and isosorbide 5-mononitrate (ISMN), both of which probably confer the majority of the activity of ISDN [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Isosorbide mononitrate (ISMN)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Has a much longer half-life (4.5 hours) compared to ISDN [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Not subject to hepatic first-pass metabolism and has an oral bioavailability of 100% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Both ISDN and ISMN are used in the prophylaxis of angina [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

## 2.7. POTASSIUM CHANNEL ACTIVATORS (NICORANDIL) 









**Potassium channel opener with a nitrate moiety, used for angina and heart failure.**

### 2.7.1. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* Nicorandil (nicotinamidoethyl nitrate) is a potassium channel activator with a nitrate moiety [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* ATP-sensitive K⁺ channels are closed during the normal cardiac cycle but are open (activated) during periods of ischemia when intracellular levels of ATP fall [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* In the open state, K⁺ passes down its concentration gradient out of the cell, resulting in hyperpolarization which closes Ca²⁺ channels, resulting in less Ca²⁺ for myocardial contraction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Nicorandil activates the ATP-sensitive K⁺ channels within the heart and arterioles [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Additionally, nicorandil relaxes venous capacitance smooth muscle by stimulating guanylate cyclase via its nitrate moiety, leading to increased intracellular cGMP [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

### 2.7.2. CLINICAL EFFECTS 

* **Cardiovascular effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Causes venodilation and arterial vasodilation resulting in reduced preand afterload [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Blood pressure falls [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Left ventricular end-diastolic pressure falls and there is improved normal and collateral coronary artery blood flow, partly induced by coronary artery vasodilation without a 'steal' phenomenon [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Increased cardiac output in patients with ischemic heart disease and cardiac failure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Effective at suppressing torsades de points associated with a prolonged QT interval [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * High concentrations in vitro result in a shortened action potential by accelerated repolarization [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * May reduce the size of experimentally induced ischemia through an as yet undefined cardioprotective mechanism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Unlike nitrate therapy, it is not associated with tolerance during prolonged administration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Contractility and atrioventricular conduction are not affected [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Other effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Central nervous system: Headaches, which usually clear with continued therapy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Metabolic: Unlike other antihypertensives, nicorandil has no effect on lipid profile or glucose control [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Hematological: Inhibits in vitro ADP-induced platelet aggregation (similar to nitrates), which is associated with an increase of intraplatelet cGMP [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * ⚠️ Miscellaneous: Giant oral apthous ulcers have been reported with its use [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

### 2.7.3. DOSING AND PHARMACOKINETICS 

* **Presentation and dosing** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Available as tablets; usual dose is 10–30 mg bid [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Used for treatment and prophylaxis of angina, treatment of congestive heart failure and hypertension [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Has been used experimentally via the intravenous route [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Pharmacokinetics** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Well absorbed from the gut with insignificant first-pass metabolism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Main metabolic route is denitration with 20% excreted as metabolites in the urine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Elimination half-life is 1 hour, although its actions last up to 12 hours, but neither is increased in the presence of renal impairment [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Not plasma protein-bound to any significant extent [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

**Bottom Line:** Nitroglycerin is primarily a venodilator that reduces preload and improves myocardial oxygen balance, making it particularly useful for ischemic heart disease. Unlike nitroprusside, it has less risk of rebound hypertension and no risk of cyanide toxicity. Nicorandil combines potassium channel activation with nitrate effects for coronary vasodilation and reduction of preload.

# 3. HYDRALAZINE 












**Direct-acting arterial vasodilator used primarily for hypertension management.**

## 3.1. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* **Direct vasodilator** that reduces intracellular Ca²⁺ concentration in vascular smooth muscles [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* Acts partly by activating KATP channels in small arteries and arterioles in coronary, cerebral, splanchnic, and renal vascular beds [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* Interferes with calcium transport causing arteriolar smooth muscle relaxation, likely via cyclic GMP [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* The exact mechanism of action is uncertain but involves the activation of guanylate cyclase and an increase in intracellular cGMP. This leads to a decrease in available intracellular Ca²⁺ and vasodilation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

## 3.2. CLINICAL EFFECTS  

* **Cardiovascular effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Causes decreased peripheral vascular resistance, lowering arterial BP [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Triggers reflex compensatory responses, increasing heart rate, myocardial contractility, and cardiac output [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * ⚠️ These reflex responses can be detrimental in patients with CAD and are minimized by concurrent administration of a β-adrenergic antagonist (use with caution due to hypotensive effect) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * LV preload is relatively preserved as it does not dilate venous capacitance vessels [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * The decline in afterload often proves beneficial to patients in congestive heart failure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Its main effect is to reduce arteriolar tone and systemic vascular resistance, whereas the capacitance vessels are less affected. As a result, postural hypotension is not usually a problem [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Reflex tachycardia and an increase in cardiac output ensue but may be effectively antagonized by β-blockade [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **CNS effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Potent cerebral vasodilator and inhibitor of cerebral blood flow autoregulation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Unless BP is markedly reduced, cerebral blood flow and ICP will rise [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Cerebral artery blood flow increases as a result of cerebral artery vasodilation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Renal effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Renal blood flow is usually maintained or increased [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Despite an increase in renal blood flow, fluid retention, edema, and a reduction in urine output is often seen. This may be overcome by concurrent administration of a diuretic [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Other effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Gut: Nausea and vomiting are common [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Miscellaneous: Peripheral neuropathy and blood dyscrasias. A lupus erythematosus-type syndrome is occasionally seen after long-term use and may be more common in slow acetylators and women. It may require long-term corticosteroid therapy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

## 3.3. PHARMACOKINETICS 

* **Formulation and dosing** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * White powder with 20mg hydralazine to be mixed with 1ml water then diluted in saline to make 20ml (1mg/ml final concentration) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Standard dose: 5-20mg IV [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Onset and duration** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Onset within 15 minutes [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Duration of action 2-4 hours [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * 💡 Delayed onset and prolonged duration makes this much less attractive for perioperative use compared to faster-acting agents [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Metabolism** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Undergoes acetylation and hydroxylation in the liver [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Available as 25–50 mg tablets and as a powder containing 20 mg for reconstitution in water before intravenous administration (5% dextrose should be avoided as it promotes its rapid breakdown) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Well absorbed from the gut but is subject to a variable first-pass metabolism resulting in an oral bioavailability of 25–55% depending on the acetylator status of the individual [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Plasma half-life is normally 2–3 hours but may be shortened to 45 minutes in rapid acetylators [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * 90% protein-bound in the plasma [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Up to 85% is excreted in the urine as acetylated and hydroxylated metabolites, some of which are conjugated with glucuronic acid [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Crosses the placenta and may cause a fetal tachycardia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

## 3.4. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS

* Primary indication: Control of pregnancy-induced hypertension (also can use nifedipine, methyldopa, labetalol). [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* May trigger lupus in susceptible individuals [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* Used orally in the control of chronic hypertension and severe chronic heart failure in conjunction with other agents [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .
* Used intravenously in acute hypertension associated with pre-eclampsia at 10–20 mg. This may take up to 20 minutes to work and repeat doses may be required [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .

**Bottom Line:** Hydralazine is a direct arterial vasodilator with minimal effect on venous capacitance, making it useful for pregnancy-induced hypertension. Its relatively slow onset and long duration of action limit its utility for acute perioperative blood pressure management.

# 4. MINOXIDIL 












**Potent arterial vasodilator used for severe hypertension and hair regrowth.**

## 4.1. MECHANISM OF ACTION AND CLINICAL EFFECTS

* **Formulations** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Prepared as 2.5–10 mg tablets, a 2% solution for intravenous use, and a 5% lotion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Used for severe hypertension and alopecia areata [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Mechanism and effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Mechanism of action and principal effects essentially the same as those of hydralazine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] . Opens K+ ATPase channels on smooth muscle membrane.
  * Mechanism by which it stimulates the hair follicle is poorly understood [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * May precipitate LVH, pericardial / pleural effusion, hypertrichosis of the face and arms and breast tenderness [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Does not cause a lupus erythematosus-type reaction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

## 4.2. PHARMACOKINETICS

* **Absorption and distribution** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Well absorbed from the gut and has an oral bioavailability of 90% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Not protein-bound in the plasma [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Metabolism and excretion** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Plasma half-life is only 3 hours but hypotensive effects may last for as long as 3 days [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Undergoes hepatic glucuronide conjugation and is subsequently excreted in the urine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

**Bottom Line:** Minoxidil is a potent vasodilator used for severe hypertension and topically for hair regrowth. Unlike hydralazine, it does not cause lupus-like syndrome but may cause significant hypertrichosis when used systemically.

# 5. DIAZOXIDE 












**Thiazide-related vasodilator used for hypertensive emergencies and hypoglycemia.**

## 5.1. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* This vasodilator is chemically related to the thiazide diuretics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Hypotensive effects are mediated through altered levels of cAMP in arterioles, producing vasodilation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* May also be due to a reduced Ca²⁺ influx [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Biochemical effects are due to inhibition of insulin secretion and increased release of catecholamines [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

## 5.2. CLINICAL EFFECTS 

* **Cardiovascular effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Produces arteriolar vasodilation with little effect on capacitance vessels [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Blood pressure falls with an increase in heart rate and cardiac output [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Postural hypotension is not a problem [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Metabolic effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Increases levels of glucose, catecholamines, renin, and aldosterone [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Causes fluid retention (despite chemical relation to thiazides), which may require treatment with a loop diuretic [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Uses** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Available as 50 mg tablets and as a solution for intravenous injection containing 15 mg/ml [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Used intravenously to treat hypertensive emergencies associated with renal disease at 1–3 mg/kg up to a maximum dose of 150 mg, which may be repeated after 15 minutes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Also used to treat intractable hypoglycemia and malignant islet-cell tumors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Adverse effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Nausea, especially at the start of treatment [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Extrapyramidal effects including oculogyric crisis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Thrombocytopenia and hyperuricemia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

## 5.3. PHARMACOKINETICS

* **Absorption and distribution** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Well absorbed from the gut with an oral bioavailability of 80% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Extensively protein-bound in the plasma (about 90%) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Duration and elimination** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Hyperglycemic effects last about 8 hours while hypotensive effects last about 5 hours [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Plasma half-life may last up to 36 hours [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Partly metabolized in the liver, excreted in the urine as unchanged drug and as inactive metabolites [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

**Bottom Line:** Diazoxide is a powerful arterial vasodilator related to thiazide diuretics that causes hyperglycemia, making it useful for treating both hypertensive emergencies and hypoglycemic conditions. However, it causes significant fluid retention and may require concurrent diuretic therapy.

# 6. FENOLDOPAM 












**Selective dopamine-1 receptor agonist used for hypertensive emergencies.**

## 6.1. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* **Rapid vasodilation by selectively activating D₁-dopamine receptors** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* The R-isomer is responsible for the racemic mixture's biological activity due to its much greater receptor affinity compared with the S-isomer [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

## 6.2. CLINICAL EFFECTS  

* **Cardiovascular effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Decreases systolic and diastolic BP [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Heart rate typically increases initially (reflex) but usually decreases over time unless at higher doses [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * 💡 Low initial doses (0.03–0.1 mcg/kg/min) titrated slowly have been associated with less reflex tachycardia than higher doses [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Renal effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Markedly increases kidney blood flow [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Ocular effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * ⚠️ Can lead to rises in intraocular pressure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Should be administered with caution or avoided in patients with a history of glaucoma or intraocular hypertension [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Clinical utility** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Reduces systolic and diastolic blood pressure in patients with malignant hypertension to an extent comparable to nitroprusside [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

## 6.3. PHARMACOKINETICS AND METABOLISM

* **Onset and duration** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Onset within 15 minutes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Discontinuation of an infusion quickly reverses effects without rebound hypertension [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Some degree of tolerance may develop 48 hours after infusion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Metabolism** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Undergoes conjugation without participation of the cytochrome P-450 enzymes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Metabolites are inactive [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * 💡 No dose adjustments needed in kidney or hepatic failure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Adverse effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Headache, flushing, nausea, tachycardia, hypokalemia, and hypotension [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Fenoldopam is a selective D₁-dopamine agonist that provides rapid, titratable blood pressure control without rebound hypertension upon discontinuation. Its renal-protective properties make it particularly useful in patients at risk for kidney injury, though its effects on intraocular pressure require caution in glaucoma patients.

# 7. CALCIUM CHANNEL BLOCKERS 












![image.png](HYPOTENSIVE_HYPOTENSIVE_image.png_e23b3c.png_0997e3.png)

## 7.1. TYPES AND STRUCTURE

* **Calcium channel structure and function** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Structure: Asymmetric biochemical pores with at least 4 subunits (alpha1, alpha2/delta, and beta with or without gamma) that traverse many biologic membranes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Character: Closed under quiescent conditions, open in a voltage-dependent or receptor-operated manner allowing Ca²⁺ entry into cell or organelle (e.g., mitochondria, sarcoplasmic reticulum) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Two types of voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels defined based on duration of opening: T (transient) and L (long) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * L-type channels are the predominant target of calcium channel blockers [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Despite their disparate chemical structures, Ca²⁺ channel antagonists specifically block the entry of Ca²⁺ through L-type channels while leaving T-, Nand P-type Ca²⁺ channels unaffected [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * The L-type channel is widespread in the cardiovascular system and is responsible for the plateau phase (slow inward current) of the cardiac action potential. It triggers the internal release of Ca²⁺ and is regulated by cAMP-dependent protein kinase [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * The T-type channel is structurally similar to the L-type channel and is present mainly in cardiac cells that lack a T-tubule system (e.g., sino-atrial node) and certain types of vascular smooth muscle. They are not present in ventricular myocardium [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * N-type channels are only found in nerve cells [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Four chemical groups of calcium channel blockers** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * 1,4-dihydropyridines (e.g., nifedipine, nicardipine, nimodipine, clevidipine) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Benzothiazepines (diltiazem) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Phenylalkylamines (verapamil) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Diarylaminopropylamine ethers (bepridil) - withdrawn from use [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Chemical classification of calcium channel antagonists** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Class I: Phenylalkylamines (verapamil) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Class II: Dihydropyridines (nifedipine, amlodipine, nimodipine) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Class III: Benzothiazepines (diltiazem) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

## 7.2. GENERAL MECHANISM OF ACTION 

* **Effects depend on drug's selectivity for myocardial and vascular smooth muscle L-type Ca²⁺ channels** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Common hemodynamic effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Generally cause greater arterial vasodilation than venodilation (LV afterload reduced relative to preload) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * L-type receptors are more prevalent on arterial than venous capacitance vessels [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Consequently, cardiac filling and preload are less affected by CCBs than by nitrates [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Cause coronary vasodilation and inhibit coronary artery vasospasm, increasing coronary blood flow provided coronary perfusion pressure is not substantially reduced from concomitant arterial vasodilation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	  * Dihydrodipyridine > Verapamil
* **Tissue selectivity differences** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Dihydropyridines exert pronounced effects at the less negative resting membrane potentials typically observed in vascular smooth muscle cells, but demonstrate lower potency in cardiac myocytes with more negative resting membrane potentials [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * This makes dihydropyridine CCBs highly selective for arterial vascular smooth muscle and nearly devoid of negative chronotropic or inotropic effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Non-dihydropyridines (diltiazem and verapamil) also reduce heart rate and contractility, reducing myocardial O₂ demand [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Dihydropyridines may cause baroreceptor-induced tachycardia, increasing myocardial O₂ demand (potentially detrimental to patients with CAD) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Calcium channel antagonists have variable affinity for L-type channels in myocardium, nodal and vascular smooth muscle resulting in variable effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * They are all useful in the treatment of essential hypertension, although some are also particularly useful in the treatment of angina or arrhythmias [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

## 7.3. DIHYDROPYRIDINE CCBS 









**Arterial-selective vasodilators with minimal effects on cardiac conduction and contractility.**

### 7.3.1. GENERAL PROPERTIES

* Examples include nifedipine, nicardipine, clevidipine, and amlodipine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Mechanism of action**: Bind to L-type calcium channels and impair calcium entry into vascular smooth muscle [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* With preload maintained, cardiac output often increases when vascular tone is reduced by dihydropyridine calcium blockers [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* May cause baroreceptor-induced tachycardia, unlike verapamil and diltiazem [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

### 7.3.2. NIFEDIPINE  

* **Cardiovascular effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Direct epicardial coronary vasodilator, indicated in variant angina (more effective than nitrates as CCBs cause more profound, consistent coronary vasodilation) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Also used in Raynaud's phenomenon and vasospasm from unstable angina [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * ⚠️ Does not improve and may worsen mortality when used in patients with acute myocardial infarction, in contrast to diltiazem and verapamil [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Reduces tone in peripheral and coronary arteries, resulting in reduced systemic vascular resistance, fall in blood pressure, increased coronary artery blood flow and reflex increases in heart rate and contractility [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Cardiac output is increased [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Occasionally these reflex changes worsen the oxygen supply/demand ratio [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Presentation and uses** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Available as capsules containing 5–10 mg, the contents of which may be administered sublingually, and tablets containing 10–60 mg, some of which are available as sustained release [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * The onset of action is 15–20 minutes following oral and 5–10 minutes following sublingual administration. Swallowing the contents of an opened capsule may further reduce the onset time [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Used in the prophylaxis and treatment of angina, hypertension and in Raynaud's syndrome [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Pharmacokinetics** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Well absorbed following oral administration although hepatic first-pass metabolism reduces its oral bioavailability to 60% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * 95% plasma protein-bound and has an elimination half-life of 5 hours following oral administration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Predominantly excreted as inactive metabolites in the urine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

### 7.3.3. CLEVIDIPINE  

* **Pharmacological properties** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Ultrashort-acting with plasma t₁/₂ of ~2 minutes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Metabolized by tissue and plasma esterases (no need for hepatic or renal adjustment) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Clinical applications** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Compares favorably with nitroglycerin, sodium nitroprusside, and nicardipine for management of acute hypertension in cardiac surgery patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Prepared as a lipid emulsion with a short half-life secondary to rapid metabolism by blood esterases, facilitating rapid titration [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * ⚠️ Contraindicated in patients with soy or egg allergies and those with impaired lipid metabolism [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Dosing** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Infusion starting at 1 to 2 mg/h, with the dose doubled until the desired effect is obtained, up to 16 mg/h [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Advantages** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Unlike other short-acting antihypertensives, not associated with tachyphylaxis and rebound hypertension [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

### 7.3.4. NICARDIPINE  

* **Pharmacology and effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Similar cardiovascular effects to nifedipine but longer half-life [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Clinical uses** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Potent coronary vasodilator to dilate arterial conduits during CABG [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * For sustained perioperative hypertension (less suitable for acute management due to long half-life) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Dosing** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Infusion titrated to effect (5–15 mg/h) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

### 7.3.5. NIMODIPINE  

* **Pharmacology** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * More lipophilic relative to other dihydropyridines, crosses blood-brain barrier more easily resulting in greater cerebral arterial vasodilation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Clinical applications** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Treatment of cerebral vasospasm after aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Mechanism of benefit unclear as nimodipine does not reverse angiographic evidence of vasospasm [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Most likely reduces cerebral arteriolar resistance and enhances blood flow through pia mater collateral vessels rather than dilating large cerebral arteries [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Attenuates Ca²⁺-mediated neurotoxicity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * More lipid-soluble analogue of nifedipine that can penetrate the blood-brain barrier [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Used in the prevention and treatment of cerebral vasospasm following subarachnoid hemorrhage and in migraine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * May be administered orally or intravenously [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Action may depend on blocking a Ca²⁺-dependent cascade of cellular processes that would otherwise lead to cell damage and destruction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

## 7.4. BENZOTHIAZEPINE CCBS (DILTIAZEM) 









**Calcium channel blocker with intermediate effects on cardiac conduction and vascular tone.**

### 7.4.1. CARDIOVASCULAR EFFECTS 

* **Differences from dihydropyridine CCBs** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Potent negative chronotropic and dromotropic effects on SA node automaticity and AV node conduction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * IV administration causes arterial vasodilation and decreases arterial pressure, stimulating baroreceptor-mediated tachycardia and increasing cardiac output initially, but heart rate eventually falls [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Oral administration reduces heart rate, arterial pressure, and myocardial O₂ demand [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Has actions both within the heart and in the peripheral circulation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Prolongs AV conduction time and reduces contractility but to a lesser extent than verapamil [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Reduces systemic vascular resistance and blood pressure falls, although a reflex tachycardia is not usually seen [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Coronary blood flow is increased due to coronary artery vasodilation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

### 7.4.2. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS

* **Treatment of hypertension and symptomatic CAD** where beta-blockers are contraindicated [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Ventricular rate control** in chronic atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, or supraventricular tachycardia (adenosine and/or cardioversion is still first-line) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* Available as 60–200 mg tablets, some of which are available as slow release. Also available in combination with hydrochlorothiazide [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .
* Used for the prophylaxis and treatment of angina and in hypertension [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .
* **Pharmacokinetics** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Almost completely absorbed from the gut but hepatic first-pass metabolism reduces the oral bioavailability to 50% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Hepatic metabolism produces an active metabolite, desacetyldiltiazem, which is excreted in the urine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * The urine also eliminates 40% of diltiazem in the unchanged form [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Approximately 75% is plasma protein-bound [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

## 7.5. PHENYLALKYLAMINE CCBS (VERAPAMIL) 









**Calcium channel blocker with pronounced effects on cardiac conduction system and moderate vasodilation.**

### 7.5.1. CARDIOVASCULAR EFFECTS 

* **Hemodynamic profile** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Less arterial vasodilation but more potent effects on automaticity, conduction, and myocardial contractility than dihydropyridines or diltiazem [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Causes minimal or no baroreceptor-mediated tachycardia from arterial vasodilation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * ⚠️ Has potential to markedly worsen preexisting LV systolic dysfunction in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (myocardial depressant) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Acts as a coronary vasodilator and decreases myocardial O₂ demand [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Is a racemic mixture and a synthetic derivative of papaverine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * The L-isomer has specific Ca²⁺ channel blocking actions with a particular affinity for those at the SA node and AV node, whereas the D-isomer acts on fast Na⁺ channels resulting in some local anesthetic activity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Acts specifically to slow the conduction of the action potential at the SA and AV node resulting in a reduced heart rate [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * To a lesser extent it produces negative inotropic effects and vasodilates peripheral vascular smooth muscle [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Is a mild coronary artery vasodilator [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Blood pressure falls [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * May lead to various degrees of heart block or cardiac failure in those with impaired ventricular function and ventricular fibrillation in those with WPW syndrome [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Vasodilates the cerebral circulation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Following chronic administration, it may potentiate the effects of non-depolarizing and depolarizing muscle relaxants [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

### 7.5.2. ELECTROPHYSIOLOGICAL EFFECTS 

* **Actions on conduction system** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Reduces SA node discharge, decreases AV node conduction velocity, and increases refractory period of AV node (class IV antiarrhythmic) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Prolongs PR interval and increases AV conduction time [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

### 7.5.3. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS

* **Primary uses** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Treatment of acute myocardial infarction in patients who cannot tolerate beta-blockers [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Alternative to adenosine in treatment of SVT except when aberrant conduction is present [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Treatment of atrial fibrillation or flutter with rapid ventricular response [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Available as 20–240 mg tablets (some prepared as modified release) and as an oral suspension containing 40 mg/5 ml [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .
  * The colorless intravenous preparation contains 2.5 mg/ml [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .
  * Used for certain supraventricular arrhythmias (not in atrial fibrillation complicating Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome) and angina [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .
  * Used to treat hypertension although its negative inotropic properties limit its usefulness in this setting [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .

### 7.5.4. CONTRAINDICATIONS 

* ⚠️ **Absolute contraindications** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * SVTs with aberrant conduction pathways (e.g., Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome - bundle of Kent) because blockade of AV conduction leaves direct transmission from atrium to ventricle through the aberrant pathway unopposed, risking malignant ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Sick sinus syndrome or AV node dysfunction [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* ⚠️ **Cautions** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Use with a beta-blocker may cause complete heart block or profound myocardial depression [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Pharmacokinetics** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Well absorbed from the gut but an extensive first-pass hepatic metabolism reduces the oral bioavailability to 20% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Demethylation produces norverapamil, which retains significant anti-arrhythmic properties [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * 90% plasma protein-bound and is mainly excreted in the urine following metabolism, although up to 20% is excreted in bile [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

**Bottom Line:** Calcium channel blockers vary in their selectivity for vascular versus cardiac tissue. Dihydropyridines primarily affect vascular smooth muscle with minimal cardiac effects, while verapamil has significant cardiac effects, making drug selection important based on the clinical situation and comorbidities.

# 8. RENIN-ANGIOTENSIN-ALDOSTERONE SYSTEM 












## 8.1. PHYSIOLOGICAL OVERVIEW

* **Components of the juxtaglomerular apparatus** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Juxtaglomerular cells form part of the afferent arteriole as it enters the glomerulus and are supplied by sympathetic nerves. They contain prorenin, which is converted to the acid protease renin before systemic release [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * The macula densa is a region of cells at the start of the distal convoluted tubule, which lie adjacent to the juxtaglomerular cells of the same nephron [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Agranular lacis cells, which lie between the afferent and efferent arterioles adjacent to the glomerulus [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Conditions that trigger renin release** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Reduced renal perfusion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Reduced Na⁺ at the macula densa [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Stimulation of the renal sympathetic fibers via β₁-adrenoceptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Cascade steps** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Renin (half-life 80 minutes) splits the decapeptide angiotensin I from the circulating plasma protein angiotensinogen, which is synthesized in the liver and is present in the α₂-globulin fraction of plasma proteins [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) converts angiotensin I to the active octapeptide angiotensin II, and also inactivates bradykinin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Angiotensin II is broken down in the kidney and liver to inactive metabolites and angiotensin III, which retains some activity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

## 8.2. ANGIOTENSIN II: MECHANISM AND EFFECTS

* **Receptor types** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Two subtypes of angiotensin II receptor exist, AT₁ and AT₂ [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Angiotensin has a greater affinity for AT₁ receptors, which are G-protein-coupled [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Physiological effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Potent vasoconstriction (about five times as potent as noradrenaline), acting directly on arterioles and indirectly via central mechanisms [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Blockade of noradrenaline re-uptake (uptake 1) at sympathetic nerves and sympathetic nervous system activation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Central effects – increases thirst and the release of ADH and ACTH [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Stimulates the release of aldosterone from the adrenal cortex and inhibits the release of renin from the juxtaglomerular cells [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Reduced glomerular filtration rate [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

**Bottom Line:** The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system is a pivotal regulator of cardiovascular and renal function. Renin released from the juxtaglomerular apparatus converts angiotensinogen to angiotensin I, which is then converted to the potent vasoconstrictor angiotensin II by ACE. Understanding this system is essential for comprehending the mechanism of action of ACE inhibitors and ARBs.

## 9.1. RENIN-ANGIOTENSIN-ALDOSTERONE SYSTEM 

* **Physiological overview** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) is a major regulator of cardiovascular homeostasis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Renal cortical juxtaglomerular cells (JGCs) secrete renin in response to three stimuli [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
	* Decreases in sodium reabsorption by macula densa [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Reduced perfusion pressure to preglomerular arterioles [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Sympathetic nervous system activation through β₁-adrenergic receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **RAAS cascade** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Renin cleaves angiotensinogen into the 10 amino acid peptide angiotensin I [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE) is synthesized in pulmonary vascular endothelium and cleaves the C-terminal histidine and leucine residues from angiotensin I to form the biologically active octapeptide angiotensin II (AG2) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * AG2 is a potent vasoconstrictor of renal and mesenteric arterioles via angiotensin subtype 1 (AT1) receptor-mediated action through Gq protein-phospholipase C-inositol triphosphate Ca²⁺ signaling [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * AG2 effects include [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
	* Release of norepinephrine and epinephrine from adrenal medulla [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Facilitation of norepinephrine release from sympathetic postganglionic neurons and augmentation of its actions in vascular smooth muscle [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Stimulation of the synthesis and release of aldosterone from the zona glomerulosa of the adrenal cortex [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Aldosterone augments AG2 effects on renal tubular sodium retention and potassium excretion, reducing sodium and water excretion and enhancing potassium excretion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * The net result is elevated arterial pressure and increased intravascular volume [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

## 9.2. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* **ACE inhibitors block conversion of angiotensin I into angiotensin II** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* There are currently 11 ACE inhibitors in clinical use in the US, differing in [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:

* **Common ACE inhibitors** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:

  * Enalapril - has an IV form (enalaprilat) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Lisinopril - oral formulation with prolonged half-life that does not require multiple daily dosing [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

  * **Group 3**: Lisinopril – an active drug that is not metabolized and is excreted unchanged in the urine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

## 9.3. CLINICAL EFFECTS  

* **Cardiovascular effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Reduces arterial vasomotor tone in patients with essential or renal vascular hypertension but not in those with primary hyperaldosteronism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Reduces LV afterload, improves arterial compliance, reduces arterial pressure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Cardiac output is unchanged or shows modest increase while LV preload is unaffected [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Despite decrease in pressure, sympathetic nervous system tone does not change substantially [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Baroreceptor reflexes remain intact - no orthostatic hypotension or limited exercise capacity unless hypovolemia is present (due to concomitant diuretic therapy or another arterial vasodilator) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Reduces systemic vascular resistance significantly, resulting in a fall in blood pressure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * The fall in afterload may increase the cardiac output, particularly in those with heart failure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Heart rate is usually unaffected but may increase [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Baroreceptor reflexes are also unaffected [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Transient hypotension may occur at the start of treatment, which may be started under specialist supervision [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Renal effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Increase renal blood flow and facilitate natriuresis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * The normal function of angiotensin II to maintain efferent arteriolar pressure (by vasoconstriction) at the glomerulus in the presence of poor renal perfusion is forfeit [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Therefore, renal perfusion pressure falls and renal failure may follow. As a result, bilateral renal artery stenosis or unilateral renal artery stenosis to a single functioning kidney is considered a contraindication [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Where normal renal perfusion is preserved, renal vasodilation may occur leading to a natriuresis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Clinical evidence** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Strong evidence for treatment of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) - halt or delay progression and improve quality of life, reduce hospitalization, decrease incidence of AMI, and reduce risk of sudden cardiac death [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Strong evidence for treatment of coronary artery disease in patients with diabetes and hypertension (also has renoprotective effect) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Used in all grades of heart failure and in patients with myocardial infarction with left ventricular dysfunction where it improves the prognosis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .
  * Used in hypertension, especially in insulin-dependent diabetics with nephropathy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .
  * Hypertension is relatively resistant to ACE inhibition in the black population where concurrent diuretic therapy may be required [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .

### 9.5.1. PERIOPERATIVE ACE-I/ARB MANAGEMENT STRATEGIES [EVIDENCE-BASED PRACTICE]

**Background and Clinical Significance:**
The perioperative management of ACE inhibitors and ARBs remains one of the most controversial topics in anesthesia practice, with conflicting recommendations from major guidelines and ongoing debate about optimal strategies. This controversy stems from the complex balance between cardiovascular protection benefits and the risk of severe intraoperative hypotension.

**Current Guideline Recommendations:**

**2014 ACC/AHA Guidelines:** 
* **Continuation recommended** - ACE-I/ARB therapy is reasonable to continue (Class IIa, Level of evidence B)
* **If withheld** - Resume postoperatively as soon as clinically feasible (Level of evidence C)

**2017 CCS Guidelines:**
* **Withhold recommended** - ACE-I/ARB treatment should be withheld for 24 hours before noncardiac surgery in patients treated chronically (Strong recommendation; low-quality evidence)

**2014 ESC/ESA Guidelines:**
* **Heart failure patients** - Continue ACE-I/ARB in patients with heart failure and left ventricular systolic dysfunction (Class IIa, level C)
* **Hypertensive patients** - Transient discontinuation of therapy for hypertensive patients (Class IIa, level C)
* **Consider initiation** - For cardiac-stable patients with heart failure and LV systolic dysfunction within week of surgery (Class IIa, level C)

**Evidence Summary:**

**The VISION Study (14,687 patients):**
* **Primary finding:** Patients who had ACE-I/ARB therapy withheld 24 hours before surgery showed:
  * 20% relative risk reduction in intraoperative hypotension (ARR 0.80; 95% CI, 0.72–0.93; P < 0.001)
  * 18% relative risk reduction in composite outcome of death, stroke, or myocardial injury (ARR 0.82; 95% CI, 0.70–0.96; P = 0.01)
* **Limitation:** Crude definition of hypotension (SBP below 90 mm Hg for any duration)

**Meta-Analysis Findings (Hollmann et al., 2018):**
* **Intraoperative hypotension:** 30% relative risk increase if ACE-Is/ARBs continued on morning of surgery
* **MACE correlation:** No association between hypotension sustained and major adverse cardiac events or mortality could be proven
* **Study limitations:** High between-study heterogeneity, differing hypotensive threshold definitions

**Randomized Controlled Trial Evidence:**
Only six RCTs have directly assessed ACE-I/ARB continuation versus withholding, all with methodological limitations:
* **Sample sizes:** Generally small (studies ranged from 50-200 patients)
* **Definition variability:** Different hypotensive thresholds (SBP < 80-90 mm Hg)
* **Heterogeneity:** Lack of protocolized anesthesia regimens
* **Mixed results:** No consistent findings regarding major adverse cardiovascular events

**The PREOP-ACEI Trial (Latest RCT):**
* **Design:** Largest published RCT comparing ACE-I withdrawal versus continuation
* **Findings:**
  * **Reduced hypotension:** ACE-I withdrawal associated with less intraoperative hypotension (55% vs 69%; RR: 0.81, 95% CI: 0.67–0.97, P = 0.03)
  * **Increased rebound hypertension:** Significantly increased risk for postoperative hypertension (RR: 1.95, 95% CI: 1.14–3.34, P = 0.01)
  * **No difference in outcomes:** No increased incidence in postoperative acute renal failure or MACE

**Clinical Decision-Making Framework:**

**Consider Withholding ACE-I/ARB if:**
* **Anticipated hypotensive events:** Expected intraoperative situations with increased reliance on intact RAS
  * Non-supine positioning (beach chair, prone, anti-Trendelenburg)
  * Major blood loss or fluid shifts anticipated
  * Large vessel cross-clamping procedures
  * Neuraxial anesthesia planned
* **Polypharmacy concerns:** Concurrent use of multiple antihypertensive agents
  * Especially concomitant diuretic or ARB therapy
  * Combined single drugs (ACE-I plus diuretic/calcium channel blocker)
* **Altered feedback systems:** Patients with compromised neurohormonal responses
  * Elderly patients
  * Diabetic autonomic neuropathy
  * Spinal cord injury

**Consider Continuing ACE-I/ARB if:**
* **Cardiac morbidity history:** Strong cardiovascular indications
  * Left ventricular systolic dysfunction
  * Previous myocardial infarction
  * High Revised Cardiac Risk Index (RCRI) score
* **Low-risk procedures:** Minor surgical procedures with minimal expected hemodynamic changes
* **Uncontrolled hypertension:** Preoperative hypertension with target organ damage

**Drug-Specific Timing Considerations:**
When withholding therapy, consider drug-specific half-lives:
* **Longer half-life drugs:** (enalapril, lisinopril, losartan) - omit 24 hours before surgery
* **Shorter-acting agents:** (captopril) - omit night before (>10 hours)

**Intraoperative Management Strategies:**

**If Therapy Continued:**
* **Tailored induction:** Less reliance on vagotonic agents (large-dose propofol)
* **Volume expansion:** Careful intravascular volume optimization with fluids
* **Early vasopressor use:** Anticipate and treat post-induction hypotension promptly
* **Alternative agents:** Consider vasopressin or terlipressin for refractory hypotension

**If Therapy Withheld:**
* **Monitor for rebound:** Watch for rebound hypertension
* **Early resumption:** Resume therapy when clinically feasible postoperatively
* **Alternative management:** Consider short-acting alternatives if blood pressure control needed

**Special Considerations:**

**Acute Renal Failure Risk:**
* **Conflicting evidence:** Some studies show increased ARF with continuation, others show protective effects
* **Proposed mechanisms:** 
  * **Increased risk:** Drug-induced systemic hypotension, decreased efferent arteriolar constriction
  * **Protective effects:** Prevention of ANGII-induced renal vasoconstriction and inflammation
* **Recent evidence:** STARsurg collaborative study (949 patients) found no difference in 7-day postoperative ARF between withholding vs. continuing therapy

**Mortality Outcomes:**
* **Inconsistent findings:** Studies demonstrate either decreased or increased mortality with ACE-I/ARB therapy
* **High-risk populations:** Some data suggest increased mortality in certain vascular surgery populations
* **Confounding factors:** Baseline comorbidities and surgical complexity influence outcomes

**Anesthetic Type Influence:**
* **Neuraxial anesthesia:** Higher risk of significant hypotension due to sympathectomy combined with ACE-I/ARB effects
* **General anesthesia:** Risk varies with technique (TIVA vs. volatile-based)
* **Regional techniques:** Consider when possible to minimize systemic effects

**Practical Implementation:**

**Preoperative Assessment:**
* **Risk stratification:** Use framework to assess individual patient risk factors
* **Shared decision-making:** Involve patient, anesthesiologist, and surgeon in decision
* **Documentation:** Clear documentation of decision rationale and plan

**Institutional Protocols:**
* **Develop guidelines:** Create institution-specific protocols based on local practice patterns
* **Regular review:** Update protocols based on emerging evidence
* **Quality improvement:** Track outcomes based on different management strategies

**Patient Education:**
* **Explain rationale:** Help patients understand the reasoning for management decisions
* **Set expectations:** Discuss potential perioperative blood pressure changes
* **Compliance planning:** Ensure clear instructions for medication resumption

**Current Expert Recommendations:**

Given the conflicting evidence and guidelines, a nuanced, individualized approach is recommended:

1. **No universal approach:** One-size-fits-all recommendations may not be appropriate
2. **Risk-benefit analysis:** Consider individual patient factors, surgical factors, and institutional capabilities
3. **Clear communication:** Ensure all team members understand the management plan
4. **Preparedness:** Have appropriate monitoring and treatment strategies available regardless of approach chosen

* **Perioperative management** [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]:
  * Often held on the day of surgery to reduce risk of post-induction hypotension [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].
  * All antihypertensives should be held in shocked or hypotensive patients undergoing surgery [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].
  * While most drugs should be continued throughout the peri-operative period, ACE inhibitors and angiotensin II receptor antagonists are often omitted due to the increased frequency of peri-operative hypotension [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]] .

## 9.6. SPECIFIC ACE INHIBITORS 

### 9.6.1. RAMIPRIL 

* **Formulation and dosing** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Available as 1.25–10 mg tablets [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Initial dose is 1.25 mg which is then titrated up depending on its indication [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Mechanism of action** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Ramipril is converted by liver esterases to the active moiety ramiprilat, which is a competitive ACE inhibitor and therefore prevents the formation of angiotensin II and its effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Afterload is reduced to a greater degree than preload [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Pharmacokinetics** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Ramipril is a prodrug and is almost completely metabolized to its active moiety ramiprilat [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Rapidly absorbed from the gut and the oral bioavailability of ramiprilat is 50% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Half-life of approximately 15 hours at high doses but longer in low doses. This is related to the saturable capacity of ACE at higher concentrations [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Excretion is essentially renal and therefore impaired in renal failure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

### 9.6.2. ENALAPRIL 

* **Pharmacokinetics** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Enalapril is a prodrug, which is hydrolyzed in the liver and kidney to the active compound enalaprilat [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * May be given orally (as enalapril) or intravenously (as enalaprilat) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Elimination half-life is 4–8 hours but increases to 11 hours in prolonged therapy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Has a duration of action of approximately 20 hours [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

**Bottom Line:** ACE inhibitors block the conversion of angiotensin I to angiotensin II, reducing blood pressure through decreased peripheral vascular resistance. They offer significant benefits in heart failure and cardiovascular protection, but can cause troublesome cough, angioedema, and refractory hypotension during anesthesia, which may require vasopressin for treatment.

# 10. ANGIOTENSIN RECEPTOR BLOCKERS 












**Selective antagonists of angiotensin II AT₁ receptors used for hypertension management.**

## 10.1. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* **Potent antihypertensives** (e.g., losartan, candesartan, valsartan) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Mechanism**: High-affinity inhibition of angiotensin subtype 1 (AT1) receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* Unlike ACE inhibitors, ARBs do not affect angiotensin II-induced activation of angiotensin subtype 2 (AT2) receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* Clinical implications of these pharmacodynamic differences between ARBs and ACE inhibitors remain unclear [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

## 10.2. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS

* **Indications** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Similar indications to ACE inhibitors [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Often used as an alternative to ACE inhibitors due to better side effect profile [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Less likely to cause cough, dermatitis, or angioedema than ACE inhibitors [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Used in the treatment of hypertension where dry cough proves an unacceptable side effect of ACE inhibitor therapy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .

## 10.3. ADVERSE EFFECTS  

* **Safety concerns** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]], [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Teratogenic (similar to ACE inhibitors) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Hyperkalemia concerns similar to ACE inhibitors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Perioperative hypotension risk similar to ACE inhibitors [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Contraindicated in bilateral renal artery stenosis and pregnancy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .

### 10.3.1. PERIOPERATIVE HYPOTENSION CONSIDERATIONS [EVIDENCE-BASED]

**Mechanism of Perioperative Hypotension:**
ARBs share similar mechanisms of perioperative hypotension as ACE inhibitors, primarily through:
* **AT1 receptor blockade:** Complete blockade of angiotensin II effects at AT1 receptors
* **Vasodilation:** Direct arterial and venous vasodilation reducing preload and afterload
* **Renin-angiotensin system disruption:** Inability to mount appropriate vasoconstrictor response to hypotension
* **Interaction with anesthetics:** Enhanced hypotensive effects with volatile anesthetics and neuraxial anesthesia

**Comparative Risk with ACE Inhibitors:**
* **Similar hypotensive risk:** ARBs demonstrate comparable intraoperative hypotension risk to ACE inhibitors
* **Duration of action:** Many ARBs have longer half-lives than ACE inhibitors, potentially prolonging hypotensive effects
* **Refractory hypotension:** Similar reports of catecholamine-resistant hypotension requiring vasopressin or angiotensin II

**ARB-Specific Pharmacokinetic Considerations:**

**Half-Life Variations:**
* **Losartan:** 2 hours (but active metabolite 7 hours)
* **Valsartan:** 9 hours  
* **Irbesartan:** 11-15 hours
* **Candesartan:** 3.5-4 hours
* **Telmisartan:** 24 hours (longest acting)

**Clinical Implications:**
* **Longer-acting ARBs** (telmisartan, irbesartan) may require earlier discontinuation (24-48 hours) if withholding strategy chosen
* **Shorter-acting ARBs** (candesartan, losartan) may be withheld closer to surgery (12-24 hours)

**Management of ARB-Associated Hypotension:**

**Predictive Factors:**
* **Higher risk:** Concurrent diuretic use, dehydration, elderly patients, neuraxial anesthesia
* **Procedural factors:** Major surgery, significant fluid shifts, prolonged procedures
* **Patient factors:** Heart failure, renal impairment, autonomic dysfunction

**Treatment Strategies:**
* **First-line:** Fluid resuscitation and direct-acting vasopressors (phenylephrine, norepinephrine)
* **Refractory cases:** Vasopressin (0.5-2 units IV) or terlipressin for angiotensin II-resistant hypotension
* **Avoid:** High-dose indirect sympathomimetics (ephedrine) may be less effective due to AT1 receptor blockade

**Perioperative Decision-Making:**
The same decision-making framework applies to ARBs as ACE inhibitors, with considerations for:
* **Drug-specific timing:** Based on individual ARB half-life and duration of action
* **Combination therapy:** ARBs often combined with diuretics or calcium channel blockers, increasing hypotension risk
* **Patient-specific factors:** Cardiovascular indications, surgical risk, and institutional protocols

## 10.4. SPECIFIC ARBS (LOSARTAN) 

* **Preparation and pharmacology** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Losartan is a substituted imidazole compound [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * As its active metabolite is up to 40 times more potent at the angiotensin II receptor, it is effectively a prodrug although losartan does also have limited efficacy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Available as 25–50 mg tablets and in combination with hydrochlorothiazide [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Mechanism** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Specific angiotensin II receptor (type AT₁) antagonist at all sites within the body [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Blocks the negative feedback of angiotensin II on renin secretion, which therefore increases, leading to increased angiotensin II. This has little impact due to comprehensive AT₁ receptor blockade [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Effects and advantages** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Effects broadly similar to those of the ACE inhibitors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * As it does not block the actions of ACE, bradykinin may be broken down in the usual manner (by ACE). As a result, bradykinin levels are not raised and the dry cough seen with ACE inhibitors does not complicate its use [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Pharmacokinetics** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Well absorbed from the gut but undergoes significant first-pass metabolism to an active carboxylic acid metabolite (which acts in a non-competitive manner), and several other inactive compounds [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Oral bioavailability of 30% and is 99% plasma protein-bound [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Elimination half-life is 2 hours while the elimination half-life of the active metabolite is 7 hours [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Less than 10% is excreted unchanged in an active form in the urine. The inactive metabolites are excreted in bile and urine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

## 10.5. COMPARISON TO ACE INHIBITORS 

* **Advantages of ARBs** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Blockade of the AT₁-receptor is the most specific way of preventing the adverse effects of angiotensin II seen in heart failure and hypertension, especially as angiotensin II may be synthesized by alternative non-ACE pathways [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * The AT₂-receptor is not blocked, which may possess cardioprotective properties [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Much lower incidence of cough and angioedema and therefore improved compliance [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

**Bottom Line:** ARBs provide a useful alternative to ACE inhibitors with similar efficacy for treating hypertension and heart failure but with fewer adverse effects such as cough. However, they share similar risks of hyperkalemia, teratogenicity, and perioperative hypotension with ACE inhibitors.

# 11. ADRENERGIC NEURONE BLOCKADE 












**Drugs that interfere with the release of noradrenaline from adrenergic neurons.**

## 11.1. PHYSIOLOGICAL BASIS

* **Norepinephrine synthesis and handling** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Noradrenaline is synthesized from tyrosine within the adrenergic nerve terminal [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Held in storage vesicles and subsequently released into the neuronal cleft to act on adrenoceptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Its actions are terminated by:
	* **Uptake 1** – noradrenaline is taken back into the nerve terminal by a high-affinity transport system. This provides the main route for terminating its effects. Within the neurone it is recycled and returned to storage vesicles. However, while in the cytoplasm some may be deaminated by MAO [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
	* **Uptake 2** – noradrenaline diffuses away from the nerve terminal into the circulation where it is taken up by extraneuronal tissues including the liver and metabolized by catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

## 11.2. GUANETHIDINE 

* **Formulation and uses** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Available as tablets and as a colorless solution for injection containing 10 mg/ml [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Has been used as an antihypertensive agent but is currently used only for the control of sympathetically mediated chronic pain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Initial anti-hypertensive dose is 10 mg/day, which is increased to a maintenance dose of 30–50 mg/day [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * A dose of 20 mg is used when performing an intravenous regional block for chronic pain. Repeated blocks are usually required [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Mechanism of action** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Gains access to the adrenergic neurone by utilizing the uptake 1 transport mechanism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Following intravenous administration there is some initial hypotension by direct vasodilation of arterioles [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Subsequently, it displaces noradrenaline from its binding sites, which may cause transient hypertension [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Finally, guanethidine reduces the blood pressure by preventing the release of what little noradrenaline is left in the nerve terminal [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Oral administration does not produce the same triphasic response, as its onset of action is much slower [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Does not alter the secretion of catecholamines by the adrenal medulla [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Cardiovascular – hypotension is its main action. Postural hypotension is common as it blocks any compensatory rise in sympathetic tone. Fluid retention leading to oedema may occur [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Gut – diarrhea is common [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Miscellaneous – failure to ejaculate [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Drug interactions – drugs that block uptake 1 (tricyclic antidepressants, cocaine) prevent guanethidine from entering the nerve terminal and disrupting noradrenaline storage. They therefore antagonize guanethidine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Up-regulation of adrenoceptors follows the long-term use of guanethidine so that these patients are very sensitive to direct-acting sympathomimetic amines [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Pharmacokinetics** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Following oral administration, guanethidine is variably and incompletely absorbed [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Hepatic first-pass metabolism results in an oral bioavailability of 50% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Not bound by plasma proteins and does not cross the blood–brain barrier [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Elimination half-life of several days [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Elimination is by hepatic metabolism and excretion of unchanged drug and its metabolites in the urine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

## 11.3. METIROSINE

* **Mechanism and use** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Competitive inhibitor of tyrosine hydroxylase and can therefore prevent the synthesis of catecholamines [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Should only be used to manage hypertension associated with pheochromocytoma [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * May cause severe diarrhea, sedation, extrapyramidal effects and hypersensitivity reactions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

**Bottom Line:** Adrenergic neurone blockers interfere with norepinephrine release, reducing sympathetic vascular tone. Guanethidine enters neurons via the uptake-1 transporter and depletes norepinephrine stores, while metirosine inhibits catecholamine synthesis. These agents are now rarely used for hypertension but may have specialized roles in pain management and pheochromocytoma.

# 12. CENTRALLY ACTING AGENTS 












**Central nervous system agents that reduce sympathetic outflow to lower blood pressure.**

## 12.1. ALPHA-2 AGONISTS 

### 12.1.1. CLONIDINE   

* **Class and uses** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Centrally acting α₂-agonist antihypertensive agent that is 200 times more selective for α₂ than α₁ receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Licensed as an antihypertensive agent; also induces sedation and provides analgesia, especially as an adjunct to conventional analgesic medications [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Is an α-agonist with an affinity for α₂-receptors 200 times that for α₁-receptors. Some studies identify it as a partial agonist [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Presentation and dose** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Clear colorless solution for injection 150μg/ml [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * 10-200mcg up to four times per day [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Can also be added to spinal anesthesia (1mcg/kg) as an adjunct [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Available as 25–300 μg tablets and as a colorless solution for injection containing 150 μg/ml [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .
  * A transdermal patch is available but this takes 48 hours to achieve therapeutic levels [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .
  * Used in the treatment of hypertension, acute and chronic pain, the suppression of symptoms of opioid withdrawal and to augment sedation during ventilation of the critically ill patient [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .
* **Mechanism of action** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * The useful effects of clonidine rest on its ability to stimulate α₂-receptors in the lateral reticular nucleus resulting in reduced central sympathetic outflow, and in the spinal cord where they augment endogenous opiate release and modulate the descending noradrenergic pathways involved in spinal nociceptive processing [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * MAC appears to be reduced by stimulation of central postsynaptic α₂-receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Transmembrane signaling of α₂-receptors is coupled to Gi, leading to reduced intracellular cAMP. K⁺ channels are also activated [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Effects and side effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Cardiovascular effects:
	* Slight increase in SVR initially due to small amount of α₁ activity, followed by much longer decrease in blood pressure with reflex tachycardia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* ⚠️ Rebound hypertension seen if stopped quickly [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Following intravenous administration the blood pressure may rise due to peripheral α₁ stimulation, but this is followed by a more prolonged fall in blood pressure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

# 13. BETA BLOCKERS 












## 13.1. PERIOPERATIVE BETA-BLOCKADE: EVIDENCE AND CONTROVERSY [EVIDENCE-BASED PRACTICE]

### 13.1.1. INTRODUCTION AND HISTORICAL PERSPECTIVE

The use of perioperative beta-blockade (PBB) for the reduction of cardiac risk in patients undergoing noncardiac surgery came to prominence in the late 1990s, and continued excitement remained strong during the first decade of the twenty-first century. Support for its initial interest came from several early successful trials and the nearly 50 years of research from the cardiology literature documenting the cardioprotective effects of beta-blockers.

The primary studied endpoint for beta-blockers in the perioperative setting was for the reduction of major adverse cardiac events (MACE). These adverse cardiac events may account for up to 40% of all perioperative mortality. Additionally, perioperative myocardial ischemia has been associated with a significantly increased risk for nonfatal myocardial infarction (MI) and cardiovascular (CV) death for up to 6 months after surgery.

**Pathophysiology of Perioperative Myocardial Infarction:**
Most perioperative MIs (PMIs) are preceded by prolonged tachycardia with ST depression–type ischemia that will develop into a non–Q-wave infarction as the resting electrocardiogram subsequently returns to baseline. Thus PMI has traditionally been ascribed mostly to prolonged stress-induced ischemia in the setting of fixed coronary stenosis, with only a small percentage associated with classic acute plaque rupture. Given these assumptions, the natural role of beta-blockers in preventing PMI has been seen as improving myocardial oxygen balance by slowing the heart rate, reducing contractility, and improving diastolic coronary blood flow, thereby decreasing myocardial oxygen consumption.

💡 **Pleiotropic Effects:** Simply improving the balance of oxygen supply and demand is not the only benefit to PBBs because the attenuation of perioperative hemodynamic stress can help prevent rupture or fissuring of the intimal surface of a vulnerable plaque. Nevertheless, there are several other pathologic effects of perioperative stress and inflammation that contribute to PMI risk that PBBs cannot readily modify.

### 13.1.2. EARLY EVIDENCE FOR PERIOPERATIVE BETA-BLOCKADE

**Early Studies:**
The first randomized trial of perioperative beta-blockers came from the Multicenter Study of Perioperative Ischemia (McSPI) study group in 1996. The study consisted of 200 Veterans Affairs patients with or at risk for coronary artery disease (CAD) undergoing noncardiac surgery. Patients were randomly assigned to receive either placebo or atenolol (50 or 100 mg) 30 minutes before surgery and continued for 7 days afterwards. A 50% reduction in postoperative ischemia (from 34%–17% in days 0–2, p = .008) based on Holter monitoring was detected. Although beta-blockers did not affect perioperative MACE, during follow-up, the incidence of postoperative cardiac events and overall mortality were both shown to be significantly lower in the atenolol group at 6 months (0% versus 8%; p < .001) and remained significant throughout the 2-year study period (10% versus 21%; p = .019).

**DECREASE I Trial:**
In contrast to the McSPI study in which patients just at "risk" for CAD were included, the first DECREASE (Dutch Echocardiographic Cardiac Risk Evaluation Applying Stress Echocardiography, 1999) trial enrolled only patients with positive results on preoperative dobutamine stress echocardiography before major vascular surgery. Patients were randomly assigned to either titrated bisoprolol therapy or standard perioperative care. Patients taking preoperative beta-blockers or those with extensive wall motion abnormalities were excluded. Bisoprolol (5–10 mg) was started at least 1 week before surgery (average, 37 days prior) and then continued for 30 days postoperatively; the target heart rate was 51 to 79 beats/min.

The results showed a significant reduction in the primary endpoint of composite death from cardiac causes or nonfatal MI within 30 days postoperatively (34% vs. 3.4%, p < .001). The trial was stopped early despite only enrolling 112 patients (20 cardiac events). ⚠️ This study was not double-blind, the degree of risk reduction was larger than many authors deemed reasonable, and the event rate in the placebo arm was also greater than expected.

### 13.1.3. THE POISE TRIAL - PARADIGM SHIFT

**POISE Trial Background:**
The largest trial evaluating perioperative beta-blocker use, the Perioperative Ischemic Evaluation (POISE, published in 2008) trial, included 8351 patients with CV disease or who were scheduled for major vascular operations or with at least three of seven risk factors (intrathoracic or intraperitoneal operations, emergency procedures, previous heart failure, history of transient ischemic attack, diabetes, serum creatinine >175 µmol/L, age >70), who were randomly assigned to placebo or controlled release (CR) metoprolol.

Initial therapy consisted of 100 mg orally 2 to 4 hours before surgery. Dosing was increased to 200 mg a day postoperatively and continued for 30 days. For those unable to take oral medications, 15 mg of intravenous metoprolol every 6 hours was given until oral therapy could be restarted. The study drug was held if heart rates were less than 50 beats/min or if systolic blood pressure (SBP) was less than 100 mm Hg; adjustments were then made in subsequent dosing.

**POISE Results:**
Metoprolol CR therapy reduced the incidence of the primary endpoint of composite CV death, nonfatal MI, and nonfatal cardiac arrest within 30 days postoperatively (5.8% vs. 6.9%, p = .04), and the reduction was driven by a decrease in PMI (4.2% vs. 5.7%, p = .0017). Despite this decrease in MACE, there was a significant increase in the overall mortality in the study group (3.1% vs. 2.3%, p = .0317) as well as a doubling in the stroke incidence (1% vs. 0.5%, p = .0053). The increase in the all-cause mortality rate occurred mostly because of an increase in what was described as mortality related to infectious complications.

⚠️ **POISE Clinical Implications:** As stated by the POISE authors, for every 1000 patients treated with PBB, metoprolol CR would prevent 15 PMIs and 7 cases of new-onset atrial fibrillation while resulting in an excess of 8 deaths and 5 strokes. The negative effect of stroke becomes even more significant when one considers that in the POISE cohort, most of those who sustained PMI had limited continued symptoms and only 6% to 9% required coronary revascularization. In contrast, only 15% to 21% of the nonfatal stroke patients had complete recovery, whereas a significant number required functional support for daily activities.

**⚠️ Hypotension as the Dominant Risk Factor:**
Evaluation of the cause of this increase in morbidity and mortality showed the dominant factor to be perioperative hypotension. Significant hypotension (SBP <90 mm Hg requiring intervention) was common in POISE, developing in 15% of the study group. The risk for death increased by fivefold, and the incidence of stroke associated with hypotension doubled.

**POISE Trial Limitations:**
- Outcome of PBB in patients with higher CV risk not known
	- Only 40% of patients had known CAD or underwent high risk vascular surgery
	- POISE showed modest benefit for MACE (5.8% vs. 6.9%), and CV benefit may be better in higher risk patients
- Inappropriate Metoprolol dose
	- Starting dose of 200mg/day is twice max recommended starting dose, and 50% max recommended daily dose
- Possible inappropriate choice of beta-blocker
	- Atenolol has longer duration of action and improved cardioselectivity → greater cardioprotection
	- Greater variability in metabolism of Metoprolol
### 13.1.4. POST-POISE EVIDENCE AND META-ANALYSES

**Recent Meta-Analyses:**
After the publication of POISE, the 2008 meta-analysis of Bengalore and colleagues demonstrated a 116% increase in stroke risk with PBB, which significantly offset the CV benefit (35% reduction in nonfatal PMI). A 2018 Cochrane systematic review of 88 RCTs (19,161 patients) investigating the perioperative use of beta-blockers showed, despite a significant reduction in the rate of PMI, myocardial ischemia, and supraventricular arrythmias, a significant increase in all-cause mortality rates and potential increase in cerebrovascular complications.

### 13.1.5. CURRENT GUIDELINES AND EVIDENCE-BASED RECOMMENDATIONS

**2014 ACC/AHA Guidelines for Perioperative Beta-Blocker Therapy:**

**Class I (Should be done):**
* Beta-blockers should be continued in patients undergoing surgery who have been on beta-blockers chronically (Level of Evidence: B)

**Class IIa (Reasonable to do):**
* It is reasonable for the management of beta-blockers after surgery to be guided by clinical circumstances, independent of when the agent was started (Level of Evidence: B)

**Class IIb (May be reasonable):**
* In patients with intermediate- or high-risk myocardial ischemia noted in preoperative risk stratification tests, it may be reasonable to begin perioperative beta-blockers (Level of Evidence: C)
* In patients with 3 or more RCRI risk factors (e.g., diabetes mellitus, heart failure, CAD, renal insufficiency, cerebrovascular accident), it may be reasonable to begin beta-blockers before surgery (Level of Evidence: B)
* In patients with a compelling long-term indication for beta-blocker therapy but no other RCRI risk factors, initiating beta-blockers in the perioperative setting as an approach to reduce perioperative risk is of uncertain benefit (Level of Evidence: B)
* In patients in whom beta-blocker therapy is initiated, it may be reasonable to begin perioperative beta-blockers long enough in advance to assess safety and tolerability, preferably more than 1 day before surgery (Level of Evidence: B)

**Class III (Should not be done - Harm):**
* Beta-blocker therapy should not be started on the day of surgery (Level of Evidence: B)

**European Society of Cardiology/European Society of Anaesthesiology Guidelines:**
The ESC/ESA 2014 guidelines state that preoperative initiation of beta-blockers may be considered for patients undergoing high-risk surgery and who have at least 2 clinical risk factors or ASA status of at least 3 (class IIb recommendation). The guidelines also advise against initiation of high-dose beta-blockers without titration and for preoperative initiation of beta-blockers for patients undergoing low-risk surgery.

### 13.1.6. AREAS OF UNCERTAINTY AND SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS

**Patient Selection Considerations:**
Although the findings of the POISE trial should cause clinicians to proceed with significant caution regarding PBB, one must consider several important issues:

1. **Cardiac Risk Stratification:** Would the CV benefits of PBB have been fully offset by increases in stroke and overall mortality rates if the cohort from POISE had higher baseline CV disease? Only approximately 40% of patients in the POISE trial had either known CAD or underwent high-risk vascular surgery.

2. **Dosing Concerns:** A potential starting dose of 200 mg/day metoprolol CR is twice the maximum recommended starting dose and 50% of the maximum recommended daily dose. The threshold for allowing hypotension before withholding metoprolol (SBP >100) was also considered quite high.

3. **Agent Selection:** Wallace and colleagues have published a comparison of outcomes showing that 30-day and 1-year mortality rates were significantly lower in patients who received atenolol versus metoprolol for prophylaxis. Possible mechanisms include the longer duration of action of atenolol and improved cardioselectivity.

4. **Timing of Initiation:** Earlier titration of therapy may allow for the early recognition of variability in drug response. There was no preoperative titration in POISE, and maximum dosing occurred as early as day 0.

**⚠️ Special Populations Requiring Caution:**
* **Depressed Myocardial Function:** Short-term beta-blockade, particularly large fixed doses, in those with depressed myocardial function should be used with great caution until better safety data are available.
* **Cerebrovascular Disease:** In the POISE cohort, a history of stroke or transient ischemic attack was a significantly better predictor of postoperative stroke than perioperative hypotension. These findings highlight the risk associated with beta-blocker–induced hypotension or hypoperfusion in those with a compromised cerebrovascular tree.

### 13.1.7. CONTINUATION OF LONG-TERM THERAPY AND WITHDRAWAL SYNDROME

Although only a few studies describe discontinuing chronic beta-blocker therapy in the perioperative time period, long-standing evidence from other settings suggests that abrupt withdrawal of patients' chronic beta-blocker therapy is harmful. Studies have shown significant increases in mortality and MI in those undergoing vascular surgery who had beta-blockers discontinued. 

⚠️ **Beta-blocker withdrawal** can lead to increases in heart rate and myocardial oxygen demand and predispose to myocardial ischemia. Rebound hypertension is seen more commonly when a dose of more than 1.2 g/day is stopped abruptly due to peripheral vasoconstriction and increased plasma catecholamines and may be exacerbated by β-blockade (leaving vasoconstriction unopposed).

**Clinical Implications for Current Practice:**
* **Continue existing therapy:** All patients on chronic beta-blocker therapy should continue perioperatively
* **Avoid day-of-surgery initiation:** Fixed, high-dose protocols should be avoided
* **Individualized approach:** New initiation should consider patient risk factors, surgical risk, and timing for titration
* **Monitor closely:** Watch for hypotension and bradycardia, especially in elderly patients or those with cerebrovascular disease
* **Gradual titration:** If starting beta-blockers, begin at low doses with gradual titration well before surgery

### 13.1.8. INTEGRATION WITH PERIOPERATIVE HYPERTENSION MANAGEMENT

**Relationship to Antihypertensive Management:**
Beta-blockers are often part of multi-drug antihypertensive regimens, creating complex perioperative management decisions:
* **Continue beta-blockers:** Strong evidence supports continuation of chronic beta-blocker therapy
* **ACE-I/ARB considerations:** May need to withhold other antihypertensive agents while continuing beta-blockers
* **Hypotension risk:** Combined effects of multiple antihypertensive classes increase perioperative hypotension risk
* **Monitoring requirements:** Enhanced hemodynamic monitoring may be needed for patients on multiple agents

**Clinical Decision Framework:**
1. **Risk assessment:** Evaluate cardiac risk factors, surgical risk, and baseline cardiovascular status
2. **Medication history:** Document all current beta-blocker therapy and indications
3. **Timing considerations:** Ensure adequate time for titration if initiating new therapy
4. **Monitoring plan:** Prepare for potential hypotension and have treatment strategies ready
5. **Postoperative planning:** Ensure continuity of appropriate long-term therapy

## 13.3. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* **Competitive antagonism at beta-adrenoceptors** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Blocks effects of endogenous catecholamines at beta-receptor sites [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Results in negative inotropy and chronotropy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
	* Prolongation of AV node conduction and reduced SA node firing rate [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Reduced cardiac output and blood pressure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Inhibits beta1 receptors on juxtaglomerular apparatus in the kidney, inhibiting the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system and reducing fluid retention [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Inhibits pre-synaptic beta2 receptors in baroreceptors, reducing the "set-point" target [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

## 13.4. CLINICAL EFFECTS  

* **Cardiovascular effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:

## 13.7. PHARMACOKINETICS

* **General pharmacokinetic principles** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Absorption: How well absorbed from the GI tract depends on the individual agent's lipid solubility, with more soluble drugs more readily absorbed [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Distribution: Lipid-soluble agents cross membranes more easily, including the blood-brain barrier, potentially causing neurological side effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Metabolism: Lipid-soluble agents undergo hepatic metabolism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Excretion: Lipophobic agents are excreted unchanged by the kidneys; metabolites of lipophilic agents undergo renal excretion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

## 13.8. PERIOPERATIVE CONSIDERATIONS 

* **Decision factors for lowering blood pressure** should include [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Minimum safe blood pressure for the patient (considering comorbidities like hypertension, CVA, IHD, PVD, renal impairment) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Possibility of reducing venous pressure first (elevating surgical site, avoiding hypercapnia, avoiding compression of veins) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Alternative measures to reduce bleeding (tranexamic acid, clotting factors, normothermia, calcium) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Perioperative management** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Most beta-blockers can be continued perioperatively [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * All antihypertensives should be held in shocked or hypotensive patients undergoing surgery [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Beta-blockers reduce blood pressure by blocking beta-adrenergic receptors, causing negative chronotropy, inotropy, and decreased renin release. Selective agents like esmolol provide rapid, titratable blood pressure control with minimal respiratory effects, while non-selective agents like labetalol offer combined alpha and beta blockade. However, evidence from the POISE trial and subsequent meta-analyses has raised significant concerns about perioperative beta-blocker initiation, particularly the risk of hypotension leading to stroke and death. Current guidelines recommend continuing chronic beta-blockers but caution against new initiation on the day of surgery. For patients with 3+ RCRI risk factors (ischemic heart disease, heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, high-risk surgery), perioperative beta-blockers may be considered if started well in advance with careful titration. The decision requires careful risk-benefit analysis, and fixed high-dose protocols should be avoided. Careful drug selection based on patient comorbidities is essential.

## 14.3. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS

* **Primary indications** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Hypertension management, particularly in patients with comorbid BPH [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Pre-surgical preparation in pheochromocytoma [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Alpha blockers reduce blood pressure primarily through arterial vasodilation, with selective alpha-1 blockers causing fewer side effects than non-selective agents. They're particularly useful for hypertension with BPH and in pheochromocytoma management, where they help control catecholamine-induced hypertension.

## 15.2. TECHNIQUES AND AGENTS 

* **Methods for achieving controlled hypotension** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Hypotensive anesthetic techniques (e.g., neuraxial anesthesia) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Administration of hypotensive drugs [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Numerous pharmacological agents effectively lower blood pressure: volatile anesthetics, spinal and epidural anesthesia, sympathetic antagonists, calcium channel blockers, and peripheral vasodilators [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Other options to consider before pharmacological hypotension** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Elevate the surgical site to reduce venous pressure [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Avoid hypercapnia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Avoid compression of veins (endotracheal tube ties, reduce PEEP) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Consider alternatives for reducing bleeding: tranexamic acid, clotting factors, normothermia, calcium [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

## 15.5. SAFE PRACTICE CONSIDERATIONS   

* **Patient-specific target blood pressure** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]], [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]:
  * Safe level of hypotension depends on patient characteristics [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Young, healthy patients: MAP 50-60 mmHg [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* Patients with chronic hypertension who have altered autoregulation of cerebral blood flow: MAP no more than 20-30% lower than baseline [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* Patients with TIA history: May not tolerate any decline in cerebral perfusion [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Decision-making factors** [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]:
  * Consider what the minimum safe blood pressure is for the specific patient based on comorbidities [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].
  * Attempt to reduce venous pressure first through positioning and avoiding hypercapnia [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].
  * Explore alternative methods to reduce bleeding beyond hypotension [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].
  * 💡 When hypotension is used, ensure adequate monitoring and readiness to treat complications [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Controlled hypotension can reduce surgical blood loss and improve visualization but carries significant risks of organ ischemia. Patient selection is critical, with individualized blood pressure targets based on age, comorbidities, and baseline blood pressure. Alternative methods to reduce bleeding should be considered before employing hypotensive techniques.
