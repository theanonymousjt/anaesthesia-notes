# 1. AUTONOMIC NERVOUS SYSTEM ANATOMY AND PHYSIOLOGY

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Autonomic Nervous System|Part A: Autonomic Nervous System]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C]]

## 1.2. FUNCTIONAL ANATOMY

**The autonomic nervous system (ANS) is the involuntary branch of the peripheral nervous system that regulates bodily functions and maintains homeostasis.**

### 1.2.1. ANS ORGANIZATION

* **ANS Organization**:
	* Central ANS and Somatic centers are integrated at all levels of CNS axis
	* Both SNS (Sympathetic) and PNS (Parasympathetic) components work in complementary opposition
	* Most organs receive dual innervation (except sweat glands and spleen - SNS only)
	* The ANS innervates smooth muscle and glands, influencing the function of internal organs that regulate heart rate, arterial blood pressure, digestion, micturition, defecation, sweating and sexual function
* **Function of ANS Divisions**:
	* **Sympathetic system**: Often summarized as "fight, flight or fright" - prepares the body for stressful situations
		* Increases cardiac output (↑HR, stroke volume, contractility)
		* Causes vasoconstriction and venoconstriction
		* Mobilizes glucose stores
		* Dilates pupils
	* **Parasympathetic system**: Often summarized as "rest and digest" - carries out basic functions required for life
		* Decreases heart rate
		* Stimulates salivation and peristalsis
		* Promotes urination
		* Constricts pupils

### 1.2.2. CENTRAL AUTONOMIC ORGANIZATION

* **Hypothalamus**:
	* Principal site of ANS organization
	* Posterolateral nuclei → SNS functions
	* Midline and anterior nuclei → PNS functions
	* Anterior nuclei → Temperature regulation
	* Supraoptic nuclei → Water metabolism (ADH)
	* Other functions: BP control, stress reactions, sleep and sexual reflexes
* **Medulla Oblongata and Pons**:
	* Integrate hemodynamic adjustments and ventilation automaticity
	* **Nucleus Tractus Solitarius**: Primary relay for chemoreceptor and baroreceptor afferents
	* Maintain visceral organ tonicity (intermediate activity state)
* **Spinal/Segmental levels**:
	* Can mediate reflex changes independently
	* Loss of higher inhibitory centers leads to ANS hyperreflexia in high spinal lesions

### 1.2.3. PERIPHERAL AUTONOMIC ORGANIZATION

* **Efferent Pathways**:
	* Different from somatic pathways
	* **Somatic motor**: Single (unipolar) neuron from ventral gray matter → muscle
	* **ANS motor**: Two-neuron chain (bipolar) to effector organ
		* **Preganglionic** neurons: Originate in CNS, myelinated (<3mm), conduction 3-15m/s
		* **Postganglionic** neurons: Cell body in ANS ganglion, unmyelinated, conduction <2m/s
* **Structural Organization**:
	* Irrespective of the ANS subdivision, action potentials reach viscera through two sets of neurons: preganglionic and postganglionic
	* Sympathetic and parasympathetic systems differ in the length of these neurons, location of their synapses (ganglia), and neurotransmitter utilized

![Schematic distribution of the craniosacral (parasympathetic) and thoracolumbar (sympathetic) nervous systems. Parasympathetic preganglionic fibers pass directly to the organ that is innervated. Their postganglionic cell bodies are situated near or within the innervated viscera. This limited distribution of parasympathetic postganglionic fibers is consistent with the discrete and limited effect of parasympathetic function. **Bottom Line:** The ANS is organized with central control in the hypothalamus and brainstem, with a two-neuron efferent system (preganglionic and postganglionic) that provides highly differentiated control of organ systems. The sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions typically have opposing effects on target organs.

## 1.3. SYMPATHETIC NERVOUS SYSTEM

**The sympathetic nervous system (SNS) is responsible for the "fight or flight" response and prepares the body for high levels of activity through thoracolumbar outflow.**

### 1.3.1. ORIGIN AND DISTRIBUTION

* **Origin**:
	* Thoracolumbar outflow (T1-L3)
	* Preganglionic fibers from intermediolateral gray column of T1-T12 and L1-L3
	* Exit spinal cord as white (myelinated) communicating rami
* **Course of SNS Fibers**:
	* Enter one of 22 paired sympathetic ganglia (paravertebral)
	* Preganglionic fibers may:
		1. Synapse directly at level of entry
		2. Course up/down sympathetic chain to synapse at other levels
		3. Pass through chain without synapsing to reach collateral ganglia

* **Distribution**:
	* Postganglionic fibers return to spinal nerves as gray (unmyelinated) communicating rami
	* Distributed to sweat glands, pilomotor muscles, blood vessels
	* ~8% of fibers in average somatic nerve are sympathetic
	* One preganglionic fiber influences multiple postganglionic neurons (1:4 ratio) → mass effect
* **Segmental Distribution**:
	* **Head and neck**: T1-2
	* **Upper limb**: T2-5
	* **Thoracic viscera**: T1-4
	* **Abdominal viscera**: T4-L2
	* **Pelvic viscera**: T10-L2
	* **Lower limb**: T11-L2

### 1.3.2. SPECIAL GANGLIA

* **Special Ganglia**:
	* First 4-5 thoracic segments → 3 paired cervical ganglia:
		* Superior cervical
		* Middle cervical
		* Cervicothoracic (stellate) ganglion → head, neck, upper extremities, heart, lungs
	* **Adrenal Medulla**: Unique as preganglionic fibers connect directly (no synapsing)
		* Medullary cells are modified neurons (equivalent to postganglionic neurons)
		* Releases catecholamines directly into circulation

### 1.3.3. SYMPATHETIC CHAIN AND GANGLIA

* **Sympathetic Trunk**:
	* Ganglionated nerve chain extending from base of skull to coccyx
	* Lies close to vertebral column (~2.5 cm from midline)
	* Ganglion formation: Originally one ganglion per peripheral nerve, reduced by fusion to 3 cervical, 12 or fewer thoracic, 2-4 lumbar, and 4 sacral ganglia
	* Only ganglia T1-L2 receive white rami directly
* **Three Fates of White Rami**:
	1. Synapse in corresponding sympathetic ganglion (only in T1-L2 segments)
	2. Ascend or descend in sympathetic chain to relay in higher or lower ganglia
	3. Traverse ganglia intact to relay in peripheral ganglia

* **Branch Types**:
	* **Somatic**: Gray rami to spinal nerves for distribution to segmental skin areas
		* Vasoconstrictor to skin arterioles
		* Sudomotor to sweat glands
		* Pilomotor to arrectores pilorum
	* **Visceral**: Postganglionic fibers to head/neck and thoracic viscera directly from sympathetic chain
		* Abdominal and pelvic viscera supplied by postganglionic fibers from peripheral ganglia (coeliac, hypogastric, pelvic plexuses)

![The three fates of sympathetic white rami](ANS_ANS_image_10.png_c8bbf3.png_a0e0d8.png)

### 1.3.4. CERVICAL GANGLIA

* **Location and Structure**:
	* Three in number: superior, middle, inferior (stellate)
	* Receive preganglionic fibers from T1-5 spinal segments
	* Relay for postganglionic fibers to head, neck, and upper limb
* **Superior Cervical Ganglion**:
	* 2.5 cm or more in length
	* Located opposite 2nd and 3rd cervical vertebrae
	* Represents fused ganglia of C1-4
	* Branches:
		* Gray rami to upper four cervical nerves
		* Internal carotid plexus: deep petrosal nerve, ciliary ganglion supply (pupillary dilation), cerebral vessels and pituitary supply
		* External carotid plexus: reaches submandibular and otic ganglia
		* Gray rami to cranial nerves VII, IX, X, XII
		* Superior cardiac nerve to cardiac plexuses
* **Middle Cervical Ganglion**:
	* Small and not always present
	* Located at level of 6th cervical vertebra
	* Represents fused ganglia of C5-6
	* Branches:
		* Gray rami to C5 and C6
		* Thyroid branch along inferior thyroid artery
		* Middle cardiac nerve to deep cardiac plexus
* **Inferior Cervical (Stellate) Ganglion**:
	* Located at level of C7-T1 intervertebral space
	* Fused with 1st thoracic ganglion in 80% of subjects to form stellate ganglion
	* Represents coalescence of 7th and 8th cervical ganglia
	* Connected to middle ganglion by sympathetic chain and ansa subclavia (looping around subclavian artery)
	* Branches:
		* Gray rami to C7 and C8
		* Vertebral artery plexus to brain
		* Inferior cardiac nerve to deep cardiac plexus

![The cervical sympathetic chain](ANS_pdf_ANS.pdf_p9_img1_d0530e.jpeg)

### 1.3.5. THORACIC GANGLIA

* **Structure and Location**:
	* Usually 12 in number (may be decreased by fusion)
	* Each connected to corresponding intercostal nerve by gray and white rami
* **Branches**:
	* Gray rami to intercostal nerves
	* Branches from T2-4 to cardiac, posterior pulmonary, and esophageal plexuses
	* Fibers to aortic wall
	* Splanchnic nerves from lower eight ganglia:
		* **Greater splanchnic nerve**: Arises from T5-9 (or T10), pierces crus of diaphragm, joins coeliac ganglion
		* **Lesser splanchnic nerve**: From T9-10 (or T10-11), pierces crus, ends in coeliac ganglion
		* **Lowest splanchnic nerve**: From lowest thoracic ganglion, passes behind medial arcuate ligament to renal plexus

### 1.3.6. LUMBAR GANGLIA

* **Structure and Location**:
	* Usually four in number
	* Upper two receive white rami from corresponding lumbar nerves
	* Lie on anterolateral surface of lumbar vertebral bodies
	* Overlapped by aorta on left and inferior vena cava on right
* **Branches**:
	* Gray rami to lumbar nerves
	* Branches to aortic plexus
	* Fibers descending over common iliac vessels to hypogastric plexus

![The lumbar sympathetic chain](ANS_pdf_ANS.pdf_p11_img1_c2fd7a.png)

![The relationships of the lumbar sympathetic chain](ANS_pdf_ANS.pdf_p12_img1_e85489.png)

### 1.3.7. SACRAL GANGLIA

* Usually four in number
* Send gray rami to sacral nerves and to the pelvic plexuses

**Bottom Line:** The sympathetic nervous system originates from T1-L3 (thoracolumbar outflow), with ganglia forming a chain near the spine. Preganglionic fibers may synapse at their level of entry, travel up or down the chain to synapse elsewhere, or pass through to peripheral ganglia. One preganglionic neuron typically influences multiple postganglionic neurons, creating a more diffuse "mass effect" response.

## 1.4. PARASYMPATHETIC NERVOUS SYSTEM

**The parasympathetic nervous system (PNS) mediates "rest and digest" functions through craniosacral outflow, with the vagus nerve providing most of this innervation.**

### 1.4.1. ORIGIN AND DISTRIBUTION

* **Origin**: Craniosacral outflow
	* **Cranial outflow**: CN3 (oculomotor), CN7 (facial), CN9 (glossopharyngeal), CN10 (vagus)
		* **CN10 (vagus)**: Accounts for >75% of all PNS activity
		* Supplies: Heart, lungs, esophagus, stomach, small intestine, proximal colon, liver, gallbladder, pancreas, upper ureters
	* **Sacral outflow**: S2-S4 → pelvic visceral nerves (nervi erigentes)
		* Supplies: Descending colon, rectum, bladder, lower ureters, sexual organs

### 1.4.2. COURSE OF PNS FIBERS

* **Course of PNS Fibers**:
	* Preganglionic fibers pass directly to innervated organ
	* Postganglionic cell bodies located near or within target viscera
	* Predominantly 1:1 to 3:1 ratio of postganglionic to preganglionic fibers
		* Exception: Auerbach plexus in distal colon (8,000:1)
	* Creates discrete, limited effects (vs. SNS mass effects)
* **Characteristic Features**:
	* Long preganglionic fibers (vs. short sympathetic preganglionic fibers)
	* Short postganglionic fibers (vs. long sympathetic postganglionic fibers)
	* At both synapses (preganglionic→postganglionic and postganglionic→target), ACh is the neurotransmitter

### 1.4.3. CRANIAL OUTFLOW

* **Oculomotor nerve (CN III)**:
	* Relayed in ciliary ganglion
	* Innervates sphincter pupillae and ciliary muscle
	* Effects: Pupillary constriction and lens accommodation
* **Facial nerve (CN VII)**:
	* Relayed in pterygopalatine and submandibular ganglia
	* Secretomotor to lacrimal, submandibular, and sublingual salivary glands
* **Glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX)**:
	* Relayed in otic ganglion
	* Secretomotor to parotid gland
* **Vagus nerve (CN X)**:
	* Most important and largest contribution of PNS
	* Efferent fibers from dorsal nucleus of X in medulla
	* Distributed through cardiac, pulmonary, and alimentary plexuses
	* Postganglionic relay from tiny ganglia in visceral walls
		* Submucosal plexus of Meissner and myenteric plexus of Auerbach in gut

### 1.4.4. SACRAL OUTFLOW

* **Structure and Distribution**:
	* Anterior primary rami of S2-3 (occasionally S4) give off pelvic splanchnic nerves (nervi erigentes)
	* Join sympathetic pelvic plexuses for distribution to pelvic organs
	* Relay in tiny ganglia in visceral walls
* **Function**:
	* Called the "mechanism for emptying"
	* Supplies visceromotor fibers to muscles of rectum (and perhaps lower colon)
	* Inhibitory fibers to internal (involuntary) anal sphincter
	* Motor fibers to bladder wall
	* Inhibitory fibers to internal vesical sphincter
	* Vasodilator fibers to erectile cavernous sinuses of penis and clitoris

**Bottom Line:** The parasympathetic nervous system originates from cranial nerves (CN3, 7, 9, 10) and sacral segments (S2-S4), with the vagus nerve providing >75% of parasympathetic activity. Parasympathetic ganglia are located near target organs, creating more targeted, discrete responses compared to the SNS. The cranial outflow supplies organs above the mid-transverse colon, while sacral outflow innervates pelvic organs.

### 1.5.2. HEART

* **ANS Effects**:
	* Chronotropism (heart rate)
	* Inotropism (contraction strength)
	* Coronary blood flow modulation
* **PNS (Vagal) Effects**:
	* **Main distribution**: SA node, AV node, atria (minimal to ventricles)
	* **Primary effect**: Chronotropic (decreased SA discharge, slowed AV conduction)
	* **Secondary effect**: Can reduce contractile force by 10-20%
* **SNS Effects**:
	* **Distribution**: Throughout heart with greater representation in ventricles
	* **Ganglionic origin**: Paired stellate ganglia
		* **Right stellate**: Anterior epicardium and interventricular septum → ↑HR
		* **Left stellate**: Posterior/lateral ventricles → ↑MAP and contractility
	* **Normal tone**: Maintains contractility ~20% above baseline

### 1.5.3. PERIPHERAL CIRCULATION

* **Primary regulator**: SNS (more important than PNS)
* **SNS effects**:
	* Originates from vasomotor center in medulla
	* Maintains basal vasomotor tone (partial arteriolar/venular constriction)
	* Venules function as reservoir (~80% of blood volume)
	* Vessel distribution varies by tissue:
		* Extensive: Skin, kidneys, spleen, mesentery
		* Less extensive: Heart, brain, muscle
* **PNS effects**:
	* Limited vasodilation (primarily genitalia)

### 1.5.4. LUNGS

* **Innervation**: Both SNS and PNS
* **SNS effects**:
	* Bronchodilation
	* Pulmonary vasoconstriction
	* Adjusts to accommodate RV output
* **PNS effects**:
	* Bronchoconstriction
	* Secretion of bronchial glands
	* Minimal pulmonary vasodilation
	* Vagal receptors in alveolar ducts regulate ventilation cycle
* **Other lung functions**:
	* Metabolizes circulating mediators (NE)
	* Converts angiotensin I to active compounds

**Bottom Line:** The autonomic nervous system provides dual innervation to most organs, with the sympathetic system generally promoting "fight or flight" responses (increased heart rate, bronchodilation, reduced digestion) and the parasympathetic system promoting "rest and digest" functions (decreased heart rate, increased secretions, enhanced digestion).

## 1.6. PLEXUSES OF THE SYMPATHETIC SYSTEM

**The sympathetic distribution to intrathoracic and intra-abdominal viscera is via a series of plexuses in which nerve relay takes place.**

### 1.6.1. CARDIAC PLEXUS

* **Subdivisions**:
	* **Superficial plexus**: Lies in front of pulmonary artery within curve of aortic arch
		* Receives superior cardiac nerve of left superior cervical sympathetic ganglion and lower cardiac branch of left vagus
		* Branches to deep cardiac plexus, left anterior pulmonary plexus, and right coronary artery plexus
	* **Deep plexus**: Lies in front of tracheal bifurcation behind aortic arch
		* Receives cardiac branches from all right cervical sympathetic ganglia, middle and inferior ganglia on left, upper four thoracic ganglia, and both vagi
		* Branches to pulmonary plexuses at lung hila and coronary artery plexuses

### 1.6.2. COELIAC PLEXUS

* **Structure and Location**:
	* Largest sympathetic plexus, surrounding root of coeliac artery at L1 level
	* Dense fiber network uniting into right and left coeliac ganglia (each ~2.5 cm in diameter)
	* Right ganglion overlapped by inferior vena cava; left by pancreas and splenic artery
	* Receives greater and lesser splanchnic nerves and coeliac branch of right vagus
* **Distribution**:
	* Large contribution of preganglionic fibers to suprarenal medulla without relay
	* Descends over abdominal aorta as aortic plexus (receiving contributions from lumbar ganglia)
	* Distributed along aortic branches as named plexuses:
		* Phrenic, hepatic, splenic, left gastric, renal, mesenteric, testicular/ovarian

![The coeliac plexus](ANS_pdf_ANS.pdf_p15_img1_c31568.jpeg)

### 1.6.3. HYPOGASTRIC PLEXUS

* **Structure and Location**:
	* Lies on sacral promontory and left common iliac vein in cleft between common iliac arteries
	* Formed by "presacral nerves" descending from aortic plexus and lumbar sympathetic trunks
	* Fibers descend along internal iliac arteries to form right and left pelvic plexuses
	* Joined by pelvic splanchnic nerves (parasympathetic contribution from S2-4)
	* Distributed to pelvic organs

**Bottom Line:** Sympathetic distribution to viscera occurs through complex plexuses (cardiac, coeliac, hypogastric) that also transmit parasympathetic fibers. These plexuses provide integrative sites where neural inputs can be processed before distribution to target organs.

## 1.7. HIGHER SYMPATHETIC CENTERS

* **Brainstem Centers**:
	* Located near midline in floor of pons and 4th ventricle
	* Vasomotor center as prominent example
* **Cortical and Hypothalamic Centers**:
	* Closely linked and termed the "limbic system"
	* Components: gyrus cinguli, hippocampal gyrus, uncus, anterior thalamic nucleus, amygdala, hypothalamus
	* Stimulation produces marked changes in heart rate, bowel motility, pupil reactions
	* Fibers traceable from lateral grey column of cord through medulla to limbic area
	* Represents highest level of autonomic control

**Bottom Line:** Higher autonomic centers in the brainstem, hypothalamus, and limbic system provide hierarchical control over autonomic functions, with the limbic system representing the highest level of integration between emotional states and autonomic responses.

# 2. AUTONOMIC NEUROTRANSMISSION

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Autonomic Nervous System|Part A: Autonomic Neurotransmission]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C]]

## 2.2. NEUROTRANSMITTER PATHWAYS

**The ANS employs specific neurotransmitters at different sites, with acetylcholine and norepinephrine being the primary mediators.**

* **Pharmacological subdivision**:
	* **Preganglionic neurons** (both SNS and PNS): Release ACh
	* **SNS postganglionic**: Release NE (exception: sweat glands release ACh)
		* Cotransmitters: ATP, neuropeptide Y
	* **PNS postganglionic**: Release ACh
* **Anatomical structure**:
	* Postganglionic fibers form terminal effector plexuses (reticulae)
	* Varicosities contain neurotransmitter vesicles (~500 Å diameter)
	* Synaptic cleft width varies: 100 Å (ganglia) to 20,000 Å (large arteries)
	* One SNS postganglionic neuron may innervate ~25,000 effector cells

![image.png](ANS_ANS_image_3.png_8a5c57.png_7a4525.png)

**Bottom Line:** The ANS uses acetylcholine at all preganglionic terminals and at parasympathetic postganglionic terminals, while sympathetic postganglionic terminals primarily use norepinephrine (with the notable exception of sweat glands, which use acetylcholine).

## 2.3. PARASYMPATHETIC TRANSMISSION

**Acetylcholine is the primary neurotransmitter for the parasympathetic nervous system, with rapid synthesis and degradation.**

* **Neurotransmitter**: Acetylcholine (ACh)
* **Synthesis**:
	* Acetylation of choline with acetyl coenzyme A (catalyzed by choline acetyltransferase)
	* ACh stored in presynaptic vesicles
	* Quantal release at rest (miniature end-plate potentials)
	* Action potential → synchronous release of hundreds of quanta
	* Ca²⁺-dependent release mechanism
* **Metabolism**:
	* Rapid hydrolysis by acetylcholinesterase
	* ACh continuously synthesized (unlike NE which is recycled)

**Bottom Line:** Parasympathetic transmission relies on acetylcholine, which is rapidly synthesized and degraded by acetylcholinesterase, resulting in relatively short-duration effects compared to sympathetic transmission.

## 2.4. SYMPATHETIC TRANSMISSION

**Sympathetic transmission primarily uses norepinephrine, with epinephrine playing a significant role through adrenal medulla release.**

* **Primary mediators**: Epinephrine (EPI) and norepinephrine (NE)
* **Adrenal medulla**:
	* SNS fibers are preganglionic and release ACh
	* Chromaffin cells substitute for postganglionic neurons
	* Release mixture of EPI > NE into circulation
	* Effects last ~10× longer than direct sympathetic stimulation

**Bottom Line:** Sympathetic transmission uses norepinephrine at most postganglionic terminals, while the adrenal medulla releases primarily epinephrine directly into circulation for longer-lasting, more widespread effects.

## 2.5. CATECHOLAMINES

**Catecholamines are a class of neurotransmitters with a catechol nucleus and amine side chain that mediate sympathetic effects.**

### 2.5.1. SYNTHESIS AND METABOLISM

* **Definition**: Compounds with catechol nucleus and amine-containing side chain
* **Endogenous catecholamines**:
	* **Dopamine (DA)**: Precursor to NE, found in CNS
	* **Norepinephrine (NE)**: Stored in postganglionic SNS neurons
	* **Epinephrine (EPI)**: Stored in adrenal medulla (80-85% of adrenal content)
	* Do not cross blood-brain barrier (brain catecholamines synthesized locally)

![image.png](ANS_ANS_image_4.png_2120cc.png_deb345.png)

* **Sympathomimetics**:
	* Activate SNS receptors due to structural similarity
	* All clinical catecholamines are sympathomimetics
	* Can be direct or indirect-acting
* **Synthesis of Catecholamines**:
	* Main site: Near postganglionic nerve endings
	* Pathway: Phenylalanine/Tyrosine → DOPA → Dopamine → NE → EPI
	* Rate-limiting step: Tyrosine hydroxylase (feedback inhibition)

![image.png](ANS_ANS_image_5.png_d3d117.png_a939b0.png)

    * Dopamine synthesis: In neuronal cytoplasm
    * NE synthesis: In vesicles containing dopamine-β-hydroxylase
    * EPI synthesis: In adrenal medulla via phenylethanolamine-N-methyltransferase
    * Ca²⁺-dependent excitation-secretion coupling
    

![image.png](ANS_ANS_image_6.png_c44735.png_e47f68.png)

### 2.5.2. REGULATION AND INACTIVATION

* **Regulation of Catecholamines**:
	* ↑ SNS activity → ↑ catecholamine synthesis
	* Glucocorticoids → ↑ phenylethanolamine-N-methyltransferase (NE → EPI)
	* NE release promoted by:
		* Nerve depolarization and ↑ Ca²⁺ permeability
		* α-receptor antagonists (block inhibitory presynaptic α₂ receptors)
	* NE release inhibited by:
		* Colchicine and prostaglandin E₂
		* Self-inhibition via presynaptic α₂ receptors
* **Inactivation of Catecholamines**:
	1. **Reuptake** into presynaptic terminals (primary mechanism)
		* Active, energy-requiring, temperature-dependent process
		* Mediated by presynaptic β-adrenergic mechanism
		* Stereospecific
		* Inhibited by TCAs and cocaine → ↑ synaptic NE

	![image.png](ANS_ANS_image_7.png_53ebba.png_ee1fc4.png)

	2. **Extraneuronal uptake** (minor pathway)
		* Metabolized by MAO and COMT → vanillylmandelic acid

	![image.png](ANS_ANS_image_8.png_904cd3.png_706d46.png)

	3. **Diffusion** (especially for exogenous catecholamines)
		* Metabolized by liver and kidney
		* <5% of released NE appears unchanged in urine
		* 80-90% converted to vanillylmandelic acid (diagnostic for pheochromocytoma)

**Bottom Line:** Catecholamines are synthesized from tyrosine through a multi-step pathway, with tyrosine hydroxylase as the rate-limiting enzyme. Their inactivation primarily occurs through reuptake into presynaptic terminals, with additional metabolism by MAO and COMT. Drugs that inhibit reuptake (TCAs, cocaine) can potentiate sympathetic effects.

# 3. AUTONOMIC RECEPTORS

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Autonomic Nervous System|Part A: Autonomic Receptors]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#2.1 Clinical Decision-Making and Procedures|Part C]]

## 3.2. CHOLINERGIC RECEPTORS

**Cholinergic receptors respond to acetylcholine and are classified as either muscarinic or nicotinic based on their pharmacological response.**

* **Classes**: Based on location and drug affinity
* **Subdivisions**:
	1. **Muscarinic Receptors**:
		* **Location**: Postganglionic PNS junctions of cardiac/smooth muscle
		* **Effects**: Bradycardia, ↓inotropism, bronchoconstriction, miosis, salivation, ↑GI motility
		* **Blockade**: By atropine (no effect on nicotinic receptors)
		* **Special locations**: Presynaptic membrane of sympathetic nerve terminals
			* When stimulated → inhibits NE release (similar to α₂ receptors)
			* Muscarinic blockade → ↑ NE release → sympathomimetic activity
	2. **Nicotinic Receptors**:
		* **Location**: Synaptic junctions of SNS/PNS ganglia and skeletal muscle
		* **Effects**: Dose-dependent
			* Low doses → ANS ganglia stimulation → ↑BP, tachycardia
			* High doses → blockade → hypotension, neuromuscular weakness

### 3.2.1. NICOTINIC RECEPTORS

* **Structure and Function**:
	* Directly linked to ion channel (ionotropic)
	* Found in postsynaptic membrane of neuromuscular junction (NMJ), autonomic ganglia, and brain
	* Named because nicotine acts as an agonist
* **Receptor Subtypes**:
	* **NMJ receptors**: Fetal (α2βγδ), Adult (α2βεδ)
	* **Ganglionic receptors**: α2β3
	* Different subunit isoforms result in different drug responses

### 3.2.2. MUSCARINIC RECEPTORS

* **Structure and Function**:
	* G protein-coupled receptors (metabotropic)
	* Act through intracellular second messenger systems
	* Named because muscarine (fungal alkaloid) also acts on these receptors
* **Receptor Subtypes**:
	* **M1 receptors**: Found in secretory glands (e.g., salivary glands) and CNS
	* **M2 receptors**: Found in the heart
	* **M3 receptors**: Found in smooth muscle of bronchioles and arterioles
	* **M4 and M5 receptors**: Found in the CNS

**Bottom Line:** Cholinergic receptors include muscarinic (found on effector organs and sympathetic nerve terminals) and nicotinic (found in autonomic ganglia and at the neuromuscular junction) types. Muscarinic effects are blocked by atropine, while both muscarinic and nicotinic receptors respond to acetylcholine.

## 3.3. ADRENERGIC RECEPTORS

**Adrenergic receptors respond to catecholamines and are classified into α, β, and dopaminergic subtypes with distinct physiological effects.**

* **Major Classifications**:
	1. **β-receptors**:
		* β₁, β₂, β₃
	2. **α-receptors**:
		* α₁ (subtypes: α₁ₐ, α₁ᵦ, α₁ᵈ)
		* α₂ (subtypes: α₂ₐ, α₂ᵦ, α₂ᶜ)
	3. **Dopamine (DA) receptors**:
		* DA₁, DA₂
* **Adrenoceptors**:
	* G protein-coupled receptors whose ligands are catecholamines
	* Metabotropic, exerting effects through intracellular second messenger systems

### 3.3.1. Α-ADRENERGIC RECEPTORS IN THE CVS

* **Coronary arteries**:
	* **Small resistance vessels**: Primarily postsynaptic α₂ with some α₁ → coronary vasoconstriction
	* **Larger epicardial arteries**: Primarily postsynaptic α₁ (minimal effect on resistance)
	* **Effects of disease**: Myocardial ischemia → ↑α₂ receptor density → ↑coronary constriction
	* **Calcium dependence**: α₁ effects don't rely on extracellular Ca²⁺; α₂ effects highly Ca²⁺-dependent
	* **Aging**: Associated with upregulation of adrenergic response → ↑risk of arrhythmias
* **Myocardium**:
	* **Contractility**: α₁ stimulation → moderate inotropic effect (2-3× vs. 6-7× with β-agonists)
	* **Basal tone**: α₁ receptors mediate 30-50% of basal inotropic tone
	* **Heart failure**: Downregulation of β₁ but not α₁ or β₂ → relative increase in α₁ density
		* May contribute to arrhythmias during reperfusion (via Ca²⁺ mobilization)
		* α₁ antagonists have antiarrhythmic activity but limited by hypotension
* **Peripheral vessels**:
	* **Presynaptic α₂**: Inhibit NE release → vasodilation (e.g., clonidine)
	* **Postsynaptic α₁ and α₂**: Vasoconstriction
	* **Distribution**: Arterial primarily α₁; Venous both α₁ and α₂ (relatively more α₂)
	* **Venoconstriction**: NE is most potent venoconstrictor (↑preload by shifting venous capacitance)
	* **Arterial constriction**: ↑afterload by increasing resistance

### 3.3.2. Α-ADRENERGIC RECEPTORS IN THE CNS

* α, β, and DA receptors present
* Circulating catecholamines don't cross BBB
* Central neuraxial α₂ agonists (e.g., clonidine) → analgesia, sedation, cardiovascular depression
* Enhance duration of epidural/intrathecal anesthesia

### 3.3.3. Α-ADRENERGIC RECEPTORS IN THE KIDNEYS

* **Distribution density**: Thick ascending limb > Distal tubules > Proximal tubules
* **Receptor types**: Both α₁ and α₂ (α₂ predominant)
* **Effects**:
	* **α₁ receptors**: Renal vasoconstriction (modulates blood flow)
	Enhance Na⁺ and water reabsorption (antinatriuresis)
	* **α₂ receptors**: Promote Na⁺ and water excretion

### 3.3.4. Β-RECEPTORS IN THE CVS

* **Myocardium**:
	* Contains both β₁ and β₂ receptors
	* **β₁ receptors**: Predominantly postsynaptic in myocardium, SA node, ventricular conduction
	* **β₂ receptors**: Same distribution but primarily presynaptic
		* Comprise 20-30% of ventricular and up to 40% of atrial β receptors
		* Presynaptic β₂ → accelerates NE release
		* May mediate chronotropic responses to EPI
* **Inotropic effects**:
	* NE: Entirely through postsynaptic β₁
	* EPI: Through both postsynaptic β₁ and β₂
* **Peripheral vessels**:
	* Virtually all β₂ → vasodilation and bronchial relaxation
	* β-blockade → modest vasoconstriction (unopposed α₁/α₂ effects)

### 3.3.5. Β-RECEPTORS IN THE KIDNEY

* Contains both β₁ and β₂ (β₁ predominant)
* **β₁ receptors**: Evoke renin release
* **β₂ receptors**: Regulate renal blood flow (vasodilation)

**Bottom Line:** Adrenergic receptors include α1 (vasoconstriction, modest positive inotropy), α2 (presynaptic inhibition of NE release, postsynaptic vasoconstriction), β1 (positive inotropy, chronotropy), and β2 (vasodilation, bronchodilation). Their distribution and density vary by organ system and are altered in disease states like heart failure.

## 3.4. DOPAMINERGIC RECEPTORS

**Dopaminergic receptors mediate unique cardiovascular, renal, and central nervous system effects distinct from other adrenergic receptors.**

### 3.4.1. DOPAMINERGIC RECEPTORS IN THE CVS

* **Myocardium**:
	* No DA receptors described in myocardium
	* Dopamine effects via α and β receptors:
		* β₁ → inotropism and chronotropism
		* β₂ → systemic vasodilation
* **Peripheral vessels**:
	* **D₁ postsynaptic receptors**: On vascular smooth muscle of kidney, mesentery, coronary, cerebral, cutaneous arteries
		* Linked to adenylate cyclase → smooth muscle relaxation → vasodilation
	* **D₂ presynaptic receptors**: Inhibit NE release → vasodilation
	* **High-dose effects**: Activate postsynaptic α₁ and α₂ → vasoconstriction (relatively weak)
		* 1/35 potency of EPI and 1/50 potency of NE on adrenergic receptors

### 3.4.2. DOPAMINERGIC RECEPTORS IN THE CNS

* **Hypothalamus**: Control prolactin release
* **Basal ganglia**: Coordinate motor function
* **Substantia nigra**: Degeneration causes Parkinson's disease
* **Chemoreceptor trigger zone**: Mediate nausea/vomiting
	* Blocked by DA antagonists (haloperidol, droperidol)

### 3.4.3. DOPAMINERGIC RECEPTORS IN KIDNEY AND MESENTERY

* **GI tract**: On smooth muscle of esophagus, stomach, small intestine
	* Effects: ↑secretion, ↓intestinal motility
	* Metoclopramide (DA antagonist) promotes gastric emptying
* **Kidney**:
	* **D₁ on renal tubule**: Inhibit Na⁺ reabsorption → natriuresis and diuresis
	* **D₁ on juxtaglomerular cells**: ↑renin release
	* Natural antihypertensive mechanism (opposed by NE)
	* Age-related changes: ↓dopaminergic activity, ↑plasma NE → reduced salt excretion by aging kidney

**Bottom Line:** Dopaminergic receptors include D1 (vasodilation, natriuresis) and D2 (presynaptic inhibition of NE release, control of prolactin, nausea/vomiting). Dopamine itself has dose-dependent effects, acting on dopaminergic receptors at low doses and on adrenergic receptors at higher doses.

## 3.5. AUTONOMIC RECEPTOR SUMMARY

| Receptor | Primary Location | Key Effects | Clinical Relevance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **α1** | Postsynaptic vascular smooth muscle, myocardium | Vasoconstriction, modest ↑ contractility | Phenylephrine effects, maintains 30-50% of basal inotropic tone |
| **α2** | Presynaptic nerve terminals, postsynaptic in veins | Inhibit NE release, venoconstriction | Clonidine effects, venoconstriction more potent than arterial |
| **β1** | Myocardium, SA/AV node | ↑ HR, ↑ contractility, ↑ conduction | Downregulated in heart failure |
| **β2** | Bronchial smooth muscle, vascular smooth muscle | Bronchodilation, vasodilation | Presynaptic location accelerates NE release |
| **Muscarinic** | PNS target organs, presynaptic on SNS terminals | Organ-specific effects, inhibit NE release | Atropine blockade enhances sympathetic effects |
| **Nicotinic** | ANS ganglia, NMJ | Stimulate postganglionic neurons, muscle contraction | D-tubocurarine blocks both NMJ and ganglia |
| **D1** | Renal and mesenteric vasculature | Vasodilation, ↓ Na⁺ reabsorption | Dopamine effects at low doses |
| **D2** | Presynaptic terminals | Inhibit NE release | Contributes to dopamine's complex effects |

**Bottom Line:** Autonomic receptors show distinctive patterns of distribution and effects, with important clinical implications for drug selection and anticipating hemodynamic responses in various physiological and pathological states.

# 4. AUTONOMIC REFLEXES AND INTERACTIONS



## 4.2. ARTERIAL BARORECEPTORS

**Arterial baroreceptors are stretch receptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch that buffer changes in blood pressure.**

* **Function**: Sample afterload
* **Location**: Aortic arch (CN9) and carotid sinuses (CN10)
* **Mechanism**: BP rise → stretch → afferent impulses → medullary vasomotor center → ↓SNS, ↑vagal tone → vasodilation, bradycardia, ↓BP

### 4.2.1. VALSALVA MANEUVER

* **Valsalva Maneuver**:
	* Forced expiration against closed glottis → ↑intrathoracic pressure
	* **Phase 1**: Blood forced into heart → ↑preload → ↑BP
	* **Phase 2**: Sustained pressure → ↓venous return → ↓CO → ↓BP → reflex vasoconstriction and tachycardia
	* **Phase 3**: Release → normalization of BP
	* **Phase 4**: Brief BP "overshoot" due to vasoconstriction and ↑venous return → followed by bradycardia
	* **Clinical use**: Identifies patients at risk for ANS instability during anesthesia
* **Signs of dysfunctional Valsalva**:
	* **SNS dysfunction**: Exaggerated/prolonged hypotension during forced expiration (>50% drop in MAP); absent overshoot
	* **PNS dysfunction**: HR doesn't respond appropriately to BP changes

![image.png](ANS_ANS_image_9.png_22b47c.png_2b4d80.png)

**Bottom Line:** Arterial baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch respond to increased blood pressure by inducing vasodilation and bradycardia. The Valsalva maneuver is a clinical test that assesses autonomic function through predictable hemodynamic phases.

## 4.3. VENOUS BARORECEPTORS

**Venous baroreceptors sense changes in central venous pressure and modulate heart rate and venous tone.**

### 4.3.1. BAINBRIDGE REFLEX

* **Function**: Sample preload
* **Bainbridge reflex**:
	* Location: Right atrium and great veins
	* Effect: Stretch (↑pressure) → ↑HR (opposite of arterial baroreceptors)
	* Does not alter vascular tone (but venoconstriction may occur when atrial pressures decline)

### 4.3.2. SPINAL ANESTHESIA PARADOX

* **Spinal anesthesia paradox**:
	* Primary effect: ↓vascular resistance (small artery vasodilation with modest venodilation)
	* Expected reflex: Tachycardia, but bradycardia more common
	* Explanations:
		* Blockade of T1-4 → ablates cardiac accelerator nerves → unopposed vagus → bradycardia
		* Bezold-Jarisch reflex activation (not correlated with block height)

### 4.3.3. BEZOLD-JARISCH REFLEX

* **Bezold-Jarisch reflex**:
	* Origin: Inhibitory mechanoreceptors in LV (particularly inferoposterior wall)
	* Stimulation by stretch (poorly filled ventricle) → ↑parasympathetic activity, ↓sympathetic activity
	* Effects: Reflex bradycardia, vasodilation, hypotension

**Bottom Line:** Venous baroreceptors in the right atrium increase heart rate when stimulated (Bainbridge reflex), while the Bezold-Jarisch reflex (triggered by LV mechanoreceptors) can cause paradoxical bradycardia during conditions like hypovolemia or spinal anesthesia.

## 4.4. DENERVATED HEART

**The transplanted heart exhibits unique physiological responses due to its denervation.**

* **Transplanted heart**: Retains recipient's innervated SA node and donor's denervated SA node
* **Response patterns**:
	* **NE**: ↑BP → vagal feedback → slowing of recipient's atrial rate but donor rate increases (no feedback)
	* **Isoproterenol** (pure β agonist): Increases both recipient and donor node rates by direct action
	* **Atropine**: Accelerates recipient's rate with no effect on donor rate
	* **β-Blockade**: Comparable slowing of both recipient and donor SA nodes
	* **Long-term**: Limited cardiac reinnervation may occur (predominantly sympathetic)

**Bottom Line:** The denervated heart in transplant patients lacks autonomic neural control, making it dependent on circulating catecholamines and direct-acting agents. It responds differently to drugs like atropine (no effect) compared to direct β-agonists (normal response).

## 4.5. INTERACTION BETWEEN ANS RECEPTORS

**Complex interactions between sympathetic and parasympathetic systems enhance physiological control and provide functional redundancy.**

* Organs with dual antagonistic innervation show strong SNS-PNS interactions
* **"Accentuated antagonism"**: Vagal inhibition of LV contractility is enhanced at higher SNS activity
* **Case study: Myocardium and coronary vessels**:
	* Both have adrenergic and cholinergic (muscarinic) receptors
	* Muscarinic stimulation → inhibits NE release (similar to α₂ and D₂ receptors)
	* Atropine → augments positive inotropic response to catecholamines
	* This suppression is "accentuated" with strong SNS stimulation
	* ACh may cause coronary spasm during high SNS tone
	* Vagal activity reduces vulnerability to fibrillation during NE infusions

**Bottom Line:** Autonomic receptors interact in complex ways, with "accentuated antagonism" allowing parasympathetic influence to be more pronounced during high sympathetic states. These interactions help fine-tune cardiovascular responses and may provide protective effects against arrhythmias.

## 4.6. INTERACTION WITH OTHER REGULATORY SYSTEMS

**The autonomic nervous system interacts with hormonal and other regulatory systems throughout the body.**

* **Pancreas**:
	* β stimulation → ↑insulin release
	* α stimulation → ↓insulin release
	* β-blockers may contribute to insulin resistance
* **Kidneys**:
	* Renin released in response to:
		* Hyponatremia
		* ↓renal perfusion pressure
		* β receptor stimulation of juxtaglomerular cells
* **Adrenocortical function**:
	* Glucocorticoid release → modulates phenylethanolamine-N-methyltransferase → ↑EPI synthesis

**Bottom Line:** The autonomic nervous system interacts with multiple regulatory systems including pancreatic insulin secretion, the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, and adrenocortical function. These interactions provide integrated control of metabolism, fluid balance, and stress responses.

# 5. AUTONOMIC PHARMACOLOGY



## 5.2. CLASSIFICATION AND MODES OF ACTION

**Autonomic drugs can be classified by their mechanism (agonists vs antagonists) and their site of action.**

* **Classification**:
	* By mechanism: Mimetic or lytic (agonist or antagonist)
		* Sympathomimetic (e.g., ephedrine)
		* Sympatholytic (e.g., β-blockers)
* **Modes of action**:
	* **Prejunctional sites**:
		1. Interfere with transmitter synthesis (α-methyl paratyrosine)
		2. Disrupt transmitter storage (reserpine)
		3. Block transmitter release (clonidine)
		4. Stimulate transmitter release (ephedrine)
		5. Interfere with transmitter reuptake (cocaine)
		6. Modify transmitter metabolism (anticholinesterases)
	* **Postjunctional sites**:
		1. Directly stimulate receptors
		2. Interfere with agonist-receptor interaction
* **Response determinants**:
	1. Drug properties
	2. Plasma concentration
	3. Receptor density
	4. Receptor binding
	5. Concurrent drug/hormone activity
	6. Cellular metabolic status
	7. Reflex adjustments

**Bottom Line:** Autonomic drugs act through diverse mechanisms at both preand post-junctional sites, with responses determined by multiple factors including receptor density, drug concentration, and concurrent autonomic activity.

## 5.3. GANGLIONIC DRUGS

**Ganglionic transmission occurs through nicotinic receptors at both sympathetic and parasympathetic ganglia.**

* **Mechanism**: ANS ganglia (both SNS and PNS) use ACh as neurotransmitter
* **Clinical utility**: Limited due to non-selectivity (affect both SNS and PNS)
* **Ganglionic agonists**:
	* No clinically useful agents
	* Nicotine:
		* Low dose → stimulates ANS ganglia and NMJ
		* High dose → ganglionic blockade and NMB
* **Ganglionic antagonists**:
	* Examples: High-dose nicotine, hexamethonium, trimethaphan, pentolinium
	* Mechanism: Nondepolarizing blockade at ANS ganglia
	* Modern use: Limited (obsolete) as other drugs produce intended effects
	* D-Tubocurarine: Blocks both motor end plates and ANS ganglia → hypotension at higher doses

💡 **Clinical Pearl**: Ganglionic transmission is mediated by nicotinic (not muscarinic) receptors

**Bottom Line:** Ganglionic drugs act at nicotinic receptors in autonomic ganglia, affecting both sympathetic and parasympathetic systems. Their clinical utility is limited by non-selective effects, though some neuromuscular blocking agents like d-tubocurarine can cause hypotension through ganglionic blockade.

## 5.4. CHOLINERGIC DRUGS

**Cholinergic drugs act on muscarinic receptors (effector organs) or nicotinic receptors (ganglia, neuromuscular junction).**

* See [5. CHOLINERGICS, CHOLINESTERASE INHIBITORS AND OTHER NMBA REVERSING AGENTS](https://www.notion.so/5-CHOLINERGICS-CHOLINESTERASE-INHIBITORS-AND-OTHER-NMBA-REVERSING-AGENTS-1cbff1c7cfc280d384f0cc95b87c993a?pvs=21)

**Bottom Line:** Cholinergic drugs can act directly as receptor agonists or indirectly by inhibiting acetylcholinesterase, with effects determined by the receptor subtype distribution in target tissues.

## 5.5. ADRENERGIC DRUGS

**Adrenergic drugs include direct and indirect sympathomimetics, as well as receptor-specific antagonists.**

* See [7. ADRENERGIC AGONISTS AND ANTAGONISTS](https://www.notion.so/7-ADRENERGIC-AGONISTS-AND-ANTAGONISTS-1cbff1c7cfc2800faf20f9705962319a?pvs=21)

**Bottom Line:** Adrenergic drugs include agonists that can directly stimulate receptors or indirectly increase neurotransmitter release, as well as antagonists that block specific receptor subtypes for targeted clinical effects.

# 6. AUTONOMIC SYNDROMES AND DRUG INTERACTIONS



## 6.2. MONOAMINE OXIDASE INHIBITORS (MAOIS)

**MAOIs block the breakdown of endogenous catecholamines and can lead to serious drug interactions.**

* **Mechanism**: Block oxidative deamination of endogenous catecholamines → accumulation of NE, EPI, dopamine, 5HT
* **Interactions**: Exaggerated response to indirect sympathomimetics (ephedrine, tyramine, meperidine)
	* Food interactions: Cheese, red Italian wine, pickled herring can precipitate hypertensive crisis
* **Anesthetic management**: Recommended discontinuation 2 weeks before surgery

⚠️ **Warning:** MAOI interaction with indirect sympathomimetics can trigger potentially fatal hypertensive crisis

**Bottom Line:** MAOIs inhibit the breakdown of catecholamines, creating potentially dangerous interactions with indirect sympathomimetics and certain foods. Traditional recommendation is to discontinue 2 weeks before surgery, though newer, reversible MAOIs may have different safety profiles.

## 6.3. TRICYCLIC ANTIDEPRESSANTS (TCAS)

**TCAs block norepinephrine reuptake and can potentiate sympathomimetic effects and interact with other anesthetic agents.**

* **Mechanism**: Block NE reuptake into adrenergic nerve endings
* **Drug interactions**:
	* Potentiated by P450 2D6 enzyme inhibitors
	* Metabolized faster with chronic barbiturate use
* **Anesthetic considerations**:
	* Ketamine may cause acute HTN and cardiac dysrhythmias
	* Discontinuation before surgery not necessary
	* Onset latency: 2-5 weeks

⚠️ **Warning:** Avoid ketamine in patients taking TCAs due to risk of severe hypertension and arrhythmias

**Bottom Line:** TCAs block norepinephrine reuptake, potentiating sympathetic effects. Unlike MAOIs, they generally do not need to be discontinued before surgery, but ketamine should be avoided due to risk of hypertension and arrhythmias.

## 6.4. SELECTIVE SEROTONIN REUPTAKE INHIBITORS (SSRIS)

**SSRIs selectively inhibit serotonin reuptake with generally favorable perioperative safety profiles.**

* **Mechanism**: Selective inhibition of neuronal serotonin uptake
* **Pharmacokinetics**: Elimination t½ 1-3 days, may accumulate with P450 2D6 inhibitors
* **Clinical considerations**:
	* Availability of sympathetic antagonists favors continuation during anesthesia
	* Special category: Patients with heart disease (SSRIs decrease morbidity/mortality)
	* Small evidence of increased bleeding risk

💡 **Clinical Pearl**: Depression significantly affects cardiovascular health; SSRIs are generally safe to continue perioperatively, especially in elderly cardiac patients

**Bottom Line:** SSRIs have a favorable safety profile and are generally continued perioperatively. They may particularly benefit cardiac patients, though there may be a slight increase in bleeding risk that should be considered in surgical contexts.

# 7. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS IN ANESTHESIA



## 7.2. AUTONOMIC ASSESSMENT

**Preoperative autonomic assessment can identify patients at risk for autonomic instability during anesthesia.**

* **Valsalva maneuver assessment**:
	* Normal response: Four distinct phases with appropriate HR and BP changes
	* Abnormal: Exaggerated hypotension, absent overshoot, inappropriate HR responses
	* Predictive of intraoperative instability
* **Autonomic dysfunction signs**:
	* Orthostatic hypotension (>20 mmHg drop in systolic BP)
	* Inappropriate HR response to positional changes
	* Abnormal sweating patterns
	* Erectile dysfunction

**Bottom Line:** Preoperative autonomic assessment using tests like the Valsalva maneuver can identify patients at risk for hemodynamic instability during anesthesia, allowing for appropriate planning and preparation.

## 7.3. STELLATE GANGLION IN ANESTHETIC PRACTICE

**The stellate ganglion is a key sympathetic structure with important implications in regional anesthesia.**

* **Anatomical considerations**:
	* Formed from fusion of inferior cervical and first thoracic ganglia
	* Located at C7 level, anterior to neck of first rib
	* Present in 75-80% of people
* **Regional anesthesia implications**:
	* Brachial plexus blocks can track to stellate ganglion → Horner's syndrome
	* Signs of stellate ganglion block:
		* Ptosis
		* Miosis
		* Anhidrosis
		* Nasal congestion
		* Enophthalmos
* **Therapeutic stellate ganglion block indications**:
	* Complex regional pain syndrome
	* Vascular insufficiency
	* Refractory angina
	* Post-herpetic neuralgia

**Bottom Line:** The stellate ganglion provides sympathetic innervation to the head, neck, upper extremities, heart, and lungs. Inadvertent blockade during brachial plexus anesthesia can cause Horner's syndrome, while therapeutic blockade can be useful for various pain conditions.

## 7.4. MANAGEMENT OF PATIENTS ON AUTONOMIC MEDICATIONS

**Patients on autonomic medications require special consideration during the perioperative period.**

* **Beta-blockers**:
	* Generally continue perioperatively
	* Abrupt withdrawal can cause rebound hypertension, tachycardia, angina
	* May blunt compensatory responses to blood loss
	* May mask hypoglycemia symptoms
	* ⚠️ **Warning:** Never discontinue beta-blockers abruptly in cardiac patients
* **Alpha-2 agonists (e.g., clonidine)**:
	* Continue perioperatively when possible
	* Abrupt withdrawal can cause rebound hypertension
	* May enhance anesthetic and analgesic effects
	* May cause bradycardia and hypotension
* **Antihypertensives**:
	* ACE inhibitors/ARBs: Consider holding on day of surgery if concern for hypotension
	* Calcium channel blockers: Generally continue
	* Diuretics: Consider holding on day of surgery if concern for hypovolemia

**Bottom Line:** Management of patients on autonomic medications requires careful consideration of the risks of continuing versus discontinuing medications. Beta-blockers and alpha-2 agonists should generally be continued to avoid withdrawal syndromes, while individualized decisions may be needed for antihypertensives.

## 7.5. SYMPATHETIC BLOCKADE PROCEDURES

**Sympathetic blockade is useful in the diagnosis and management of sympathetically mediated pain and other conditions.**

### 7.5.1. LUMBAR SYMPATHETIC BLOCK/SYMPATHECTOMY

* **Indications**:
	* Symptomatic vasospastic disorders
	* Inoperable arterial occlusive disease with pain at rest, limited ulceration, or superficial gangrene
	* Complex regional pain syndromes of the lower extremity
	* Phantom limb and stump pain
	* Herpes zoster and post-herpetic neuralgia in lower limb dermatomes
	* Acute and chronic renal colic
* **Technique**:
	* Usually performed under radiographic guidance (fluoroscopy or CT)
	* Patient in lateral or prone position
	* Needle inserted 10-12 cm lateral to midline at L2/L3 level
	* Advanced to anterolateral border of vertebral body
	* Radioopaque dye injected to confirm proper spread along vertebral column
	* Local anesthetic (bupivacaine with epinephrine) for diagnostic block
	* 10% phenol with contrast for neurolytic block
	* Radiofrequency lesioning at L2-4 levels as alternative

![Lumbar sympathetic block](ANS_pdf_ANS.pdf_p13_img1_8fe5d5.jpeg)

**Bottom Line:** Lumbar sympathetic blockade or sympathectomy can be valuable for diagnosing and treating sympathetically mediated pain in the lower extremities, with techniques ranging from temporary local anesthetic blocks to more permanent neurolytic procedures or radiofrequency lesioning.

### 7.5.2. COELIAC PLEXUS BLOCK

* **Indications**:
	* Acute and chronic pancreatitis
	* Upper gastrointestinal tract malignancy pain
	* Regional anesthesia for intra-abdominal surgery (with intercostal blocks)
* **Physiological effects**:
	* Interruption of pain-conducting sympathetic afferents
	* Visceral sympathetic block causing vasodilation → possible hypotension
		* IV fluids typically given before procedure
* **Technique**:
	* Performed under radiographic guidance (fluoroscopy or CT)
	* Patient prone with IV sedation and local anesthetic infiltration
	* Bilateral injections typically needed
	* Needles inserted ~8-10 cm from midline at L2 level, passing below 12th rib
	* Directed toward L1 vertebral body at ~45° angle to coronal plane, slightly cephalad
	* Left needle positioned just posterior to aorta; right needle slightly anterolateral to aorta
	* Contrast injection confirms proper position
	* Local anesthetic (10 ml per side) for diagnostic block
	* Phenol or 50% alcohol for neurolytic block
	* Alternative: Radiofrequency lesioning of splanchnic nerves
* **Post-procedure considerations**:
	* Temporary postural hypotension possible after bilateral block
	* Splanchnic nerve radiofrequency lesioning as alternative to chemical neurolysis

![Diagram showing a coeliac plexus block](ANS_pdf_ANS.pdf_p16_img1_e85489.jpeg)

**Bottom Line:** Coeliac plexus block interrupts pain transmission from upper abdominal viscera and is particularly useful for pancreatic pain. The procedure requires careful technique to avoid complications, and patients should be monitored for hypotension due to sympathetic blockade.

# 8. 🔑 HIGH-YIELD POINTS

* **Sympathetic outflow**: T1-L3 (thoracolumbar)
* **Parasympathetic outflow**: Cranial nerves III, VII, IX, X and sacral segments S2-S4 (craniosacral)
* **Vagus nerve**: Supplies >75% of parasympathetic innervation
* **Stellate ganglion**: Critical for sympathetic supply to head, neck, upper extremities, heart, lungs
* **Neurotransmitters**:
	* All preganglionic fibers (SNS and PNS): Acetylcholine
	* PNS postganglionic fibers: Acetylcholine
	* SNS postganglionic fibers: Norepinephrine (exception: sweat glands use acetylcholine)
* **Catecholamine synthesis**: Tyrosine → DOPA → Dopamine → Norepinephrine → Epinephrine (rate-limiting step: tyrosine hydroxylase)
* **Bezold-Jarisch reflex**: Key mechanism for bradycardia during spinal anesthesia
* **Mass action effect**: SNS tends to have widespread effects due to 1:4 preganglionic to postganglionic neuron ratio
* **Discrete PNS effects**: PNS has more localized effects due to 1:1 to 3:1 ratio of postganglionic to preganglionic fibers
* **Adrenal medulla**: Special case where preganglionic SNS fibers connect directly to modified postganglionic neurons (chromaffin cells)
* **Coeliac plexus**: Largest sympathetic plexus, surrounds root of coeliac artery at L1 level

# 9. 💡 CLINICAL PEARLS

* **TCA patients**: Avoid ketamine due to risk of hypertension and arrhythmias
* **MAOI patients**: Discontinue 2 weeks before surgery; avoid indirect sympathomimetics
* **SSRI patients**: Generally continue perioperatively; benefits outweigh risks especially in cardiac patients
* **Spinal anesthesia**: Paradoxical bradycardia occurs even with low blocks due to Bezold-Jarisch reflex
* **Valsalva maneuver**: Useful preoperative test for ANS instability risk
* **Heart failure**: Shifting balance from β1 to α1 receptors affects drug responses
* **Ganglionic transmission**: Mediated by nicotinic (not muscarinic) receptors
* **Stellate ganglion block**: Watch for Horner's syndrome (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis)
* **Lumbar sympathetic block**: Useful for complex regional pain syndrome and ischemic pain in lower extremities
* **Atropine in transplanted hearts**: No effect on donor heart rate due to denervation

# 10. ⚠️ CLINICAL WARNINGS

* **MAOI + indirect sympathomimetics**: Can trigger hypertensive crisis (includes certain foods like aged cheese)
* **Aging**: Associated with upregulation of adrenergic response → increased arrhythmia risk
* **Myocardial ischemia**: Increases α2 receptor density → enhanced coronary vasoconstriction
* **Denervated heart** (transplant): Responds differently to autonomic drugs; direct-acting agents preferred
* **Beta-blocker withdrawal**: Can cause rebound hypertension, tachycardia, and angina
* **Alpha-2 agonist withdrawal**: May cause rebound hypertension; continue perioperatively
* **Coeliac plexus block**: Watch for hypotension due to visceral sympathetic blockade
* **Sympathetic blocks**: Confirm needle position with contrast before injecting local anesthetic/neurolytic agents
* **D-tubocurarine**: Can cause hypotension at higher doses through ganglionic blockade
* **Spinal cord injury**: Loss of higher inhibitory centers leads to autonomic hyperreflexia in high spinal lesions
