# 1. ANTICONVULSANTS AND HYPNOTICS









## 1.1. MECHANISMS OF ACTION OF ANTICONVULSANTS

**Medications used to prevent or treat seizures by suppressing inappropriate neuronal discharge in the CNS.**

Anticonvulsants can be classified based on their mechanism of action:

### 1.1.1. SUPPRESSION OF EXCITATORY NEURONAL ACTIVITY

* **Sodium channel blockers** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Block inactive fast sodium channels to reduce action potential propagation:
	* Phenytoin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Carbamazepine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Valproate [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Stabilize presynaptic sodium channels, preventing neurotransmitter release:
	* Lamotrigine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Calcium channel modulators** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Ethosuximide - used specifically for absence seizures [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Gabapentin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Pregabalin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Glutamate antagonists** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Valproate - thought to inhibit NMDA receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Topiramate - inhibits AMPA receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 1.1.2. ENHANCEMENT OF INHIBITORY NEURONAL ACTIVITY

* **GABA receptor facilitators** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Benzodiazepines and barbiturates [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Allow chloride ions in and hyperpolarize the cell [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Benzodiazepines bind to benzodiazepine binding sites on GABA₁ receptors, increasing the receptor's affinity for GABA [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Barbiturates prolong the duration of chloride channel opening [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **GABA agonists** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Baclofen - used primarily for muscle spasticity rather than as an anticonvulsant [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **GABA transaminase inhibitors** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Sodium valproate [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Vigabatrin - irreversible inhibition of GABA-transaminase [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **GABA reuptake inhibitors** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Tiagabine - prevents reuptake of GABA [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 1.1.3. MISCELLANEOUS MECHANISMS

* **Levetiracetam (Keppra)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Binds synaptic vesicle protein SV2 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Inhibits pre-synaptic calcium channels [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Complete mechanism remains unclear [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

![Anticonvulsants.png](LESS_COMMON_LESS_COMMON_Anticonvulsants.png_952a57.png_7f749d.png)

**Bottom Line:** Anticonvulsants work through multiple mechanisms including sodium channel blockade, calcium channel modulation, glutamate antagonism, and GABA enhancement, with each drug having a specific profile that determines its clinical applications.

## 1.2. PHENYTOIN








### 1.2.1. CLINICAL USES

* Complex and partial seizures [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Trigeminal neuralgia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Status epilepticus [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Digoxin toxicity [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Ventricular arrhythmias following TCA overdose [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 1.2.2. PRESENTATION AND DOSING

* Available in IV or oral forms [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* ⚠️ **Must not be given intramuscularly** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Loading dose for status epilepticus: 15-20 mg/kg IV [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Target plasma levels: 10-20 μg/ml [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* ⚠️ Incompatible with 5% dextrose, in which it becomes gelatinous [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 1.2.3. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* **Voltage-dependent blockade** of fast voltage-gated sodium channels [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Stabilizes the channel in the **inactive state**, stabilizing the neuronal membrane [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Binds during refractory period, affecting high-frequency neuronal firing (seizure activity) more than physiological activity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Also exhibits Type IB anti-arrhythmic activity (used for digoxin toxicity) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* May also reduce calcium entry into neurons, blocking transmitter release and enhancing GABA effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 1.2.4. ADVERSE EFFECTS

* **Neurological** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Confusion and headache
  * Tremor and ataxia
  * Blurred vision
  * Peripheral ataxia
  * Slurred speech
  * Nystagmus [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Vertigo [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Paresthesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Cardiovascular** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Class IB antiarrhythmic effects
  * Hypotension
  * Heart block
  * ⚠️ Ventricular fibrillation if administered too rapidly [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Hematological** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Blood dyscrasias
  * Folate-dependent megaloblastic anemia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Aplastic anemia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Teratogenic effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Craniofacial abnormalities
  * Limb defects
  * Cardiac anomalies
  * Learning difficulties
  * Growth retardation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Idiosyncratic reactions** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Acne
  * Coarsening of facial features
  * Hirsutism
  * Gum hyperplasia
  * Various skin rashes
  * Peripheral neuropathy

### 1.2.5. PHARMACOKINETICS

* **Absorption** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * 90% bioavailability when taken orally
  * ⚠️ Cannot be mixed with 5% dextrose [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Distribution** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Highly protein bound (90%)
  * Volume of distribution: 0.75 L/kg
* **Metabolism** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Mixed-order non-linear elimination kinetics
  * Initially first-order kinetics but quickly becomes zero-order (narrow therapeutic window)
  * Hydroxylation in the liver to inactive metabolites
  * Saturable hepatic metabolism resulting in zero-order kinetics just above the therapeutic range [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Elimination** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Mostly excreted in bile as inactive metabolites
  * 1-5% eliminated unchanged via renal excretion
  * 💡 Clearance is non-linear: below 10 μg/ml follows first-order kinetics, above which trends toward zero-order kinetics

### 1.2.6. DRUG INTERACTIONS

* Induces hepatic enzymes responsible for metabolism of [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Warfarin
  * Benzodiazepines
  * Combined oral contraceptive pill (COCP)
* Metabolism inhibited by [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Metronidazole
  * Chloramphenicol
  * Isoniazid
* Can induce its own metabolism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Its metabolism may be induced by carbamazepine or alcohol resulting in reduced plasma levels [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

**Bottom Line:** Phenytoin is a voltage-dependent sodium channel blocker used for seizures and as an antiarrhythmic, with significant non-linear pharmacokinetics requiring careful dosing and monitoring of levels due to its narrow therapeutic window and multiple drug interactions. Its wide range of side effects includes significant teratogenicity, making it problematic for use in pregnancy.

## 1.3. VALPROATE








### 1.3.1. CLINICAL USES

* Partial seizures [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Myoclonic epilepsy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Trigeminal neuralgia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Absence (petit mal) seizures [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 1.3.2. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* Not completely characterized but appears to [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Stabilize voltage-gated fast sodium channels (similar to phenytoin) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Inhibit NMDA glutamate receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Potentiate GABA via inhibition of GABA transaminase [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Stimulate central GABA-ergic inhibitory pathways [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 1.3.3. PHARMACOKINETICS

* **Absorption** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Well absorbed orally and well tolerated
* **Distribution** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * 90% protein bound
* **Metabolism** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Hepatic metabolism to products (some of which are active) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Excretion** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Renally excreted

### 1.3.4. SIDE EFFECTS

* Nausea and vomiting [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Gastric irritation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Thrombocytopenia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Reduced platelet aggregation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Transient hair loss [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* ⚠️ Neural tube defects (teratogenic) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* ⚠️ Potentially fatal hepatotoxicity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Pancreatitis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

**Bottom Line:** Valproate is a versatile antiepileptic with multiple mechanisms of action including sodium channel stabilization, NMDA inhibition, and GABA potentiation. Its use requires monitoring for adverse effects including thrombocytopenia and is contraindicated in pregnancy due to neural tube defects. It also carries risk of serious hepatotoxicity and pancreatitis.

## 1.4. CARBAMAZEPINE










### 1.4.1. CLINICAL USES

* Trigeminal neuralgia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Partial and generalized seizures [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 1.4.2. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* Similar to phenytoin - binds to and stabilizes inactivated sodium channels [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Prevents the further generation of action potentials central to seizure activity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 1.4.3. EFFECTS AND SIDE EFFECTS

* **Central nervous system** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Mild neurotoxic effects
  * Headache
  * Diplopia
  * Ataxia
  * Vomiting
  * Drowsiness
* **Metabolic** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Antidiuretic effect leading to water retention
* **Miscellaneous** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Drug-induced hepatitis
  * Rashes in 5-10% of patients
  * Rarely agranulocytosis
* **Teratogenicity** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Facial abnormalities
  * Intrauterine growth retardation
  * Microcephaly
  * Mental retardation
  * Incidence increases with dose (approximately 1/300-2000 live births)

### 1.4.4. KINETICS

* Well absorbed from the gut with high oral bioavailability [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Approximately 75% plasma protein-bound [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Undergoes extensive hepatic metabolism to carbamazepine 10,11-epoxide, which retains about 30% of carbamazepine's anticonvulsant properties [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Powerfully induces hepatic enzymes and induces its own metabolism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Excretion is almost entirely in the urine as unconjugated metabolites [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 1.4.5. DRUG INTERACTIONS

* Induces hepatic enzymes, demonstrating many of the interactions seen with phenytoin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Levels of concurrently administered phenytoin may be elevated or reduced [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Erythromycin can increase serum levels of carbamazepine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

**Bottom Line:** Carbamazepine works similarly to phenytoin but has a somewhat different side effect profile. It is effective for trigeminal neuralgia as well as seizures. Like other anticonvulsants, it has significant teratogenic effects and causes significant enzyme induction, leading to numerous drug interactions.

## 1.5. NEWER ANTICONVULSANTS









### 1.5.1. LAMOTRIGINE

* Used in a range of seizure types in adults and children (2+ years) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Also used in bipolar disorder and as a mood stabilizer [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Mechanism**: Inactivation of voltage-sensitive sodium channels on neurons that produce glutamate and aspartate [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Can cause hepatotoxicity, pancreatitis, and nausea
  * ⚠️ Associated with life-threatening skin reactions
  * Rare cases of neuroleptic malignant syndrome
* **Kinetics** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Half-life of 14 hours
  * Oral bioavailability of 98%
  * Conjugated to an inactive metabolite

### 1.5.2. GABAPENTIN









**Structural GABA analog primarily used for neuropathic pain and epilepsy with multiple potential mechanisms of action.**

#### 1.5.2.1. CLINICAL USES

* Chronic neuropathic pain [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Trigeminal neuralgia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Herpetic neuralgia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Focal seizures at a dose of 900-1200 mg/day [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Used as premedication to reduce postoperative pain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

#### 1.5.2.2. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* Structural analog of GABA but doesn't directly target GABA receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Targets voltage-gated calcium channels at the presynaptic α2δ subunit [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Multiple other potential mechanisms (not fully elucidated) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * May increase GABA production in the brain
  * May inhibit glutamate
  * May increase serotonin
  * May inhibit voltage-gated sodium channels
* May also have NMDA antagonist activity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

#### 1.5.2.3. SIDE EFFECTS

* Sedation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Dizziness [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Peripheral edema [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* ⚠️ Infections are relatively common while on gabapentin [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Range of psychiatric symptoms [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Ataxia, fatigue, and fever [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

#### 1.5.2.4. PHARMACOKINETICS

* Oral bioavailability of about 60% which decreases with increasing doses [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Not bound to plasma proteins [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Not metabolized, which simplifies use with other anticonvulsants [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Eliminated solely by the kidneys [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Elimination half-life of 6 hours [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

**Bottom Line:** Gabapentin is primarily used for neuropathic pain conditions despite being a GABA analog, working mainly through calcium channel modulation with additional possible mechanisms. Its side effect profile includes sedation and increased infection risk, but its pharmacokinetic profile is advantageous due to lack of metabolism and plasma protein binding.

### 1.5.3. VIGABATRIN

* Used as an adjunct with other anti-epileptic agents for complex partial seizures at a maximum dose of 1.5 g twice daily [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Mechanism**: Irreversible inhibition of GABA-transaminase, the enzyme responsible for the breakdown of GABA [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Duration of action is 24 hours [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Kinetics** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Excreted unchanged in the urine
  * Interacts with phenytoin by an unknown mechanism, reducing its concentration by up to 25%
  * Elimination half-life of 6 hours

### 1.5.4. PREGABALIN

* Used as an adjunct for partial seizures in adults and in anxiety [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Specifically designed to be a successor to gabapentin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Mechanism**: Binds to the α2δ subunit of voltage-gated calcium channels in cortical neurons (similar to gabapentin) but does not appear to alter synaptic GABA concentration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Commonly causes dizziness and drowsiness
  * Weight gain, confusion, ataxia, GI upset
  * Erectile dysfunction
* **Kinetics** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Rapidly absorbed with 90% oral bioavailability (unaffected by dose but delayed by food)
  * Not bound to plasma proteins
  * Not metabolized
  * Eliminated in the urine

**Bottom Line:** Newer anticonvulsants offer alternative treatment options with different mechanisms of action and side effect profiles. Lamotrigine works on sodium channels, while gabapentin, pregabalin, and vigabatrin affect GABA pathways through various mechanisms. Each has specific advantages and limitations that determine their clinical applications.

## 1.6. ANTICONVULSANTS IN PREGNANCY










### 1.6.1. RISKS OF ANTICONVULSANTS IN PREGNANCY

* Most anticonvulsants carry risks of [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Neural tube defects
  * Coagulation disorders in the newborn
  * Teratogenicity
* ⚠️ The greater risk may be from uncontrolled seizures [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

### 1.6.2. MANAGEMENT CONSIDERATIONS

* Mother must be counseled regarding risks and benefits [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
* Screening for fetal abnormalities recommended [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Consider supplementation with [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Folate - reduces neural tube defect risk
  * Vitamin K - reduces risk of coagulation disorders
* 💡 Lamotrigine is considered relatively safer in pregnancy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Anticonvulsant use in pregnancy requires careful consideration of risks vs. benefits, with appropriate counseling, screening, and supplementation. Uncontrolled seizures may pose greater risk to mother and fetus than medication side effects.

## 1.7. MANAGEMENT OF STATUS EPILEPTICUS










### 1.7.1. DEFINITION

* Continued seizure activity for more than 5 minutes or recurring without regaining consciousness [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* ⚠️ Previously defined as 30 minutes but updated to 5 minutes for earlier intervention [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

### 1.7.2. GENERAL MANAGEMENT

Based on NICE guidelines 2004 for status epilepticus [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:

#### 1.7.2.1. STAGE 1 (0-10 MINUTES)

* Secure airway and resuscitate [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Administer oxygen [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Assess cardiorespiratory function [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Establish intravenous access [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

#### 1.7.2.2. STAGE 2 (0-30 MINUTES)

* Institute regular monitoring [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Consider possibility of non-epileptic status [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Emergency anti-epileptic drug (AED) therapy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Emergency investigations [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Administer glucose (50 ml of 50% solution) if hypoglycemia suspected [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Consider IV thiamine (250 mg as Pabrinex) if alcohol abuse or impaired nutrition suspected [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Treat severe acidosis if present [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

#### 1.7.2.3. STAGE 3 (0-60 MINUTES - ESTABLISHED STATUS)

* Establish etiology [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Alert anesthetist and intensive care unit [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Identify and treat medical complications [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Consider pressor therapy when appropriate [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

#### 1.7.2.4. STAGE 4 (30-90 MINUTES - REFRACTORY STATUS)

* Transfer to intensive care [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Establish intensive care and EEG monitoring [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Initiate intracranial pressure monitoring where appropriate [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Initiate long-term maintenance AED therapy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

### 1.7.3. SPECIFIC PHARMACOLOGICAL MANAGEMENT

#### 1.7.3.1. PREMONITORY STAGE (PRE-HOSPITAL)

* Diazepam 10-20 mg rectally, may repeat once after 15 minutes if status continues [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* OR Midazolam 10 mg buccally [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* If seizures continue, treat as early status [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

#### 1.7.3.2. EARLY STATUS

* Lorazepam (IV) 0.1 mg/kg (usually 4 mg bolus, may repeat once after 10-20 minutes) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Continue usual AED medication if patient already on treatment [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* For sustained control or if seizures continue, treat as established status [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

#### 1.7.3.3. ESTABLISHED STATUS

* Phenytoin infusion 15-18 mg/kg at 50 mg/minute [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* OR Fosphenytoin infusion 15-20 mg phenytoin equivalents (PE)/kg at 50-100 mg PE/minute [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* AND/OR Phenobarbital bolus 10-15 mg/kg at 100 mg/minute [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

#### 1.7.3.4. REFRACTORY STATUS

* General anesthesia with one of [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Propofol (1-2 mg/kg bolus, then 2-10 mg/kg/hour) titrated to effect [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Midazolam (0.1-0.2 mg/kg bolus, then 0.05-0.5 mg/kg/hour) titrated to effect [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Thiopental sodium (3-5 mg/kg bolus, then 3-5 mg/kg/hour) titrated to effect [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
	* 💡 After 2-3 days, infusion rate needs reduction as fat stores become saturated [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Anesthetic should be continued for 12-24 hours after the last clinical or electrographic seizure, then dose tapered [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Status epilepticus is a neurological emergency requiring a staged approach to management, progressing from benzodiazepines to phenytoin/fosphenytoin and ultimately to general anesthesia for refractory cases, with close monitoring and supportive care throughout.

## 1.9. ANESTHETIC CONSIDERATIONS FOR EPILEPTIC PATIENTS










### 1.9.1. PREOPERATIVE CONSIDERATIONS

* In addition to usual anesthetic assessment [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Assess frequency, nature, and timing of seizures [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Detailed drug history including timing and dosing [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * 💡 Continue all anticonvulsants up until surgery [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Consider regional anesthesia if possible [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

### 1.9.2. INTRAOPERATIVE CONSIDERATIONS

* ⚠️ Avoid hyperventilation as hypocapnia can reduce seizure threshold [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* All induction agents have anticonvulsant properties [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * 💡 Thiopentone particularly powerful as an anticonvulsant and should be considered if available [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Consider using atracurium as common anticonvulsants that induce hepatic enzymes will cause rapid metabolism of aminosteroid muscle relaxants (rocuronium and vecuronium) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* For antiemetics [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Ondansetron and cyclizine are safe [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Avoid metoclopramide, droperidol, and prochlorperazine [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

### 1.9.3. POSTOPERATIVE CONSIDERATIONS

* Resume oral intake of usual anticonvulsants early [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Use parenteral forms if needed [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

### 1.9.4. PROPOFOL USE IN EPILEPTIC PATIENTS

* Can cause abnormal movements and hiccups but doesn't cause seizures [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Rapid emergence may trigger seizures in epileptic patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Thiopentone may be better if dealing with poorly controlled epilepsy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* 💡 Coadministration of propofol with benzodiazepine may reduce postoperative seizures and abnormal movements [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Anesthetic management of epileptic patients requires continuation of anticonvulsants, careful drug selection, avoidance of triggers like hyperventilation, and prompt resumption of antiepileptic medications postoperatively to minimize seizure risk throughout the perioperative period.

# 2. BENZODIAZEPINES









## 2.1. GABA PHYSIOLOGY

* **GABA** (γ-Aminobutyric acid) is the main inhibitory neurotransmitter in the CNS [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Acts via two different receptor subtypes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * **GABAA**:
	* Ligand-gated Clion channel
	* Consists of five subunits (2α, β, δ, and γ) arranged to form a central ion channel
	* GABA binding increases opening frequency of Clchannel, hyperpolarizing the neuronal membrane
	* Primarily postsynaptic and widely distributed throughout CNS
  * **GABAB**:
	* Metabotropic (acts via G-protein and second messengers)
	* Increases K+ conductance, hyperpolarizing neuronal membrane
	* Located both presynaptically and postsynaptically
* **Benzodiazepine Receptor Subtypes** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * **BZ1**: Found in spinal cord and cerebellum - responsible for anxiolysis
  * **BZ2**: Found in spinal cord, hippocampus, cerebral cortex - responsible for sedative and anticonvulsant activity

## 2.2. TYPES AND PROPERTIES

**A class of medications with anxiolytic, sedative, hypnotic, anticonvulsant, muscle relaxant, and amnestic properties.**

### 2.2.1. MIDAZOLAM

* **Distinctive feature**: pH-dependent ring closure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * At pH 3.5 (formulation) - ring structure is open, making it water-soluble
  * At pH > 4 (physiological) - ring structure closes, making it lipid-soluble
  * pKa is 6.5, so at physiological pH 89% is unionized and can cross lipid membranes
  * Water-soluble formulation causes less pain on injection
* **Pharmacokinetics**:
  * Fast onset and short acting due to redistribution, with elimination T1/2 around 3 hours [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * 40% bioavailability and 95% protein bound [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Metabolized by hydroxylation to active compound 1-α hydroxymidazolam, then conjugated with glucuronic acid [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Shares metabolic enzymes (P450 3A3/4) with alfentanil, prolonging each other's duration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Approximately 5% metabolized to oxazepam [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Clearance: 6-10 ml/kg/min - larger than diazepam and lorazepam, resulting in more rapid offset following infusion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Administration routes**:
  * Intravenous
  * Oral (40% bioavailability)
  * Intranasal or intramuscular (premedication)
* **Uses**:
  * Sedation for minor procedures [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Powerful amnesic properties [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Sedation for ventilated patients in intensive care [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 2.2.2. DIAZEPAM

* **Pharmacokinetics**:
  * **Highly fat soluble** - well absorbed from GI tract and rapidly distributes into CNS [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * 95% protein bound [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Hepatic metabolism to **active metabolites** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
	* Oxazepam [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Temazepam [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Desmethyldiazepam [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Renal excretion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Very long context-sensitive half-life due to accumulation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Lowest clearance of commonly used benzodiazepines (0.2-0.5 ml/kg/min) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Effects and side effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Slight drop in blood pressure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Some respiratory depression [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Reduces MAC (Minimum Alveolar Concentration) requirement for volatile anesthetics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Factors affecting duration**:
  * Liver failure and cimetidine prolong action by reducing metabolism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * More pronounced respiratory depression when administered with opioids or alcohol [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 2.2.3. LORAZEPAM

* Forms **inactive metabolites** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* First-line treatment in status epilepticus [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* 95% protein bound [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Undergoes hepatic metabolism (conjugation with glucuronic acid) with renal excretion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Well absorbed following oral or intramuscular administration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Used for premedication as an anxiolytic and amnesic [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Dose for status epilepticus: 50-100 μg/kg IV, sublingual, or per rectum (1-12 years, max 4 mg; >12 years, 4 mg) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 2.2.4. TEMAZEPAM

* Used as nighttime sedative and anxiolytic premedicant [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Well absorbed in gut [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* 75% protein bound [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Volume of distribution: 0.8 L/kg [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* 80% excreted unchanged in urine while glucuronidation occurs in liver [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Small amount demethylated to oxazepam [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Half-life of about 8 hours which may result in hangover effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 2.2.5. CHLORDIAZEPOXIDE

* Used as oral preparation for alcohol withdrawal [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* First BDZ derivative available for clinical use [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Dose for anxiety: 5 mg tds up to 25 mg qds, titrated to individual needs [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* High oral bioavailability [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Complex metabolic pathway into multiple active metabolites [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Elimination half-life: 5-30 hours, may increase in elderly [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 2.2.6. FLUMAZENIL

* Despite benzodiazepine structure, acts as a **competitive antagonist** at the GABA receptor [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Imidazobenzodiazepine structure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Uses [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Reversal of benzodiazepine sedation at the end of short procedures [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Treatment of benzodiazepine overdose (careful administration as it can precipitate seizures and withdrawal) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Dosing: Given by intravenous injection in 100 μg increments, acts within 2 minutes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Risks [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * ⚠️ Can trigger seizures and withdrawal symptoms [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Short half-life compared to other benzodiazepines (1 hour vs. 3 hours for midazolam), so repeat doses may be needed [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Can increase intracranial pressure if administered rapidly [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Side effects: Nausea, vomiting, anxiety, agitation, seizures (especially in epileptic patients) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Has some agonist activity (ability to precipitate seizures may be from inverse agonist activity) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Pharmacokinetics: 50% plasma protein bound, undergoes significant hepatic metabolism to inactive compounds excreted in urine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

## 2.3. MECHANISM OF ACTION

**Enhance the effect of the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA at the GABA-A receptor.**

* Benzodiazepines bind to the benzodiazepine binding sites on GABA-A receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* They don't directly activate the receptor but increase its affinity for GABA [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* This leads to opening of the chloride ion channel and allowing chloride ions into the cell, hyperpolarizing the cell membrane [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* While the GABA binding site is between the alpha and beta subunits, the **benzodiazepine** binding site is between the **alpha** and **gamma** subunits [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Two main subtypes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * BZ1 - anxiolytic, found in the spinal cord and cerebellum [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * BZ2 - sedative, anticonvulsant, found in the cortex and hippocampus [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

## 2.4. CLINICAL USES

**Wide range of applications in anesthesia and beyond due to diverse effects.**

* Anticonvulsants [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Anxiolytics [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Hypnotics [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Sedation and premedication for anesthesia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

## 2.5. NON-BENZODIAZEPINE HYPNOTICS (Z-DRUGS)

* Zopiclone, zaleplon, and zolpidem act via benzodiazepine receptor but aren't structurally benzodiazepines [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Developed to overcome side effects of benzodiazepines (dependence and next-day sedation) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Elimination half-lives:
  * Zopiclone: ~5 hours
  * Zaleplon: ~1 hour
  * Zolpidem: ~2 hours
* Limited clinical impact as they carry the same side effects as benzodiazepines [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Only recommended when specific intolerance to a specific benzodiazepine exists [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

**Bottom Line:** Benzodiazepines potentiate GABA-mediated inhibition in the CNS, with different agents showing varying pharmacokinetic profiles that determine their clinical uses. Their mechanism is through binding to specific sites on GABA-A receptors, increasing the receptor's affinity for GABA and enhancing chloride conductance. Flumazenil serves as a competitive antagonist when benzodiazepine reversal is needed.

# 3. ALPHA-AGONISTS









## 3.1. DEXMEDETOMIDINE









**Highly selective α2-adrenergic agonist used for ICU sedation and procedural sedation with minimal respiratory depression.**

### 3.1.1. PRESENTATION AND USES

* S-enantiomer of medetomidine (R-form has no pharmacological activity) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Presented as colorless solution in vials containing 100 μg/ml, typically diluted to 4-8 μg/ml prior to use [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Used for [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Light to moderate sedation (Richmond Agitation-Sedation scale) for ventilated patients in ICU
  * Procedural sedation (including pediatric patients)
  * Balanced general anesthesia

### 3.1.2. DOSING

* For sedation in ICU: initial rate 0.7 μg/kg/hr, adjusted to effect (range 0.2-1.4 μg/kg/hr) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* For use in anesthesia: loading dose 1 μg/kg/hr for 10 minutes followed by maintenance 0.2-0.6 μg/kg/hr [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Larger infusion rates (5-10 μg/kg/hr) described for use as sole anesthetic agent (uncommon) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 3.1.3. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* Highly selective for α2-receptor (α2:α1 ratio 1600:1), eight times more selective than clonidine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Inhibits noradrenaline release at the locus coeruleus of the pons, linked with natural sleep pathways [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 3.1.4. EFFECTS

* **Central Nervous System** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Sedation and anxiolysis resembling physiological sleep rather than pharmacological sedation (supported by EEG studies)
  * Associated with less delirium compared to some drug protocols
  * Produces analgesia via actions in substantia gelatinosa of spinal cord dorsal horn
* **Cardiovascular** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Biphasic effects:
	* Initial hypertension (mediated by α2B receptors on vascular smooth muscle)
	* Followed by hypotension and bradycardia (reduced central noradrenaline release)
  * ⚠️ Rarely may progress to asystole when used as sole agent
  * Should be used with extreme caution in patients with conduction defects
* **Respiratory** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Minimal respiratory depression
  * Maintains patent airway
  * Useful during extubation
  * Decongestant and antisialagogue effects (advantageous for fiberoptic intubation)

### 3.1.5. KINETICS

* **Absorption** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Oral absorption unpredictable (bioavailability <20%)
  * Improved when given buccally (bioavailability >75%)
  * Parenteral administration causes initial hypertension
* **Distribution** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Highly protein-bound (94%)
  * Crosses easily into brain
  * Volume of distribution: 118 liters (increased in hypoalbuminemia)
* **Metabolism** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Both Phase I (hydroxylation) and Phase II (glucuronidation and methylation) hepatic metabolism
  * Inactive metabolites excreted in urine
  * Only hepatic impairment should prompt dose reduction
* **Elimination** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Distribution half-life: 6 minutes
  * Elimination half-life: ~2 hours
  * Clearance: approximately 40 L/hr

**Bottom Line:** Dexmedetomidine is a highly selective α2-agonist that produces a sedative state resembling natural sleep with minimal respiratory depression, making it useful for procedural sedation and ICU sedation. Its biphasic cardiovascular effects require careful monitoring, especially during loading doses.

## 4.2. TRICYCLIC ANTIDEPRESSANTS (TCAS)








### 4.2.1. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* Competitive inhibition of the uptake 1 pathway, reducing reuptake of noradrenaline and serotonin from the synaptic cleft [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Additional antagonistic effects at [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * α-adrenergic receptors
  * Histamine receptors
  * Muscarinic acetylcholine receptors
* Antidepressant effects don't occur immediately, taking up to 2 weeks [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 4.2.2. EFFECTS AND SIDE EFFECTS

* Postural hypotension [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Reduced seizure threshold [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* ⚠️ 'Anticholinergic' side effects (*red as a beet, dry as a bone, blind as a bat, mad as a hatter, hot as a hare, full as a flask*) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Flushing
  * Dry mouth
  * Blurred vision
  * Confusion
  * Hyperthermia
  * Constipation
  * Urinary retention
* Sedation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 4.2.3. TCA OVERDOSE: DIAGNOSIS AND MANAGEMENT

* **Diagnosis** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **History**:
	* History of depression or previous overdoses
	* Known quantity of tablets taken
	* Known history of renal or hepatic impairment
  * **Examination**:
	* Anticholinergic side effects as above
	* Labile blood pressure, with shock and refractory hypotension in extreme cases
  * **Investigations**:
	* ECG findings [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
	  * Sinus tachycardia
	  * QT prolongation and broad QRS
	  * Right bundle branch block (RBBB)
	  * Ventricular arrhythmias more likely when QRS complex longer than 0.16 seconds [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 
* **Management** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **General**:
	* ABCDE approach, identifying and treating reversible issues
	* Rehydration and optimization of cardiovascular status
	* Management of seizures with benzodiazepines or phenytoin
	* Management of arrhythmias
  * **Specific**:
	* Activated charcoal if ingested within the last hour
	* Alkalinization to increase protein binding
	* ⚠️ Be prepared for cardiovascular collapse, severe arrhythmias, and refractory seizures
	* Ventricular arrhythmias may be treated with phenytoin or lidocaine [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 
	* Inotropes should be avoided where possible as may precipitate arrhythmias [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 
	* Intravascular volume expansion usually sufficient to correct hypotension [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 
	* Anticholinergic effects may be reversed by anticholinesterase, but not recommended as may precipitate seizures, bradycardia, and heart failure [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 

**Bottom Line:** TCAs work by inhibiting monoamine reuptake and antagonizing multiple receptor types, causing various side effects including anticholinergic symptoms and cardiovascular toxicity. TCA overdose is a serious emergency requiring airway management, cardiovascular support, and specific interventions like alkalinization.

## 4.3. SELECTIVE SEROTONIN REUPTAKE INHIBITORS (SSRIS)








### 4.3.1. EXAMPLES

* Fluoxetine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Sertraline [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Paroxetine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Venlafaxine (also affects noradrenaline and dopamine reuptake) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 4.3.2. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* Selective inhibition of serotonin reuptake from the synaptic cleft [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Venlafaxine appears to block reuptake of both noradrenaline and 5-HT (and to a lesser extent dopamine) while having little effect on muscarinic, histaminergic or α-adrenoceptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 4.3.3. SIDE EFFECTS

* Less sedating with fewer side effects than traditional antidepressants [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Common side effects include [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Nausea and vomiting
  * Constipation
  * Headache
  * Insomnia (in some cases) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Reduced libido [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Mania or hypomania (up to 1%) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* ⚠️ Caution must be taken with other uptake inhibitors as can trigger serotonin syndrome [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* May inhibit 5-HT uptake in platelets, impairing their function (significant with other antiplatelet agents) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* ⚠️ Serotonin syndrome: Characterized by hyper-reflexia, agitation, clonus, and hyperthermia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Commonest combination is MAOI and SSRI
  * Phenylpiperidine opioids (particularly pethidine) have weak serotonin reuptake inhibitor properties and can precipitate the syndrome

### 4.3.4. SPECIFIC SSRI FEATURES

* **Fluoxetine** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * 50:50 mix of two equally active isomers
  * Metabolized by cytochrome P450 enzymes with non-saturable enzymes preventing unchecked rise in levels
  * Dose should be reduced in renal failure as accumulation may occur

**Bottom Line:** SSRIs selectively inhibit serotonin reuptake with fewer side effects than TCAs, but require caution when used with other serotonergic medications due to risk of serotonin syndrome. They may affect platelet function and should be used carefully with antiplatelet agents.

## 4.4. MONOAMINE OXIDASE INHIBITORS (MAOIS)








### 4.4.1. USES

* Depression [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Chronic pain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Resistant depression [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Migraine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 4.4.2. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* Inhibit MAO A and B [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Type A inhibition - increases concentrations of catecholamines and serotonin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Type B inhibition - affects tyramine and phenylethylamine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* MAO is present as various isoenzymes within presynaptic neurons and is responsible for deamination of amine neurotransmitters [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Following inhibition, there is an increase in amine neurotransmitter levels, thought to be the basis of central activity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 4.4.3. TYPES AND EXAMPLES

#### 4.4.3.1. NON-SELECTIVE IRREVERSIBLE MAOIS

* Tranylcypromine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Phenelzine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Isocarboxazid [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Phenelzine and isocarboxazid are hydrazines; tranylcypromine is nonhydrazine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Tranylcypromine potentially most dangerous as it possesses stimulant activity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

#### 4.4.3.2. SELECTIVE REVERSIBLE INHIBITORS OF MAO-A (RIMAS)

* Moclobemide [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Causes less potentiation of tyramine than older MAOIs [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Patients generally need less dietary restriction, but some patients are especially sensitive to tyramine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Completely absorbed from gut but significant first-pass metabolism (bioavailability 60-80%) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Metabolized by cytochrome P450 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* 2% of Caucasian and 15% of Asian population are slow metabolizers [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

#### 4.4.3.3. OTHER MAOIS

* Linezolid (antibiotic with MAOI properties) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 4.4.4. EFFECTS AND INTERACTIONS

* Sedation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Hypotension [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* ⚠️ Tyramine crisis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Can occur with foods high in tyramine such as cheese and chocolate [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Inhibit hepatic enzymes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* ⚠️ Avoid pethidine and ephedrine as can trigger excitatory crisis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Hydrazine compounds may cause hepatotoxicity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Interaction with pethidine may precipitate cerebral irritability, hyperpyrexia, and cardiovascular instability [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Interaction with fentanyl has also been reported [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 4.4.5. MAOIS AND GENERAL ANESTHESIA

* Patients receiving MAOIs presenting for emergency surgery should not receive [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Pethidine
  * Indirectly acting sympathomimetic amines (e.g., ephedrine)
* If cardiovascular support is needed, direct-acting agents should be used with extreme caution (may still precipitate exaggerated hypertension) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* For elective cases [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Traditional approach was to withdraw MAOI 14-21 days before surgery
  * Risk: Patient may relapse into depression
  * Newer agents (RIMAs) may control depression more effectively and reduce chance of serious perioperative interaction
* Indirectly acting sympathomimetics are heavily dependent on MAO for metabolism and may produce exaggerated hypertension and arrhythmias with MAOIs [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Directly acting sympathomimetics should be used with caution but also metabolized by COMT, so less exaggerated response [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* MAOIs should be stopped for 2 weeks before starting alternative antidepressant therapy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* 2 weeks should elapse from end of TCA therapy to start of MAOI therapy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

**Bottom Line:** MAOIs inhibit monoamine oxidase enzymes leading to increased levels of catecholamines and serotonin, requiring careful dietary restrictions (tyramine-containing foods) and caution with multiple medications including pethidine and sympathomimetics to avoid potentially fatal interactions. For anesthesia, emergency cases require avoiding specific drugs, while elective cases present complex decisions about MAOI continuation.

## 4.5. ATYPICAL AGENTS









### 4.5.1. MIANSERIN

* Tetracyclic compound used in depressive illness, especially where sedation is required [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Mechanism**: Unlike TCAs, does not block neuronal reuptake of transmitters [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Blocks presynaptic α2-adrenoceptors, reducing negative feedback, resulting in increased synaptic neurotransmitters
* Very little ability to block muscarinic and peripheral α-adrenoceptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Less antimuscarinic effects or postural hypotension than TCAs
* **Side Effects**: Important side effects include agranulocytosis and aplastic anemia (more common in elderly) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

**Bottom Line:** Atypical antidepressants like mianserin work through different mechanisms than traditional antidepressants, offering alternatives for patients who need sedation but with fewer anticholinergic and hypotensive effects, though potentially serious hematological side effects require monitoring.

## 4.6. LITHIUM








### 4.6.1. USES

* Bipolar disorder [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Mania [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Depression [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 4.6.2. DOSING AND MONITORING

* Mimics sodium at sodium channels and reduces depolarization and production of neurotransmitters [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* In excitable cells, lithium imitates Na+ and decreases neurotransmitter release [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

# 5. RECREATIONAL DRUGS AND ADDICTION









## 5.1. CONCEPTS OF ADDICTION, DEPENDENCE, AND TOLERANCE

**Key terminology related to substance use disorders relevant to anesthetic practice.**

### 5.1.1. ADDICTION

* The compulsive use of a substance despite causing harm (physical, social, or psychological) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 5.1.2. DEPENDENCE

* A physiological change as the body adapts to the presence of a substance, causing withdrawal symptoms when the substance is removed [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 5.1.3. TOLERANCE

* An increasing requirement in dose of a substance needed to achieve the same physiological or psychological effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Mechanisms include [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Downregulation of receptors
  * Reduced downstream response to receptor stimulation
  * Induction or inhibition of enzymes in substance's metabolic pathway
  * Physiological changes in other systems to compensate for substance's effects

### 5.1.4. TACHYPHYLAXIS

* A **rapid** reduction in the clinical response seen to a substance when multiple doses are given in a relatively short time span [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* 💡 Classic example is ephedrine, which rapidly depletes endogenous noradrenaline stores, leading to diminished response with repeated doses [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

## 5.2. EFFECTS OF SMOKING ON ANESTHESIA

**Tobacco smoking has significant implications for anesthetic management across multiple body systems.**

### 5.2.1. RESPIRATORY EFFECTS

* Increased sputum, coughing, and atelectasis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] due to:
  * Paralysis of cilia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Stimulation of goblet cells [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Increased risk of bronchospasm [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Increased risk of postoperative respiratory tract infection [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Left shift of oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* ⚠️ If COPD is present [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Risk of hypercapnia
  * Risk of bullae and pneumothorax

### 5.2.2. CARDIOVASCULAR EFFECTS

* Nicotine has sympathomimetic effects causing [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Tachycardia
  * Vasospasm
  * Hypertension
* Increased cardiac oxygen demand [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Increased blood viscosity [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* ⚠️ Increased risk of perioperative myocardial infarction [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

### 5.2.3. OTHER EFFECTS

* Impaired wound healing [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Impaired fracture healing [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Recreational drug use can lead to addiction, dependence, and tolerance, with each requiring specific considerations in anesthesia. Smoking particularly affects anesthetic management through respiratory complications, cardiovascular stress, and impaired healing, necessitating comprehensive preoperative assessment and optimization.
