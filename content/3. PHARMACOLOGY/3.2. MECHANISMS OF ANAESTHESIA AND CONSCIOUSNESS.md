## 1. DEFINITIONS AND CONCEPTS

**General anesthesia is a drug-induced reversible depression of the central nervous system resulting in specific behavioral and physiological endpoints.**

### 1.2. KEY COMPONENTS OF GENERAL ANESTHESIA

* **Unconsciousness** (loss of awareness) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Amnesia** (inability to form new memories) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Analgesia** (reduction in pain perception) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Immobility** (suppression of movement in response to noxious stimuli) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Attenuation of autonomic responses** to noxious stimulation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

### 1.3. REQUIREMENTS FOR ANESTHETIC MECHANISMS

Any comprehensive mechanism of general anesthetic action must explain:

* **Loss of conscious awareness** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Loss of response to noxious stimuli** (anti-nociceptive effect) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Reversibility** (perhaps most important of all) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

### 1.4. CHALLENGES IN STUDYING ANESTHESIA MECHANISMS

* **Behavioral definition difficulties** – precisely defining unconsciousness and amnesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Translation limitations** – translating effects in lower organisms to humans [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Bridging levels of complexity** – mapping cellular/molecular effects to observed behaviors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Incomplete understanding** of consciousness mechanisms [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Neural inertia phenomenon** – induction requiring higher anesthetic concentration than maintenance [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** General anesthesia encompasses multiple endpoints achieved through complex drug effects on various neural systems, with scientific research hampered by challenges in defining and measuring consciousness.

## 2. MEASUREMENTS OF ANESTHESIA

### 2.1. MINIMUM ALVEOLAR CONCENTRATION (MAC)

* **Definition:** The alveolar partial pressure at which 50% of humans don't respond to surgical incision [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Advantages:**
  * Reproducible endpoint [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Acts as a surrogate for free plasma concentration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Limitations:**
  * Quantal endpoint (all-or-none response) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Population average value rather than individual-specific [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Only applicable to volatile anesthetics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Dependent on specific anesthetic endpoint being measured [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

### 2.2. PARENTERAL ANESTHETIC EQUIVALENT

* Free drug concentration in plasma preventing response to noxious stimulus in 50% of subjects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Analogous to MAC but for intravenous anesthetics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** MAC provides a standardized way to quantify volatile anesthetic potency but has important limitations, while parenteral anesthetics require alternative measurement approaches.

## 3. MOLECULAR THEORIES AND TARGET SITES

#### 3.2. MEYER-OVERTON RULE

![image.png](MECHANISM_OF_ANAESTHESIA_MECHANISM_OF_ANAESTHESIA_image.png_fcd469.png_d3027d.png)

* **Key principle:** Anesthetic potency correlates with solubility in olive oil (hydrophobic environments) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Octanol/water partition coefficient provides the best correlation with potency [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* At the beginning of the twentieth century, Overton and Meyer independently described the linear correlation between lipid solubility and anesthetic potency [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* This correlation was impressive given the great structural variation among anesthetic agents [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Initially suggested a non-specific mechanism of action based on physicochemical properties [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Exceptions to the rule:**
  * Some halogenated ethers, barbiturates, and neurosteroids can be convulsants rather than anesthetics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Cutoff effect in homologous series (long-chain compounds lose anesthetic properties) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Enantiomers differ in potency despite identical solubility [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Some general anesthetics like ketamine are extreme outliers [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * R-etomidate and S-etomidate have identical lipid solubility but only R-etomidate has anesthetic properties [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

### 3.3. LIPID THEORIES OF ANESTHESIA

* **Theory:** Anesthetics dissolve in lipid bilayers, perturbing membrane properties [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Counterarguments:**
  * Changes at clinical concentrations are too small to disrupt function [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Membrane heterogeneity complicates effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

### 3.4. MEMBRANE LIPID THEORIES

* Several potential lipophilic sites in cell membranes, including the lipid bilayer itself and annular lipids surrounding ionic channels [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Initially suggested that anesthetics could penetrate the bilayer and alter the molecular arrangement of phospholipids [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* This led to membrane expansion and disruption of membrane-spanning ionic channel function [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Critical volume hypothesis:** Calculations identifying the volume of anesthetic agent required to expand membranes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Evidence against:** A 1°C rise in temperature increases membrane thickness to a similar extent as volatile agents, yet doesn't enhance anesthesia - the opposite is true [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Annular lipid theory:** Suggested anesthetics act at specific lipid sites near ion channels [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* The composition of phospholipids in the immediate vicinity of ion channels differs from the general lipid bilayer [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Proposed disruption of annular lipids associated with specific ion channels led to the perturbation theory [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

### 3.5. PROTEIN THEORIES OF ANESTHESIA

* **Theory:** Anesthetics bind to specific hydrophobic sites on proteins [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Likely binding sites:**
  * Endogenous hydrophobic ligand sites (orthosteric/allosteric modulators) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Hydrophobic amino acids in membrane-spanning α-helices [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Advances in receptor protein identification within the CNS have led to theories based on interactions with specific proteins [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* It now seems likely that the correlation between potency and lipid solubility reflects the lipophilic nature of specific protein-based binding sites [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Despite the correlation between lipid solubility and anesthetic potency (Meyer-Overton rule), evidence increasingly supports specific protein binding sites as the primary targets for anesthetics. The original lipid theories have largely been replaced by more targeted protein-binding theories, though the fundamental relationship between lipophilicity and anesthetic action remains important.

## 4. SPECIFIC ANESTHETIC TARGETS

#### 4.2. GABA(A) RECEPTORS

![image.png](MECHANISM_OF_ANAESTHESIA_MECHANISM_OF_ANAESTHESIA_image_1.png_fffb31.png_9b6742.png)

* GABA is the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter in the CNS [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* GABA(A) receptors allow chloride entry, hyperpolarizing neurons [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Structure:** Pentameric receptor with two α, two β, one γ/δ subunits [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Each subunit contains:
	* Transmembrane domain (TMD) with four α-helices (TM1-TM4) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
	* Extracellular GABA-binding domain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
	* Cytosolic domain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Binding sites:**
  * **Etomidate/Propofol:** Located between TM3 of β-subunit and TM1 of α-subunit (extracellular half) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Neurosteroids:** Same β(+)–α(−) interface but in the cytoplasmic half [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Barbiturates:** Located at α(+)–β(−) and γ(+)–β(−) interfaces [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Benzodiazepines:** α/γ interface (distinct from anesthetic sites) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Anesthetic effects:**
  * **Potentiation:** Increased current with low GABA concentrations [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Direct gating:** Activation without GABA [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Inhibition:** Preventing GABA-initiated current [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Anesthetics increase channel opening time, allowing increased chloride entry resulting in hyperpolarization [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Effect seen for etomidate, propofol, barbiturates, and halogenated volatiles [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Receptor subtype specificity:**
  * **Benzodiazepines:** Require γ2 subunit [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Etomidate:** Requires β2 or β3 subunit (more sensitive than β1) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * δ/ε subunits confer insensitivity to some anesthetics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Stereoselectivity evidence:**
  * R(+) etomidate is clinically active while S(-) form is inactive [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * At the GABA(A) receptor, there is a 30-fold difference in activity between enantiomers [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Primary relevant target for etomidate and propofol** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Likely target for volatile anesthetics** (along with TREK-1) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

### 4.3. GLYCINE RECEPTORS

* Major inhibitory neurotransmitter in the spinal cord and brain stem [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Associated with a chloride channel similar to the GABA(A) receptor [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* All volatile anesthetics markedly potentiate the action of glycine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* No evidence of stereoselectivity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* The spinal cord is suggested as an important site of action for volatile rather than intravenous anesthetic agents [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Efficacy correlates more with immobility than awareness [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

### 4.4. NMDA GLUTAMATE RECEPTORS

* Structure: Four subunits (2 GluN1, 2 GluN2) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Ketamine, nitrous oxide, xenon:** Potent, stereoselective inhibitors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Involved in long-term signal potentiation associated with learning and memory [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Activated by glutamate, modulated by magnesium [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Inhibited in a non-competitive manner by ketamine, nitrous oxide and xenon [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* This glutamate-mediated mechanism represents an additional pathway for anesthesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Other anesthetic agents like barbiturates can reduce glutamate effectiveness but with lower potency than for GABA(A) inhibition [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

### 4.5. OTHER POTENTIAL TARGETS

* **Hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide-gated channels (HCN):**
  * Modulated by volatile anesthetics and some IV anesthetics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Pass depolarizing current (*Ih*) consisting of sodium and potassium ions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Activated by hyperpolarization and modulated by cAMP [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Two-pore potassium channels:**
  * Background/leak K+ channels (TREK1, TASK-1, TASK-3) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Activated by volatile anesthetics and nitrous oxide [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Open at all voltages including resting membrane potential [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Effect: Neuronal hyperpolarization [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Voltage-gated sodium channels:**
  * Inhibited by volatile anesthetics and IV anesthetics (at supraclinical doses) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Effect: Reduced synaptic function [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **T-type calcium channels:**
  * Sensitive to volatile anesthetics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Effect: Decreased neuronal excitability [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Shaker potassium channels:**
  * Kv1.x family sensitive to clinical volatile anesthetic concentrations [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Sevoflurane activates these channels [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Mitochondrial complex I:**
  * Mutations alter sensitivity to anesthetics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ **Clinical Practice:** Pediatric patients with Complex I defects show hypersensitivity to volatile anesthetics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Synaptic vesicle release machinery:**
  * Syntaxin 1A mutations affect volatile anesthetic sensitivity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

### 4.6. SUMMARY OF EFFECTS AT CENTRAL RECEPTORS

* **Table of general anesthetic effects at central receptors:**

| Agent | GABA(A) (Inhibitory) | Glycine (Inhibitory) | NMDA (Excitatory) | Neuronal nACh (Excitatory) |
|-------|----------------------|----------------------|-------------------|----------------------------|
| Propofol | ++++ | + | 0 | - |
| Thiopental | +++++ | ++ | 0 | - |
| R-etomidate | +++++ | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| S-etomidate | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| Ketamine | 0 | 0 | - | 0 |
| Isoflurane | ++++ | +++ | 0 | - |
| Nitrous oxide | 0 | 0 | - | 0 |
| Xenon | 0 | 0 | - | 0 |

* Key: + enhances effect of neurotransmitter; - reduces effect of neurotransmitter; 0 no effect on neurotransmitter [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* nACh: central nicotinic acetylcholine receptor [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Multiple protein targets contribute to anesthetic action, with GABA(A) receptors being primary for many agents. Different anesthetics act on different combinations of targets, explaining their varied clinical effects. Understanding these mechanisms is crucial for anesthetic development and explaining patient variability in responses.

## 5. ANESTHETIC EFFECTS ON NEURAL FUNCTION

#### 5.2. NEURONAL EXCITABILITY

* **Definition:** A neuron's ability to generate and propagate action potentials [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Key determinants:**
  * Resting membrane potential [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Threshold potential for action potential generation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Action potential size and propagation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Anesthetic effects:**
  * Hyperpolarize both spinal motor neurons and cortical neurons [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Effect correlates with anesthetic potency [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Even small hyperpolarization may inhibit action potential initiation but not propagation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

### 5.3. SYNAPTIC TRANSMISSION

* **Primary subcellular site of general anesthetic action** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **General effects:**
  * Inhibit excitatory synaptic transmission (with variable sensitivity) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Both enhance and depress inhibitory transmission depending on agent [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Presynaptic effects:**
  * Inhibit neurotransmitter release at glutamatergic synapses (volatile anesthetics > IV anesthetics) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Variable effects on GABA release (inhibition, stimulation, or no effect depending on agent and concentration) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Postsynaptic effects:**
  * Multiple anesthetics potentiate response to GABA (barbiturates, etomidate, neurosteroids, propofol, volatile anesthetics) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Anesthetics primarily affect neural function by altering synaptic transmission, with effects on both preand postsynaptic mechanisms. While many agents enhance inhibitory (GABAergic) transmission, they also suppress excitatory (glutamatergic) neurotransmission.

## 6. ANATOMICAL SITES OF ANESTHETIC ACTION

#### 6.2. SPINAL CORD - IMMOBILITY

* **Primary site inhibiting motor responses to noxious stimulation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Mechanism basis for MAC measurements** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Anesthetic effects:**
  * Alter descending, afferent, efferent, and modulatory limbs of spinal reflexes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Volatile anesthetics:** Reduce excitatory synaptic transmission [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Propofol:** Depress ventral horn neurons via GABAergic mechanism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Isoflurane:** Inhibit dorsal horn and motoneurons (GABA-independent), suppress interneurons [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

### 6.3. BRAINSTEM - AUTONOMIC CONTROL

* **Affects cardiopulmonary and thermoregulatory circuits:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Halothane:** Suppresses dorsal respiratory group in medulla [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Propofol/Isoflurane:**
	* Augment inhibitory potentials of cardiac vagal neurons (nucleus ambiguous) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
	* Cause GABA-mediated inhibition of nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

### 6.4. SUBCORTICAL AND CORTICAL STRUCTURES - CONSCIOUSNESS

![image.png](MECHANISM_OF_ANAESTHESIA_MECHANISM_OF_ANAESTHESIA_image_2.png_767e11.png_38c196.png)

* **Consciousness components:**
  * **Awareness:** Processing/storing information to interact with environment [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Arousal:** State of receptivity to external environment (mediated by subcortical structures) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Sedation vs General anesthesia:**
  * **Sedation:** Easily arousable with altered awareness/arousal [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **General anesthesia:** Robust impairment of both awareness and arousal [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

### 6.5. RETICULAR ACTIVATING SYSTEM

* **Components:** Neurons in midbrain and pons [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Reticular formation (RF) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Tuberomammillary nucleus (TMN) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Ventral tegmental area (VTA) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Thalamic intralaminar nucleus [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Key structures and functions:**
  * **TMN/VLPO:** Bistable switch for wakefulness (TMN) or NREM sleep (VLPO) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
	* TMN: Wake-active neurons, sole source of histaminergic efferents [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Median preoptic nucleus (MnPO):** Stabilizes sleep, maintains sleep homeostasis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Perifornical area (PF):** Critical for anesthetic emergence (sole orexin source) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
	* **Orexin:** Stabilizes VLPO/TMN switch to favor arousal [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
	* Activation of orexin neurons speeds emergence from isoflurane [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Lateral habenula (LHb):** Connects midbrain and limbic forebrain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
	* Regulates dopaminergic/serotoninergic tone [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
	* Output affects propofol sedation and motor activity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Mesopontine tegmentum anesthetic locus (MPTA):** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
	* Regulates sleep-wake states [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
	* Affects sensitivity to GABAergic anesthetics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Ventral tegmental area (VTA):** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
	* Dopaminergic input to prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, amygdala [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
