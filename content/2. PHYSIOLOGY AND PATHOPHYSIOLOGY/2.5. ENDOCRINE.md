## 1.1. GENERAL PRINCIPLES OF HORMONE ACTION

### 1.1.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Endocrine control mechanisms, feedback loops, receptor function  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part C:** Integration of endocrine physiology in clinical practice  [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1-medical-knowledge|ⓘ]].



**Hormones are chemical messengers secreted into the blood by endocrine organs that act at distant tissues**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

* Hormones generally act upon specific receptors at target tissues to induce a response via secondary messenger systems  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* Usually produced as pro-hormones and stored in vesicles before release  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* Hormone effects are determined by:
  * Concentration of hormone in circulation
  * Number and sensitivity of receptors on target cells
  * Presence of other hormones that may have synergistic or antagonistic effects
  * Half-life in circulation (affected by plasma protein binding and metabolism)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* Key control mechanisms include:
  * Negative feedback (most common): rising levels of a hormone or its effects suppress further hormone production
  * Positive feedback (less common): rising levels stimulate further production (e.g., oxytocin during labor)
  * Neural control: direct nervous system influence on endocrine organs
  * Circadian rhythms: daily variations in hormone secretion (e.g., cortisol, melatonin)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]

💡 Most hormones show pulsatile secretion patterns rather than continuous release, which helps prevent receptor desensitization  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Hormones function as the body's chemical messengers, with tightly regulated feedback systems controlling their secretion and effects on target tissues. Understanding these mechanisms is crucial for interpreting endocrine disorders and administering hormone-based therapies.

## 1.2. HORMONE TYPES AND CLASSIFICATION

### 1.2.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Hormone structure, synthesis, and classification  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].



**There are three main groups of hormones with distinct properties affecting their onset and duration of action**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

1. **Steroid Hormones**
   * Derived from cholesterol
   * Lipid-soluble; can cross cell membranes
   * Act via nuclear receptors to alter gene transcription
   * Immerdiate release after production
   * Slower onset but longer duration of action
   * Examples: cortisol, aldosterone, sex steroids (estrogen, testosterone, progesterone)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]

2. **Amino Acid Derivatives**
   * Tyrosine derivatives
	 * Catecholamines (epinephrine, norepinephrine)
	 * Thyroid hormones (T3, T4)
   * Tryptophan derivatives
	 * Melatonin
	 * Serotonin  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]

3. **Peptide/Protein Hormones**
   * Range from small peptides to large glycoproteins
   * Synthesis via mRNA transcription and translation
   * Released via exocytosis
   * Rapid onset of action
   * Act via membrane receptors and second messenger systems
   * Examples: insulin, glucagon, ACTH, PTH, growth hormone  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]

* Other classification includes:
  * **Lipid and phospholipid-derived hormones**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]] 
	* Steroid hormones (e.g., aldosterone, testosterone, estrogen, cortisol)
	* Eicosanoids (e.g., prostaglandins, thromboxanes, leukotrienes)
  * **Monoamine derivatives** - hormones derived from a single amino acid  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]] 
	* Catecholamines from phenylalanine or tyrosine
	* Serotonin from tryptophan
	* Thyroxine from tyrosine

💡 Steroid hormones are primarily protein-bound in plasma (to albumin and specific binding globulins), whereas peptide hormones circulate freely, affecting their half-lives and bioavailability  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** The three main classes of hormones (steroids, amino acid derivatives, and peptides) differ in their synthesis pathways, mechanisms of action, and pharmacokinetic properties, which influences their clinical applications and the management of related disorders.

## 1.3. ENDOCRINE CONTROL SYSTEMS AND FEEDBACK MECHANISMS

### 1.3.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Feedback mechanisms, endocrine axes  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].



**Endocrine systems typically function through hierarchical axes with multiple levels of control and feedback**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

* **Major endocrine axes:**
  * Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Adrenal (HPA) axis
  * Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Thyroid (HPT) axis
  * Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Gonadal (HPG) axis
  * Growth Hormone axis
  * Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System (RAAS)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Typical three-tier control system:**
  * Hypothalamus releases stimulating/inhibiting hormones
  * Pituitary releases tropic hormones in response
  * Target endocrine gland releases final effector hormone
  * End-organ response provides feedback at multiple levels  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Feedback mechanisms:**
  * **Short-loop feedback:** Target gland hormone inhibits pituitary hormone release  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]] 
  * **Ultra-short loop feedback:** Hormone inhibits its own release
  * **Long-loop feedback:** End-organ effects provide feedback to central control  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]] 
* **Factors influencing hormone release:**
  * Neural stimuli (stress, pain, emotional state)
  * Metabolic stimuli (glucose, calcium, osmolality levels)
  * Environmental factors (temperature, light/dark cycles)
  * Other hormones (permissive or antagonistic effects)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]

⚠️ During anesthesia and surgery, normal feedback mechanisms may be altered, leading to potentially significant changes in hormone levels and responses  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Endocrine systems typically function as hierarchical axes with tightly regulated negative feedback loops. Understanding these control mechanisms helps explain normal physiology, pathophysiology of endocrine disorders, and the body's response to stressors like surgery and anesthesia.

# 2. HYPOTHALAMUS AND PITUITARY





## 2.1. ANATOMY AND PHYSIOLOGY

### 2.1.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Hypothalamo-pituitary structural and functional relationships  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Relevance to perioperative management  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].



**The hypothalamus and pituitary function as a central unit regulating multiple endocrine systems, with direct neural and vascular connections between them**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

* **Hypothalamus**
  * Located in the floor of the third ventricle, posterior to the optic chiasm
  * Main function is homeostasis maintenance
  * Has widespread connections to brainstem, limbic system, and higher centers
  * Coordinates activity of the endocrine system
  * Also involved in monitoring and managing:
	* Autonomic nervous system function
	* Thirst and fluid balance
	* Appetite and feeding behavior
	* Thermoregulation
	* Sexual activity and reproductive function  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
  * Functions can be classified as:  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]] 
	* **Autonomic**: Relays inputs from limbic system to medulla oblongata (emotional stress triggers sympathetic response)
	* **Thermoregulation**: Controls heat loss and heat gain centers
	* **Hunger regulation**: Controls feeding and satiety centers
	* **Body water regulation**: Controls thirst center and osmoreceptors
	* **Sleep-wake cycles**: Anterior hypothalamus triggers sleep, posterior hypothalamus causes wakefulness
	* **Behavioral control**: Contains 'punishment' and 'reward' centers
	* **Sexual function control**: Controls pulsatile gonadotropin release
* **Pituitary Gland**
  * Located in the sella turcica
  * Divided into anterior (adenohypophysis) and posterior (neurohypophysis) parts
  * Connected to hypothalamus via:
	* Hypophyseal portal system for anterior pituitary
	* Direct neural connections for posterior pituitary  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
  * Anatomical position:  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]] 
	* Situated directly below hypothalamus, connected by pituitary stalk
	* Almost entirely covered by diaphragma sella (fold of dura mater)
	* Close to important structures:
	  * Superiorly: pituitary stalk, optic chiasm, third ventricle
	  * Laterally: cavernous sinus (containing cranial nerves III, IV, VI, V1, V2, and internal carotid artery)
  * Embryological origins:  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]] 
	* Anterior lobe (adenohypophysis): develops from Rathke's pouch (oral ectoderm)
	* Posterior lobe (neurohypophysis): develops from neural ectoderm downgrowth
	* Pars intermedia: thin layer of cells between anterior and posterior lobes, derived from Rathke's pouch
* **Hypothalamic-Pituitary Connection**
  * **Anterior pituitary:** Hypothalamic releasing and inhibiting hormones travel via hypophyseal portal circulation
  * **Posterior pituitary:** Direct axonal transport of hormones from hypothalamic neurons  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
  * **Blood supply**:  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]] 
	* Anterior lobe receives blood from:
	  * Superior hypophyseal artery (branch of internal carotid)
	  * Long portal veins (connect hypothalamic capillaries to anterior lobe capillaries)
	  * Short portal veins (from posterior to anterior lobe)
	* Posterior lobe receives blood from inferior hypophyseal artery
	* Venous blood drains into cavernous sinuses

💡 The hypophyseal portal system allows for precise control of anterior pituitary hormone release without systemic dilution of hypothalamic signals  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** The hypothalamus and pituitary function as an integrated central unit that regulates multiple endocrine systems through direct neural connections and the hypophyseal portal circulation. This anatomical arrangement enables precise control of endocrine function and maintains physiological homeostasis.

## 2.2. ANTERIOR PITUITARY

### 2.2.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Anterior pituitary structure and function  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Clinical significance of anterior pituitary disorders  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Part C:** Perioperative management considerations  [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1-medical-knowledge|ⓘ]].


**Competency Focus:** , 

**The anterior pituitary secretes multiple hormones that regulate target endocrine glands and directly influence metabolism and growth**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

### 2.2.2. STRUCTURE AND DEVELOPMENT

* Derived from Rathke's pouch (oral ectoderm)
* Three anatomical subdivisions:
  * Pars tuberalis
  * Pars intermedius (produces melanocyte-stimulating hormone)
  * Pars distalis (produces the six main anterior pituitary hormones)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
	![[Pasted image 20250723201618.png]]
	
![[Pasted image 20250723201547.png]]

* Capillary plexus in hypothalamus derived from superior hypophyseal artery
* Parvocellular neurons release hypophysiotropic hormones (TRF, GNRF, SRF, & CRF)
* Parvocellular neuron activity regulated by noradrenaline, serotonin, & dopamine
* Hypophyseal portal system transports hypophysiotropic hormones to anterior pituitary capillary plexus
* Stimulate anterior pituitary hormone production and release
* 3 levels of negative feedback
  * Ultra-short loop - hypophysiotropic hormone inhibits own production
  * Short loop - anterior pituitary hormone inhibits hypothalamic activity
  * Long loop - hormones from peripheral organs inhibit hypothalamic activity
* Hypophyseal portal system supplies 90% of blood to anterior pituitary (Sheehan's syndrome)

### 2.2.3. HORMONES AND FUNCTIONS

* **Growth Hormone (GH)**
  * Stimulates growth via insulin-like growth factors (IGFs)
  * Promotes protein anabolism
  * Enhances lipolysis
  * Increases glucose production
  * Released in pulsatile fashion, especially during sleep
  * Regulated by GHRH (stimulatory) and somatostatin (inhibitory)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Adrenocorticotropic Hormone (ACTH)**
  * Stimulates release of glucocorticoids and mineralocorticoids from adrenal cortex
  * Derived from larger precursor, pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC)
  * Exhibits diurnal rhythm (peaks in early morning)
  * Regulated by CRH and negative feedback from cortisol  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Thyroid-Stimulating Hormone (TSH)**
  * Stimulates production of thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3) by thyroid
  * Glycoprotein hormone with alpha and beta subunits
  * Regulated by TRH and negative feedback from thyroid hormones  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Luteinizing Hormone (LH) and Follicle-Stimulating Hormone (FSH)**
  * Glycoprotein hormones that regulate reproductive function
  * In males: FSH stimulates spermatogenesis; LH stimulates testosterone production
  * In females: FSH stimulates follicle growth; LH triggers ovulation and stimulates estrogen/progesterone secretion
  * Both regulated by GnRH and gonadal hormone feedback  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Prolactin**
  * Stimulates milk production in mammary glands (lactation) and suppresses LH (amenorrhea)
  * Stimulated by suckling
  * Primarily under inhibitory control by dopamine (increasing prolactin release-inhibiting factor - PRIF)
  * Increased by TRH, estrogen, and stress  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]

### 2.2.4. DISORDERS OF ANTERIOR PITUITARY

* **Hypopituitarism**
  * Causes: tumors, surgery, radiation, trauma, infarction (Sheehan syndrome), infiltrative diseases
  * Manifestations depend on specific hormone deficiencies
  * Treatment: specific hormone replacements
  * Perioperative management: stress-dose corticosteroids if ACTH deficiency present  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Hypersecretion Syndromes**
  * Usually caused by pituitary adenomas
  * Includes acromegaly/gigantism (GH excess), Cushing's disease (ACTH excess), hyperprolactinemia
  * Treatment: surgical resection, medical therapy, radiation therapy  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Pituitary Adenoma** - clinical presentation:  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * Hormone hypersecretion (functional tumors become apparent at smaller size)
  * Hormone hyposecretion (larger tumors compress normal pituitary cells)
  * Mass effect (compression of optic chiasm causing bitemporal hemianopia, cranial nerve palsies, obstructive hydrocephalus)
  * Incidental finding on imaging

💡 Panhypopituitarism after pituitary surgery may require lifelong multiple hormone replacement therapy. Most critical is cortisol replacement, as its absence can be life-threatening during stress  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].

⚠️ Patients with acromegaly may present challenges for airway management due to macroglossia, prognathism, and laryngeal hypertrophy, with difficult intubation occurring in 20-30% of cases  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** The anterior pituitary secretes six major hormones that regulate multiple endocrine target organs and physiological processes. Disorders of the anterior pituitary can lead to either hormone deficiencies requiring replacement therapy or hypersecretion syndromes that may present significant perioperative management challenges, especially for airway management.

## 2.3. POSTERIOR PITUITARY

### 2.3.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Posterior pituitary structure and function  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Clinical significance of posterior pituitary disorders  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Part C:** Perioperative management considerations  [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1-medical-knowledge|ⓘ]].


**Competency Focus:** , 

**The posterior pituitary stores and releases two main hormones (vasopressin and oxytocin) that are synthesized in the hypothalamus and transported to the posterior pituitary via direct neural connections**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

### 2.3.2. STRUCTURE OF POSTERIOR PITUITARY GLAND

- Capillary plexus in posterior pituitary derived from inferior hypophyseal artery
- Magnocellular neurons neurons arise from supraoptic nuclei (SON) and paraventricular nuclei (PVN)
- Magnocellular neuron activity regulated by acetylcholine and noradrenaline
- Hormone (ADH & oxytocin) synthesis in magnocellular cell bodies and storage at nerve terminals as hormone-neurophysin complexes
- Direct hormone release into posterior pituitary capillary plexus via exocytosis
- Vascular transport of ADH and oxytocin out of posterior pituitary
### 2.3.3. VASOPRESSIN (ADH) PHYSIOLOGY

* **Structure and Synthesis**
  * 9-amino acid peptide hormone
  * Synthesized in supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei of hypothalamus
  * Transported down axons to posterior pituitary for storage and release  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Functions**
  * Primary role: water conservation and osmolality regulation
  * Increases water reabsorption in collecting ducts of kidney by increasing permeability
  * Promotes recruitment of aquaporin water channels
  * At higher doses: vasoconstriction (hence the alternative name "vasopressin")  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Receptors**
  * V1 receptors: vascular smooth muscle (IP3 and DAG as messengers)
  * V2 receptors: kidney collecting ducts (cAMP as messenger)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
	  * Increases protein kinase A phosphorylates aquaporin 2
	  * Aquaporin 2 inserts into luminal membrane
	  * Augments free water absorption
	  * Aquaporin 3 and 4 facilitates free water transport across basolateral membrane
	![[Pasted image 20250723203600.png]]
* **Regulation**
  * Osmoreceptors in hypothalamus detect changes as small as 1% in serum osmolality (>280mOsm/kg)
  * Volume receptors in left atrium and pulmonary veins detect volume changes (hypovolaemia) > CN9/10 to hypothalamus > decreased magnocellular cell inhibition and increased ADH secretion
  * Other stimuli: pain, stress, nausea, hypotension, positive pressure ventilation, certain medications
  * Inhibited by: alcohol, atrial natriuretic peptide, increased stretch receptor activity  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Additional Effects**
  * Promotes hemostasis through increased von Willebrand factor and Factor VIII
  * Used clinically in vasodilatory shock, GI bleeding, diabetes insipidus  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]

### 2.3.4. DIABETES INSIPIDUS

* **Definition:** Disorder characterized by excessive urine output due to insufficient ADH secretion or action  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Types**
  * Central (neurogenic): insufficient ADH production
  * Nephrogenic: renal resistance to ADH effects  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Causes**
  * Central: head trauma, neurosurgery (especially pituitary surgery), tumors, infiltrative diseases
  * Nephrogenic: medications (lithium, demeclocycline), electrolyte disorders (hypokalemia, hypercalcemia), renal disease  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Clinical Features**
  * Polyuria (3-20 L/day)
  * Polydipsia
  * Hypernatremia if fluid losses not replaced
  * Dehydration  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Diagnosis**
  * Water deprivation test
  * Urine and plasma osmolality measurements
  * Response to ADH administration  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Treatment**
  * Central: desmopressin (DDAVP) - intranasal, oral, or parenteral
  * Nephrogenic: treat underlying cause, thiazide diuretics, NSAIDs, dietary salt restriction  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Perioperative Management**
  * Continue DDAVP treatment
  * Monitor fluid balance and electrolytes
  * In acute postoperative DI: IV fluid replacement, electrolyte monitoring, DDAVP administration  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

### 2.3.5. SYNDROME OF INAPPROPRIATE ADH SECRETION

* **Definition:** Excessive, inappropriate ADH secretion leading to water retention and hyponatremia  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Causes**
  * CNS disorders: head injury, meningitis, encephalitis, tumors
  * Pulmonary disorders: pneumonia, tuberculosis, COPD, positive pressure ventilation
  * Medications: carbamazepine, SSRIs, TCAs, opioids, oxytocin
  * Malignancies: small cell lung cancer, pancreatic cancer, lymphomas
  * Surgery, pain, nausea  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Clinical Features**
  * Hyponatremia (may be severe, <120 mmol/L)
  * Decreased serum osmolality with inappropriately concentrated urine
  * Absence of edema or hypovolemia
  * Neurological symptoms: confusion, lethargy, seizures, coma (severity related to rate of sodium decline)  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Treatment**
  * Mild/asymptomatic: fluid restriction (800-1000 mL/day)
  * Severe/symptomatic: hypertonic saline (3%), careful correction rate to avoid osmotic demyelination
  * Pharmacologic: vasopressin receptor antagonists (vaptans), demeclocycline
  * Treat underlying cause  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

### 2.3.6. OXYTOCIN PHYSIOLOGY

* **Structure and Synthesis**
  * Small peptide hormone structurally similar to ADH
  * Synthesized in hypothalamus, stored in posterior pituitary  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]] 
* **Functions**
  * Contraction of uterine smooth muscle during labor (released in response to cervical stretching)
  * Let-down reflex in lactation (secreted in response to suckling)
  * Psychological effects: pair bonding between mother and child, generation of trust between adults  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]] 

⚠️ Correction of chronic hyponatremia must be gradual (not exceeding 8-10 mmol/L/day) to prevent osmotic demyelination syndrome, which can cause permanent neurological damage  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** The posterior pituitary releases vasopressin (ADH) and oxytocin, which regulate water balance, vascular tone, and reproductive functions. Disorders such as diabetes insipidus and SIADH represent opposite ends of the ADH spectrum and require careful perioperative management to avoid dangerous electrolyte imbalances and associated complications.

# 3. THYROID GLAND





## 3.1. ANATOMY AND PHYSIOLOGY

### 3.1.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Structure and function of the thyroid gland  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Clinical applications in perioperative care  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].



**The thyroid gland is an endocrine organ weighing approximately 25g, situated in the anterior neck bilaterally overlying the trachea**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

* **Anatomical Structure**
  * Two lobes connected by an isthmus overlying the third tracheal ring
  * Occasionally has a central pyramidal lobe
  * Encapsulated by pretracheal fascia
  * Located anterior to the recurrent laryngeal nerves  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Blood Supply**
  * Superior thyroid artery from external carotid
  * Inferior thyroid artery from thyrocervical trunk of subclavian
  * Superior, middle, and inferior thyroid veins  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Innervation**
  * Sympathetic: superior, middle, and inferior cervical ganglia
  * Recurrent laryngeal nerve passes closely behind the gland (critical surgical relationship)
  * External laryngeal nerve relates to superior pole  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Cellular Structure**
  * Follicular cells (principal cells): produce thyroid hormones T3 and T4
  * Parafollicular cells (C-cells): produce calcitonin
  * Organized into follicles containing colloid (stored thyroglobulin)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Hormones Synthesized**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]] 
  * **Triiodothyronine (T3)**
	* Strongly biologically active
	* Comprises only 10% of hormones released by thyroid
	* 99.7% protein-bound (mainly to albumin)
	* Short half-life (24 hours)
	* Only unbound fraction exerts effects
  * **Thyroxine (T4)**
	* Weakly biologically active
	* Main hormone synthesized (90%)
	* Highly protein-bound to thyroxine-binding globulin
	* Long half-life (7 days)
	* Half converted to active T3, half to inactive reverse T3
	* Bound hormones provide large reservoir

💡 The close relationship between the thyroid gland and the recurrent laryngeal nerves is of critical importance during thyroid surgery, as injury to these nerves can result in voice changes or airway compromise  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** The thyroid gland is a butterfly-shaped organ in the anterior neck that produces thyroid hormones and calcitonin. Its intimate anatomical relationship with the recurrent laryngeal nerves and its rich blood supply have important implications for thyroid surgery and perioperative management.

## 3.2. THYROID HORMONE METABOLISM AND FUNCTION

### 3.2.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Thyroid hormone synthesis, secretion, transport, and metabolism  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part C:** Integration of thyroid physiology in clinical states  [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1-medical-knowledge|ⓘ]].



**Thyroid hormones are the major regulators of cellular metabolic activity, exerting diverse effects by regulating the synthesis and activity of various proteins**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
![[Pasted image 20250723202357.png]]
* **Thyroid Hormone Synthesis**
  * Initial step: Active uptake and concentration of iodide in thyroid gland
  * Dietary iodine is reduced to iodide in GI tract and actively transported into thyroid
  * Within thyroid, iodide is:
	* Oxidized to iodine by thyroid peroxidase
	* Bound to tyrosine residues on thyroglobulin to form monoiodotyrosine (MIT) and diiodotyrosine (DIT)
	* MIT and DIT are coupled to form T3 (triiodothyronine) and T4 (thyroxine)
	* T3 and T4 remain attached to thyroglobulin and stored as colloid  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
  * Release involves proteolysis from thyroglobulin and diffusion into circulation  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
![[Pasted image 20250723202628.png]]

* **Detailed Synthesis Process**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]] 
  * Iodine trapping
	  * Iodide uptake: Active transport of iodide through Na+/Ico-transporter (TSH-stimulated)
	  * Iodide oxidation: Conversion to reactive iodine (I2) by thyroid peroxidase
  * Iodotyrosine formation
	  * Reaction with tyrosine: I2 reacts with tyrosine residues in thyroglobulin to form MIT or DIT
  * Iodotyrosine activation
	  * Oxidative coupling: Two iodinated tyrosines couple together - two DITs form T4, MIT+DIT forms T3
  * Storage: T3/T4 dispersed throughout thyroglobulin (sufficient for 1-3 months)
  * Release: TSH triggers endocytosis of thyroglobulin, T3/T4 separated and released into circulation
* **Regulation of Secretion**
  * Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) from anterior pituitary
	* Controls uptake of iodide and proteolytic release of thyroid hormone
  * TSH secretion regulated by:
	* Thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH) from hypothalamus
	* Negative feedback from circulating thyroid hormone
  * Excess iodide inhibits synthesis and secretion of thyroid hormone (Wolff-Chaikoff effect)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Production and Metabolism**
  * Thyroid gland secretes primarily T4 (80-100 μg/day)
  * T3 is more potent but less abundant in circulation
	* 80% of T3 produced by peripheral deiodination of T4
	* 20% produced by direct thyroid secretion
  * T4 half-life: approximately 7 days (99.9% protein bound, 67% thyroxin-binding globulin)
  * T3 half-life: 24-30 hours (99.7% protein bound; 53% albumin)
  * T4 is metabolized by monodeiodination to either:
	* T3 (active form) - in peripheral conversion
	* Reverse T3 (rT3, inactive form)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Transport in Blood**
  * Majority bound to plasma proteins:
	* Thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG)
	* Albumin
	* Transthyretin
  * Less than 0.1% present as free, unbound hormone (physiologically active fraction)
  * Changes in serum binding proteins affect total T3/T4 levels but not free hormone  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Physiological Actions**
  * Increases basal metabolic rate
  * Increases oxygen consumption
  * Promotes carbohydrate absorption from GI tract
  * Enhances lipolysis
  * Increases protein synthesis and degradation
  * Cardiovascular effects:
	* Increases heart rate and contractility
	* Increases cardiac output
	* Increases sensitivity to catecholamines (increases β-adrenergic receptors)
  * Neurological development (critical in early life)
  * Bone growth and maturation  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **System-Specific Effects**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]] 
  * Metabolism: Affects lipolysis, gluconeogenesis; increases BMR
  * Growth and development: Essential for CNS development; stimulates neuronal myelination
  * Respiratory system: Increases O2 consumption, CO2 production, and ventilation
  * Cardiovascular system: Increases β-receptors, heart rate, contractility, and cardiac output
  * CNS: Affects mood (hypothyroidism associated with depression; hyperthyroidism with anxiety)
  * Musculoskeletal: In hyperthyroidism, induces protein catabolism causing proximal myopathy

⚠️ Many drugs can affect thyroid function, including amiodarone (contains iodine and can cause both hyper and hypothyroidism) and dopamine (suppresses TSH)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Thyroid hormones are critical regulators of metabolic activity, with effects on virtually all body systems. T4 is the major secreted form, with T3 being the more potent active hormone formed primarily by peripheral conversion. Understanding thyroid hormone regulation is essential for managing thyroid disorders and recognizing drug interactions affecting thyroid function.

## 3.3. TESTS OF THYROID FUNCTION

### 3.3.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Laboratory assessment of thyroid function  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Application to perioperative evaluation  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].



**Multiple laboratory tests are available to assess thyroid function, with TSH being the most sensitive initial screening test for suspected thyroid dysfunction**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

* **Thyroid-Stimulating Hormone (TSH)**
  * Most sensitive and specific initial test for thyroid dysfunction
  * Modern immunochemiluminometric assays detect very small changes
  * Normal range: 0.4-4.5 μIU/mL
  * Interpretation:
	* Elevated TSH (>20 μIU/mL): Primary hypothyroidism
	* Depressed TSH: Hyperthyroidism
	* Elevated TSH with normal T4: Subclinical hypothyroidism
	* Low TSH with clinical hypothyroidism: Secondary (pituitary) or tertiary (hypothalamic) hypothyroidism  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
  * TSH levels affected by:
	* Starvation, fever, stress, corticosteroids (all can depress TSH)
	* Exogenous T3 or T4 administration  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Serum Thyroxine (T4)**
  * Measures total T4 (bound and free)
  * Normal range: 5-12 μg/dL
  * Elevated in approximately 90% of hyperthyroid patients
  * Low in 85% of hypothyroid patients
  * Limited by influence of binding proteins:
	* Increases in TBG (pregnancy, estrogens, acute liver disease) raise total T4 without affecting free T4
	* Decreases in TBG (nephrotic syndrome, androgens) lower total T4  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
  * Should never be used alone to evaluate thyroid disease due to binding protein effects  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Serum Triiodothyronine (T3)**
  * Not commonly measured in initial assessment
  * Useful for:
	* Detecting T3 thyrotoxicosis (elevated T3 with normal T4)
	* Evaluating nonthyroidal illness (formerly "sick euthyroid syndrome")
  * May remain normal in 50% of hypothyroid patients  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Tests for Assessing Thyroid Hormone Binding**
  * Free T4 index: Calculated using T3 resin uptake and total T4
  * Direct measurement of free T3 and T4
	* These tests account for binding abnormalities
	* Better reflection of true thyroid status than total hormone levels  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Radioactive Iodine Uptake**
  * Measures thyroid gland activity using 131I or 123I
  * Elevated in hyperthyroidism (except thyroiditis)
  * Helps differentiate causes of hyperthyroidism:
	* High uptake: Graves' disease, toxic adenoma
	* Low uptake: Thyroiditis, exogenous thyroid hormone, iodine excess
  * Factors increasing uptake: Iodine deficiency, renal failure, heart failure
  * Factors decreasing uptake: Elevated free T4, corticosteroids  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]

💡 "Nonthyroidal illness" (sick euthyroid syndrome) refers to abnormal thyroid function tests in patients with acute or chronic nonthyroidal illnesses, typically showing low T3 due to impaired peripheral conversion of T4 to T3  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** TSH is the most sensitive initial test for thyroid dysfunction, with elevated levels indicating primary hypothyroidism and suppressed levels suggesting hyperthyroidism. Interpretation of thyroid function tests requires consideration of the effects of binding proteins, medications, and nonthyroidal illness on results, making free hormone measurements often more reliable than total hormone levels.

## 3.4. HYPERTHYROIDISM

### 3.4.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Pathophysiology of hyperthyroidism  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Perioperative implications and management  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Part C:** Critical clinical reasoning for thyrotoxic patients  [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1-medical-knowledge|ⓘ]].


**Competency Focus:** , 

**Hyperthyroidism results from exposure of tissues to excessive amounts of thyroid hormone, most commonly from Graves' disease or toxic multinodular goiter**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

### 3.4.2. ETIOLOGY AND PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

* **Causes of Hyperthyroidism**
  * Intrinsic thyroid disease
	* Graves' disease (most common): Diffuse toxic goiter with autoantibodies stimulating TSH receptors
	* Toxic multinodular goiter
	* Hyperfunctioning thyroid adenoma
  * Disorders of hormone storage or release
	* Thyroiditis (subacute, postpartum, silent)
  * Exogenous sources
	* Iatrogenic (excess thyroid replacement)
	* Iodine-induced (Jod-Basedow phenomenon)
	* Amiodarone-induced thyrotoxicosis
  * Other causes
	* TSH-secreting pituitary adenoma (rare)
	* Trophoblastic tumors
	* Metastatic thyroid carcinoma  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Pathophysiology**
  * In Graves' disease, thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulins (TSI) bind to TSH receptors
  * Increased thyroid hormone production leads to:
	* Increased metabolic rate and oxygen consumption
	* Enhanced sensitivity to catecholamines
	* Accelerated protein turnover
	* Increased heat production  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]

### 3.4.3. CLINICAL MANIFESTATIONS

* **General**
  * Weight loss despite increased appetite
  * Heat intolerance
  * Fatigue
  * Diaphoresis
  * Warm, moist skin  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Cardiovascular**
  * Increased left ventricular contractility and ejection fraction
  * Tachycardia, palpitations
  * Increased systolic blood pressure
  * Decreased diastolic blood pressure
  * Widened pulse pressure
  * Increased myocardial oxygen consumption
  * Decreased vascular resistance
  * Atrial fibrillation (especially in elderly)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Neuromuscular**
  * Tremor
  * Hyperreflexia
  * Skeletal muscle weakness and stiffness
  * Nervousness, anxiety, emotional lability  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Other**
  * Diarrhea
  * Hypercalcemia
  * Thrombocytopenia
  * Mild anemia
  * In Graves' disease: ophthalmopathy, dermopathy, clubbing  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Atypical Presentations**
  * "Apathetic hyperthyroidism" in elderly: depression and withdrawal without typical systemic signs  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
  * T3 thyrotoxicosis: normal T4 but elevated T3  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]

### 3.4.4. TREATMENT AND ANESTHETIC CONSIDERATIONS

* **Medical Management**
  * Thionamides (propylthiouracil, methimazole)
	* Inhibit organification of iodide and hormone synthesis
	* Propylthiouracil also decreases peripheral conversion of T4 to T3
	* Usually require 6-8 weeks to achieve euthyroidism
	* Side effects: rash, agranulocytosis, hepatitis, arthralgias  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
  * Iodide compounds
	* Inhibit hormone release (Wolff-Chaikoff effect)
	* Reduce gland size and vascularity
	* Used in preoperative preparation
	* Should be started after thionamides to prevent worsening thyrotoxicosis  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
  * β-adrenergic antagonists
	* Attenuate cardiovascular and sympathetic manifestations
	* Propranolol impairs peripheral T4 to T3 conversion over 1-2 weeks
	* Any β-blocker can be used; long-acting agents more convenient
	* Do not inhibit hormone synthesis or prevent thyroid storm  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
  * Radioactive iodine
	* Effective for Graves' disease and toxic nodules
	* Contraindicated in pregnancy
	* Side effect: hypothyroidism (10-60% in first year, additional 2% annually)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Management Options for Graves' Disease**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]] 
  * Anti-thyroid drugs (carbimazole, propylthiouracil): inhibit thyroid peroxidase
  * Radioiodine (131I): actively concentrated in thyroid, causing tissue damage
  * Surgery (total thyroidectomy): permanent solution but carries risks
  * All options usually lead to hypothyroidism requiring T4 replacement
  * None affect Graves' eye disease (requires corticosteroids or surgical debulking)
* **Preoperative Optimization**
  * Goal: Achieve euthyroidism before surgery
  * Typical regimen: β-blocker plus antithyroid drugs for 7-14 days
  * Adjuncts: iodide solution, glucocorticoids in severe cases  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Anesthetic Management**
  * Continue all antithyroid medications through morning of surgery
  * Maintain adequate depth of anesthesia to prevent exaggerated sympathetic response
  * Avoid:
	* Ketamine (sympathetic stimulation)
	* Indirect-acting vasopressors (use direct-acting agents instead)
	* Epinephrine-containing solutions in regional anesthesia
  * Consider reduced initial dose of muscle relaxants (higher incidence of myasthenia gravis)
  * Use neuromuscular monitoring
  * MAC requirements unaffected by hyperthyroidism
  * Regional anesthesia is an excellent alternative when appropriate  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

### 3.4.5. THYROID STORM

* **Definition**
  * Life-threatening exacerbation of hyperthyroidism  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Triggers**
  * Surgery (especially on thyroid gland)
  * Trauma
  * Infection
  * Medication noncompliance
  * Stress
  * Nonthyroidal illness  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Clinical Features**
  * Hyperthermia (often >40°C)
  * Marked tachycardia, dysrhythmias
  * Myocardial ischemia
  * Congestive heart failure
  * Agitation, confusion, delirium
  * Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea
  * Extreme hypermetabolism  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Differential Diagnosis**
  * Malignant hyperthermia
  * Pheochromocytoma
  * Neuroleptic malignant syndrome
  * Sepsis
  * Light anesthesia with sympathetic response  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Management**
  * IV fluids
  * Sodium iodide 250 mg PO or IV q6h
  * Propylthiouracil 200-400 mg PO or via nasogastric tube q6h
  * Hydrocortisone 50-100 mg IV q6h
  * β-blockade (propranolol 10-40 mg PO q4-6h or esmolol infusion)
  * Cooling measures, acetaminophen (avoid salicylates)
  * Supportive care, management of organ dysfunction
  * Identify and treat precipitating event  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

⚠️ Thyroid storm is a medical emergency with mortality rates of 20-30% if untreated. It requires aggressive management with multiple modalites to block hormone synthesis, release, and peripheral effects while providing supportive care  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Hyperthyroidism manifests with multisystem effects due to increased metabolic rate and enhanced catecholamine sensitivity. Preoperative preparation with antithyroid drugs and β-blockers is essential to reduce perioperative risk. Thyroid storm is a life-threatening emergency requiring immediate multimodal therapy including antithyroid drugs, β-blockers, iodide, and corticosteroids.

## 3.5. HYPOTHYROIDISM

### 3.5.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Pathophysiology of hypothyroidism  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Perioperative implications and management  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Part C:** Clinical decision-making for hypothyroid patients  [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1-medical-knowledge|ⓘ]].


**Competency Focus:** , 

**Hypothyroidism is a relatively common disease (0.3-5% of adults) resulting from inadequate circulating levels of thyroid hormones, with varied presentations based on severity and duration**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

### 3.5.2. ETIOLOGY AND PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

* **Types and Causes**
  * Primary hypothyroidism (95% of cases): thyroid gland failure
	* Autoimmune (Hashimoto's thyroiditis) - most common
	* Iatrogenic (post-radioiodine therapy, surgical thyroidectomy)
	* Irradiation to the neck
	* Severe iodine depletion
	* Medications (amiodarone, lithium, iodides, antithyroid drugs)
	* Infiltrative disorders (amyloidosis, sarcoidosis)
	* Congenital defects
  * Secondary/tertiary hypothyroidism (5% of cases)
	* Pituitary or hypothalamic disease
	* Usually associated with other pituitary hormone deficiencies  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Pathophysiology**
  * Thyroid hormone deficiency leads to:
	* Decreased metabolic rate
	* Reduced oxygen consumption
	* Decreased heat production
	* Impaired substrate metabolism
	* Accumulation of glycosaminoglycans in interstitial spaces
	* Decreased responsiveness to catecholamines  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]

### 3.5.3. CLINICAL MANIFESTATIONS

* **General**
  * Fatigue, lethargy
  * Cold intolerance
  * Weight gain despite decreased appetite
  * Dry, cool skin
  * Myxedema (facial and periorbital edema)
  * Slow speech and movements
  * Delayed relaxation of deep tendon reflexes
  * Slow mental functioning  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Cardiovascular**
  * Bradycardia
  * Decreased cardiac output
  * Increased peripheral resistance
  * Narrow pulse pressure
  * Low-voltage ECG (due to pericardial effusion)
  * Reduced heart sounds
  * Cardiomegaly (heart failure rare without coexisting heart disease)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Respiratory**
  * Decreased ventilatory responsiveness to hypoxia and hypercapnia
  * Potential for sleep apnea
  * Risk of respiratory depression with sedatives and anesthetics  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Other**
  * Anemia (normocytic or macrocytic)
  * Coagulopathy
  * Hypothermia
  * Impaired renal free water clearance with hyponatremia
  * Decreased GI motility
  * Blunted stress response
  * Potential adrenal depression in longstanding disease  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]

### 3.5.4. TREATMENT AND ANESTHETIC CONSIDERATIONS

* **Medical Management**
  * Hormone replacement therapy (levothyroxine)
  * Starting dose based on age, weight, and cardiac status
  * Gradual titration in elderly or those with cardiac disease
  * Goal: normalize TSH levels
  * Special considerations during pregnancy (increased requirements)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Perioperative Considerations**
  * Controversy exists regarding management of hypothyroid surgical patients
  * Current evidence suggests:
	* Mild to moderate hypothyroidism: generally safe to proceed with surgery
	* Severe hypothyroidism: postpone elective surgery until treated
	* Higher incidence of intraoperative hypotension and postoperative complications (GI, neuropsychiatric) even with mild-moderate disease  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Anesthetic Management**
  * Most anesthetic medications can be used safely
  * Consider:
	* Slower drug metabolism
	* Increased sensitivity to CNS depressants
	* Risk of hypotension (maintain intravascular volume)
	* No significant decrease in MAC for volatile agents
	* Regional anesthesia appropriate if volume status maintained
  * Monitor closely for:
	* Hypotension
	* Bradycardia
	* Heart failure
	* Hypothermia (maintain normothermia scrupulously)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Patients with Coronary Artery Disease**
  * Careful balance between risks:
	* Untreated hypothyroidism may exacerbate heart failure
	* Rapid thyroid replacement can precipitate myocardial ischemia
  * Consider coronary revascularization before thyroid replacement in symptomatic patients  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

### 3.5.5. MYXEDEMA COMA

* **Definition**
  * Severe, decompensated hypothyroidism
  * Medical emergency with high mortality (25-50%)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Clinical Features**
  * Stupor or coma
  * Hypoventilation
  * Profound hypothermia
  * Hypotension
  * Hyponatremia
  * Hypoglycemia  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Triggers**
  * Infection
  * Trauma
  * Surgery
  * Exposure to cold
  * Sedatives, narcotics, anesthetics
  * Acute illness  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Management**
  * Only proceed with life-saving surgery
  * IV thyroid replacement
	* Loading dose of T4 (levothyroxine) 200-300 mcg IV
	* Followed by maintenance dose 50-200 mcg/day IV
	* Alternative: T3 (more rapid onset)
  * Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV (rule out coexisting adrenal insufficiency)
  * Supportive care, including:
	* Careful rewarming (avoid vasodilation)
	* Mechanical ventilation as needed
	* Cautious fluid management
	* Treatment of precipitating causes  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

⚠️ Thyroid replacement in patients with myxedema coma may precipitate myocardial ischemia, so careful cardiovascular monitoring is essential. Patients frequently have primary adrenal insufficiency requiring concurrent steroid coverage  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Hypothyroidism presents with multisystem effects of reduced metabolic rate. Mild to moderate cases generally tolerate surgery well, while severe hypothyroidism requires treatment before elective procedures. Anesthetic management focuses on cardiovascular stability, temperature maintenance, and cautious drug dosing. Myxedema coma is a life-threatening emergency requiring immediate thyroid hormone replacement and supportive care.

## 3.6. ANESTHESIA FOR THYROID SURGERY

### 3.6.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part B:** Specific considerations for thyroid surgery  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Part C:** Procedural skills and complication management  [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1-medical-knowledge|ⓘ]].


**Competency Focus:** , 

**Thyroidectomy requires special attention to airway management, nerve monitoring, and postoperative complications including bleeding, recurrent laryngeal nerve damage, and hypocalcemia**  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].

* **Indications for Surgery**
  * Thyroid cancer
  * Symptomatic goiter
  * Hyperthyroidism refractory to medical therapy
  * Suspicious nodules  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Preoperative Assessment**
  * Thyroid function status
  * Airway evaluation (potential difficult intubation)
  * Evaluate for tracheal compression/deviation on imaging
  * Vocal cord function assessment
  * Large substernal goiters can behave like anterior mediastinal masses  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Anesthetic Techniques**
  * General endotracheal anesthesia most common
  * Laryngeal mask airway increasingly used (allows real-time vocal cord visualization)
  * Bilateral superficial cervical plexus block for limited procedures
  * For minimally invasive approaches:
	* Robot-assisted transaxillary approach
	* Transoral thyroidectomy (requires nasal intubation)  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Airway Management**
  * Incidence of difficult intubation: 5-8% in thyroid surgery
  * Risk factors:
	* Thyroid cancer (stronger predictor than goiter size)
	* Large substernal component
	* Significant airway obstruction or tracheal deviation
  * For high-risk cases, consider awake fiberoptic intubation  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Intraoperative Nerve Monitoring**
  * Recurrent laryngeal nerve identification and preservation is critical
  * Methods include:
	* Direct nerve stimulation by surgeon to observe laryngeal muscle contraction
	* Nerve integrity monitor (NIM) endotracheal tube
	  * Has embedded electrodes at vocal cord level
	  * Allows electromyographic monitoring
	  * Requires proper positioning
	  * Avoid muscle relaxants and topical laryngeal anesthesia after intubation  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Postoperative Complications**
  * Recurrent laryngeal nerve damage
	* Unilateral: hoarseness, paralyzed vocal cord
	* Bilateral: stridor, aphonia, airway obstruction requiring immediate reintubation
  * External laryngeal nerve injury: voice weakness, change in pitch
  * Tracheal compression from hematoma (surgical emergency)
  * Tracheomalacia
  * Hypoparathyroidism
	* Develops 24-96 hours after total thyroidectomy
	* Signs: perioral numbness, paresthesia, carpopedal spasm, laryngeal stridor
	* Management: IV calcium chloride or gluconate
	* Monitor magnesium levels
  * Pneumothorax (with substernal goiter resection)  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Postoperative Care**
  * Evaluate vocal cord function before and after surgery
  * Perform extubation under optimal conditions
  * Be prepared for emergency reintubation
  * Monitor for signs of hypocalcemia and bleeding
  * Continue thyroid hormone replacement in total thyroidectomy  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Specialized Anesthesia Considerations**  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * Induction of anesthesia may require gaseous or awake fiber-optic technique for tracheal compression
  * Occasionally, tracheostomy under local anesthesia is the only option
  * Post-operative complications requiring immediate attention:
	* Hemorrhage causing rapid airway obstruction (requires urgent surgical review and clip removal)
	* Bilateral recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy (causes complete airway obstruction)
	* Severe hypocalcemia (may cause laryngospasm)
	* Tracheomalacia in patients with long-standing or very large goiters

💡 Assessment of vocal cord function can be performed by direct laryngoscopy or by asking the patient to phonate (saying "E") both preand postoperatively to detect nerve injury  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].

⚠️ Bilateral recurrent laryngeal nerve injury is an airway emergency requiring immediate reintubation. Neck hematoma causing airway compromise requires emergency evacuation, often at bedside prior to returning to the operating room  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Thyroid surgery poses unique challenges including difficult airway management, nerve preservation, and specific postoperative complications. Careful preoperative evaluation, appropriate airway management strategy, nerve monitoring, and vigilant postoperative observation for bleeding and hypocalcemia are essential for successful outcomes.

## 4.1. CALCIUM PHYSIOLOGY

### 4.1.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Calcium homeostasis and regulation  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Clinical implications of calcium disorders  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].



**Calcium is essential for numerous physiological processes including skeletal muscle contraction, cardiac function, coagulation, neurotransmitter release, and endocrine secretion**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

* **Total Body Calcium Distribution**
  * Normal adult contains approximately 1-2 kg of calcium
  * 99% of calcium is found in the skeleton as hydroxyapatite
  * 1% is in extracellular fluid and soft tissues  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
	  * 50% ionised calcium 1.2mmol/L
	  * 41% protein-bound calcium 1.2mmol/L
	  * 9% anion-bound calcium 0.2mmol/L
	  * The ionised calcium and anion-bound calcium are diffusable whereas the protein bound are non-diffusable
  * Total body calcium 25mmol
  * Daily turnover 15mmol
* **Plasma Calcium Forms**
  * Total serum calcium: 8.8-10.4 mg/dL
  * Present in three forms:
	* Protein-bound fraction (50%)
	  * Primarily bound to albumin (90% of protein-bound calcium)
	  * Albumin relationship: 0.8 mg/dL change in total Ca²⁺ per 1 g/dL change in albumin
	* Ionized fraction (45%) - physiologically active
	* Complexed but non-ionized fraction (5%) - bound to phosphate, bicarbonate, citrate  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Factors Affecting Ionized Calcium**
  * pH: Acidosis decreases protein binding (increases ionized Ca²⁺)
  * Temperature: Ionized calcium increases as temperature falls
  * Albumin concentration: Affects total but not ionized calcium
  * Free fatty acids: Can bind calcium and decrease ionized fraction  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Calcium Regulation**
  * Three mechanisms work together:
	* Absorption via GI tract
	* Excretion via kidneys
	* Mobilization from bone  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
  * Main regulatory hormones:
	* Parathyroid hormone (PTH)
	* Vitamin D (1,25-dihydroxycholecalciferol)
	* Calcitonin (minor role in humans)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Physiological Functions of Calcium**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]] 
  * Structural: Gives bone rigidity as calcium phosphate
  * Haemostasis: Essential cofactor in coagulation cascade
  * Resting membrane potential: Affects cell membrane sodium permeability
  * Neurotransmitter release: Calcium influx triggers exocytosis of vesicles
  * Excitation-contraction coupling: Essential for myosin-actin binding
  * Cell signaling: Important second messenger
* **Body Distribution of Calcium**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]] 
  * Total mass in adult ~1 kg
  * 99% in bone (cannot be rapidly mobilized)
  * 1% in teeth and soft tissues
  * Only 0.1% in extracellular fluid (one-third in plasma)
  * Normal plasma range: 2.2-2.6 mmol/L
  * Ionized Ca²⁺: 1.1-1.4 mmol/L (45% of total)

💡 Ionized calcium is the physiologically active form and should be measured directly in critically ill patients, patients with abnormal protein levels, or acid-base disturbances  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Calcium homeostasis is tightly regulated through the coordinated actions of PTH, vitamin D, and calcitonin acting on bone, kidneys, and the GI tract. The ionized fraction represents the physiologically active portion and can be affected by pH, albumin levels, and other factors independent of total calcium concentration.

## 4.2. PARATHYROID HORMONE AND VITAMIN D FUNCTION

### 4.2.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** PTH and vitamin D metabolism and function  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].



**Parathyroid hormone (PTH) and vitamin D work together as the primary regulators of calcium homeostasis, with complementary actions on bone, kidney, and intestine**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

![[Pasted image 20250723211129.png]]

* **Parathyroid Hormone (PTH)**
  * Secreted by chief cells in parathyroid glands in response to hypocalcemia (low extracellular ionised calcium concentration) and hypomagnesemia, and beta-adrenergic stimulation
  * Direct effects:
	* Bone: Increases calcium mobilization through actions on osteoclasts and osteoblasts (increase RANK receptor activity)
	* Kidney: Increases calcium reabsorption in distal tubule and loop of Henle
	* Kidney: Increases phosphate excretion in proximal tubule (phosphaturic) by increasing renal 1-alpha-hydroxylase
  * Indirect effects:
	* Upregulates 1-alpha-hydroxylase in kidney, increasing 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D production
	* Influences GI calcium absorption via vitamin D effects  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
  * Secretion regulated primarily by:
	* Serum ionized calcium (negative feedback)
	* Magnesium levels (acute hypomagnesemia stimulates, chronic depletion inhibits)
	* Phosphate levels (indirectly via effects on calcium)
	* Vitamin D (negative feedback)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Vitamin D**
  * Sources:
	* Dietary absorption
	* Synthesis in skin via ultraviolet irradiation of 7-dehydrocholesterol
  * Metabolism:
	* Vitamin D (cholecalciferol) is inactive
	* Hydroxylated in liver to 25-hydroxycholecalciferol (25-OHD)
	  * Major circulating form, not regulated by hormones
	* Further hydroxylated in kidney to active metabolites:
	  * 1,25-dihydroxycholecalciferol [1,25(OH)₂D] - active form
	  * 24,25-dihydroxycholecalciferol [24,25(OH)₂D] - less active  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
  * Regulation of 1,25(OH)₂D production:
	* Stimulated by: hypocalcemia, hypophosphatemia, PTH
	* Inhibited by: hypercalcemia, hyperphosphatemia
  * Actions:
	* Increases intestinal absorption of calcium and phosphate
	* Increases renal reabsorption of calcium and phosphate
	* Enhances bone mineralization and remodeling  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Vitamin D Metabolism Process**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]] 
  * In skin: 7-dehydrocholesterol converted to cholecalciferol (vitamin D3) by UVB radiation
  * In liver: vitamin D3 is 25-hydroxylated to calcidiol (25-OHD)
  * In kidney: calcidiol is 1-α-hydroxylated to calcitriol (1,25-OH2D) - active form
  * Rate-determining step: 1-α-hydroxylation, upregulated by PTH
* **Calcitonin**
  * Produced by parafollicular (C) cells of thyroid gland
  ![[Pasted image 20250723211452.png]]
  * Secretion stimulated by:
	* Hypercalcemia (>2.4mmol/L)
	* Gastrin
	* Catecholamines
  * Actions:
	* Reduces calcium mobilization from bone
	* Increases calcium and phosphate excretion by kidney
	* Decreases calcium absorption by GI tract
	* Minimal physiologic importance in adult humans, but pharmacologic uses  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Calcium Homeostasis Regulation**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]] 
  * Low plasma ionized Ca²⁺:
	* Increases PTH secretion
	* Increases vitamin D activation
	* Increases intestinal absorption and renal reabsorption
  * High plasma ionized Ca²⁺:
	* Decreases PTH secretion
	* Decreases vitamin D activation
	* Increases calcitonin
	* Decreases intestinal absorption and renal reabsorption

**Bottom Line:** PTH and vitamin D are the principal regulators of calcium homeostasis, with PTH primarily responding to acute changes in serum calcium by mobilizing calcium from bone and reducing renal excretion, while vitamin D enhances intestinal calcium absorption and facilitates bone mineralization. Understanding this coordinated system is essential for managing calcium disorders.

## 4.3. HYPERPARATHYROIDISM

### 4.3.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Pathophysiology of hyperparathyroidism  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Perioperative implications and management  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Part C:** Clinical decision-making for hypercalcemic patients  [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1-medical-knowledge|ⓘ]].


**Competency Focus:** , 

**Hyperparathyroidism is characterized by excessive PTH secretion leading to hypercalcemia and associated clinical manifestations**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

### 4.3.2. ETIOLOGY AND PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

* **Primary Hyperparathyroidism**
  * Causes:
	* Parathyroid adenoma (90% of cases)
	* Parathyroid hyperplasia (9%)
	* Parathyroid carcinoma (rare, <1%)
	* Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia (MEN) syndromes
	  * MEN I: parathyroid, pancreatic, pituitary
	  * MEN IIA: parathyroid, medullary thyroid carcinoma, pheochromocytoma  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
  * Pathophysiology:
	* Autonomous overproduction of PTH
	* Increased calcium mobilization from bone
	* Enhanced renal calcium reabsorption
	* Increased intestinal calcium absorption via vitamin D  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Secondary Hyperparathyroidism**
  * Increased parathyroid function due to chronic hypocalcemic or hyperphosphatemic conditions
  * Common causes:
	* Chronic renal disease (decreased phosphate excretion, decreased vitamin D metabolism)
	* Vitamin D deficiency
	* Malabsorption syndromes
	* Chronic hypocalcemia from any cause  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Tertiary Hyperparathyroidism**
  * Development of autonomous hyperparathyroidism after prolonged secondary hyperparathyroidism
  * Parathyroid glands continue to secrete excess PTH despite normalization of calcium
  * Common in long-term renal failure patients, especially after transplantation  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]

### 4.3.3. CLINICAL MANIFESTATIONS

* **Renal Manifestations**
  * Nephrolithiasis (most common, 60-70% of patients)
  * Nephrocalcinosis
  * Polyuria and polydipsia  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Skeletal Manifestations**
  * Increased bone turnover and demineralization
  * Subperiosteal bone resorption
  * Osteitis fibrosa cystica (advanced cases)
  * Pathologic fractures
  * Bone pain  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Gastrointestinal Manifestations**
  * Constipation
  * Nausea and vomiting
  * Peptic ulcer disease
  * Pancreatitis
  * Cholelithiasis  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Neuromuscular Manifestations**
  * Generalized weakness and fatigue
  * Proximal myopathy
  * Depression, memory loss, confusion
  * Psychosis in severe cases  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Cardiovascular Manifestations**
  * Hypertension (20-50% of patients)
  * Enhanced cardiac contractility (in early stages)
  * Shortened QT interval on ECG
  * At high calcium levels (>15-20 mg/dL):
	* Prolonged PR interval and QRS complex
	* Heart block or bundle branch block
	* Bradycardia
	* Cardiac arrest with extreme hypercalcemia  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]

### 4.3.4. TREATMENT AND ANESTHETIC CONSIDERATIONS

* **Medical Management of Hypercalcemia**
  * Target corrected calcium <3 and asymptomatic
  * Acute severe hypercalcemia (>15 mg/dL):
	* Volume expansion with normal saline
	* Furosemide to promote calcium excretion
	* Correction of hypophosphatemia if present
	* Bisphosphonates to inhibit osteoclast activity
	* Calcitonin for temporary reduction
	* Glucocorticoids for specific causes (sarcoidosis, vitamin D intoxication)
	* Hemodialysis for extreme cases  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Surgical Management**
  * Indications:
	* Symptomatic disease
	* Asymptomatic with significant hypercalcemia
	* Reduced bone density
	* Age <50 years
	* Impaired renal function
  * Procedures:
	* Conventional bilateral neck exploration
	* Minimally invasive parathyroidectomy (with preoperative localization)
	* Cortical-sparing subtotal parathyroidectomy (for hyperplasia)  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Preoperative Considerations**
  * Correct intravascular volume and electrolyte abnormalities
  * Evaluate for renal, cardiac, and CNS abnormalities
  * Localization studies:
	* Sestamibi scan
	* Ultrasound
	* CT/MRI
	* Selective venous sampling  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Anesthetic Management**
  * No specific advantage to particular anesthetic technique
  * General anesthesia with endotracheal tube most common
  * Supraglottic airways can be used successfully
  * Monitored anesthesia care with bilateral cervical plexus block for minimally invasive approach
  * Special considerations:
	* Altered responses to neuromuscular blocking drugs (increased requirement for vecuronium)
	* Careful positioning for osteopenic patients
	* Some surgeons prefer avoiding propofol within 15 minutes of intraoperative PTH assay  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Intraoperative Considerations**
  * Rapid PTH assay
	* Measures intact PTH with half-life of 3-5 minutes
	* Drop of >50% from baseline 10 minutes after gland removal suggests cure
	* Requires freely back-flowing IV catheter for sampling  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Postoperative Complications**
  * Recurrent laryngeal nerve injury
	* Unilateral: hoarseness
	* Bilateral: aphonia, airway obstruction requiring immediate intubation
  * Bleeding/hematoma
  * Hypocalcemia
	* "Hungry bone syndrome" after removal of hyperactive gland
	* May be severe and prolonged
	* Monitor calcium, magnesium, phosphorus closely
	* Typically nadirs 3-7 days postoperatively
	* Treat with calcium and vitamin D supplements  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Treatment Options for Hyperparathyroidism**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]] 
  * Besides surgery, hyperparathyroidism may be treated with "calcimimetics" like cinacalcet
  * Cinacalcet increases sensitivity of calcium-sensing receptors, decreasing PTH secretion
  * Used in primary, secondary, and tertiary hyperparathyroidism

⚠️ Careful positioning of osteopenic patients is necessary during surgery to prevent pathologic bone fractures. Additionally, monitoring for hypocalcemia is critical after parathyroidectomy, as "hungry bone syndrome" can cause severe, prolonged hypocalcemia requiring aggressive supplementation  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Hyperparathyroidism presents with multisystem manifestations of hypercalcemia including renal, skeletal, gastrointestinal, and cardiovascular effects. Anesthetic management focuses on volume status optimization, careful drug selection, and postoperative monitoring for hypocalcemia. Minimally invasive approaches are increasingly used, facilitated by intraoperative PTH assays to confirm successful gland removal.

## 4.4. HYPOPARATHYROIDISM

### 4.4.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Pathophysiology of hypoparathyroidism  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Clinical implications and management  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].


**Competency Focus:** , 

**Hypoparathyroidism results from underproduction of PTH or end-organ resistance to PTH, leading to hypocalcemia and associated symptoms**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

### 4.4.2. ETIOLOGY AND PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

* **Causes of PTH Deficiency**
  * Iatrogenic (most common)
	* Surgical removal during thyroidectomy or parathyroidectomy
	* Damage from neck radiation or 131I therapy
  * Idiopathic
	* Autoimmune
	* Genetic disorders
  * Secondary causes
	* Severe hypomagnesemia (<0.8 mEq/L)
	  * Suppresses PTH secretion
	  * Interferes with PTH action
	* Infiltrative diseases
	  * Malignancy
	  * Amyloidosis
	  * Granulomatous diseases
	* Neck trauma  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Pseudohypoparathyroidism**
  * End-organ resistance to PTH
  * Normal or elevated PTH levels
  * Hypocalcemia and hyperphosphatemia
  * Often associated with phenotypic characteristics (Albright's hereditary osteodystrophy)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Other Causes of Hypocalcemia**
  * Renal insufficiency
	* Phosphorus retention
	* Impaired 1,25(OH)₂D synthesis
  * Vitamin D deficiency or resistance
  * Pancreatitis
  * Burns (calcium sequestration)
  * Rapid blood transfusion (citrate binding)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]

### 4.4.3. CLINICAL FEATURES AND TREATMENT

* **Clinical Manifestations**
  * Neuronal irritability and muscle dysfunction
	* Tetany
	* Skeletal muscle spasms
	* Seizures
	* Paresthesias
	* Carpopedal spasm
  * Latent tetany signs
	* Chvostek sign (facial muscle contraction when tapping facial nerve)
	* Trousseau sign (carpopedal spasm after blood pressure cuff inflation for 3 minutes)
  * Other symptoms
	* Fatigue
	* Depression
	* Skeletal muscle cramps
	* Stridor and apnea (acute onset after surgery)
  * Cardiovascular effects
	* Congestive heart failure
	* Hypotension
	* Reduced sensitivity to β-adrenergic agonists
	* Prolonged QT interval on ECG
  * Chronic manifestations
	* Cataracts
	* Basal ganglia calcifications
	* Dental abnormalities
	* Dry, coarse skin  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Treatment**
  * Acute symptomatic hypocalcemia
	* IV calcium gluconate 10-20 mL of 10% solution diluted and given slowly
	* Followed by continuous infusion (0.5-1.5 mg/kg/h of elemental calcium)
	* Monitor serum calcium levels and symptoms
  * Chronic management
	* Oral calcium supplementation
	* Vitamin D or analogs (calcitriol)
	* Monitor for hypercalciuria and nephrolithiasis
  * Special considerations
	* Correct hypomagnesemia if present
	* Treat hyperphosphatemia with dietary restriction and phosphate binders
	* Thiazide diuretics may reduce urinary calcium excretion  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Perioperative Management**
  * Continue calcium and vitamin D supplementation
  * Monitor ionized calcium levels
  * Have IV calcium available for acute symptoms
  * Be alert for laryngospasm or stridor in acute postoperative hypocalcemia
  * Avoid alkalosis (decreases ionized calcium)
  * Cautious use of neuromuscular blockers (may have altered response)  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Impact of Underlying Kidney and Liver Disease**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]] 
  * In chronic kidney disease:
	* 1-α-hydroxylation of vitamin D impaired
	* Renal calcium excretion exceeds intestinal absorption
	* Secondary hyperparathyroidism develops, causing bone demineralization (renal osteodystrophy)
	* Treatment: pre-activated vitamin D (alfacalcidol)
  * In liver disease:
	* Complex etiology (dietary deficiency, gonadal failure in alcoholism)
	* Impaired 25-hydroxylation in severe dysfunction

⚠️ Laryngeal stridor progressing to laryngospasm may be one of the first indications of hypocalcemic tetany after thyroid or parathyroid surgery, requiring immediate treatment with IV calcium  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Hypoparathyroidism manifests with signs of neuromuscular irritability due to hypocalcemia, most commonly occurring after thyroid or parathyroid surgery. Acute management focuses on IV calcium administration, while chronic therapy includes oral calcium and vitamin D supplementation. Perioperative management includes vigilance for signs of hypocalcemia and readiness to treat acute symptoms.

## 4.5. ANESTHESIA FOR PARATHYROID SURGERY

### 4.5.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part B:** Specific considerations for parathyroid surgery  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Part C:** Procedural skills and complication management  [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1-medical-knowledge|ⓘ]].


**Competency Focus:** , 

**Parathyroid surgery has evolved toward minimally invasive approaches for solitary adenomas, though conventional bilateral exploration remains common for hyperplasia or multiple gland disease**  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].

* **Preoperative Considerations**
  * Evaluate and manage hypercalcemia
  * Review localization studies
  * Assess airway and comorbidities
  * Evaluate for MEN syndromes if suspected  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Surgical Approaches**
  * Conventional bilateral neck exploration
	* Used for hyperplasia or negative localization studies
	* Allows examination of all four glands
  * Minimally invasive parathyroidectomy
	* Focused approach based on preoperative localization
	* Smaller incision, less dissection
	* Associated with shorter operative time and hospital stay
	* Lower complication rates
	* Options include:
	  * Focused lateral approach
	  * Minimally invasive radio-guided parathyroidectomy
	  * Video-assisted or endoscopic approaches  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Anesthetic Techniques**
  * General anesthesia with endotracheal intubation most common
  * Supraglottic airway can be used successfully
  * Monitored anesthesia care with bilateral cervical plexus block for minimally invasive approaches
	* Provides excellent analgesia
	* Allows for shorter hospital stay
	* Avoids general anesthesia complications  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Intraoperative Management**
  * Positioning: neck extension with shoulder roll
  * Intraoperative PTH monitoring
	* Baseline PTH drawn after induction but before incision
	* Additional measurement 10 minutes after gland excision
	* > 50% drop from baseline indicates likely cure
	* May require freely back-flowing IV for sampling
	* Some surgeons prefer avoiding propofol within 15 minutes of sampling (potential interference with assay)  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Postoperative Complications**
  * Recurrent laryngeal nerve injury
	* Unilateral: hoarseness
	* Bilateral (rare): airway obstruction requiring immediate intubation
  * Bleeding and hematoma
  * Hypocalcemia
	* Temporary in 10-30% of patients
	* Permanent in 1-2%
	* "Hungry bone syndrome" in patients with significant preoperative bone disease
	* Typically develops within 24-48 hours
	* Monitor calcium, magnesium, and phosphorus levels  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Postoperative Care**
  * Monitor for hypocalcemia (may be delayed 3-4 days)
  * Assess vocal cord function
  * Pain management
  * Most patients can be discharged same day or next morning for minimally invasive procedures  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

💡 Intraoperative PTH monitoring has revolutionized parathyroid surgery by allowing confirmation of successful adenoma removal during the procedure, enabling minimally invasive approaches in many patients  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Parathyroid surgery has evolved toward minimally invasive approaches guided by preoperative localization and intraoperative PTH monitoring. Anesthetic options include general anesthesia or regional techniques for minimally invasive cases. Key considerations include managing hypercalcemia preoperatively and monitoring for hypocalcemia postoperatively.

# 5. ADRENAL CORTEX





## 5.1. ANATOMY AND PHYSIOLOGY

### 5.1.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Structure and function of the adrenal cortex  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Clinical application to perioperative care  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].



**The adrenal cortex functions to synthesize and secrete three types of steroid hormones: glucocorticoids, mineralocorticoids, and androgens**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

* **Anatomical Structure**
  * Bilateral triangular-shaped organs located at the superior pole of each kidney at T12 level
  * Retroperitoneal, encased in protective fatty capsule
  * Divided into cortex (outer 70%) and medulla (inner 30%)
  * Cortex organized into three distinct zones:
	* Zona glomerulosa (15%): Outermost layer, produces mineralocorticoids (aldosterone)
	* Zona fasciculata (50%): Middle layer, produces glucocorticoids (cortisol)
	* Zona reticularis (5%): Inner layer, produces sex steroids (mainly androgens)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Blood Supply**
  * Arterial: Superior, middle, and inferior adrenal arteries
  * Venous: Single vein on each side
	* Right adrenal vein drains directly into inferior vena cava
	* Left adrenal vein drains into left renal vein
  * Rich vascular supply relative to gland size  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Hormone Synthesis**
  * All adrenal steroids derived from cholesterol
  * Synthesized on demand rather than stored in large amounts
  * Specific enzymatic pathways determine which hormones are produced in each zone
  * Cortisol and aldosterone are essential hormones
  * Adrenal androgens of minor physiological significance in adults  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Regulatory Systems**
  * Glucocorticoids: Hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis
  * Mineralocorticoids: Renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system
  * Androgens: Partially influenced by ACTH  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Detailed Anatomy**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]] 
  * Triangular organs close to superior poles of kidneys at T12 level
  * Retroperitoneal location, surrounded by fat pad and renal fascia
  * Embryological origins:
	* Adrenal cortex: derived from mesoderm, 70% of gland weight
	* Adrenal medulla: derived from neural crest cells, innervated by T5-T9 pre-ganglionic sympathetic neurons

**Bottom Line:** The adrenal cortex is organized into three distinct layers producing mineralocorticoids, glucocorticoids, and sex steroids from cholesterol. The major biologic effects of adrenal cortical hyperfunction or hypofunction occur due to cortisol or aldosterone excess or deficiency, which can significantly impact perioperative management.

## 5.2. GLUCOCORTICOID PHYSIOLOGY

### 5.2.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Glucocorticoid synthesis, regulation, and physiological effects  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part C:** Application to stress response and perioperative care  [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1-medical-knowledge|ⓘ]].



**Cortisol (hydrocortisone) is the most potent endogenous glucocorticoid, produced under the control of ACTH from the anterior pituitary and affecting multiple physiological systems**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

* **Synthesis and Secretion**
  * Produced in zona fasciculata
  * Daily production approximately 10 mg (maximal output rarely more than 200 mg)
  * Circulating hormone mostly bound to cortisol-binding globulin (α-globulin)
  * Only free hormone (small fraction) exerts biological effects
  * Metabolism primarily by liver; excreted in urine as 17-hydroxycorticosteroids
  * In elderly, cortisol secretion decreases by 30%, but plasma levels remain normal due to decreased clearance  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Regulation - Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Adrenal (HPA) Axis**
  * Corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) from hypothalamus stimulates ACTH
  * ACTH (from pro-opiomelanocortin precursor) stimulates cortisol production
  * Cortisol exerts negative feedback on CRH and ACTH
  * ACTH follows diurnal pattern:
	* Peak levels in early morning (around wakening)
	* Lowest levels in evening
  * Stress (physical or psychological) stimulates ACTH release regardless of circulating cortisol levels  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Physiological Effects**
  * Carbohydrate, Protein, and Fat Metabolism
	* Enhances gluconeogenesis
	* Elevates blood glucose
	* Promotes hepatic glycogen synthesis
	* Catabolic effect on muscle tissue (protein degradation)
	* Negative nitrogen balance
	* In supraphysiologic amounts, suppresses growth hormone
  * Anti-inflammatory Actions
	* Stabilizes lysosomes
	* Promotes capillary integrity
	* Antagonizes leukocyte migration
	* Reduces white cell adherence to vascular endothelium
	* Diminishes monocyte and macrophage killing potential
  * Cardiovascular and Respiratory
	* Facilitates catecholamine action on blood vessels and heart
	* Maintains normal vascular reactivity
	* Essential for normal cardiac contractility
  * Other Effects
	* Facilitates free water clearance
	* Maintains blood pressure
	* Weak mineralocorticoid effect
	* Promotes appetite
	* Stimulates hematopoiesis
	* Induces liver enzymes  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Mechanism of Action**
  * Diffuses into cytoplasm of target cells
  * Binds to specific high-affinity receptor proteins
  * Hormone-receptor complex enters nucleus
  * Alters gene transcription
  * Leads to synthesis of regulatory proteins  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Chronic Excess Effects**
  * Osteoporosis
  * Anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects
  * CNS effects (psychosis, memory loss)
  * Peptic ulceration  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]

💡 The combined immunosuppressive and anti-inflammatory properties of glucocorticoids make them valuable therapeutic agents for a wide range of conditions, including autoimmune diseases, allergic reactions, and organ transplantation  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Cortisol, the primary glucocorticoid, affects virtually all body systems with metabolic, anti-inflammatory, and cardiovascular effects. Its production follows a diurnal pattern and is regulated by the HPA axis with negative feedback. Understanding cortisol physiology is essential for managing perioperative stress and steroid replacement in patients with adrenal insufficiency.

## 5.3. MINERALOCORTICOID PHYSIOLOGY

### 5.3.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Mineralocorticoid synthesis, regulation, and physiological effects  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part C:** Application to fluid and electrolyte management  [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1-medical-knowledge|ⓘ]].



**Aldosterone is the most potent mineralocorticoid produced by the adrenal gland, regulating extracellular volume and potassium homeostasis through actions on the distal convoluted tubule of the kidney**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

* **Synthesis and Actions**
  * Produced in zona glomerulosa
  * Not stored in significant amounts; synthesized on demand
  * Acts on receptors in:
	* Distal convoluted tubule of kidney (primary site)
	* Sweat glands
	* Salivary glands
	* Gastrointestinal tract
  * Major functions:
	* Promotes sodium reabsorption
	* Enhances potassium secretion
	* Increases hydrogen ion secretion
	* Regulates extracellular volume  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Regulation - Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System (RAAS)**
  * Primary regulatory system for aldosterone production
  * Process:
	* Juxtaglomerular apparatus in kidney produces renin in response to:
	  * Decreased renal perfusion pressure
	  * Sympathetic stimulation
	  * Decreased sodium delivery to distal tubule
	* Renin converts angiotensinogen (from liver) to angiotensin I
	* Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE, primarily in lung) converts angiotensin I to angiotensin II
	* Angiotensin II stimulates adrenal cortex to produce aldosterone
  * Other stimuli for aldosterone secretion:
	* Hyperkalemia (direct effect, independent of renin)
	* Hyponatremia (to lesser degree)
	* ACTH (minor effect)
	* Prostaglandin E  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
	![[Pasted image 20250723204431.png]]
* **Cellular Mechanism of Action**
  * Binds to mineralocorticoid receptors in target tissues
  * Increases:
	* Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump activity
	* Expression of sodium channels in luminal membrane
	* Mitochondrial activity in tubular cells
  * Results in:
	* Increased sodium reabsorption
	* Increased potassium excretion
	* Increased hydrogen ion secretion (contributing to metabolic alkalosis)
	* Increased water retention (secondary to sodium retention)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Physiological Significance**
  * Most important regulator of extracellular volume status
  * Critical for maintaining sodium and potassium balance
  * Essential for normal blood pressure regulation
  * Interacts with other systems (natriuretic peptides, vasopressin) to fine-tune fluid balance  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]

⚠️ The renin-angiotensin system produces angiotensin II, which is the most potent vasopressor produced in the body. This system's activity can be significantly altered by medications such as ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, and diuretics, impacting perioperative hemodynamics  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Aldosterone is the key mineralocorticoid that regulates sodium retention, potassium excretion, and consequently extracellular fluid volume. Its secretion is primarily controlled by the renin-angiotensin system and serum potassium levels, providing a critical link between renal function, electrolyte balance, and blood pressure regulation.

## 5.4. GLUCOCORTICOID EXCESS (CUSHING SYNDROME)

### 5.4.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Pathophysiology of glucocorticoid excess  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Perioperative implications and management  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Part C:** Management of steroid excess in perioperative period  [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1-medical-knowledge|ⓘ]].


**Competency Focus:** , 

**Cushing syndrome is characterized by excess cortisol production from either endogenous overproduction or exogenous glucocorticoid therapy, resulting in specific clinical manifestations and metabolic derangements**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

* **Etiology**
  * Exogenous (most common overall)
	* Prolonged administration of glucocorticoids for medical conditions
  * Endogenous causes:
	* ACTH-dependent (80% of endogenous cases)
	  * Pituitary microadenoma (Cushing disease)
	  * Ectopic ACTH production (lung, kidney, pancreatic tumors)
	* ACTH-independent (20% of endogenous cases)
	  * Adrenal adenoma
	  * Adrenal carcinoma (approximately half of adrenal-dependent cases)
	  * Bilateral adrenal hyperplasia  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Clinical Manifestations**
  * Physical Appearance
	* Central obesity with thin extremities
	* "Moon facies"
	* Buffalo hump
	* Supraclavicular fat pads
	* Facial plethora
	* Abdominal striae (purple)
	* Thin skin, easy bruising
  * Metabolic Effects
	* Hyperglycemia (60% of patients)
	* Overt diabetes mellitus (up to 20%)
	* Dyslipidemia
  * Cardiovascular
	* Hypertension (80% of patients)
	* Fluid retention
	* Increased cardiovascular risk
  * Musculoskeletal
	* Proximal muscle weakness
	* Osteopenia/osteoporosis
	* Pathologic fractures
	* Growth retardation in children
  * Neuropsychiatric
	* Emotional lability
	* Depression
	* Psychosis
	* Sleep disturbances
  * Other
	  * Increased gastric acid secretion
	* Increased susceptibility to infection
	* Menstrual irregularities
	* Hirsutism
	* Impaired wound healing
	* Hypokalemic alkalosis (especially with ectopic ACTH)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Diagnosis**
  * Laboratory findings:
	* Elevated plasma and urinary cortisol
	* Elevated urinary 17-hydroxycorticosteroids
	* Loss of normal diurnal variation in cortisol levels
	* Failure to suppress with low-dose dexamethasone
  * Differential diagnosis between causes:
	* Simultaneous measurement of plasma ACTH and cortisol
	* High-dose dexamethasone suppression test
	  * Pituitary adenomas often show some suppression
	  * Adrenal tumors and ectopic ACTH sources typically don't suppress
	* Inferior petrosal sinus sampling for ACTH (distinguishes pituitary from ectopic source)
	* Imaging studies (pituitary MRI, adrenal CT/MRI)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Anesthetic Management**
  * Preoperative preparation:
	* Treat hypertension
	* Control diabetes
	* Normalize fluid and electrolyte abnormalities
	* Consider diuresis with spironolactone to mobilize fluid and normalize potassium
  * Intraoperative considerations:
	* Careful positioning of osteopenic patients
	* Conservative approach to muscle relaxants if weakness present
	* Anticipate difficult IV access and fragile vessels
	* Stress-dose steroids for bilateral adrenalectomy
  * Perioperative glucocorticoid management:
	* For bilateral adrenalectomy: full replacement at stress doses
	* Dose reduction by approximately 50% per day until maintenance (20-30 mg/d)
	* Maintenance mineralocorticoid (fludrocortisone 0.05-0.1 mg/d) typically needed after day 5
	* For unilateral adrenalectomy: contralateral gland may recover function over time  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

⚠️ Patients with Cushing syndrome have thin, fragile skin, osteoporosis, and hypercoagulability. Careful padding and positioning, gentle tissue handling, and consideration of thromboprophylaxis are important perioperative considerations  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Cushing syndrome results from excess glucocorticoids causing characteristic physical changes, metabolic derangements, and multisystem effects. Perioperative management focuses on controlling comorbidities, providing appropriate steroid coverage, careful positioning, and vigilance for cardiovascular and metabolic complications. Postoperative steroid management depends on whether unilateral or bilateral adrenalectomy was performed.

## 5.5. MINERALOCORTICOID EXCESS

### 5.5.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Pathophysiology of mineralocorticoid excess  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Clinical implications and management  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].



**Excess aldosterone secretion increases renal tubular exchange of sodium for potassium and hydrogen ions, leading to hypertension, hypokalemic alkalosis, skeletal muscle weakness, and fatigue**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

* **Primary Hyperaldosteronism (Conn Syndrome)**
  * Caused by autonomous adrenal production of aldosterone
  * Etiology:
	* Aldosterone-producing adrenal adenoma (60-70%)
	* Bilateral adrenal hyperplasia (30-40%)
	* Adrenal carcinoma (rare)
	* Familial hyperaldosteronism (rare)
  * Prevalence: Approximately 1% of unselected hypertensive patients  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Secondary Hyperaldosteronism**
  * Results from elevated renin production
  * Causes:
	* Renal artery stenosis
	* Accelerated hypertension
	* Renin-secreting tumors
	* Cirrhosis with ascites
	* Nephrotic syndrome
	* Congestive heart failure
	* Volume depletion  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Clinical Manifestations**
  * Hypertension (often resistant to standard therapy)
  * Hypokalemia (but may be normal in 40% of cases)
  * Metabolic alkalosis
  * Muscle weakness and fatigue
  * Polyuria and polydipsia
  * Headaches
  * Absence of edema (distinguishing feature from other causes of hypertension)
  * Normal or low plasma renin activity (in primary hyperaldosteronism)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Diagnosis**
  * Screening:
	* Plasma aldosterone-to-renin ratio (elevated in primary hyperaldosteronism)
	* Most useful in patients with hypertension and hypokalemia not on potassium-wasting diuretics
  * Confirmatory tests:
	* Saline suppression test
	* Fludrocortisone suppression test
	* Captopril challenge test
  * Localization:
	* Adrenal CT or MRI
	* Adrenal venous sampling (gold standard for lateralization)  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Anesthetic Considerations**
  * Preoperative preparation:
	* Restore intravascular volume
	* Normalize electrolyte concentrations
	* Control hypertension and hypokalemia with:
	  * Aldosterone antagonist (spironolactone)
	  * Sodium restriction
	* Potassium supplementation (total body deficit may exceed 300 mEq)
	* Assess and treat complications of chronic hypertension
  * Intraoperative management:
	* Similar approach to pheochromocytoma surgery
	* Invasive hemodynamic monitoring for extensive disease or significant cardiac dysfunction
	* Careful fluid and electrolyte management
	* Vigilance for arrhythmias related to electrolyte disturbances  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

💡 Despite significant volume expansion, patients with primary hyperaldosteronism characteristically do not have edema due to the "aldosterone escape" phenomenon, where increased atrial natriuretic peptide and pressure natriuresis limit sodium retention  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Mineralocorticoid excess primarily manifests as hypertension and hypokalemic alkalosis. Preoperative management focuses on restoring normal volume status, correcting electrolyte abnormalities, and controlling blood pressure with aldosterone antagonists. Perioperative care includes careful hemodynamic monitoring and vigilance for cardiac arrhythmias related to electrolyte disturbances.

## 5.6. ADRENAL INSUFFICIENCY (ADDISON DISEASE)

### 5.6.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Pathophysiology of adrenal insufficiency  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Perioperative implications and management  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Part C:** Management of adrenal crisis  [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1-medical-knowledge|ⓘ]].


**Competency Focus:** , 

**Adrenal insufficiency results from inadequate production of adrenal steroid hormones, leading to characteristic symptoms and potentially life-threatening adrenal crisis**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

* **Types and Etiology**
  * Primary Adrenal Insufficiency (Addison Disease)
	* Direct failure of adrenal gland to produce sufficient hormones
	* Causes:
	  * Autoimmune adrenalitis (most common in developed countries)
	  * Tuberculosis (historically common, still prevalent in developing countries)
	  * HIV infection
	  * Fungal infections
	  * Metastatic cancer
	  * Adrenal hemorrhage (Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome)
	  * Medications (ketoconazole, etomidate, mitotane)
	  * Congenital adrenal hyperplasia
	* Results in deficiency of both glucocorticoids and mineralocorticoids
  * Secondary Adrenal Insufficiency
	* Due to insufficient ACTH production by pituitary
	* Causes:
	  * Pituitary tumor or surgery
	  * Pituitary radiation
	  * Exogenous glucocorticoid therapy (most common cause overall)
	  * Pituitary infarction (Sheehan syndrome)
	* Primarily glucocorticoid deficiency; mineralocorticoid function often preserved  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Clinical Presentation**
  * Chronic/Gradual Onset
	* Fatigue, weakness, anorexia, weight loss
	* Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain
	* Hypotension, orthostatic hypotension
	* Salt craving (primary adrenal insufficiency)
	* Hyperpigmentation (only in primary disease, due to increased ACTH)
	* Vitiligo and other autoimmune manifestations (in autoimmune etiology)
  * Acute Crisis
	* Profound hypotension/shock
	* Severe abdominal pain, vomiting, diarrhea
	* Fever
	* Altered mental status
	* Hypoglycemia
	* Laboratory findings:
	  * Primary: Hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis
	  * Secondary: Hyponatremia, usually normal potassium
	* Precipitating factors include:
	  * Infection
	  * Trauma
	  * Surgery
	  * Cessation of glucocorticoid therapy  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Diagnosis**
  * Basal cortisol levels
	* Morning cortisol <3 μg/dL suggests adrenal insufficiency
	* > 18 μg/dL essentially rules it out
	* Intermediate values require stimulation testing
  * ACTH stimulation test
	* Administer 250 μg synthetic ACTH intravenously
	* Measure cortisol at 0, 30, and 60 minutes
	* Normal response: Peak cortisol >18-20 μg/dL
	* Differentiating primary vs. secondary:
	  * Primary: Elevated baseline ACTH, minimal/no cortisol response
	  * Secondary: Low/normal ACTH, subnormal cortisol response
  * Other tests:
	* CRH stimulation test (for secondary forms)
	* Insulin tolerance test
	* Electrolytes, BUN, creatinine
	* Abdominal CT (for primary disease)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Treatment**
  * Chronic Management
	* Glucocorticoid replacement:
	  * Hydrocortisone 15-25 mg daily (divided doses, higher in morning)
	  * Prednisone 5 mg morning, 2.5 mg evening (alternative)
	* Mineralocorticoid replacement (for primary disease):
	  * Fludrocortisone 0.05-0.1 mg daily
	* Education regarding stress dosing
	* Medical alert bracelet/identification  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
  * Acute Adrenal Crisis
	* Fluid resuscitation (isotonic crystalloid)
	* Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV bolus, then 100 mg every 6-8 hours
	* Dextrose for hypoglycemia
	* Identify and treat precipitating cause
	* Monitor electrolytes, volume status
	* Once stable, transition to maintenance therapy  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

⚠️ Adrenal crisis is a life-threatening emergency with high mortality if untreated. Treatment should begin immediately upon clinical suspicion, without waiting for diagnostic confirmation. The classic presentation includes hypotension refractory to fluids and vasopressors, fever, abdominal pain, and altered mental status  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Adrenal insufficiency can present as a chronic condition with nonspecific symptoms or as an acute life-threatening crisis. Diagnosis is confirmed with ACTH stimulation testing, and treatment involves glucocorticoid replacement (with mineralocorticoid for primary disease). Acute adrenal crisis requires immediate treatment with IV hydrocortisone, fluid resuscitation, and management of precipitating factors.

## 5.7. EXOGENOUS GLUCOCORTICOID THERAPY

### 5.7.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Pharmacology of glucocorticoid preparations  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Therapeutic considerations  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Part C:** Clinical applications and complications  [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1-medical-knowledge|ⓘ]].



**The therapeutic use of supraphysiologic doses of glucocorticoids has expanded, and their various preparations differ in potency, duration of action, and mineralocorticoid effects**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

* **Glucocorticoid Preparations**
  * Short-acting
	* Hydrocortisone (cortisol)
	  * Reference standard for comparison
	  * Equal glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid activity
	  * Duration: 8-12 hours
	  * Equivalent dose: 20 mg
	* Cortisone
	  * Slightly lower potency
	  * Must be converted to hydrocortisone in liver
	  * Equivalent dose: 25 mg
  * Intermediate-acting
	* Prednisone/Prednisolone
	  * 4x anti-inflammatory potency of hydrocortisone
	  * Lower mineralocorticoid effect
	  * Duration: 12-36 hours
	  * Equivalent dose: 5 mg
	  * Prednisone requires hepatic conversion to prednisolone
	* Methylprednisolone
	  * 5x anti-inflammatory potency of hydrocortisone
	  * Minimal mineralocorticoid effect
	  * Duration: 12-36 hours
	  * Equivalent dose: 4 mg
	* Triamcinolone
	  * 5x anti-inflammatory potency of hydrocortisone
	  * No mineralocorticoid effect
	  * Duration: 12-36 hours
	  * Equivalent dose: 4 mg
  * Long-acting
	* Dexamethasone
	  * 30x anti-inflammatory potency of hydrocortisone
	  * No mineralocorticoid effect
	  * Duration: 36-54 hours
	  * Equivalent dose: 0.75 mg
	  * Particularly useful for treating cerebral edema  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Clinical Applications**
  * Anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive therapy
	* Autoimmune disorders
	* Allergic reactions
	* Asthma and COPD
	* Rheumatologic diseases
  * Replacement therapy for adrenal insufficiency
  * Diagnostic tests (dexamethasone suppression)
  * Cerebral edema
  * Spinal cord injury
  * Organ transplantation
  * Chemotherapy protocols
  * Cancer-related symptom management  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Adverse Effects of Chronic Therapy**
  * Iatrogenic Cushing syndrome
  * Metabolic effects
	* Hyperglycemia, insulin resistance
	* Dyslipidemia
	* Weight gain
  * Skeletal effects
	* Osteoporosis
	* Avascular necrosis
	* Growth retardation in children
  * Cardiovascular
	* Hypertension
	* Atherosclerosis
	* Fluid retention
  * Immunologic
	* Increased susceptibility to infections
	* Reactivation of latent infections (TB, HSV)
	* Impaired wound healing
  * Other
	* Cataracts, glaucoma
	* Psychiatric effects (mood changes, psychosis)
	* Skin thinning, striae
	* Myopathy
	* HPA axis suppression  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Etomidate and Adrenal Suppression**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]] 
  * Etomidate is an intravenous anesthetic with cardiovascular stability
  * Inhibits cortisol synthesis by blocking 11-β-hydroxylase (final step in converting 11-deoxycortisol to cortisol)
  * Continuous infusion shown to increase mortality in critically ill trauma patients
  * Single-dose effects remain controversial but have theoretical risk in critically ill patients

💡 Medications with greater anti-inflammatory potency generally have lower mineralocorticoid effects. This is important when selecting glucocorticoids for specific clinical indications, particularly when fluid retention or hypertension is a concern  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Various glucocorticoid preparations differ in potency, duration of action, and mineralocorticoid effects. Understanding these differences is essential for appropriate therapeutic use and minimizing adverse effects. Chronic therapy can cause significant complications including HPA axis suppression, which has important perioperative implications.

## 5.8. MINERALOCORTICOID INSUFFICIENCY

### 5.8.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Pathophysiology of mineralocorticoid insufficiency  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Clinical implications and management  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].



**Isolated mineralocorticoid insufficiency can occur due to various causes and presents with hypotension, hyperkalemia, and metabolic acidosis disproportionate to any coexisting renal impairment**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

* **Etiology**
  * Congenital biosynthetic defects
  * Unilateral adrenalectomy for aldosterone-secreting adenoma
  * Protracted heparin therapy
  * Deficiency in renin production
	* Common in patients with mild renal failure and longstanding diabetes
	* Termed "hyporeninemic hypoaldosteronism"
  * Critical feature: Failure to increase aldosterone production in response to salt restriction or volume contraction  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Clinical Presentation**
  * Hypotension (may be orthostatic)
  * Hyperkalemia (can be life-threatening)
  * Metabolic acidosis out of proportion to renal impairment
  * Salt craving
  * Volume depletion
  * Weakness, fatigue
  * Normal cortisol levels and ACTH stimulation response
  * Normal glucose metabolism  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Diagnostic Features**
  * Hyperkalemia
  * Hyponatremia
  * Metabolic acidosis
  * Low serum aldosterone
  * Low or normal plasma renin activity (in hyporeninemic hypoaldosteronism)
  * Elevated renin with low aldosterone (in primary aldosterone deficiency)
  * Normal cortisol response to ACTH stimulation  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Treatment**
  * Mineralocorticoid replacement
	* Fludrocortisone 0.05-0.1 mg orally daily
	* Higher doses in low renin states
	* Monitor for hypertension and heart failure
  * Alternative approaches
	* Furosemide (promotes potassium excretion)
	* Dietary modification (sodium supplementation, potassium restriction)
	* Combination of mineralocorticoid and loop diuretic in resistant cases  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Perioperative Considerations**
  * Continue mineralocorticoid replacement
  * Monitor electrolytes closely
  * Ensure adequate volume status
  * Treat hyperkalemia if present
  * Be vigilant for hypotension, particularly during induction and in response to blood loss
  * Consider stress-dose steroids if combined with glucocorticoid deficiency  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

⚠️ Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, which inhibit prostaglandin synthesis, may further inhibit renin release and exacerbate mineralocorticoid insufficiency. These medications should be used cautiously in patients with known or suspected hypoaldosteronism  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Isolated mineralocorticoid insufficiency presents primarily with electrolyte abnormalities (hyperkalemia, hyponatremia) and metabolic acidosis. Treatment involves fludrocortisone replacement, with careful monitoring for hypertension and heart failure. Perioperative management focuses on electrolyte and volume status to prevent hemodynamic instability.

## 5.9. PERIOPERATIVE STEROID MANAGEMENT

### 5.9.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Principles of perioperative steroid replacement  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Evidence-based protocols  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Part C:** Clinical decision-making for steroid management  [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1-medical-knowledge|ⓘ]].


**Competency Focus:** , 

**Patients with adrenal insufficiency and those with HPA suppression from chronic steroid use require additional corticosteroids perioperatively to mimic the increased output of the normal adrenal gland during stress**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

* **Physiology of Stress Response**
  * Normal adrenal gland can secrete up to 100 mg/m² of cortisol per day during stress
  * Cortisol levels correlate with duration and extent of surgical trauma
  * Mean maximal plasma cortisol levels:
	* Major surgery (colectomy, hip osteotomy): 47 μg/dL
	* Minor procedures (herniorrhaphy): 28 μg/dL
  * Regional anesthesia can partially block cortisol elevation
  * Deep general anesthesia may suppress stress hormone elevation  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **HPA Suppression Risk Assessment**
  * High risk of suppression:
	* Current or recent (within 3 months) long-term (>3 weeks) glucocorticoid therapy
	* Cushingoid appearance
	* Previous HPA axis suppression
  * Factors influencing HPA suppression:
	* Dose: Prednisone ≥20 mg/day (or equivalent)
	* Duration: >3 weeks of therapy
	* Timing: Morning dosing less suppressive than evening
	* Formulation: Topical, inhaled, or regional steroids can cause suppression at high doses
	* Regimen: Alternate-day therapy reduces risk  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Recovery of HPA Function**
  * Gradual process taking 9-12 months after cessation of steroids
  * Faster recovery with shorter courses
  * Return of normal ACTH levels precedes normal adrenal response to stress  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Identifying Patients Requiring Supplementation**
  * Options for assessment:
	* Clinical history (duration, dose, timing of steroid therapy)
	* Morning cortisol level
	* ACTH stimulation testing
  * Practical approach:
	* Supplement patients on steroids within past year for >3 weeks
	* Daily morning doses of prednisone <5 mg typically don't cause significant suppression
	* Patients with clinical features of Cushing syndrome should receive supplementation  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Perioperative Steroid Replacement Protocols**
  * Based on surgical stress severity:
	* Minor surgery (e.g., hernia repair, colonoscopy):
	  * Take usual morning steroid dose
	  * No supplementation needed
	* Moderate surgery (e.g., joint replacement, hysterectomy):
	  * Usual morning dose plus hydrocortisone 50 mg IV at induction
	  * Followed by hydrocortisone 25 mg IV every 8 hours for 24-36 hours
	  * Resume usual regimen on second postoperative day
	* Major surgery (e.g., cardiothoracic, Whipple procedure):
	  * Usual morning dose plus hydrocortisone 100 mg IV at induction
	  * Followed by hydrocortisone 50 mg IV every 8 hours for 24-36 hours
	  * Taper over 2-3 days to usual regimen  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Alternative Approaches**
  * Low-dose continuous infusion:
	* Hydrocortisone 25 mg IV bolus before induction
	* Followed by continuous infusion of 100 mg over 24 hours
	* Shown to match normal stress response in some studies
  * Some evidence suggests that patients may do well with just their usual steroid dose
  * Individualize based on:
	* Type and duration of surgery
	* Comorbidities
	* Previous response to stress
	* Hemodynamic stability  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Monitoring and Complications**
  * Watch for signs of inadequate steroid coverage:
	* Hypotension refractory to fluids/vasopressors
	* Unexplained fever
	* Confusion, lethargy
	* Electrolyte abnormalities
	* Hypoglycemia
  * No conclusive evidence that short-term supplemental steroids increase infection or wound healing complications  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]

💡 The use of dexamethasone for postoperative nausea and vomiting prophylaxis (4-8 mg) may provide adequate stress-dose coverage for minor to moderate procedures, potentially negating the need for additional supplementation  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Perioperative steroid supplementation is essential for patients with adrenal insufficiency or chronic steroid use. Dosing should be based on the expected surgical stress, with higher doses for major procedures. While various protocols exist, the principle is to provide sufficient coverage to prevent adrenal crisis while avoiding excessive steroid administration. Monitoring for signs of inadequate coverage is crucial during the perioperative period.

# 6. ADRENAL MEDULLA





## 6.1. ANATOMY AND PHYSIOLOGY

### 6.1.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Structure and function of the adrenal medulla  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Clinical relevance to anesthesia  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].



**The adrenal medulla is derived embryologically from neuroectodermal cells and functions as a specialized part of the sympathetic nervous system, synthesizing and secreting catecholamines**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

* **Anatomical Structure**
  * Comprises approximately 30% of the adrenal gland
  * Located in the central portion of the adrenal gland
  * Essentially a modified sympathetic ganglion
  * Composed of chromaffin cells derived from neural crest
  * Receives direct innervation from preganglionic sympathetic fibers (T5-9)
	* Preganglionic fibers bypass paravertebral ganglia
	* Pass directly from spinal cord to adrenal medulla
  * Adrenal medulla is analogous to a postganglionic neuron
	* But its secretions act as hormones rather than neurotransmitters  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
	* Chromaffin cell degranulation releases the hormones
* **Functional Relationship with Adrenal Cortex**
  * Intra-adrenal portal system allows cortisol-rich blood to flow from cortex to medulla
  * High cortisol concentration induces phenylethanolamine-N-methyltransferase (PNMT)
  * PNMT is essential for conversion of norepinephrine to epinephrine
  * This anatomical arrangement explains why the adrenal medulla is the primary source of circulating epinephrine  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Hormone Production**
  * Secretes primarily epinephrine (80%) and norepinephrine (20%)
  * Also produces small amounts of:
	* Acetylcholine
	* Met-encephalin
	* Chromogranin A
  * Catecholamines stored in chromaffin granules complexed with ATP and calcium
  * Release occurs via exocytosis in response to stimulation by preganglionic sympathetic neurons  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Physiological Regulation**
  * Controlled by higher cortical centers connected to brainstem
  * Sympathetic outflow influenced by:
	* Emotional states (anger, fear)
	* Physical stimuli (hypotension, hypoglycemia, hypoxemia)
	* Chemical properties of extracellular fluid
  * Adrenal medulla and sympathetic nervous system often activated together
	* But can also act independently in specific physiological conditions  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]

💡 Unlike the direct neurotransmitter action of norepinephrine released from postganglionic sympathetic nerve endings, catecholamines from the adrenal medulla have a longer circulatory half-life (10-30 seconds) and act as true hormones on distant target tissues  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** The adrenal medulla functions as a specialized extension of the sympathetic nervous system, primarily secreting epinephrine and norepinephrine that act as hormones. Its anatomical relationship with the adrenal cortex is critical for epinephrine synthesis, and its activity is integrated with the broader sympathetic response to stress and other physiological stimuli.

## 6.2. CATECHOLAMINE SYNTHESIS AND METABOLISM

### 6.2.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Biochemistry of catecholamine production and degradation  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part C:** Relevance to pharmacologic interventions  [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1-medical-knowledge|ⓘ]].



**Catecholamines are synthesized from the amino acid tyrosine through a series of enzymatic steps, with biosynthesis coupled to release to maintain appropriate levels**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

* **Synthesis Pathway**
  * Begins with tyrosine (dietary or synthesized from phenylalanine)
  * Sequential enzymatic steps:
	1. Tyrosine hydroxylase converts tyrosine to dihydroxyphenylalanine (DOPA)
	   * Rate-limiting step in catecholamine biosynthesis
	   * Regulated to couple synthesis with release
	2. DOPA decarboxylase converts DOPA to dopamine
	3. Dopamine β-hydroxylase converts dopamine to norepinephrine
	4. Phenylethanolamine-N-methyltransferase (PNMT) converts norepinephrine to epinephrine
	   * PNMT induced by high local concentrations of glucocorticoids
	   * Explains why adrenal medulla primarily produces epinephrine  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Detailed Synthesis Process**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]] 
  * Tyrosine is acquired from diet or through hydroxylation of phenylalanine in liver
  * Tyrosine → L-DOPA (by tyrosine hydroxylase - rate-limiting step)
  * L-DOPA → Dopamine (by DOPA decarboxylase)
  * Dopamine → Norepinephrine (by dopamine β-hydroxylase)
  * Norepinephrine → Adrenaline (by PNMT, only in adrenal medulla)
  * Catecholamines packaged into granules with ATP, chromogranin A, and opioid peptides
* **Storage and Release**
  * Stored in chromaffin granules
	* Complexed with ATP and calcium
	* Protected from degradation by monoamine oxidase
  * Release triggered by:
	* Preganglionic sympathetic stimulation (acetylcholine)
	* Splanchnic nerve stimulation
	* Stress response
  * Mechanism of release is exocytosis
	* Requires calcium influx
	* Similar to neurotransmitter release at synapses  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Catecholamine Release Process**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]] 
  * Pre-ganglionic sympathetic nerves release acetylcholine
  * ACh activates nicotinic receptors on chromaffin cells
  * Membrane depolarization opens voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels
  * Ca²⁺ influx triggers exocytosis of granules
  * Adrenaline and norepinephrine released into circulation
* **Metabolism and Elimination**
  * Circulatory half-life: 10-30 seconds
  * Two main enzymatic pathways:
	1. Catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT)
	   * Located primarily in liver and kidney
	   * Main pathway for circulating catecholamines
	   * Produces O-methylated derivatives
	2. Monoamine oxidase (MAO)
	   * Located in mitochondria of many tissues
	   * Less important for circulating catecholamines
	   * More important for intraneuronal metabolism
  * Major end products:
	* Metanephrine and normetanephrine (via COMT)
	* Vanillylmandelic acid (VMA, final common pathway)
	* 3-methoxy-4-hydroxyphenylglycol (MHPG)
  * Elimination:
	* Mainly in urine
	* Small amount (1%) excreted unchanged  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Reuptake Mechanisms**
  * Two processes for terminating norepinephrine action:
	1. Uptake 1:
	   * High-affinity transport into nerve terminal
	   * Rapid process
	   * Sequesters norepinephrine for repackaging or metabolism
	   * Target of many antidepressant medications
	2. Uptake 2:
	   * Slower process
	   * Norepinephrine passes into systemic circulation
	   * Broken down by COMT in liver and other tissues  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Clinical Relevance of Metabolites**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]] 
  * Metanephrine is an intermediate in catecholamine metabolism
  * Vanillylmandelic acid (VMA) is the final product
  * Elevated levels may indicate pheochromocytoma
  * Measurement of plasma metanephrines used for diagnosis

**Bottom Line:** Catecholamines are synthesized from tyrosine through a regulated enzymatic pathway, with the rate-limiting step controlled to match production with physiological needs. After release, their action is terminated by reuptake and metabolism by COMT and MAO enzymes, producing metabolites that can be measured diagnostically. This pathway is the target of numerous pharmacological interventions in anesthesia and critical care.

## 6.3. PHEOCHROMOCYTOMA

### 6.3.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Pathophysiology of pheochromocytoma  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Perioperative management  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Part C:** Critical care considerations and crisis management  [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1-medical-knowledge|ⓘ]].


**Competency Focus:** , 

**Pheochromocytoma is a catecholamine-producing tumor of the adrenal medulla that can cause life-threatening cardiovascular complications if not properly diagnosed and managed perioperatively**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

### 6.3.2. ETIOLOGY AND PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

* **Definition and Classification**
  * Tumor arising from chromaffin cells of the adrenal medulla
  * Paragangliomas are similar tumors arising from extra-adrenal autonomic ganglia
  * Produce, store, and secrete catecholamines (primarily norepinephrine)
  * Most secrete both epinephrine and norepinephrine, with the proportion of norepinephrine higher than normal gland  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Epidemiology**
  * Rare: <0.2% of hypertensive patients
  * Most (85-90%) are solitary adrenal tumors
  * 10% bilateral
  * 10% extra-adrenal (paragangliomas)
	* 95% of extra-adrenal tumors in abdomen
	* Can occur in thorax, urinary bladder, neck
  * Malignant in approximately 10% of cases
  * Genetic predisposition in 5% of cases
	* Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia (MEN) IIA and IIB
	* von Recklinghausen neurofibromatosis
	* von Hippel-Lindau disease  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Pathophysiology**
  * Tumors not innervated; catecholamine release independent of neurogenic control
  * Release may be spontaneous, episodic, or triggered by:
	* Physical pressure on tumor
	* Medications
	* Changes in blood flow/tumor perfusion
  * Cardiovascular effects:
	* Hypertension (sustained or paroxysmal)
	* Increased peripheral vascular resistance
	* Tachyarrhythmias
	* Cardiomyopathy with prolonged exposure
	* Volume contraction due to pressure natriuresis  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]

### 6.3.3. CLINICAL PRESENTATION

* **Symptoms and Signs**
  * Classic triad: headache, sweating, tachycardia
  * Other common manifestations:
	* Hypertension (sustained in 50-60%, paroxysmal in remainder)
	* Palpitations
	* Anxiety, nervousness
	* Tremor
	* Pallor or flushing
	* Weight loss
	* Heat intolerance
	* Hyperglycemia
  * Paroxysms (attacks):
	* Can be spontaneous or triggered
	* Often unpredictable
	* Extreme blood pressure elevation (risk of stroke, heart failure)
	* Triggered by physical exertion, emotional stress, certain foods/medications
	* In bladder pheochromocytoma, triggered by micturition  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Atypical Presentations**
  * Asymptomatic with incidental finding (increasingly common)
  * Heart failure or cardiomyopathy
  * Arrhythmias
  * Postural hypotension
  * Shock (if tumor produces primarily epinephrine)
  * Fever, mimicking infection
  * Mimicking malignant hyperthermia  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

### 6.3.4. DIAGNOSIS

* **Biochemical Testing**
  * First-line tests:
	* 24-hour urinary catecholamines and metanephrines
	* Plasma free metanephrines (most sensitive)
  * Interpretation challenges:
	* Certain drugs interfere with assays
	* Paroxysmal disease may have normal values between attacks
	* Stress can increase catecholamines
  * Additional tests:
	* Urinary vanillylmandelic acid (VMA)
	* Clonidine suppression test
	* Chromogranin A (tumor marker)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Imaging Studies**
  * Anatomical localization:
	* CT scan (sensitivity 90-95%)
	* MRI (preferred for pregnant patients, children)
	* Ultrasound (limited utility except during pregnancy)
  * Functional imaging:
	* ¹²³I-metaiodobenzylguanidine (MIBG) scintigraphy
	  * High specificity for catecholamine-producing tumors
	  * Particularly useful for metastatic disease, recurrent tumors, extra-adrenal locations
	* Positron emission tomography (PET)
	  * Using specific tracers (¹⁸F-fluorodopamine, ¹⁸F-fluorodopa)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

### 6.3.5. PREOPERATIVE PREPARATION

* **Pharmacologic Management**
  * Goals:
	* Control blood pressure
	* Prevent paroxysms
	* Restore intravascular volume
	* Normalize heart rate
	* Treat catecholamine-induced myocarditis if present
  * Alpha-adrenergic blockade (cornerstone of preparation)
	* Traditional approach: phenoxybenzamine
	  * Long-acting, noncompetitive, nonselective α-blocker
	  * Starting dose 10 mg every 8 hours, titrated to effect
	  * Typical requirement: 80-200 mg/day
	  * Side effects: postural hypotension, nasal congestion, fatigue
	* Alternative: selective α₁-blockers
	  * Doxazosin, terazosin, prazosin
	  * Competitive antagonists
	  * Less postural hypotension
	  * Shorter duration
	* Duration: typically 10-14 days before surgery
	  * Can be shortened to 3-5 days if necessary
	* Adequacy assessed by:
	  * Blood pressure control (target <130/80 mmHg)
	  * No significant ST/T wave changes on ECG
	  * No more than 1 ventricular premature contraction/5 minutes
	  * Orthostatic hypotension present but not symptomatic
	  * No paroxysms for >48 hours  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Beta-adrenergic blockade
	* Added only after adequate α-blockade established
	* Indications:
	  * Persistent tachycardia
	  * Arrhythmias
	  * Angina
	* ⚠️ Never use before α-blockade (risk of unopposed α-vasoconstriction)
	* Options:
	  * Propranolol, metoprolol, atenolol for oral therapy
	  * Esmolol advantageous perioperatively due to short half-life
	  * Labetalol (combined α/β-blocker) sometimes used as second-line medication  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Additional medications
	* Calcium channel blockers
	  * Alternative or adjunct to α-blockers
	  * Particularly useful for hemodynamic control
	  * Options: nicardipine, nifedipine, diltiazem
	* α-Methyltyrosine (metyrosine)
	  * Inhibits tyrosine hydroxylase (rate-limiting enzyme)
	  * Reduces catecholamine synthesis
	  * Used for metastatic disease or preparation for complex cases
	  * Limited by side effects (sedation, extrapyramidal symptoms)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Volume Expansion**
  * Critical aspect of preparation
  * Chronic catecholamine excess causes volume contraction
  * Salt and fluid intake encouraged during α-blockade
  * IV fluid administration may be necessary
  * Goal: restore normal intravascular volume and prevent severe hypotension after tumor removal  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Special Considerations**
  * Pregnancy
	* Diagnosed in pregnancy has high maternal and fetal morbidity
	* Preferably operate during first trimester or at time of cesarean delivery
	* MRI and ultrasound preferred for imaging
	* Phenoxybenzamine generally considered safe in pregnancy
  * Incidental discovery
	* Increasing with widespread imaging
	* Biochemical testing essential before any intervention
	* Preparation still required even if asymptomatic  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

### 6.3.6. ANESTHETIC MANAGEMENT

* **Preoperative Assessment**
  * Review of preoperative preparation adequacy
  * Evaluation of end-organ damage
	* Cardiomyopathy
	* Coronary artery disease
	* Cerebrovascular disease
	* Renal dysfunction
  * Continue all medications through morning of surgery
  * Consider premedication to reduce anxiety  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Monitoring**
  * Standard ASA monitors
  * Invasive arterial blood pressure (essential)
  * Central venous pressure (useful for volume assessment)
  * Consider pulmonary artery catheter or transesophageal echocardiography for patients with cardiac dysfunction
  * Arterial blood gases and glucose
  * Urine output  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Induction and Maintenance**
  * Avoid drugs that stimulate sympathetic activity or histamine release
  * Avoid depolarizing muscle relaxants if possible
  * Induction options:
	* Propofol, etomidate, or thiopental with opioid
	* Achieve adequate depth before laryngoscopy
  * Maintenance:
	* Balanced technique with volatile agent and opioid
	* Avoid ketamine and pancuronium
	* Regional anesthesia generally avoided as sole technique  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Intraoperative Hemodynamic Management**
  * Hypertensive episodes
	* Most likely during induction, intubation, and tumor manipulation
	* Treatment options:
	  * Sodium nitroprusside (1-5 mcg/kg/min)
	  * Phentolamine (2-5 mg IV bolus or infusion)
	  * Nicardipine, clevidipine
	  * Magnesium sulfate
	  * Fenoldopam
	  * Nitroglycerin
	  * Esmolol for tachyarrhythmias
  * Hypotension after tumor resection
	* Can be abrupt after venous ligation
	* Management:
	  * Volume replacement first-line therapy
	  * Vasopressors if needed: phenylephrine, norepinephrine, vasopressin
	  * Avoid pure α-agonists if possible (risk of receptor hypersensitivity)
  * Communication with surgical team
	* Crucial for anticipating hemodynamic changes
	* Alert surgeon when hemodynamically stable for tumor manipulation
	* Surgeon should warn anesthesiologist before venous ligation  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Postoperative Care**
  * Continued hemodynamic monitoring
  * Watch for:
	* Persistent or recurrent hypertension (residual tumor, pain)
	* Hypoglycemia (from insulin release after catecholamine withdrawal)
	* Adrenal insufficiency (if bilateral adrenalectomy)
  * Catecholamine levels return to normal over several days
  * Approximately 75% of patients become normotensive within 10 days  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

⚠️ Preoperative alpha-blockade is considered essential to reduce perioperative morbidity and mortality. Historically, mortality has decreased from about 45% to less than 3% with appropriate preoperative preparation  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Pheochromocytoma management requires meticulous preoperative preparation with alpha-blockade, volume expansion, and sometimes beta-blockade. Intraoperative care focuses on invasive hemodynamic monitoring and rapid management of blood pressure fluctuations, especially during tumor manipulation and after venous ligation. Close communication between the anesthetic and surgical teams is essential for anticipating and managing hemodynamic changes.

## 7.1. CLASSIFICATION

### 7.1.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Types and classification of diabetes mellitus  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Clinical implications of different diabetes types  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].



**Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a heterogeneous group of metabolic disorders characterized by hyperglycemia resulting from defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

* **Major Types of Diabetes**
  * **Type 1 Diabetes (5-10% of cases)**
	* Due to pancreatic β-cell destruction, usually leading to absolute insulin deficiency
	* Typically presents in childhood or adolescence, but can occur at any age
	* Autoimmune etiology in most cases
	* Requires insulin therapy
	* Prone to ketosis
	* Formerly called juvenile-onset or insulin-dependent diabetes  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
  
  * **Type 2 Diabetes (90-95% of cases)**
	* Progressive loss of insulin secretion on a background of insulin resistance
	* Usually occurs in adults but increasing in adolescents due to obesity
	* Strong genetic predisposition
	* Often associated with obesity, physical inactivity
	* May range from predominantly insulin resistance to predominantly insulin secretory defect
	* Many patients initially have normal or elevated insulin levels
	* Relative resistance to ketosis
	* Formerly called adult-onset or non-insulin-dependent diabetes  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
  
  * **Gestational Diabetes**
	* Diagnosed in second or third trimester of pregnancy
	* Not clearly overt diabetes prior to gestation
	* May presage future type 2 diabetes
	* Requires specific diagnostic criteria (glucose challenge test, oral glucose tolerance test)
	* Increases risk of maternal and fetal complications  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
  
  * **Other Specific Types**
	* Secondary to pancreatic disease:
	  * Pancreatitis
	  * Pancreatic surgery
	  * Cystic fibrosis
	  * Hemochromatosis
	* Endocrine diseases:
	  * Cushing syndrome
	  * Acromegaly
	  * Pheochromocytoma
	  * Glucagonoma
	* Drug-induced:
	  * Glucocorticoids
	  * Thiazide diuretics
	  * Atypical antipsychotics
	  * Tacrolimus, other immunosuppressants
	* Genetic defects:
	  * Monogenic diabetes (MODY)
	  * Mitochondrial diabetes
	  * Cystic fibrosis-related diabetes  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

💡 The traditional paradigms of type 1 DM occurring only in children and type 2 DM only in adults are no longer accurate. Both types can present at any age, making accurate diagnosis important for appropriate treatment selection  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Diabetes mellitus is classified into four major types: type 1 (autoimmune β-cell destruction leading to absolute insulin deficiency), type 2 (progressive insulin secretory defect on a background of insulin resistance), gestational diabetes, and diabetes due to specific causes. Understanding the pathophysiology of each type is essential for appropriate management, especially in the perioperative period.

## 7.2. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

### 7.2.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Physiological mechanisms in diabetes mellitus  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part C:** Integration with perioperative metabolic changes  [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1-medical-knowledge|ⓘ]].



**Insulin has complex interactions with glucose, lipid, and protein metabolism, with its deficiency or resistance leading to the metabolic derangements characteristic of diabetes mellitus**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

* **Normal Insulin Physiology**
  * Synthesis and Secretion
	* Produced by β cells of pancreatic islets of Langerhans
	* Protein hormone synthesized as proinsulin and cleaved to insulin and C-peptide
	* Basal secretion ~1 unit/hour
	* Stimulated secretion increases 5-10 fold after meals
	* Normal daily production: 40-50 units
	* Half-life in circulation: 5 minutes
	* Metabolized in liver and kidneys  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
  
  * Regulation of Secretion
	* Primary stimuli:
	  * Glucose (most potent stimulus)
	  * Amino acids - arginine, leucine, lysine, phenylalanine
	* Other factors affecting secretion:
	  * Incretin hormones: GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1) and GIP (glucose-dependent insulinotropic peptide)
	  * Autonomic nervous system: vagal stimulation increases
	  * Adrenergic effects: β-stimulation increases, α-inhibits
	  * Potassium depletion decreases
	  * Other hormones: cortisol, growth hormone, glucagon  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
	
  * Primary targets: skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, liver
	* Actions continue after insulin is cleared (receptor binding)
	* Saturation of binding sites leads to longer duration rather than more potent effect  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]

* **Metabolic Effects of Insulin**
	* Binds to specific receptors on target cells
	* Tyrosine kinase activation → phosphorylation of intracellular tyrosine
	* Intracellular signalling
		* Phosphatidylinositol 3 kinase (PI 3 kinase) - CHO3, protein, & lipid metabolism
		* Mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) - mitogenic effects
  * Glucose Metabolism
	  * Increased GLUT4 expression → increased glucose uptake (muscle, liver, adipose tissues) → increased glycogen synthesis
	* Stimulates cellular glucose uptake in:
	  * Skeletal muscle (primary site of insulin-mediated glucose disposal)
	  * Adipose tissue
	  * Cardiac muscle
	* Brain, liver, and immune cells take up glucose independently of insulin
	* Promotes glycogen formation
	* Suppresses gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis
	* Lowers blood glucose  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
  
  * Protein Metabolism
	* Increases amino acid uptake into muscle cells
	* Stimulates protein synthesis
	* Inhibits protein catabolism
	* Promotes positive nitrogen balance  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
	* Valine, leucine, tyrosine, & phenylalanine uptake
  
  * Lipid Metabolism
	* Inhibits lipolysis
	* Promotes fatty acid and triglyceride synthesis
	* Increases lipid storage in adipose tissue
	* Inhibits ketone body formation  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
  
  * Electrolyte Effects
	* Promotes cellular potassium uptake
	* Insulin-potassium relationship critical in DKA management  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Pathophysiology of Diabetes Types**
  * Type 1 Diabetes
	* Autoimmune destruction of pancreatic β cells
	* Absolute insulin deficiency
	* Rapid development of hyperglycemia
	* Uninhibited lipolysis leading to ketosis
	* Cannot be controlled with oral agents; requires insulin
	* Absence of endogenous insulin leads to:
	  * Uncontrolled hepatic glucose production
	  * Impaired peripheral glucose utilization
	  * Accelerated lipolysis and ketogenesis
	  * Protein catabolism and negative nitrogen balance  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
  
  * Type 2 Diabetes
	* Two primary defects:
	  * Insulin resistance (reduced tissue response to insulin)
	  * Progressive β-cell dysfunction and inadequate insulin secretion
	* Initially, β cells increase insulin output to overcome resistance
	* Over time, β-cell function declines, leading to relative insulin deficiency
	* Usually some insulin production remains, preventing ketosis
	* Characterized by:
	  * Hepatic glucose overproduction
	  * Impaired glucose utilization
	  * Dyslipidemia (elevated triglycerides, decreased HDL)
	  * Varying degrees of insulin deficiency  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
  
  * Role of Glucagon
	* Polypeptide released from pancreatic α cells
	* Actions oppose insulin:
	  * Stimulates glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis
	  * Increases blood glucose
	* Release stimulated by hypoglycemia, epinephrine, cortisol
	* Suppressed by glucose ingestion
	* In diabetes, abnormal glucagon secretion (failure to suppress appropriately) contributes to hyperglycemia  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]
* **Stress and Diabetes**
  * Stress hormones (cortisol, catecholamines, glucagon, growth hormone) all have hyperglycemic effects
  * These hormones stimulate:
	* Gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis
	* Inhibition of insulin secretion
	* Insulin resistance
  * Inflammatory mediators enhance counter-regulatory hormone release
  * Therefore, mild hyperglycemia often occurs in stressed non-diabetic patients
  * In diabetic patients, stress can severely worsen glycemic control
  * Surgical stress significantly impacts glucose homeostasis  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]]

⚠️ In patients with renal or hepatic dysfunction, insulin metabolism is impaired, prolonging insulin action and increasing the risk of hypoglycemia. Dosing adjustments are necessary in these populations  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Insulin regulates glucose, protein, and lipid metabolism through its action on multiple tissues. Type 1 diabetes results from absolute insulin deficiency due to β-cell destruction, while type 2 diabetes involves a combination of insulin resistance and progressive β-cell dysfunction. Understanding these mechanisms is crucial for appropriate diabetes management, especially during periods of stress like surgery when counter-regulatory hormones further disrupt glycemic control.

## 7.3. DIAGNOSIS

### 7.3.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Diagnostic criteria and testing for diabetes mellitus  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Perioperative assessment  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].



**Diagnosis of diabetes mellitus is based on plasma glucose criteria, either fasting plasma glucose, 2-hour value in oral glucose tolerance test, random glucose with symptoms, or hemoglobin A1c**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

* **Diagnostic Criteria**
  * Any one of the following:
	* Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) ≥6.5%
	  * Using a method certified and standardized to the DCCT assay
	* Fasting plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L)
	  * Fasting defined as no caloric intake for at least 8 hours
	* 2-hour plasma glucose ≥200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) during oral glucose tolerance test
	  * Using 75g anhydrous glucose dissolved in water
	* Random plasma glucose ≥200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) in a patient with classic symptoms
	  * Polyuria, polydipsia, unexplained weight loss
  * In the absence of unequivocal hyperglycemia, results should be confirmed by repeat testing  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c)**
  * Reflects average plasma glucose over previous 2-3 months
  * Advantages:
	* Greater convenience (no fasting required)
	* Less day-to-day variability
	* Standard measure for assessing glycemic control
  * Limitations:
	* Affected by conditions that alter red cell turnover
	  * Hemolytic anemia, recent blood loss/transfusion
	  * Pregnancy, hemodialysis
	  * Hemoglobinopathies
	* Some ethnic variability
	* May underestimate hyperglycemia in certain populations
  * HbA1c-glucose correlation:
	* 5.7% ~ 117 mg/dL average glucose
	* 6.0% ~ 126 mg/dL
	* 6.5% ~ 140 mg/dL
	* 7.0% ~ 154 mg/dL
	* 8.0% ~ 183 mg/dL  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Categories of Increased Risk (Prediabetes)**
  * Individuals with:
	* HbA1c 5.7-6.4%
	* Impaired fasting glucose (IFG): Fasting glucose 100-125 mg/dL
	* Impaired glucose tolerance (IGT): 2-hour OGTT glucose 140-199 mg/dL
  * Increased risk for developing diabetes and cardiovascular disease
  * Target for preventive interventions  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Screening Recommendations**
  * Consider testing all adults age ≥45 years
  * Consider earlier testing in individuals with risk factors:
	* Physical inactivity
	* First-degree relative with diabetes
	* High-risk ethnic populations (African American, Latino, Native American, Asian American, Pacific Islander)
	* Women who delivered a baby weighing >9 lb or had gestational diabetes
	* Hypertension
	* HDL <35 mg/dL and/or triglycerides >250 mg/dL
	* Polycystic ovary syndrome
	* HbA1c ≥5.7%, IGT, or IFG on previous testing
	* Other conditions associated with insulin resistance
	* History of cardiovascular disease  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

💡 The correlation between HbA1c and average glucose allows clinicians to explain glycemic control to patients in terms of day-to-day glucose measurements, enhancing patient understanding and engagement  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Diabetes can be diagnosed based on HbA1c ≥6.5%, fasting glucose ≥126 mg/dL, 2-hour OGTT glucose ≥200 mg/dL, or random glucose ≥200 mg/dL with symptoms. HbA1c has been included in diagnostic criteria since 2009 and provides a good measure of long-term glycemic control. Prediabetes represents an intermediate risk state with increased likelihood of developing diabetes and cardiovascular complications.

## 7.4. TREATMENT APPROACHES

### 7.4.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Medical management of diabetes mellitus  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Implications for perioperative management  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Part C:** Evidence-based approach to glycemic management  [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1-medical-knowledge|ⓘ]].


**Competency Focus:** , 

**Treatment of diabetes aims to achieve glycemic control while minimizing hypoglycemia and other complications, with approaches varying by diabetes type and individual characteristics**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

* **Treatment Goals**
  * Glycemic targets:
	* HbA1c <7% for most adults
	* More stringent targets (6.0-6.5%) for selected individuals if achievable without hypoglycemia
	* Less stringent targets (7.5-8.0%) for elderly, those with comorbidities, limited life expectancy
  * Prevention of acute complications (hypoglycemia, DKA, HHS)
  * Prevention of long-term complications (microvascular, macrovascular)
  * Preservation of quality of life  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Type 1 Diabetes Management**
  * Insulin therapy is essential (absolute requirement)
  * Approaches:
	* Multiple daily injections (MDI):
	  * Basal insulin (long-acting): glargine, detemir, degludec
	  * Prandial insulin (rapid-acting): lispro, aspart, glulisine
	* Continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (CSII) or insulin pump:
	  * Delivers variable basal rates
	  * Boluses for meals and corrections
	  * Requires carbohydrate counting
	* Regimens aim to mimic physiologic insulin secretion
  * Newer developments:
	* Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) systems
	* Hybrid closed-loop systems (artificial pancreas)
  * Intensive treatment reduces microvascular complications
  * Particularly important before and during pregnancy  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Type 2 Diabetes Management**
  * Stepwise approach:
	1. Lifestyle modifications (diet, exercise, weight loss)
	2. Monotherapy (typically metformin as first-line)
	3. Dual therapy
	4. Triple therapy
	5. Injectable therapy (GLP-1 receptor agonists and/or insulin)
  * Medication classes:
	* Biguanides (metformin)
	  * Decreases hepatic glucose production
	  * Enhances insulin sensitivity
	  * First-line therapy for most patients
	  * Advantages: weight neutral/modest weight loss, no hypoglycemia, cardiovascular benefits
	* Sulfonylureas (glipizide, glyburide, glimepiride)
	  * Enhance β-cell insulin secretion
	  * Risk of hypoglycemia
	  * Weight gain
	* Meglitinides (repaglinide, nateglinide)
	  * Stimulate insulin secretion
	  * Shorter-acting than sulfonylureas
	* Thiazolidinediones (pioglitazone, rosiglitazone)
	  * Increase insulin sensitivity
	  * Risk of fluid retention, heart failure, weight gain
	* α-Glucosidase inhibitors (acarbose, miglitol)
	  * Slow carbohydrate absorption
	  * GI side effects
	* DPP-4 inhibitors (sitagliptin, saxagliptin, linagliptin, alogliptin)
	  * Increase incretin levels
	  * Weight neutral
	  * Low hypoglycemia risk
	* GLP-1 receptor agonists (exenatide, liraglutide, semaglutide, dulaglutide)
	  * Increase glucose-dependent insulin secretion
	  * Decrease glucagon
	  * Slow gastric emptying
	  * Promote weight loss
	  * Cardiovascular benefits in high-risk patients
	* SGLT2 inhibitors (canagliflozin, dapagliflozin, empagliflozin)
	  * Block renal glucose reabsorption
	  * Reduce BP, promote weight loss
	  * Cardiovascular and renal benefits
	  * Risk of DKA
	* Insulin therapy
	  * Consider early in severe hyperglycemia
	  * Eventually needed in many patients due to progressive β-cell decline  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Special Treatment Considerations**
  * Gestational diabetes:
	* First-line: lifestyle modifications
	* Second-line: insulin (preferred) or oral agents in selected cases
  * Comorbidities:
	* Cardiovascular disease: Prefer agents with proven CV benefit (metformin, SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists)
	* Chronic kidney disease: Dose adjustments needed for many agents; SGLT2 inhibitors show renal protective effects
	* Heart failure: SGLT2 inhibitors beneficial; avoid thiazolidinediones
  * Bariatric surgery:
	* Considered for adults with BMI >35 kg/m² and type 2 diabetes
	* High remission rates, especially with procedures that bypass portions of small intestine  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

⚠️ SGLT2 inhibitors carry a risk of euglycemic DKA, particularly during periods of stress like surgery or illness. They should be discontinued 3-4 days before surgery and during acute illness  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Type 1 diabetes requires insulin therapy, typically using regimens that mimic physiologic insulin secretion. Type 2 diabetes management follows a stepwise approach starting with lifestyle modifications and metformin, progressively adding agents from different classes based on patient characteristics, comorbidities, and glycemic control. Treatment decisions should consider not only glucose-lowering effects but also impacts on weight, hypoglycemia risk, and cardiovascular outcomes.

## 7.5. ANESTHETIC MANAGEMENT

### 7.5.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Perioperative concerns in diabetic patients  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Evidence-based approach to perioperative management  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Part C:** Integration of diabetes management principles in perioperative care  [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1-medical-knowledge|ⓘ]].


**Competency Focus:** , 

**Successful management of diabetic patients undergoing surgery requires thorough preoperative evaluation, understanding of end-organ complications, appropriate perioperative medication management, and careful intraoperative monitoring**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

### 7.5.2. PREOPERATIVE EVALUATION

* **General Assessment**
  * Detailed history of:
	* Diabetes type and duration
	* Current treatment regimen
	* Glycemic control (recent HbA1c)
	* Frequency and severity of hypoglycemic episodes
	* Presence of complications
	* Comorbid conditions
  * Laboratory tests:
	* HbA1c (if not available in previous 3 months)
	* Blood glucose
	* Renal function (BUN, creatinine)
	* Electrolytes
	* Urinalysis
  * ECG (recommended for most diabetic patients)
  * Assessment of recent glucose patterns/variability if available  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Glycemic Control Assessment**
  * HbA1c as indicator of long-term control:
	* <7.0%: Good control
	* 7.0-8.0%: Moderate control
	* > 8.0%: Poor control
  * Consider postponing elective surgery if:
	* HbA1c >8.5-9.0% (if safe and practical)
	* Acute metabolic decompensation (DKA, HHS)
	* Acute hyperglycemia >400 mg/dL
  * Practical considerations:
	* Urgency of surgery
	* Risk of postponement
	* Potential for improving control within a reasonable timeframe
	* Current symptoms caused by the condition requiring surgery  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

### 7.5.3. END-ORGAN COMPLICATIONS

* **Cardiovascular Disease**
  * Accelerated atherosclerosis
	* Coronary artery disease (2-4× higher risk)
	* Peripheral vascular disease
	* Cerebrovascular disease
	* Increased risk of postoperative MI
  * Special considerations:
	* Silent ischemia more common (autonomic neuropathy)
	* Atypical presentation of CAD
	* Diabetic cardiomyopathy possible even with normal coronaries
	* Consider stress testing based on functional capacity, risk factors
	* Follow ACC/AHA guidelines for non-cardiac surgery  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Autonomic Neuropathy**
  * Cardiovascular manifestations:
	* Resting tachycardia
	* Fixed heart rate (reduced variability)
	* Orthostatic hypotension
	* Reduced response to hypovolemia
	* Silent myocardial ischemia
  * Increased risk of:
	* Intraoperative cardiovascular instability
	* Perioperative cardiac arrest
	* Exaggerated pressor response to intubation
  * Diagnostic tests:
	* Heart rate variability with deep breathing
	* Valsalva response
	* Orthostatic blood pressure changes
  * Management implications:
	* Careful fluid management
	* Consider invasive monitoring for major surgery
	* Cautious use of vasoactive medications  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Nephropathy**
  * Occurs in 20-40% of diabetic patients
  * Leading cause of end-stage renal disease
  * Screening: Microalbuminuria (30-299 mg/24h) - earliest sign
  * Perioperative implications:
	* Altered drug clearance
	* Electrolyte abnormalities
	* Acid-base disturbances
	* Increased risk of acute kidney injury
	* May require perioperative renal replacement therapy  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Gastrointestinal Neuropathy**
  * Delayed gastric emptying (gastroparesis)
  * Increased risk of aspiration
  * Evaluation:
	* Symptomatic assessment
	* Autonomic function tests can predict solid food particles in gastric contents
	* Consider prokinetic agents (metoclopramide, erythromycin)
	* Consider rapid sequence induction  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Airway and Other Complications**
  * Difficult laryngoscopy in up to 40% of juvenile diabetics with renal disease
  * "Stiff joint syndrome" (decreased mobility of atlanto-occipital joint)
  * "Prayer sign" (inability to approximate palmar surfaces) predicts difficult laryngoscopy
  * Additional concerns:
	* Increased risk of perioperative infection
	* Poor wound healing
	* Cognitive decline and dementia
	* Obstructive sleep apnea
	* Increased risk of stroke  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

### 7.5.4. GLUCOSE-LOWERING REGIMENS AND PREOPERATIVE COUNSELING

* **Oral Antihyperglycemic Medications**
  * General recommendation:
	* Discontinue night before surgery
	* Hold on day of surgery
	* Resume when normal diet restarted
  * Metformin:
	* Hold day of surgery
	* Traditionally withheld 24-48h before procedures with contrast media or risk of acute kidney injury
	* Recent evidence suggests similar outcomes without discontinuation
  * Sulfonylureas:
	* Particular risk of hypoglycemia when fasting
	* Hold day before surgery for long-acting agents
  * SGLT2 inhibitors:
	* Discontinue 3-4 days before surgery
	* Risk of euglycemic DKA
  * Other oral agents:
	* Generally hold on day of surgery  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Insulin Management**
  * Type 1 diabetes:
	* Must receive some basal insulin at all times
	* Risk of DKA if insulin completely withheld
  * Multiple daily injection regimens:
	* Evening before surgery:
	  * Take usual dose of short/rapid-acting insulin with dinner
	  * Reduce intermediate/long-acting insulin by 20%
	* Morning of surgery:
	  * Omit morning short/rapid-acting insulin
	  * Reduce intermediate insulin by 50% or long-acting by 20% (take only if fasting glucose >120 mg/dL)
	  * For prolonged fasting, consider further reduction of basal insulin
  * Premixed insulin:
	* Evening before: Reduce by 20%
	* Morning of surgery: Hold completely
  * Insulin pump therapy:
	* Continue basal rate (consider 20-25% reduction)
	* Omit boluses while fasting
	* Hourly glucose monitoring if pump continued during surgery
	* Alternative: Transition to IV insulin for major procedures  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

### 7.5.5. PREOPERATIVE GLYCEMIC CONTROL

* **Target Glucose Levels**
  * Preoperative glucose targets:
	* Ideal range: 80-180 mg/dL
	* No evidence-based guidelines for cancellation thresholds
  * Factors to consider for surgical cancellation:
	* Urgency of surgery
	* Metabolic stability
	* Presence of DKA/HHS
	* Ability to achieve better control if postponed
  * Practical approach:
	* Consider postponing non-urgent surgery for glucose >300-400 mg/dL
	* Evaluate for ketoacidosis if severely elevated
	* Treat hyperglycemia before proceeding with non-urgent cases  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

### 7.5.6. INTRAOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT

* **Monitoring**
  * Frequent blood glucose monitoring:
	* Before induction
	* Every 1-2 hours during surgery
	* More frequent for insulin infusions, unstable patients
	* Consider arterial line for major cases (facilitates sampling)
  * Awareness of glucose meter limitations:
	* Accuracy varies by ±20%
	* Affected by hematocrit, hypotension, certain medications
	* Consider central laboratory verification for critical values
  * Avoid routine administration of glucose-containing fluids
	* Standard glucose dosage: 5-10 g/h (100-200 mL of 5% dextrose solution hourly)
	* Administer glucose based on measured levels
	* Separate dextrose administration from fluid replacement in records  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Technical Considerations**
  * Careful positioning to avoid peripheral nerve injury
	* Diabetic patients at higher risk of pressure/stretch injuries
	* Peripheral nerves may already be compromised
  * Temperature management (autonomic dysfunction)
  * Consider invasive monitoring based on comorbidities
  * Regional anesthesia considerations:
	* May provide better postoperative analgesia
	* May reduce stress response
	* Consider existing neuropathy in risk-benefit assessment  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

⚠️ Patients with type 1 diabetes must receive some basal insulin at all times, even when fasting, to prevent ketoacidosis. This is a fundamental difference from type 2 diabetes management during fasting  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Perioperative management of diabetic patients requires comprehensive assessment of glycemic control and end-organ complications, particularly cardiovascular and autonomic neuropathy. Medication management should be individualized based on diabetes type, with maintenance of basal insulin essential for type 1 diabetes. Careful monitoring and a structured approach to glucose management help minimize perioperative complications from both hyperand hypoglycemia.

## 7.6. HYPERGLYCEMIA AND PERIOPERATIVE OUTCOMES

### 7.6.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Impact of hyperglycemia on surgical outcomes  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Evidence-based perioperative risk assessment  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Part C:** Critical evaluation of the literature  [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1-medical-knowledge|ⓘ]].



**Perioperative hyperglycemia, whether in known diabetics or as stress-induced hyperglycemia, is associated with increased risk of adverse outcomes including infection, renal and pulmonary complications, and mortality**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

* **Epidemiology of Perioperative Hyperglycemia**
  * Prevalence in hospitalized patients:
	* 32-38% in community hospitals
	* 41% in patients with acute coronary syndromes
	* 80% after cardiac surgery
  * Significant proportion (one-third of non-ICU, 80% of ICU patients) have no prior history of diabetes  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Pathophysiology of Hyperglycemia-Related Complications**
  * Immune dysfunction:
	* Impaired chemotaxis
	* Decreased phagocytosis
	* Reduced reactive oxygen species generation
	* Impaired intracellular bacterial killing
  * Vascular effects:
	* Decreased nitric oxide production
	* Endothelial dysfunction
	* Impaired microcirculation
  * Proinflammatory state:
	* Increased inflammatory mediators
	* Enhanced oxidative stress
	* Cellular damage  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Evidence for Adverse Outcomes**
  * Surgical site infections:
	* Dose-dependent relationship between glucose levels and infection rates
	* Each 40 mg/dL increase above 110 mg/dL associated with 30% higher risk
  * Mortality:
	* Higher perioperative mortality with poor glycemic control
	* Stronger association in non-diabetic patients with stress hyperglycemia
  * Other complications:
	* Increased length of stay
	* Pulmonary and renal complications
	* Myocardial infarction
	* Stroke
	* Wound complications  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Predictive Factors Beyond Absolute Glucose Level**
  * Glucose variability:
	* Independent predictor of mortality
	* Measures include:
	  * Standard deviation of glucose
	  * Mean amplitude of glycemic excursion (MAGE)
	  * Glycemic lability index (GLI)
  * Hypoglycemia:
	* Associated with increased mortality
	* Independent risk factor from hyperglycemia
  * Preoperative HbA1c:
	* Predictor of perioperative complications
	* Associated with postoperative infections, acute kidney injury, and mortality
	* Each 1% increase associated with 14% higher mortality risk after cardiac surgery  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

💡 The association between hyperglycemia and poor outcomes appears stronger in patients without known diabetes developing stress hyperglycemia than in patients with established diabetes. This may reflect a more pronounced stress response or lack of adaptation to hyperglycemia  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Perioperative hyperglycemia is associated with increased risks of infection, multiorgan complications, and mortality, with evidence suggesting a dose-dependent relationship. Beyond absolute glucose levels, glucose variability and hypoglycemic episodes are independent predictors of adverse outcomes. These associations provide the rationale for perioperative glucose control, though the optimal target range remains debated.

## 7.7. PERIOPERATIVE GLYCEMIC CONTROL

### 7.7.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Factors influencing perioperative glucose levels  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Approaches to perioperative glycemic management  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].


**Competency Focus:** , 

**Multiple factors influence glucose levels in the perioperative period, creating a complex environment that challenges glycemic control**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

* **Key Factors Influencing Perioperative Glucose Levels**
  * Endogenous insulin secretion
	* Affected by anesthetics
	* Impaired by surgical stress
  * Exogenous insulin administration
	* Absorption affected by peripheral perfusion
	* Dosing adjustments needed for fasting/feeding
  * Insulin resistance
	* Develops rapidly postoperatively
	* Directly related to surgical trauma magnitude
	* Enhanced by inflammatory state
	* Modified by nutritional intake and activity level
  * Endogenous glucose production
	* Increased due to stress hormone release
	* Enhanced by inflammatory mediators
  * Exogenous glucose administration
	* IV fluids containing dextrose
	* Enteral or parenteral nutrition
  * Glucose consumption
	* Altered by metabolic state
	* Affected by physical activity, tissue perfusion
	* Changed by medications  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Surgical Stress Response and Insulin Resistance**
  * Characterized by:
	* Increased counter-regulatory hormone release (cortisol, catecholamines, glucagon, growth hormone)
	* Enhanced inflammatory cytokine production
	* Resulting in significant insulin resistance
  * Time course:
	* Develops within hours of surgical incision
	* Peaks 1-2 days postoperatively
	* May persist for days to weeks
	* Severity proportional to surgical magnitude
  * Specific mediators:
	* TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1
	* Stress hormones
	* Free fatty acids  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Predictable Patterns of Perioperative Hyperglycemia**
  * High-risk patient groups:
	* Known diabetes, especially with poor control
	* Cardiac surgery patients
	* Major trauma or critical illness
	* Patients receiving corticosteroids
	* Obesity
  * High-risk procedures:
	* Cardiac surgery
	* Major vascular procedures
	* Transplantation
	* Extensive procedures with significant tissue trauma  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Impact of Anesthesia Technique**
  * Regional anesthesia:
	* May attenuate stress response
	* Can reduce insulin resistance compared to general anesthesia
	* Potentially less hyperglycemia
  * General anesthesia:
	* Various agents have differing effects on insulin secretion and resistance
	* Deeper anesthesia may reduce stress hormone release
	* Opioids can attenuate stress response  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

⚠️ Insulin absorption from subcutaneous tissue may be unpredictable perioperatively due to vasoconstriction, hypothermia, or altered tissue perfusion. This can lead to erratic glucose control when using subcutaneous insulin in the immediate perioperative period  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Perioperative glucose levels are influenced by a complex interplay of factors including surgical stress-induced insulin resistance, changes in insulin secretion/action, altered glucose production and utilization, and exogenous factors like medication and glucose administration. Understanding these factors is crucial for anticipating hyperglycemia risk and designing effective perioperative glucose management strategies.

## 7.8. GLYCEMIC GOALS

### 7.8.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Evolution of perioperative glucose targets  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Evidence-based recommendations  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Part C:** Critical appraisal of glycemic control literature  [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1-medical-knowledge|ⓘ]].


**Competency Focus:** , 

**Optimal glycemic targets for perioperative and critically ill patients have evolved significantly based on emerging evidence, with a trend away from very tight control toward more moderate targets to balance hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia risks**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

* **Evolution of Glycemic Control Recommendations**
  * Historical approach (pre-2001):
	* Permissive hyperglycemia ("stress hyperglycemia")
	* Limited intervention unless extreme hyperglycemia
	* Glucose often >180-200 mg/dL
  * Intensive insulin therapy era (2001-2009):
	* Initiated by Van den Berghe et al. landmark study (2001)
	* Target glucose: 80-110 mg/dL (tight control)
	* Showed mortality benefit in surgical ICU patients
	* Led to widespread adoption of intensive insulin therapy (IIT)
  * Reassessment period (2009-present):
	* NICE-SUGAR study (2009) showed increased mortality with IIT
	* Multiple studies showed high hypoglycemia rates (8-28%) with IIT
	* Recognition of hypoglycemia as independent mortality risk
	* Shift toward more moderate targets (140-180 mg/dL)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Key Studies Informing Current Practice**
  * Van den Berghe et al. (2001):
	* Single-center, surgical ICU study
	* Target: 80-110 mg/dL vs. 180-200 mg/dL
	* Showed 42% relative reduction in ICU mortality
	* Significantly reduced bloodstream infections, acute kidney injury, critical illness polyneuropathy
	* Patients received significant exogenous glucose early in ICU stay  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
  
  * NICE-SUGAR (2009):
	* Multicenter, multinational RCT (6,104 ICU patients)
	* Target: 81-108 mg/dL vs. 144-180 mg/dL
	* Found 90-day mortality was higher in intensive group (27.5% vs. 24.9%)
	* Severe hypoglycemia much more common in intensive group (6.8% vs. 0.5%)
	* Contradicted Van den Berghe findings  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
  
  * Meta-analyses (post-NICE-SUGAR):
	* Showed no overall mortality benefit of IIT
	* Confirmed increased hypoglycemia rates
	* Suggested possible benefit in surgical (not medical) patients
	* Indicated potential benefit when glucose targets achieved  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Current Recommendations by Professional Organizations**
  * American Association of Clinical Endocrinologists (AACE)/American Diabetes Association (ADA):
	* Start insulin therapy at threshold ≤180 mg/dL
	* Target range: 140-180 mg/dL for most critically ill patients
	* Lower targets (110-140 mg/dL) may be appropriate for select patients if hypoglycemia can be avoided
  * Society of Critical Care Medicine (Surviving Sepsis Campaign):
	* Target blood glucose <180 mg/dL
  * American College of Physicians:
	* Target glucose 140-200 mg/dL in ICU patients
  * Society of Thoracic Surgeons:
	* Target 150-180 mg/dL for cardiac surgery
  * European Society of Cardiology:
	* Maintain glucose <180 mg/dL, avoid hypoglycemia  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Special Considerations**
  * Cardiac surgery patients:
	* May benefit from moderate glucose control
	* Target 140-180 mg/dL commonly used
  * Patients receiving total parenteral nutrition:
	* May benefit from tighter control
	* Similar to original Van den Berghe study population
  * Stroke patients:
	* Hyperglycemia associated with worse outcomes
	* Hypoglycemia particularly detrimental
	* Moderate targets recommended
  * Neurosurgical patients:
	* Moderate control appropriate
	* Avoid both hyperand hypoglycemia
  * Non-critically ill hospitalized patients:
	* Pre-meal glucose <140 mg/dL
	* Random glucose <180 mg/dL
	* Less stringent targets for terminally ill, elderly, frail patients  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

⚠️ Very tight glycemic control (80-110 mg/dL) appears to increase the risk of hypoglycemic complications without clearly reducing hyperglycemic complications. Hypoglycemia is an independent predictor of mortality and should be avoided as diligently as severe hyperglycemia  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Current evidence supports a moderate approach to perioperative and critical care glycemic control, targeting 140-180 mg/dL for most patients. This range balances the risks of hyperglycemia-related complications against the dangers of hypoglycemia seen with more intensive control. Individual patient factors, surgical procedure, and clinical setting should inform the specific approach and target range.

## 7.9. MANAGEMENT OF PERIOPERATIVE HYPERGLYCEMIA

### 7.9.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Practical approaches to glucose management  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Implementation of perioperative protocols  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Part C:** Clinical decision-making in glycemic management  [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1-medical-knowledge|ⓘ]].


**Competency Focus:** , 

**Maintaining perioperative glucose levels within a desired range requires a systematic approach with protocols tailored to the specific clinical setting and patient population**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

* **General Management Principles**
  * Target glucose range: 140-180 mg/dL for most patients
  * More resource-intensive protocols required for narrower target ranges
  * Balance risks of hyperand hypoglycemia
  * Consider patient factors:
	* Type and severity of diabetes
	* Preoperative control
	* Comorbidities
	* Surgical procedure
  * Consider institutional factors:
	* Monitoring capabilities
	* Nursing ratios
	* Availability of standardized protocols  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Insulin Administration Methods**
  * Subcutaneous sliding scale
	* Simplest approach
	* Limitations:
	  * Unpredictable absorption in perioperative setting
	  * Reactive rather than proactive
	  * Maintains glycemic control only 40-60% of time
	  * Inadequate for timely correction
	* Appropriate for:
	  * Stable, non-critically ill patients
	  * Minor procedures
	  * Well-controlled diabetes  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
  
  * Continuous intravenous insulin infusion
	* Most effective for perioperative control
	* Advantages:
	  * Predictable pharmacokinetics
	  * Rapid titration capability
	  * Short half-life (safer if feeding interrupted)
	  * Allows quick response to changing conditions
	* Requires:
	  * Standardized protocol
	  * Frequent glucose monitoring (hourly)
	  * More intensive nursing care
	* Recommended for:
	  * Major surgery in diabetic patients
	  * Cardiac surgery
	  * Critically ill patients
	  * Patients with poor control  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
  
  * Basal-bolus regimen
	* Mimics physiologic insulin secretion
	* Components:
	  * Basal: Long-acting insulin (glargine, detemir, degludec)
	  * Prandial: Rapid-acting insulin with meals
	  * Correction: Additional rapid-acting insulin for hyperglycemia
	* Superior to sliding scale for hospitalized patients
	* Transition from IV insulin:
	  * Calculate 24-hour insulin requirement
	  * Administer 50-80% as basal insulin
	  * Remainder as prandial and correction doses
	* Appropriate for:
	  * Patients resuming oral intake
	  * Transition from IV to subcutaneous insulin
	  * Stable ward patients  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Glucose Monitoring**
  * Methods:
	* Central laboratory analysis (gold standard)
	* Blood gas analyzers
	* Point-of-care glucose meters
  * Considerations:
	* Point-of-care device accuracy varies by ±20%
	* Affected by hematocrit, medications, perfusion state
	* Arterial, venous, and capillary samples may differ
  * Frequency:
	* Every 1-2 hours during IV insulin therapy
	* Every 4-6 hours for subcutaneous regimens
	* More frequent with unstable glucose or insulin adjustments
  * Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM):
	* Emerging role in hospital setting
	* May allow earlier detection of trends
	* Requires validation with direct glucose measurements
	* Studies assessing effectiveness ongoing  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Specific Patient Populations**
  * Type 1 Diabetes:
	* Must maintain some insulin to prevent ketoacidosis
	* Options:
	  * IV insulin infusion 0.5-2 units/hour
	  * Subcutaneous basal insulin (50-80% of usual dose)
	  * Concurrent dextrose infusion (5% at 75-125 mL/hr) while fasting
  * Type 2 Diabetes:
	* Various approaches based on procedure and control:
	  * IV insulin for major surgery or poor control
	  * Basal insulin plus correction for stable patients
	  * Avoid sole reliance on sliding scale
  * Post-bariatric surgery:
	* Rapid resolution of glucose intolerance may occur
	* Reduce or discontinue oral agents and insulin
	* Monitor closely for hypoglycemia  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

💡 When transitioning from intravenous to subcutaneous insulin, administer the first dose of subcutaneous basal insulin 1-2 hours before discontinuing the infusion to prevent rebound hyperglycemia due to the gap in insulin coverage  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Perioperative glucose management requires structured protocols tailored to individual patient needs. Continuous IV insulin infusion provides the most precise control for major surgery, critical illness, or poorly controlled diabetes, while basal-bolus regimens better serve patients resuming oral intake. Regular glucose monitoring and attention to both hyperand hypoglycemia risks are essential components of effective management.

## 7.10. DIABETIC EMERGENCIES

### 7.10.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Recognition and management of acute diabetic complications  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Perioperative management of emergencies  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Part C:** Critical care management  [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1-medical-knowledge|ⓘ]].


**Competency Focus:** , 

**Hyperglycemic hyperosmolar state (HHS) and diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) represent two extremes in the spectrum of decompensated diabetes mellitus that may present perioperatively or be triggered by surgical stress**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

### 7.10.2. HYPERGLYCEMIC HYPEROSMOLAR STATE

* **Definition and Epidemiology**
  * Syndrome of profound hyperglycemia, hyperosmolarity, and dehydration without significant ketosis
  * Less common than DKA but higher mortality (10-20% vs. 5%)
  * Typically affects elderly with mild or undiagnosed type 2 diabetes
  * Often precipitated by:
	* Infection
	* Stroke
	* Myocardial infarction
	* Surgery
	* Medications (glucocorticoids, thiazides)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Pathophysiology**
  * Relative insulin deficiency:
	* Sufficient to prevent lipolysis and ketogenesis
	* Insufficient for glucose utilization
  * Hyperglycemia-induced osmotic diuresis
  * Impaired thirst response and decreased fluid intake
  * Progressive dehydration (9-12L deficit common)
  * Increased plasma viscosity
  * Electrolyte depletion  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Clinical Presentation**
  * Extreme hyperglycemia (often >600 mg/dL, can exceed 1000 mg/dL)
  * Hyperosmolarity (>320 mOsm/L)
  * Profound dehydration
  * Altered mental status, lethargy, progressing to coma
  * Absence of significant acidosis
  * Minimal or no ketonemia
  * Laboratory findings:
	* Hypernatremia (corrected for glucose)
	* Pre-renal azotemia
	* Hyperosmolality
	* Variable electrolyte abnormalities  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Management**
  * Fluid resuscitation (top priority):
	* Initial: Isotonic saline 1-2 L (15-30 mL/kg) over first hour
	* Subsequent: Guided by hemodynamics, electrolytes, urine output
	* Total deficit replacement over 24-72 hours
  * Insulin therapy:
	* Begin after initial volume resuscitation
	* Lower doses than DKA typically required
	* Regular insulin 0.1 units/kg IV bolus followed by 0.1 units/kg/hr infusion
	* Transition to subcutaneous when stable and glucose <300 mg/dL
  * Electrolyte replacement:
	* Potassium based on serum levels and renal function
	* Phosphate if severely depleted
  * Identification and treatment of precipitating cause
  * Glucose monitoring and adjustment:
	* When glucose reaches 250-300 mg/dL, add dextrose to IV fluids
	* Continue insulin to maintain glucose 200-250 mg/dL until osmolality normalized  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Complications and Monitoring**
  * Cerebral edema with rapid correction
  * Thromboembolism due to hyperviscosity
  * Delayed recovery of mental status
  * Cardiac arrhythmias from electrolyte shifts
  * Monitor:
	* Mental status
	* Vital signs
	* Input/output
	* Electrolytes
	* Glucose
	* Calculated osmolality  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

### 7.10.3. DIABETIC KETOACIDOSIS

* **Definition and Epidemiology**
  * Acute metabolic complication defined by triad of:
	* Hyperglycemia (typically >250 mg/dL)
	* Ketosis (increased ketone bodies)
	* Metabolic acidosis (pH <7.3, bicarbonate <18 mmol/L)
  * Mortality rate approximately 1-5%
  * Accounts for approximately 1% of diabetes-related emergencies
  * Most common in type 1 diabetes but can occur in type 2  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Pathophysiology**
  * Absolute insulin deficiency
  * Uninhibited counter-regulatory hormone activity (glucagon, catecholamines, cortisol)
  * Enhanced lipolysis and fatty acid metabolism
  * Production of ketone bodies (acetoacetate, β-hydroxybutyrate)
  * Osmotic diuresis from hyperglycemia
  * Volume depletion and electrolyte disturbances
  * Euglycemic DKA:
	* DKA with normal or mildly elevated glucose
	* Associated with SGLT2 inhibitor use, pregnancy, starvation  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Clinical Presentation**
  * Polyuria, polydipsia
  * Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain
  * Kussmaul respirations (deep, rapid breathing)
  * Fruity breath odor (acetone)
  * Altered mental status (varies with severity)
  * Laboratory findings:
	* Hyperglycemia (typically 250-600 mg/dL)
	* Metabolic acidosis with increased anion gap
	* Ketonemia and ketonuria
	* Elevated BUN/creatinine (pre-renal)
	* Hyponatremia (dilutional)
	* Variable potassium (often normal/high initially despite total body deficit)
	* Hypophosphatemia  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Management**
  * Fluid replacement:
	* 1-2 L isotonic saline in first 1-2 hours
	* Continued at 15-20 mL/kg/hr initially
	* Transition to 0.45% saline when osmolality improves
  * Insulin therapy:
	* Regular insulin 0.1 units/kg IV bolus
	* Followed by continuous infusion at 0.1 units/kg/hr
	* If glucose doesn't fall by 10% in first hour, double infusion rate
	* When glucose <250 mg/dL, add dextrose to IV fluids
	* Continue insulin until acidosis resolves
  * Electrolyte replacement:
	* Potassium: Start replacement when K+ <5.3 mEq/L if adequate renal function
	* Phosphate: Replace if severe (<1.0 mg/dL)
	* Bicarbonate: Generally not recommended unless pH <6.9 or hemodynamic instability
  * Monitoring:
	* Hourly glucose until stable
	* Electrolytes every 2-4 hours
	* Anion gap to track resolution
	* Mental status, vital signs, input/output
  * Transition to subcutaneous insulin:
	* When ketoacidosis resolves
	* Patient able to eat
	* Calculate appropriate doses based on IV requirements  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Special Considerations**
  * Euglycemic DKA with SGLT2 inhibitors:
	* Can occur with glucose <200 mg/dL
	* Higher insulin doses may be needed
	* More dextrose supplementation required
	* Pharmacologic effect may persist 10-14 days after discontinuation
  * Perioperative DKA:
	* Can be precipitated by surgical stress
	* May manifest atypically
	* Requires aggressive management before non-emergent surgery
	* Consider arterial line and central venous access for severe cases  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

### 7.10.4. HYPOGLYCEMIA

* **Definition and Classification**
  * Level 1: Glucose <70 mg/dL but ≥54 mg/dL
  * Level 2: Glucose <54 mg/dL (neuroglycopenic threshold)
  * Level 3: Severe event requiring assistance for recovery
  * Major limiting factor in glycemic management  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Etiology in Perioperative Setting**
  * Insulin or sulfonylurea administration without adequate glucose
  * Missed meals or prolonged fasting
  * Decreased clearance of insulin (renal or hepatic dysfunction)
  * Decreased counter-regulatory hormone response
  * Alcohol consumption
  * Sepsis (late phase)  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Clinical Presentation**
  * Autonomic symptoms (often blunted or absent in anesthetized patients):
	* Sweating
	* Palpitations
	* Tremor
	* Hunger
	* Anxiety
  * Neuroglycopenic symptoms:
	* Confusion
	* Altered consciousness
	* Visual disturbances
	* Seizures
	* Coma
  * May be misinterpreted as light anesthesia (sympathetic hyperactivity)
  * Symptoms blunted in patients with autonomic neuropathy or taking β-blockers  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Management**
  * Alert patients: 15-20g oral carbohydrate (juice, glucose tablets)
  * Unconscious/NPO patients:
	* 25g IV dextrose (50 mL of D50W)
	* 1 mg glucagon IM if IV access unavailable
  * Goal: Achieve blood glucose >100 mg/dL
  * Monitor for recurrence (especially with long-acting insulin or sulfonylureas)
  * Identify and address cause
  * Document episode and adjust subsequent therapy  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

⚠️ Hypoglycemia can be difficult to diagnose in anesthetized patients as the typical autonomic symptoms may be masked. Consider hypoglycemia in any case of unexplained hemodynamic instability, especially in diabetic patients, and check blood glucose promptly  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Diabetic emergencies including HHS, DKA, and hypoglycemia require prompt recognition and management. HHS is characterized by extreme hyperglycemia, hyperosmolarity, and dehydration without significant ketosis, while DKA presents with the triad of hyperglycemia, ketosis, and metabolic acidosis. Treatment priorities include fluid resuscitation, insulin therapy, electrolyte replacement, and addressing the underlying cause. Vigilance for hypoglycemia is essential, particularly in anesthetized patients where symptoms may be masked.

# 8. ENDOCRINE RESPONSE TO SURGICAL STRESS





## 8.1. NEUROENDOCRINE PATHWAYS

### 8.1.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Physiological mechanisms of stress response  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Clinical relevance to anesthesia  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].



**Anesthesia, surgery, and trauma elicit a generalized endocrine metabolic response characterized by increased levels of stress hormones and significant metabolic changes including hyperglycemia and negative nitrogen balance**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

* **Definition and Overview**
  * Multi-system, neuroendocrine and metabolic response to injury
  * Aims to produce a state of catabolism and maintain cardiovascular stability
  * Begins as a neuroendocrine reflex
  * Involves complex interplay between neural and humoral factors
  * Duration and magnitude proportional to injury severity
  * Evolutionary adaptation to promote survival  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Activation Pathways**
  * Afferent signals:
	* Nociceptive stimuli from surgical site
	* Baroreceptor activation (hypotension)
	* Chemoreceptor stimulation (hypoxemia, acidosis)
	* Peripheral tissue factors (cytokines, damage-associated molecular patterns)
	* Psychological factors (anxiety, fear)
  * Integration centers:
	* Hypothalamus (primary coordinator)
	* Brainstem
	* Spinal cord
	* Limbic system
  * Efferent pathways:
	* Hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis
	* Sympathetic nervous system
	* Renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Key Hormonal Responses**
  * HPA axis activation:
	* Corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) from hypothalamus
	* Adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) from anterior pituitary
	* Cortisol from adrenal cortex
  * Sympathetic stimulation:
	* Catecholamine release (epinephrine, norepinephrine)
	* Renin release leading to angiotensin II production
  * Other hormones:
	* Vasopressin (ADH): Fluid retention, vasoconstriction
	* Growth hormone: Tissue repair, protein anabolism
	* Glucagon: Glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis
	* Aldosterone: Sodium and water retention
	* Prolactin: Immune modulation
	* Endorphins: Endogenous analgesia  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** The endocrine response to surgical stress involves complex neuroendocrine pathways that activate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and sympathetic nervous system. This results in increased levels of stress hormones that prepare the body for the physiological challenges of trauma or surgery, though the response may be maladaptive in some clinical contexts.

## 8.2. METABOLIC EFFECTS

### 8.2.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Metabolic consequences of stress response  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Clinical implications of metabolic alterations  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].



**The metabolic component of the stress response is characterized by catabolism, hyperglycemia, fluid retention, and increased metabolic rate, aimed at providing substrates and energy for wound healing and immune function**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

* **Carbohydrate Metabolism**
  * Hyperglycemia due to:
	* Increased gluconeogenesis (from amino acids, glycerol, lactate)
	* Enhanced glycogenolysis
	* Insulin resistance in peripheral tissues
	* Relative insulin deficiency
  * Mediated by:
	* Cortisol, catecholamines, glucagon
	* Inflammatory cytokines
  * Clinical implications:
	* Stress hyperglycemia (even in non-diabetics)
	* Exacerbation of pre-existing diabetes
	* Associated with increased infection risk and mortality
	* Impaired wound healing above certain thresholds  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Protein Metabolism**
  * Accelerated protein catabolism:
	* Skeletal muscle breakdown
	* Negative nitrogen balance
	* Increased urinary nitrogen excretion
	* Release of amino acids for gluconeogenesis
  * Mediated primarily by cortisol
  * Clinical implications:
	* Muscle weakness
	* Delayed wound healing
	* Immune dysfunction
	* Prolonged recovery  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Lipid Metabolism**
  * Increased lipolysis:
	* Breakdown of triglycerides
	* Release of free fatty acids and glycerol
	* Enhanced fat oxidation
  * Ketone production increased
  * Mediated by:
	* Catecholamines
	* Cortisol
	* Growth hormone
  * Clinical implications:
	* Alternative energy source
	* Substrate for gluconeogenesis
	* May contribute to insulin resistance  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Fluid and Electrolyte Changes**
  * Salt and water retention due to:
	* Increased ADH (vasopressin)
	* Elevated aldosterone
	* Altered renal handling of water and electrolytes
  * Potassium and magnesium loss
  * Clinical implications:
	* Fluid volume shifts
	* Edema
	* Electrolyte disturbances
	* Potential cardiac arrhythmias  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Energy Expenditure**
  * Increased metabolic rate:
	* Elevated oxygen consumption
	* Increased CO2 production
	* Enhanced thermogenesis
  * Body temperature often elevated
  * Clinical implications:
	* Increased cardiorespiratory demands
	* Increased nutritional requirements
	* Potential for respiratory insufficiency in compromised patients  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Inflammatory Response**
  * Increased cytokine production:
	* TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6
	* Acute phase reactants
  * Complement activation
  * Neutrophil and macrophage migration
  * Clinical implications:
	* Systemic inflammatory response
	* Potential progression to organ dysfunction
	* Interaction with metabolic derangements  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

⚠️ The hypermetabolic response to surgery, with increased oxygen consumption and CO2 production, has important implications for patients with limited cardiopulmonary reserve. Adequate ventilation and cardiovascular support are essential during the perioperative period  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** The metabolic effects of surgical stress include hyperglycemia, protein catabolism, increased lipolysis, salt and water retention, and elevated metabolic rate. While these changes represent an adaptive response to provide energy and substrates for healing, they can also contribute to complications, particularly in vulnerable patients or when the response is excessive or prolonged.

## 8.3. PERIOPERATIVE MODIFICATIONS

### 8.3.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Factors affecting stress response  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].
* **Part B:** Strategies to modulate stress response  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Part C:** Clinical application of modulation techniques  [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1-medical-knowledge|ⓘ]].


**Competency Focus:** , 

**The endocrine-metabolic stress response to surgery can be modified by anesthetic technique, medication selection, and surgical approach, with potential implications for patient outcomes**  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]].

* **Effects of Anesthetic Techniques**
  * General Anesthesia:
	* Induction increases catecholamines (form of metabolic stress)
	* Deep general anesthesia may suppress stress hormone elevation
	* Different agents have varying effects on stress response
	* Does not completely block adrenocortical and glycemic responses
  * Regional Anesthesia:
	* Can block part of metabolic stress response
	* Neural blockade prevents afferent signals from surgical area
	* More effective below umbilicus (upper abdominal/thoracic procedures have additional pathways)
	* Postpones cortisol elevation
	* Attenuates hyperglycemia and protein catabolism
	* May reduce postoperative insulin resistance
  * Combined Techniques:
	* General plus epidural/spinal provides more complete blockade
	* Effective for major procedures
	* Reduces complications in high-risk patients  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Pharmacologic Modifications**
  * Opioids:
	* High-dose opioids reduce hypothalamic and pituitary secretory responses
	* Used historically in cardiac surgery to blunt stress response
	* Dose-dependent effect
  * α2-Agonists (clonidine, dexmedetomidine):
	* Reduce sympathetic outflow
	* Decrease catecholamine levels
	* Attenuate hyperglycemia
  * β-Blockers:
	* Block peripheral effects of catecholamines
	* Reduce myocardial oxygen demand
	* Limited effect on metabolic response
  * Etomidate:
	* Inhibits cortisol production by blocking 11β-hydroxylase
	* Even single dose can suppress adrenal function for 8 hours
	* Long infusions can cause adrenal insufficiency
  * Propofol:
	* Modest effect on stress response
	* Some anti-inflammatory properties
  * Ketamine:
	* Increases catecholamine levels
	* May enhance aspects of stress response  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Surgical Factors**
  * Surgical Technique:
	* Minimally invasive approaches reduce stress response
	* Magnitude proportional to tissue trauma
	* Laparoscopic procedures demonstrate attenuated response compared to open surgery
  * Procedure Duration:
	* Longer procedures typically elicit greater response
	* Prolonged elevation of stress hormones
  * Surgical Site:
	* Lower extremity and lower abdominal procedures - easier to block response
	* Upper abdominal, thoracic, and cranial procedures - more difficult to control response
	* Multiple pathways involved in upper body procedures  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Other Modulatory Approaches**
  * Temperature Management:
	* Hypothermia enhances stress response
	* Normothermia reduces catecholamine and cortisol elevation
  * Pain Management:
	* Multimodal analgesia reduces afferent nociceptive input
	* Preemptive analgesia may modulate central sensitization
  * Anti-inflammatory Strategies:
	* Steroids
	* NSAIDs
	* Specialized pro-resolving mediators
  * Enhanced Recovery Protocols:
	* Combine multiple approaches
	* Evidence for reduced complications and length of stay
	* Include elements targeting stress response modulation  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Clinical Implications and Outcomes**
  * Short-term Benefits of Modulation:
	* Improved hemodynamic stability
	* Better glycemic control
	* Reduced protein catabolism
	* Decreased postoperative pain
  * Potential Long-term Benefits:
	* Reduced complication rates
	* Improved immune function
	* Faster functional recovery
	* Potentially improved oncologic outcomes
  * Individualized Approach:
	* Consider underlying conditions
	* Procedure type and extent
	* Patient risk factors
	* Available resources  [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#endocrine-functions|ⓘ]],  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]

💡 While complete blockade of the stress response might seem desirable, some components of the response may be beneficial for recovery and healing. The goal should be modulation rather than complete elimination of the stress response  [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c5-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** The endocrine-metabolic stress response to surgery can be significantly modified through choice of anesthetic technique, pharmacologic interventions, and surgical approach. Regional anesthesia, particularly when combined with general anesthesia, provides the most effective blockade of the stress response for procedures below the umbilicus. These modifications may improve short-term perioperative outcomes and potentially affect long-term recovery.
