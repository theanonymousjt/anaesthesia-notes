# 1. DEFINITION AND EPIDEMIOLOGY OF OBESITY
<!-- EPA-1:K1:obesity EPA-1:EQ6 EPA-2:K-B1-B9:bariatric EPA-2:EQ9:morbidly_obese -->

## 1.1. DEFINITION OF OBESITY

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|Part A: CVS Responses]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|Part B: Obesity]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Patient Care and Procedural Skills|Part C: Patient Care]]

* **World Health Organization definition:** A condition with excess body fat to the extent that health and well-being are adversely affected [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]].
* **Classification by BMI (kg/m²):**
  * <18.5 = Underweight [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * 18.5-24.9 = Normal/Healthy weight
  * 25-29.9 = Overweight
  * 30-34.9 = Obesity (class I)
  * 35-39.9 = Obesity (class II) (formerly known as 'morbid obesity' if associated with an obesity-related comorbidity) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * ≥40 = Morbid obesity/Obesity class III (formerly known as 'morbid obesity')
  * ≥50 = Super obesity
  * ≥60 = Super-super obesity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]

## 1.2. BODY COMPOSITION CONCEPTS

* **Ideal Body Weight (IBW):** Weight associated with lowest mortality for given height and gender [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Calculated using Broca index: IBW (kg) = height (cm) − x, where x is 100 for adult males and 105 for adult females [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
* **Predicted Body Weight (PBW):** Similar to IBW, used in medical literature [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Males: PBW (kg) = 50 + 0.91 × (height [cm] − 152.4)
  * Females: PBW (kg) = 45.5 + 0.91 × (height [cm] − 152.4) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
* **Lean Body Weight (LBW):** Total body weight minus adipose tissue [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Approximates 80% and 75% of total body weight for males and females respectively
  * More accurate formulas:
	* Males: 1.10 × TBW – 0.0128 × BMI × TBW
	* Females: 1.07 × TBW – 0.0148 × BMI × TBW [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * In morbidly obese patients, increasing IBW by 20-30% gives estimate of LBW [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * The Schnider and Minto models use the following formulas for LBW [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
	* LBM men = 1.1 × TBW – 128 × (TBW/height (in cm))²
	* LBM women = 1.07 × TBW – 148 × (TBW/height (in cm))² [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]

## 1.3. BODY FAT DISTRIBUTION

* **Android (central) obesity:** Adipose tissue predominantly in upper body (truncal distribution) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Associated with increased oxygen consumption
  * Increased incidence of cardiovascular disease
  * Visceral fat particularly associated with cardiovascular disease and left ventricular dysfunction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
* **Gynecoid (peripheral) obesity:** Adipose tissue predominantly in hips, buttocks, and thighs [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Less metabolically active
  * Less associated with cardiovascular disease [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
* **Body circumference indices:** Helpful in classifying patterns of obesity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Waist circumference
  * Waist-to-height ratio
  * Waist-to-hip ratio
  * These correlate with mortality and risk for developing obesity-related diseases [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]

## 1.4. EPIDEMIOLOGY

* Rates of obesity worldwide have nearly tripled since 1975 [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
* Age-adjusted prevalence of obesity in US increased from 30.5% to 42.4% from 1999-2000 to 2017-2018 [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
* Prevalence of severe obesity also increased from 4.7% to 9.2% during the same period [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
* Distribution is uneven by geography, race, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
* Over 60% of adults in the UK are overweight or obese, particularly in lower socioeconomic groups [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
* Pediatric obesity is an increasing problem, especially with Type II diabetes on the rise [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]

## 1.5. INFLAMMATORY PROCESSES IN OBESITY

* **Proinflammatory state:** There is increasing evidence that obesity stimulates the innate immune system to increase production of proinflammatory mediators including [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α)
  * Interleukin-6 (IL-6) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
* This low-grade state of chronic inflammation is thought to be at least partially responsible for many of the pathophysiological changes associated with obesity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
* The state of inflammation promotes development of insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and hyperlipidemia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
* There is also an association between obesity and osteoarthritis (OA) in non-weight-bearing joints (e.g., hands), suggesting inflammatory processes beyond biomechanical effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]] 

**Bottom Line:** Obesity is defined as BMI ≥30 kg/m², with further classifications based on severity. The distribution of excess fat (android vs. gynecoid) significantly impacts associated health risks. Various body weight concepts (IBW, PBW, LBW) are used for different clinical applications, particularly drug dosing. Obesity involves chronic low-grade inflammation that contributes to many of its pathophysiological effects. Obesity prevalence continues to rise globally, with significant health and economic impacts.

# 2. MANAGEMENT OF OBESITY

## 2.1. MEDICAL THERAPY

> **Syllabus**: [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|Part B: Obesity Management]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Patient Care and Procedural Skills|Part C: Patient Care]]

* **Indications for drug treatment:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
  * BMI ≥30 kg/m²
  * BMI between 27-29.9 kg/m² with obesity-related medical complications [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
* **FDA-approved medications:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
  * **Phentermine (Adipex-P):**
	* Noradrenergic and dopaminergic sympathomimetic amine that decreases appetite
	* Can induce tachycardia, palpitations, hypertension
	* Risk of dependence, abuse, withdrawal symptoms
	* Approved for 3 months' use only [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
  * **Phentermine-topiramate (Qsymia):**
	* Side effects include dry mouth, paresthesias, constipation, insomnia, dizziness [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
  * **Lorcaserin:** Serotonin receptor antagonist that stimulates serotonin type 2c receptor [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
  * **Bupropion-naltrexone (Contrave):**
	* Bupropion is dopamine and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor
	* Stimulates pro-opiomelanocortin neurons
	* Naltrexone enhances efficacy by releasing feedback inhibition [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
  * **Liraglutide (Saxenda):**
	* Promotes weight loss by preventing resorption of glucose and water in renal tubules [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
  * **Orlistat (OTC Alli, prescribed Xenical):**
	* Blocks absorption of dietary fat by inhibiting lipases in GI tract
	* Side effects: oily spotting, liquid stools, fecal urgency, flatulence, abdominal cramping
	* Chronic use may result in fat-soluble vitamin deficiency
	* May cause prolonged prothrombin time with normal PTT reflecting vitamin K deficiency [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
* **Over-the-counter preparations and herbal supplements:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
  * Limited evidence for efficacy and safety
  * Common categories:
	* Pancreatic lipase inhibitors (caffeine, green/black tea)
	* Appetite suppressants (hoodia, Korean ginseng, ephedra, sunflower oil)
	* Stimulants of energy expenditure (acai berry, caffeine)
	* Regulators of lipid metabolism (soybean, fish oil, oolong tea, caffeine) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Can interfere with anesthesia and cause complications during surgery [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
* **Role of leptin in appetite control:**
  * Leptin is a peptide hormone secreted by adipose cells that acts at the hypothalamus to inhibit hunger [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * It is involved in a negative-feedback loop to maintain body fat stores – more adipose cells lead to more leptin secretion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * However, leptin levels are paradoxically high in obese patients: the hypothalamus becomes resistant to circulating leptin and fails to produce a feeling of satiety [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Other appetite-regulating hormones include [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
	* Appetite suppressors: cholecystokinin
	* Appetite stimulators: ghrelin (gut peptide), insulin, dopamine, serotonin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]] 

## 2.2. BARIATRIC SURGERY

* **Current indications:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
  * BMI >40 kg/m²
  * BMI >35 kg/m² with obesity-related comorbidities not controlled with medical therapy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
  * Recent calls to alter threshold to BMI 35 kg/m² or BMI 30 kg/m² with comorbidities [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
* **Classification of procedures:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
  * **Malabsorptive:**
	* Jejunoileal bypass (no longer performed)
	* Biliopancreatic diversion
	* Biliopancreatic diversion with duodenal switch [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
  * **Restrictive:**
	* Vertical-banded gastroplasty
	* Adjustable gastric banding
	* Sleeve gastrectomy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
  * **Combined:**
	* Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) - combines gastric restriction with minimal malabsorption [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
* **Common procedures:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
  * **Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB):**
	* Most commonly performed bariatric procedure
	* Patients lose 50-60% excess body weight
	* BMI decrease of approximately 10 kg/m² during first 12-24 postoperative months
	* Type II diabetes resolves in majority of patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
  * **Sleeve gastrectomy:** Second most commonly performed restrictive procedure [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
  * **Laparoscopic adjustable gastric banding (LAGB):** Uses adjustable inflatable band to alter stomach capacity [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
  * **Vertical-banded gastroplasty:** Restricts food intake [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
* **Less invasive techniques:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
  * **Implantable abdominal vagal nerve stimulator:**
	* Placed laparoscopically
	* Emits electrical impulses to control gastric emptying and signal satiety
	* ⚠️ Can be adversely affected by defibrillation, electrocautery, lithotripsy, MRI, and therapeutic radiation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]
  * **Intragastric balloons and prostheses:**
	* Placed endoscopically as temporary measure to increase satiety
	* Often bridge to more definitive bariatric procedures
	* ⚠️ Adequate control of PONV critical to avoid balloon dislodgement [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Different Physiological States|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Medical therapy for obesity includes FDA-approved medications targeting appetite, energy utilization, or nutrient absorption, though with limited long-term success. Leptin, a satiety hormone, is paradoxically elevated in obesity due to hypothalamic resistance. Bariatric surgery remains the most effective treatment for morbid obesity, with various procedures (restrictive, malabsorptive, or combined) offering different risk-benefit profiles. RYGB is the most common procedure, producing 50-60% excess weight loss and resolution of Type II diabetes in most patients.

# 3. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF OBESITY

## 3.1. RESPIRATORY SYSTEM

* **Changes in respiratory mechanics:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Decreased chest wall and lung compliance** due to fat accumulation on thorax and abdomen [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Decreased lung compliance** partially explained by increased pulmonary blood volume [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Increased elastic resistance** and decreased compliance of chest wall [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]]
  * Compliance further reduced when supine, leading to shallow and rapid breathing [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]]
  * Increased work of breathing and limited maximum ventilatory capacity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]]
  * Respiratory muscle efficiency is below normal [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]]
* **Lung volume changes:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Decreased functional residual capacity (FRC)** primarily due to reduced expiratory reserve volume (ERV) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Decreased vital capacity and total lung capacity** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Relationship between FRC and closing capacity adversely affected** (Fig. 45-1) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]]
  * ERV reductions are most commonly reported abnormalities [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]]
  * Residual volume and closing capacity remain unchanged [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]]
  * 💡 Reduced FRC can result in lung volumes below closing capacity during normal tidal ventilation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]]
	* Leads to small airway closure, V/Q mismatch, right-to-left shunting, and arterial hypoxemia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Anesthesia and supine position worsen this situation - up to 50% reduction in FRC in obese anesthetized patients (vs. 20% in non-obese) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]]
  * FEV1 and FVC usually within normal limits [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]]

![Effects of obesity, positioning, and anesthesia on lung volumes](BARIATRIC_image-1.png_6a05d6.png)

* **Metabolic effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Increased oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production** even at rest [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]]
	* Due to metabolic activity of excess fat and increased workload on supportive tissues [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]]
  * Body compensates by increasing cardiac output and alveolar ventilation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]]
  * Basal metabolic activity usually within normal limits in relation to body surface area [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]]
  * Normocapnia usually maintained by increased minute ventilation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]]
  * Requires increased oxygen consumption [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]]
  * Most obese patients retain normal response to hypoxemia and hypercapnia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Arterial oxygen tension** lower than predicted for similarly aged non-obese subjects in both sitting and supine positions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]]
  * Chronic hypoxemia may lead to polycythemia, pulmonary hypertension, and cor pulmonale [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]]
* **Additional respiratory effects:** 
  * **Increased prevalence of asthma:** Obese patients are twice as likely to develop asthma as the non-obese population [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]] 
	* Cause is unclear but may be related to chronic inflammation associated with obesity or decreased airway caliber due to reduced lung volumes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]] 
  * **Rapid desaturation:** Obese patients have the potential for rapid desaturation at induction of anesthesia due to increased O₂ consumption combined with decreased FRC [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]] 
	* Pre-oxygenation prior to induction is of great importance and is perhaps best achieved using high-flow nasal oxygen therapy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Changes with Physiology/Pathophysiology|ⓘ]] 

## 3.2. OBSTRUCTIVE SLEEP APNEA (OSA)

* **Definition and pathophysiology:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Causes/Effects of Hypoxia and Ventilation Disorders|ⓘ]]
  * Periodic, partial, or complete obstruction of the upper airway during sleep [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Causes/Effects of Hypoxia and Ventilation Disorders|ⓘ]]
  * In obese patients, more likely from airway obstruction produced by excess soft tissue [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Causes/Effects of Hypoxia and Ventilation Disorders|ⓘ]]
  * Centrally mediated forms can also occur [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Causes/Effects of Hypoxia and Ventilation Disorders|ⓘ]]
  * Increased prevalence of upper airway collapse [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Causes/Effects of Hypoxia and Ventilation Disorders|ⓘ]] 
* **Physiologic abnormalities:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Causes/Effects of Hypoxia and Ventilation Disorders|ⓘ]]
  * Hypoxemia, hypercapnia
  * Pulmonary hypertension
  * Systemic vasoconstriction, hypertension
  * Secondary polycythemia (from recurrent hypoxemia) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Causes/Effects of Hypoxia and Ventilation Disorders|ⓘ]]
  * Increased risk of ischemic heart disease and cerebrovascular disease [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Causes/Effects of Hypoxia and Ventilation Disorders|ⓘ]]
  * Right ventricular failure can occur from hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Causes/Effects of Hypoxia and Ventilation Disorders|ⓘ]]
  * Respiratory acidosis usually limited to periods of sleep [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Causes/Effects of Hypoxia and Ventilation Disorders|ⓘ]]
* **Diagnosis:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Causes/Effects of Hypoxia and Ventilation Disorders|ⓘ]]
  * Gold standard: Overnight polysomnography (OPS) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Causes/Effects of Hypoxia and Ventilation Disorders|ⓘ]]
  * Screening questionnaires (STOP-BANG) increasingly used [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Causes/Effects of Hypoxia and Ventilation Disorders|ⓘ]]
  * Suggestive signs:
	* Witnessed episodes of apnea during sleep
	* BMI ≥35
	* Neck circumference ≥16 inches (40 cm)
	* Hyperinsulinemia
	* Elevated glycosylated hemoglobin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Causes/Effects of Hypoxia and Ventilation Disorders|ⓘ]]
  * Common but not predictive symptoms:
	* Snoring
	* Frequent arousals during sleep
	* Daytime sleepiness
	* Impaired concentration
	* Memory problems
	* Morning headaches [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Causes/Effects of Hypoxia and Ventilation Disorders|ⓘ]]
* **Management considerations:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Causes/Effects of Hypoxia and Ventilation Disorders|ⓘ]]
  * Thorough preoperative evaluation should occur long before elective surgery [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Causes/Effects of Hypoxia and Ventilation Disorders|ⓘ]]
  * Preoperative CPAP initiation should be considered, especially in severe cases [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Causes/Effects of Hypoxia and Ventilation Disorders|ⓘ]]
  * Blood pressure and glucose/lipid metabolism improvements seen with CPAP plus weight loss [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Causes/Effects of Hypoxia and Ventilation Disorders|ⓘ]]
  * Alternative therapies for CPAP-intolerant patients include implantable hypoglossal stimulator [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Causes/Effects of Hypoxia and Ventilation Disorders|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Patients with confirmed or suspected OSA should be considered high risk for:
	* Difficult airway
	* Postoperative pulmonary complications [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Causes/Effects of Hypoxia and Ventilation Disorders|ⓘ]]
  * Postoperative management recommendations:
	* Semi-upright position
	* Continuous pulse oximetry monitoring
	* Avoiding opioid-based analgesia
	* Titrated oxygen and/or CPAP as needed [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Causes/Effects of Hypoxia and Ventilation Disorders|ⓘ]]

## 3.3. OBESITY HYPOVENTILATION SYNDROME (OHS)

* **Definition and characteristics:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Causes/Effects of Hypoxia and Ventilation Disorders|ⓘ]]
  * Also known as Pickwickian syndrome
  * Combination of obesity and chronic hypoventilation
  * Seen in 5-10% of morbidly obese patients
  * Frequently misdiagnosed and untreated [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Causes/Effects of Hypoxia and Ventilation Disorders|ⓘ]]
  * Diagnostic criteria:
	* Obesity (BMI >30 kg/m²)
	* Awake arterial hypercapnia (PaCO₂ >45 mmHg)
	* Absence of other known causes of hypoventilation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Causes/Effects of Hypoxia and Ventilation Disorders|ⓘ]]
* **Pathophysiology:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Causes/Effects of Hypoxia and Ventilation Disorders|ⓘ]]
  * May result from long-term OSA
  * Alters control of breathing, leading to CNS-mediated apneic events
  * Increased reliance on hypoxic drive for ventilation
  * Main ventilatory impairment is alveolar hypoventilation independent of intrinsic lung disease [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Causes/Effects of Hypoxia and Ventilation Disorders|ⓘ]]
* **Clinical features and complications:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Daytime hypersomnolence
  * Hypercapnia
  * Hypoxemia
  * Polycythemia
  * Pulmonary hypertension
  * Cor pulmonale
  * Right ventricular failure eventually ensues [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Increased sensitivity to respiratory depressant effects of general anesthetics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Results in increased risk of postoperative complications and death [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Causes/Effects of Hypoxia and Ventilation Disorders|ⓘ]]

## 3.4. CARDIOVASCULAR AND HEMATOLOGIC SYSTEMS

* **Blood volume and flow:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Total blood volume increased but lower on volume-to-weight basis (50 mL/kg vs. 70 mL/kg in non-obese) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Most extra volume distributed in adipose tissue [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Renal and splanchnic blood flows increased [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Cardiac output increases with increasing weight (20-30 mL/kg of excess body fat) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Blood flow to fat is 2-3 mL/100g of tissue [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
* **Cardiac adaptations:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Ventricular dilation and increased stroke volume [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Increased left ventricular wall stress leads to:
	* Hypertrophy
	* Reduced compliance
	* Impaired left ventricular filling (diastolic dysfunction)
	* Elevated left ventricular diastolic pressure
	* Pulmonary edema [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * When left ventricular wall thickening can't keep pace with dilation:
	* Systolic dysfunction ("obesity cardiomyopathy")
	* Eventually biventricular failure (Fig. 45-2) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Accelerated atherosclerosis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * 💡 Morbidly obese patients often have limited mobility and may appear asymptomatic even with significant cardiovascular disease [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]

![Interrelationship of cardiovascular and pulmonary sequelae of obesity](BARIATRIC_image-2.png_de59ee.png)

* **Adaptation to excess body mass:** 
  * Excess body mass requires an increase in intravascular volume and cardiac output (mainly due to increased stroke volume) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]] 
  * Systemic hypertension is common in obesity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]] 
  * The combination of increased stroke volume and hypertension results in left ventricular hypertrophy and increased left ventricular work [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]] 
  * Respiratory changes (hypoxemia) can stimulate hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, leading to pulmonary hypertension and increased right ventricular work [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]] 
  * This can lead to right ventricular hypertrophy and failure (cor pulmonale) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]] 
* **Potential complications:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * **Intraoperative ventricular failure** may occur from:
	* Rapid IV fluid administration (indicating LV diastolic dysfunction)
	* Negative inotropism of anesthetic agents
	* Pulmonary hypertension from hypoxia/hypercapnia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * **Cardiac dysrhythmias** precipitated by:
	* Fatty infiltration of conduction system
	* Hypoxia, hypercapnia
	* Electrolyte imbalance
	* Coronary artery disease
	* Increased circulating catecholamines
	* OSA
	* Myocardial hypertrophy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
* **ECG findings:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Low QRS voltage
  * Multiple criteria for left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH)
  * Left atrial enlargement
  * T-wave flattening in inferior and lateral leads
  * Leftward shift of P-wave, QRS complex, and T-wave axes
  * Lengthening of corrected QT interval
  * Prolongation of QT interval
  * 💡 Substantial weight reduction reverses many of these ECG abnormalities [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
* **Hemodynamic changes:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Cardiac output rises faster in response to exercise in morbidly obese
  * Often associated with rise in left ventricular end-diastolic pressure and pulmonary capillary wedge pressure
  * Similar changes occur during perioperative period
  * Many obese patients have mild-to-moderate hypertension
	* 3-4 mmHg increase in systolic and 2 mmHg in diastolic pressure for every 10 kg weight gained [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Normotensive obese patients have reduced systemic vascular resistance [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Expanded blood volume causes increased cardiac output with lower calculated SVR, leading to same arterial pressure level [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
* **Neurohumoral changes:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Renin-angiotensin system plays major role in hypertension of obesity
	* Increased circulating levels of angiotensinogen, aldosterone, and ACE
	* Even 5% weight reduction leads to significant reduction in renin-angiotensin activity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Normal-to-increased sympathetic nervous system activity
	* Predisposes to insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and hypertension
	* Insulin resistance enhances vasopressor activity of norepinephrine and angiotensin II
	* Hyperinsulinemia activates sympathetic nervous system, causing sodium retention [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
* **Ventricular adaptations:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Hypertension causes concentric hypertrophy in normal-weight individuals but eccentric dilation in obese individuals
  * Associated with increased preload and stroke work
  * Combination of obesity and hypertension causes LV wall thickening, larger heart volume, and increased likelihood of cardiac failure (Fig. 45-3) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]

![Adaptation of the heart to obesity and hypertension](BARIATRIC_image-3.png_b09721.png)

* **Hypercoagulability:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Adipose tissue releases bioactive mediators causing abnormal lipids, insulin resistance, inflammation, and coagulopathies [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Higher levels of:
	* Fibrinogen (marker for atherosclerosis)
	* Factor VII, Factor VIII
	* von Willebrand factor
	* Plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Increased fasting triglyceride levels correlate with increased factor VII
  * Postprandial lipemia causes activation of factor VII
  * Endothelial dysfunction induced by insulin increases von Willebrand factor and factor VIII
  * Increased PAI-1 inhibits fibrinolytic system and is associated with visceral obesity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ These changes increase risk for perioperative thromboembolic events [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Increased risk of venous thromboembolism; intermittent calf compressors should be used intraand post-operatively until patient is adequately mobile [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]] 

## 3.5. GASTROINTESTINAL SYSTEM

* **Gastric changes:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Increased gastric volume and acidity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Many fasting morbidly obese patients have gastric volumes >25 mL and pH <2.5 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Delayed gastric emptying due to:
	* Increased abdominal mass causing antral distention
	* Gastrin release
	* Decrease in pH with parietal cell secretion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Abdominal obesity increases intragastric pressure, increasing:
	* Frequency of transient lower esophageal sphincter relaxation
	* Hiatal hernia formation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * BMI increase >3.5 kg/m² associated with 2.7-fold increased risk for reflux symptoms [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Increased incidence of hiatal hernia and gastroesophageal reflux [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ These factors increase aspiration risk [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Gastroesophageal reflux is common due to lower esophageal incompetence and increased intragastric pressure; general anesthesia is associated with an increased aspiration risk [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]] 
* **Gastric emptying characteristics:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * Faster with high-energy content intake (e.g., fat emulsions)
  * Larger gastric volume (up to 75% larger) leads to increased residual volume [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#CVS Responses to Changes|ⓘ]]
  * 💡 Unpremedicated non-diabetic fasting obese surgical patients without significant gastroesophageal pathology are unlikely to have high-volume, low-pH gastric contents after routine preoperative fasting [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Liver Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Should follow same fasting guidelines as non-obese patients (clear liquids up until 2 hours before elective surgery) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Liver Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Weight loss significantly improves gastroesophageal reflux symptoms [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Liver Physiology|ⓘ]]
* **Hepatic changes:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Liver Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Morphologic and biochemical abnormalities:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Liver Physiology|ⓘ]]
	* Fatty infiltration (high prevalence of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [NAFLD])
	* Inflammation (non-alcoholic steatohepatitis [NASH])
	* Focal necrosis
	* Cirrhosis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Liver Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Fatty infiltration reflects duration rather than degree of obesity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Liver Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Abnormal liver function tests seen in up to one-third of obese patients without concomitant liver disease [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Liver Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Most common abnormality is increased alanine aminotransferase (ALT) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Liver Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Despite histologic and enzymatic changes, no clear correlation exists between liver function abnormalities and capacity to metabolize drugs [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Liver Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Morbidly obese patients who have undergone intestinal bypass surgery have particularly high prevalence of hepatic dysfunction and cholelithiasis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Liver Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Cholelithiasis common in general obese population due to abnormal cholesterol metabolism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Liver Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Features suggestive of NASH:
	* Hepatomegaly
	* Elevated liver enzymes
	* Abnormal liver histology (steatosis, steatohepatitis, fibrosis, cirrhosis) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Liver Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Increased prevalence of gallstone disease and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Liver Physiology|ⓘ]] 

## 3.6. RENAL AND ENDOCRINE SYSTEMS

* **Glucose metabolism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]
  * Impaired glucose tolerance common in morbidly obese
  * High prevalence of type II diabetes mellitus due to peripheral adipose tissue insulin resistance [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]
  * Many obese patients have abnormal glucose tolerance test
  * Relative risk of developing diabetes increases by 25% for every 1 kg/m² increase in BMI above 22 kg/m² [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]
  * Hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, and diabetes predispose to:
	* Wound infections
	* Increased risk of myocardial infarction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Exogenous insulin may be required perioperatively even in type II diabetics to oppose catabolic response to surgery [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]
* **Thyroid function:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]
  * Subclinical hypothyroidism occurs in about 25% of morbidly obese patients
  * Thyroid-stimulating hormone levels frequently elevated
  * Suggests obesity may lead to thyroid hormone resistance in peripheral tissues
  * Hypothyroidism may be associated with:
	* Hypoglycemia
	* Hyponatremia
	* Impaired hepatic drug metabolism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]
* **Renal function:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]
  * Glomerular hyperfiltration evidenced by:
	* Increased renal blood flow
	* Increased glomerular filtration rate [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]
  * Excessive weight gain causes:
	* Increased renal tubular resorption
	* Impaired natriuresis via sympathetic and renin-angiotensin system activation
	* Physical compression of the kidney [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]
  * With prolonged obesity, may be loss of nephron function with:
	* Further impairment of natriuresis
	* Further increases in arterial pressure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]
  * Obesity-related glomerular hyperfiltration decreases after weight loss [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]
  * Decreases incidence of overt glomerulopathy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]

## 3.7. METABOLIC SYNDROME

* **Definition:** Cluster of metabolic abnormalities associated with increased risk of diabetes and cardiovascular events [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]
  * Also referred to as syndrome X and insulin resistance syndrome [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]
* **Associated risk:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]
  * Up to fivefold greater risk of developing type 2 diabetes (if not already present)
  * Twice as likely to die from myocardial infarction or stroke compared to those without syndrome
  * More likely to have perioperative adverse events:
	* Cardiovascular complications
	* Pulmonary complications
	* Renal complications
	* Wound infections [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]
* **Diagnostic criteria (NCEP ATP III):** Three or more of the following five conditions: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]
  1. Central obesity: Waist circumference ≥102 cm (40 in) in males, ≥88 cm (35 in) in females
  2. Dyslipidemia: Triglycerides ≥150 mg/dL
  3. Dyslipidemia: HDL <40 mg/dL in males, <50 mg/dL in females
  4. Hypertension: ≥130/85 mmHg or use of antihypertensives
  5. Elevated fasting glucose: ≥100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L) or use of medication for hyperglycemia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]

* **Management:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]
  * Weight loss and lifestyle changes can improve metabolic syndrome features
  * Bariatric surgery resolves metabolic syndrome in >95% of patients who achieve expected weight loss [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]

## 3.8. MUSCULOSKELETAL SYSTEM CHANGES 

* **Increased prevalence:** Obesity is associated with higher rates of [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]] 
  * Osteoarthritis (OA)
  * Gout [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]] 
* **Mechanism:** Not purely biomechanical [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]] 
  * Expected association between OA in weight-bearing joints and obesity
  * Also increased prevalence of OA in non-weight-bearing joints (e.g., hands)
  * Suggests inflammatory mechanisms beyond simple mechanical stress
  * Low-grade inflammation may play a role in joint disease [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]] 

**Bottom Line:** Obesity profoundly affects multiple organ systems. Respiratory changes include decreased lung compliance, reduced FRC, and increased work of breathing, leading to V/Q mismatch and potential hypoxemia. OSA (common in obese patients) and OHS (untreated OSA) increase perioperative risk through hypoxemia, hypercarbia, and altered ventilatory responses. Cardiovascular adaptations include increased blood volume, cardiac output, and LV hypertrophy, often progressing to diastolic then systolic dysfunction. Gastrointestinal changes include delayed gastric emptying, GERD, and hepatic steatosis. Metabolic syndrome (present in many obese patients) combines central obesity, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and glucose intolerance, significantly increasing perioperative risk.

# 4. PHARMACOLOGY IN OBESITY

## 4.1. PHARMACOKINETIC PRINCIPLES

* **General dosing principles:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]
  * Drug dosing should consider:
	* Volume of distribution (VD) for loading dose
	* Clearance for maintenance dose [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]
  * Loading dose calculation:
	* For drugs mainly distributed to lean tissues: based on lean body weight (LBW)
	* For drugs equally distributed between adipose and lean tissues: based on total body weight (TBW) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]
  * Maintenance dose calculation:
	* For drugs with similar clearance in obese and non-obese: based on LBW
	* For drugs with clearance increased with obesity: based on TBW [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]
* **Body composition effects:** `[A:22-23]`
  * Central compartment volume relatively unchanged in obese patients
  * Absolute body water content decreased
  * Lean body and adipose tissue mass increased, affecting lipophilic and polar drug distribution (Fig. 45-4) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]

![Body composition in extremely obese and weight-reduced states](BARIATRIC_image.png_23694e.png)

* **Factors affecting volume of distribution:** `[A:22-23]`
  * Reduced total body water
  * Increased total body fat
  * Increased lean body mass
  * Altered protein binding
  * Increased blood volume
  * Increased cardiac output
  * Increased blood concentrations of free fatty acids, triglycerides, cholesterol, and α1-acid glycoprotein
  * Lipophilicity of the drug
  * Organomegaly [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]
* **Pharmacokinetic alterations:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
  * **Increased redistribution** of drugs prolongs elimination half-life even when clearance is unchanged or increased [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
  * **Protein binding alterations:**
	* Hyperlipidemia and increased α1-acid glycoprotein may reduce free drug concentration
	* Plasma albumin and total plasma protein concentrations generally unchanged
	* Relative increase in plasma protein binding may be evident compared to normal-weight individuals [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
  * **Bioavailability:**
	* Splanchnic blood flow, blood volume, and cardiac output all increased
	* Despite expected decrease in bioavailability of oral medications due to increased splanchnic flow, no significant difference in absorption and bioavailability compared to normal-weight subjects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
* **Drug metabolism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
  * **Phase I metabolism** (oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis) generally unaffected by obesity
  * **Phase II reactions** (glucuronidation, sulfation) enhanced by obesity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
  * **Hepatic clearance:**
	* Histologic abnormalities and deranged liver function tests common
	* Drug clearance not usually affected [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
  * **Renal clearance:**
	* Increased due to increased renal blood flow and glomerular filtration rate
	* Drugs dependent on renal excretion (e.g., cimetidine, aminoglycosides) may require increased dosing [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
* **Distribution based on lipophilicity:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
  * **Highly lipophilic substances** (barbiturates, benzodiazepines):
	* Significant increases in VD for obese individuals
	* More selective distribution to fat stores
	* Longer elimination half-life but comparable clearance values [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
  * **Less lipophilic compounds:** Little or no change in VD with obesity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
  * **Exceptions:** Highly lipophilic drugs digoxin, procainamide, and remifentanil [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
  * **Weak/moderate lipophilicity drugs:** May be dosed on LBW basis
	* Adding 20% to estimated IBW dose of hydrophilic medications sufficient for extra lean mass
	* Nondepolarizing muscle relaxants can be dosed this way [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
* **Special considerations:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
  * **Increased blood volume** decreases plasma concentrations of rapidly injected IV drugs
  * **Poor blood flow to fat** means doses calculated on actual body weight could lead to excessive plasma concentrations
  * 💡 **Practical approach:** Calculate initial doses based on LBW with subsequent doses determined by response to initial dose
  * ⚠️ **Repeated injections** may accumulate in fat, leading to prolonged response from subsequent release from this large depot [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
* **Pharmacokinetic alterations in obesity:** 
  * In obese patients, the pharmacokinetics of many drugs are altered due to [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]] 
	* Increased body lipid
	* Reduced lean body mass (LBM) ratio
	* Decreased total body water [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]] 
  * **Absorption issues:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]] 
	* Gastric emptying may be increased or decreased
	* Subcutaneous absorption is decreased due to poor blood supply
	* Intravenous access may be challenging
	* Intramuscular route may fail if needles are too short [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]] 
  * **Volatile anesthetics:** Show similar time to recovery in obese and non-obese patients [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]] 
	* Desflurane (least lipophilic) has only a minor advantage over isoflurane (most lipophilic) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]] 

## 4.2. SPECIFIC DRUG DOSING GUIDELINES

* **Induction agents:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
  * **Thiopental:**
	* Induction: LBW (somewhat increased)
	* Increased initial dose due to increased blood volume, CO, muscle mass
	* Prolonged duration due to high lipophilicity and increased VD
	* Longer elimination half-life [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
  * **Propofol:**
	* Induction: LBW (somewhat increased)
	* Maintenance infusion: TBW
	* Short duration due to rapid redistribution
	* Highly lipophilic
	* Cardiac output is major determinant of peak plasma concentration
	* Increased VD at steady state and increased clearance prevent increases in elimination half-life
	* High hepatic extraction and conjugation relate to TBW [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
* **Neuromuscular blocking agents:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
  * **Succinylcholine:** TBW
	* Pseudocholinesterase activity increases linearly with increasing weight
	* Dose should be increased [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
  * **Rocuronium:** LBW
	* Faster onset and longer duration when dosed according to TBW
	* Dosing according to LBW prevents delayed recovery due to increased VD [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
  * **Vecuronium:** LBW
	* Prolonged action when dosed according to TBW
	* Dosing according to LBW prevents delayed recovery due to increased VD and impaired hepatic clearance [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
  * **Atracurium:** LBW
	* VD, absolute clearance, and elimination half-life unchanged by obesity
	* Unchanged dose per unit body weight without prolongation of recovery due to organ function-independent elimination [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
  * **Cisatracurium:** LBW
	* Pharmacokinetics similar to atracurium but prolonged duration when dosed according to TBW [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
* **Sedatives & anxiolytics:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
  * **Benzodiazepines:**
	* Highly lipophilic drugs with larger VD in obese patients result in longer duration of action
	* Midazolam has potential for prolonged sedation because larger initial doses are required to achieve adequate serum concentrations [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
* **Opioids:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
  * **Fentanyl:** LBW
	* Measured total body clearance has nonlinear relationship to TBW
	* Fentanyl dosing based on derived LBW or "pharmacokinetic mass" model correlates better with clearance than TBW dosing
	* Dosing based on TBW overestimates dose requirements [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
  * **Sufentanil:** LBW
	* Highly lipid soluble
	* Increased VD and prolonged elimination half-life correlating with degree of obesity
	* Distributes extensively in excess body fat
	* Similar pharmacokinetic parameters in obese and non-obese predict similar plasma concentrations
	* Overestimation of plasma concentration occurs in morbidly obese range (BMI >40 kg/m²) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
  * **Remifentanil:** LBW
	* Pharmacokinetics similar in obese and non-obese patients
	* More closely related to lean body mass than LBW
	* Systemic clearance and VD corrected per kilogram of TBW significantly smaller in obese patients
	* Consider age and lean body mass for dosing [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
* **Alpha-2 agonists:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
  * **Dexmedetomidine:** TBW
	* Highly selective α2-adrenergic agonist
	* Sedative-hypnotic, anesthetic-sparing analgesic, sympatholytic properties
	* Lacks significant effects on respiration
	* Ideal analgesic adjuvant in morbidly obese
	* As part of balanced anesthetic, infusion rates of 0.2-0.7 μg/kg/h produce clinically effective sedation with decreased analgesic and anesthetic requirements [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
* **Reversal agents:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
  * **Neostigmine:** TBW
	* Prompt early reversal but delayed full recovery during neostigmine-induced reversal of vecuronium dosed according to TBW [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
  * **Sugammadex:**
	* Modified γ-cyclodextrin compound that encapsulates rocuronium (and other steroid-based neuromuscular blockers to lesser extent)
	* Recovery time does not correlate with BMI
	* Dosing can be based on actual body weight in both obese and non-obese patients
	* Valuable for rapid and complete neuromuscular blockade reversal in obese patients [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]

## 4.3. OTHER PERIOPERATIVE AGENTS

* **Preoperative medication management:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
  * Patients' usual medications should be continued until time of surgery
  * Possible exceptions:
	* Certain antihypertensives
	* Insulin
	* Oral hypoglycemics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
* **Antibiotic prophylaxis:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
  * Usually indicated due to increased incidence of wound infections
  * Continuous infusion of cefazolin throughout procedure shown to reduce surgical site infection rate (1.55%) compared to bolus ampicillin/sulbactam (4.16%) or ertapenem (1.98%) in RYGB patients [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
* **Preoperative medications:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
  * **Anxiolysis:** Oral benzodiazepines reliable; IV midazolam can be titrated in small doses
  * **Dexmedetomidine:** Advantageous due to minimal respiratory depression
  * **Aspiration prophylaxis:** H2-receptor antagonists, non-particulate antacids, or proton pump inhibitors reduce gastric volume, acidity, or both [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]

## 4.4. TARGET CONTROLLED INFUSIONS (TCI) IN OBESITY

* **Challenges with TCI in obesity:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]] 
  * Total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) is challenging in morbidly obese patients [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
  * Volume of distribution and rate constants of all models were derived from normal-weight subjects – the pharmacokinetic models are not formally validated in the morbidly obese [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
* **Propofol models:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]] 
  * **Marsh model:**
	* Uses TBW to calculate induction dose and maintenance infusion rates
	* In morbidly obese, induction bolus based on TBW likely represents significant overdose causing unwanted cardiovascular effects
	* Pump manufacturers limit input weight to 150 kg
	* Some anesthetists use modified input weight (e.g., Servin's formula: input weight = IBW + 0.4 × (TBW – IBW))
	* This modification accurately predicts plasma propofol concentration early but increasingly underdoses patients after 40 min, risking awareness [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]] 
  * **Schnider model:**
	* Uses age, height, and TBW inputs
	* Calculates LBM for algorithm
	* LBM calculation accurate up to BMI of 42 kg/m² in males and 37 kg/m² in females
	* At higher BMIs, calculated LBM paradoxically decreases
	* As Schnider model uses LBM to correct rate constant for drug elimination from central compartment, the effect is higher maintenance infusion and anesthetic overdose [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
* **Remifentanil model:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]] 
  * **Minto model:**
	* Uses same LBM formula as Schnider model with inaccuracies at high BMI
	* Clinical effect is underdosing of remifentanil during maintenance infusion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]]
* **Recommended approach:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]] 
  * Despite pharmacokinetic model flaws in morbidly obese, TIVA can still be used safely
  * Best strategy: Use Schnider and Minto models titrated to surgical depth of anesthesia using processed electroencephalogram monitoring [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacokinetics|ⓘ]] 

![Bariatric Drug Dosing](BARIATRIC_BARIATRIC_Bariatric_Drug_Dosing.001.png_a8ddaf.png_cb8bda.png)

**Bottom Line:** Obesity significantly alters drug pharmacokinetics through changes in volume of distribution, protein binding, and clearance. Lipophilic drugs typically have increased volume of distribution and prolonged elimination half-life. Drug dosing should generally use lean body weight for loading doses of drugs distributed primarily to lean tissue, with maintenance dosing based on clearance characteristics. TCI systems using Marsh, Schnider, or Minto models require careful consideration in obese patients due to their derivation from normal-weight individuals. Processed EEG monitoring can help titrate TCI administration in obese patients.

# 5. PREOPERATIVE EVALUATION

## 5.1. GENERAL APPROACH

* **Importance and focus:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Comprehensive preoperative evaluation is critical to:
	* Identify and address multisystem comorbidities
	* Develop individualized perioperative care plan [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Important risk predictors (can be assessed without complicated testing):
	* Central obesity
	* Metabolic syndrome
	* Peripheral oxygen saturation <95% breathing air [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Previous anesthetic experiences and records are valuable information sources [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]

## 5.2. AIRWAY ASSESSMENT

* **Importance of airway assessment:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Particularly critical in obese patients due to increased risk of difficult airway [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Both anatomic and physiologic changes contribute to airway management difficulties [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
* **Anatomic changes affecting airway:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Limitation of movement of atlantoaxial joint and cervical spine by upper thoracic/low cervical fat pads
  * Excessive tissue folds in mouth and pharynx
  * Short, thick neck
  * Thick submental fat pad
  * Suprasternal, presternal, and posterior cervical fat
  * Large breasts in females
  * Excess pharyngeal tissue in lateral pharyngeal walls (may not be noticed during routine examination) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Increased prevalence of upper airway collapse [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
* **Risk assessment:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Obesity is accepted risk factor for difficult mask ventilation and airway management [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Closed claims database shows obesity as risk factor in 68% of claims associated with difficult endotracheal intubation in US [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Morbidly obese patients (BMI >40) are four times as likely to experience airway management-related complication [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ A neck circumference of ≥40 cm is biggest predictor of:
	* Difficult mask ventilation
	* Difficult laryngoscopy
	* Difficult endotracheal intubation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * OSA contributes to difficult mask ventilation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Correct patient head positioning before induction is crucial; a head-elevating laryngoscopy pillow may be required [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]

## 5.3. CARDIOPULMONARY EVALUATION

* **Cardiovascular focus:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Evaluation should focus on issues peculiar to obese patients:
	* Systemic hypertension
	* Pulmonary hypertension
	* Signs of right and/or left ventricular failure
	* Ischemic heart disease [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Hypertension common due to:
	* Increased sympathetic activation
	* Proinflammatory state [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Hypertension management recommendations (American Heart Association 2017):
	* Systolic blood pressure <120 mmHg
	* Diastolic pressure <80 mmHg [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Detection challenges:
	* Difficult to detect signs of cardiac failure (elevated jugular venous pressure, pathologic heart sounds, pulmonary crackles, hepatomegaly, peripheral edema) due to excess adiposity [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Pulmonary hypertension:
	* More common due to chronic pulmonary impairment
	* Common symptoms: exertional dyspnea, fatigue, syncope (reflect inability to increase cardiac output during activity)
	* Further cardiac testing may be individually required based on initial findings [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Diagnostic tests:
	* Tricuspid regurgitation on echocardiography is most useful confirmatory test for pulmonary hypertension
	* ECG may show signs of right ventricular hypertrophy (tall precordial R waves, right axis deviation, right ventricular strain)
	* ECG sensitivity increases as pulmonary artery pressure increases
	* Chest radiographs may show underlying lung disease and prominent pulmonary arteries [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Increased incidence of ischemic heart disease due to systemic hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes mellitus [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Symptoms of significant coronary artery stenosis may be masked by physical inactivity [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
* **Respiratory evaluation:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Evidence of OSA and OHS should be obtained preoperatively
	* Frequently associated with difficult airway management
	* Increased perioperative pulmonary complications [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * OSA risk factors:
	* History of hypertension
	* Neck circumference >40 cm
  * ⚠️ OSA is legitimate reason to delay elective surgery for proper evaluation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Management considerations:
	* OSA patients generally treated as inpatients
	* Outpatient surgery possible under certain circumstances:
	  * Mild OSA
	  * Use of local/regional anesthesia with minimal sedation
	  * Availability of 23-hour observation PACU
	  * Patient can resume oral medication at discharge
	* OSA patients using CPAP at home should bring device to hospital (may be needed postoperatively)
	* Discuss possibility of invasive monitoring, prolonged intubation, and postoperative mechanical ventilation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Additional tests:
	* Arterial blood gas measurements help evaluate ventilation and need for perioperative oxygen/postoperative ventilation
	* Routine pulmonary function tests and liver function tests not cost-effective in asymptomatic obese patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]

## 5.4. METABOLIC ASSESSMENT

* **Metabolic and nutritional evaluation:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Abnormalities common in obese patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Glycemic management:
	* High prevalence of insulin resistance and diabetes justifies preoperative glycemia checks
	* Assess therapies for glycemia control, last administration timing/dosing, usual glucose values [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Electrolytes:
	* Should be checked before surgery
	* Particularly in patients with poor medication compliance or acutely ill patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Nutritional deficiencies:
	* Common deficiencies: vitamin B12, iron, calcium, folate
	* Can lead to acute postgastric reduction surgery (APGARS) neuropathy
	  * Polynutritional multisystem disorder
	  * Characterized by protracted postoperative vomiting, hyporeflexia, muscular weakness
	* Differential diagnoses: thiamine deficiency (Wernicke encephalopathy, beriberi), vitamin B12 deficiency, Guillain-Barré syndrome
	* Close attention to neuromuscular blocking agent dosing/monitoring recommended in suspected/diagnosed cases
	* Chronic vitamin K deficiency may lead to coagulation abnormalities requiring vitamin K analog or fresh-frozen plasma [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Repeat bariatric surgery:
	* Screen for long-term metabolic and nutritional abnormalities preoperatively [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]

## 5.5. THROMBOEMBOLIC RISK ASSESSMENT

* **Thromboembolism risk:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Morbid obesity is known risk factor for perioperative thromboembolic events
	* Includes sudden death from acute pulmonary embolism [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Thromboprophylaxis:
	* Guidelines exist, especially for bariatric surgery patients
	* American Society of Chest Physicians recommendations:
	  * Combination of intermittent pneumatic compression devices with heparin (unfractionated or LMWH)
	  * Greater doses may be needed in obese vs. non-obese patients
	  * Prolonged postoperative regimens (1-3 weeks) being explored in bariatric patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Significant risk factors for postoperative DVT:
	* Venous stasis disease
	* BMI ≥60
	* Central obesity
	* OHS and/or OSA
	* If present, consider preoperative prophylactic placement of inferior vena cava filter [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Alternative approach:
	* Combination of short surgery duration, lower extremity pneumatic compression, and early ambulation may preclude mandatory heparin anticoagulation
	* Exception: patients with previous DVT history, known hypercoagulable state, or significant DVT family history [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Preoperative Management|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Comprehensive preoperative evaluation of obese patients should focus on airway assessment (particularly with neck circumference ≥40 cm), cardiopulmonary status (including screening for OSA/OHS), metabolic evaluation (especially glucose control), and thromboembolic risk assessment. OSA is a legitimate reason to delay elective surgery, and nutritional deficiencies should be identified and corrected. Thromboprophylaxis is essential, with higher doses often needed in obese patients.

# 6. INTRAOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT

## 6.1. EQUIPMENT AND MONITORING

* **Operating room tables:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Special considerations:
	* May require specially designed tables or two regular OR tables
	* Regular OR tables have maximum weight limit ~200 kg
	* Tables capable of holding up to 455 kg with greater width/side accessories are available
	* Strapping patients to OR table with malleable beanbag helps prevent falls [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Monitoring challenges:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Blood pressure measurement:**
	* Careful selection of proper size cuff and location important
	* ⚠️ Measurements falsely elevated if cuff too small
	* Cuffs with bladders encircling minimum 75% (preferably entire arm) of upper arm circumference should be used
	* In supine position, forearm measurements with standard cuff can be used when upper arm measurements challenging [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
	* An upper arm non-invasive blood pressure cuff may have poor fit and thus be unreliable. Instead, either position the blood pressure cuff at the forearm or calf or consider invasive arterial line [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 
  * **Invasive monitoring considerations:**
	* Invasive arterial pressure monitoring not routine, but may be indicated for:
	  * Super morbidly obese patients
	  * Patients with cardiopulmonary disease
	  * When non-invasive BP cuff doesn't fit properly [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
	* Central venous catheterization:
	  * Not routinely needed
	  * May be required for IV access with inadequate peripheral access
	  * Used for perioperative fluid management [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
	* Advanced monitoring:
	  * Central venous catheters, pulmonary artery catheters, and/or TEE used selectively in:
		* Patients with significant cardiopulmonary disease
		* Patients undergoing extensive surgery [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Other practical difficulties:**
  * **Venous access:** Practical aspects of obesity increase difficulty of venous access procedures [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Regional anesthesia:** Technical challenges due to obscured landmarks and depth [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Surgical access:** May be more difficult [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Limb tourniquets:** May not adequately fit [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Patient positioning and lifting:** More difficult, requiring greater number of staff [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 
	* Special equipment may be required, such as:
	  * Intubation positioning aid
	  * Hover mattress
	  * Bariatric operating table and/or bed [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

## 6.2. POSITIONING CONSIDERATIONS

* **Supine position effects:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Causes ventilatory impairment
  * Inferior vena cava and aortic compression
  * Decreased FRC and oxygenation
  * Further worsening with head-down positioning (often required during bariatric procedures) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Physiologic changes with positioning:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Simply changing from sitting to supine can cause significant increase in oxygen consumption and cardiac output
  * Head-up position provides longest safe apnea period during induction by precluding hypoxemia if intubation delayed [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Intraoperative PEEP and head-up position both:
	* Significantly decrease alveolar-arterial oxygen tension difference
	* Increase total respiratory compliance to similar degree
	* Head-up position results in lower airway pressures
	* Both interventions decrease cardiac output significantly, partially counteracting beneficial effects on oxygenation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Alternative positions:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Prone positioning (rarely required):
	* Should be correctly performed with freedom of abdominal movement
	* Prevents detrimental effects on lung compliance, ventilation, and arterial oxygenation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Lateral decubitus allows better diaphragmatic excursion
	* Should be favored over prone positioning when surgical procedure permits [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Complications of positioning:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Particular care needed to protect pressure areas
  * Risk of pressure sores, neural injuries, and rhabdomyolysis
  * Brachial plexus and lower extremity nerve injuries frequent
  * Carpal tunnel syndrome is most common mononeuropathy after bariatric surgery
  * Other reported neurologic complications:
	* Encephalopathy (Wernicke)
	* Optic neuropathy
	* Myelopathy associated with vitamin B12 and copper deficiencies [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

## 6.3. AIRWAY MANAGEMENT

* **Preoxygenation:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Airway Management|ⓘ]]
  * **Importance:**
	* Vital in obese patients due to rapid desaturation after loss of consciousness
	* Related to increased oxygen consumption and decreased FRC [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Airway Management|ⓘ]]
  * **Technique considerations:**
	* 100% oxygen extends non-hypoxic apnea period despite increasing atelectasis formation
	* Add head-up position during preoxygenation to prolong non-hypoxic apneic period
	* Head-up position achieved with reversed Trendelenburg or semi-sitting position
	* May help prevent aspiration and facilitate visualization during laryngoscopy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Airway Management|ⓘ]]
  * **Additional strategies:**
	* Preoperative NIPPV, CPAP, or oxygen administration during induction delays peri-induction hypoxemia
	* Passive apneic oxygenation techniques can delay apneic hypoxemia:
	  * Oxygen supplementation by nasal cannula
	  * High-flow nasal cannula (HFNC)
	  * Laryngeal mask airway (LMA) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Airway Management|ⓘ]]
* **Mask ventilation:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Airway Management|ⓘ]]
  * Obese patients more likely to present with difficult mask ventilation and intubation
	* Especially with short, thick neck and OSA
  * When needed, exhaled tidal volumes may be greater in 25-degree head-up position versus supine
  * Two-handed technique preferred:
	* V-E position (thumbs along mask sides, first/second fingers behind mandible angle for jaw thrust) more effective than
	* C-E position (thumbs/first fingers on mask, fourth/fifth fingers on mandible) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Airway Management|ⓘ]]
* **Difficult airway management:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Airway Management|ⓘ]]
  * If difficult intubation anticipated, develop strategy based on:
	* Type of surgery
	* Skills of anesthesiologist
	* Patient cooperation and condition [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Airway Management|ⓘ]]
  * Strategy should include plans for:
	1. Awake intubation
	2. Patient who can be adequately ventilated but difficult to intubate
	3. Patient who cannot be ventilated or intubated
	4. Emergency invasive airway rescue [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Airway Management|ⓘ]]
  * **Awake intubation considerations:**
	* Reduce sedative-hypnotic medications to minimum
	* Dexmedetomidine provides adequate anxiolysis/analgesia without respiratory depression
	* ⚠️ Prevent hypoxia and aspiration of gastric contents at all costs
	* Have experienced colleague present or immediately available
	* Surgeon capable of surgical airway access should be readily available [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Airway Management|ⓘ]]
* **Positioning for intubation:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Airway Management|ⓘ]]
  * "Ramped" position elevates upper body above chest, improving laryngoscopic view compared to standard "sniffing" position
  * Use towels/folded blankets under shoulders and head to compensate for exaggerated flexed position from posterior cervical fat (Fig. 45-5)
  * Objective: Position patient so tip of chin is at higher level than chest to facilitate laryngoscopy and intubation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Airway Management|ⓘ]]

![Ramped position with "stacking" of towels and blankets](BARIATRIC_image-4.png_10767d.png)

* **Intubation equipment:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Airway Management|ⓘ]]
  * Direct laryngoscopy can be successful with optimal positioning
  * ⚠️ Immediately available alternative intubation tools highly recommended:
	* Videolaryngoscopes
	* Intubating stylettes (e.g., Eschmann stylet, tube exchanger)
	* LMAs
	* Fiberoptic bronchoscopes [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Airway Management|ⓘ]]
  * Videolaryngoscopes proven efficient for intubating obese patients:
	* Reduce intubation duration and number of attempts
	* Similar or better glottic view than standard direct laryngoscopy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Airway Management|ⓘ]]
  * Multiple laryngoscopic attempts associated with increased airway and hemodynamic complications
  * Closed claims data supports limiting conventional intubation attempts to three before using other strategies
  * LMAs useful for:
	* Temporarily achieving airway patency in difficult mask ventilation/intubation
	* Placing definitive endotracheal tube via intubating LMA [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Airway Management|ⓘ]]

## 6.4. INDUCTION AND MAINTENANCE

* **Induction considerations:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Oxygenation|ⓘ]]
  * **Rapid sequence induction (RSI):**
	* Often used due to concerns of hypoxemia, gastric regurgitation, and aspiration
	* Gastroesophageal reflux disease relatively common
	* Increased incidence of regurgitation and severe pneumonitis if aspiration occurs [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Oxygenation|ⓘ]]
  * **Gastric content considerations:**
	* Unpremedicated, non-diabetic fasting obese surgical patients without significant gastroesophageal pathology unlikely to have high-volume, low-pH gastric contents after routine fasting
	* Standard preoperative fasting guidelines (6 hours for solids, 2 hours for clear liquids) may be safe
	* RSI versus standard induction should be individually evaluated based on patient's risk of:
	  * Difficult mask ventilation
	  * Difficult intubation
	  * Hypoxemia
	  * Gastric aspiration [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Oxygenation|ⓘ]]
* **Anesthetic agents:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **General considerations:**
	* No systematic comparison of anesthetic agents/techniques available in obese patients
	* ⚠️ Larger doses of induction agents may be required due to increased blood volume, muscle mass, and cardiac output
	* Any commonly available IV induction agent may be used after considering individual patient problems [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Neuromuscular blocking agents:**
	* **Succinylcholine:**
	  * Increased dose necessary due to increased pseudocholinesterase activity
	  * Myalgia not frequently seen in morbidly obese patients
	  * Highly recommended for tracheal intubation, especially in challenging airways or high aspiration/hypoxemia risk
	  * Advantages: rapid onset and limited duration [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
	* **Rocuronium:**
	  * Can be considered for tracheal intubation
	  * More prolonged duration than succinylcholine [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Maintenance of anesthesia:**
	* Continuous infusion of short-acting IV agent (e.g., propofol)
	* Any inhalation agent
	* Combination approach [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
	* **Volatile agents:**
	  * Desflurane, sevoflurane, isoflurane minimally metabolized, useful in obese patients
	  * Desflurane possibly provides faster washout
	  * Nitrous oxide use limited by high oxygen demand despite rapid elimination and analgesic properties [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
	* **Opioids:**
	  * Short-acting preferred to provide analgesia without postoperative respiratory depression
	  * Remifentanil and fentanyl (carefully titrated) most common choices [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
	* **Adjuncts:**
	  * Dexmedetomidine (α2-agonist) has no clinically significant adverse respiratory effects
	  * Attractive anesthetic adjunct that reduces postoperative opioid requirements [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Muscle relaxation for laparoscopic procedures:**
	* Profound relaxation important to:
	  * Facilitate ventilation
	  * Maintain adequate working space for visualization
	  * Allow safe instrument manipulation
	  * Facilitate extraction of excised tissues
	* Collapse of pneumoperitoneum and tightening around port sites indicates inadequate relaxation
	* Vecuronium, rocuronium, and cisatracurium useful for maintenance
	* ⚠️ Pneumoperitoneum should not exceed 15 mmHg (≥20 mmHg can cause caval compression and decreased cardiac output)
	* Cephalad displacement of diaphragm/carina by pneumoperitoneum can displace secured ETT into main stem bronchus [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Special considerations for bariatric procedures:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Anesthesia personnel may need to:
	* Facilitate proper placement of intragastric balloon to help size gastric pouch
	* Assist with leak tests (saline or methylene blue through nasogastric tube)
	* ⚠️ Ensure tight endotracheal tube cuff seal to prevent aspiration of saline/methylene blue
	* Ensure complete removal of all endogastric tubes before gastric division to avoid unplanned stapling/transection [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

## 6.5. FLUID MANAGEMENT

* **Challenges and considerations:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Excess adipose tissue may mask peripheral perfusion, making fluid balance difficult to assess
  * Blood loss usually greater than in non-obese patients for same procedure:
	* Due to technical difficulties in accessing surgical site
	* Necessitates larger incisions and more extensive dissection [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * IV fluid requirements generally greater than predicted
  * Normovolemia should be the goal to avoid:
	* Increased hemodynamic instability
	* Postoperative nausea and vomiting
	* Acute tubular necrosis (ATN) from hypovolemia:
	  * Occurs in ~2% of bariatric surgery patients
	  * Risk factors: BMI >50 kg/m², prior renal disease, intraoperative hypotension, prolonged surgical times [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
	* Hypervolemic complications (congestive heart failure, peripheral edema, pulmonary complications)
  * ⚠️ Avoid rapid IV fluid infusion due to common pre-existing congestive cardiac failure
  * IBW estimates and appropriate monitoring help avoid potential hyperhydration
  * Preliminary findings suggest:
	* Urine output during laparoscopic bariatric surgery doesn't correlate with intraoperative fluid administration rate
	* Total volume of fluids infused doesn't affect incidence of postoperative rhabdomyolysis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

## 6.6. MECHANICAL VENTILATION

* **Challenges of ventilatory management:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Obesity makes ventilatory settings titration challenging (increasing weight doesn't imply proportional lung growth)
  * Obese patients likely to receive high tidal volumes due to:
	* Miscalculation of predicted/ideal body weight
	* High airway pressures from decreased respiratory system compliance
	* Low PEEP levels [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Similar ventilatory parameters to non-obese can be used, but maintaining plateau pressures ≤30 cmH₂O may be challenging
  * Greater inflation pressures may be tolerated:
	* Extra adipose tissue pressure on chest wall acts against inspiratory airway pressure
	* Partially attenuates lung overdistention [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* **Ventilation strategies:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * **Tidal volume:**
	* 6-8 mL/kg PBW tidal volumes recommended
	* Larger tidal volumes offer no added advantages
	* Further increasing tidal volumes only increases:
	  * Peak inspiratory airway pressure
	  * End-expiratory (plateau) airway pressure
	  * Lung compliance
	  * Without significantly improving arterial oxygen tension [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * **Ventilatory mode:**
	* No specific mode (volume vs. pressure control) significantly better for oxygenation and CO₂ clearance
	* Some studies show pressure modes correlate with increased oxygenation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * **PEEP and recruitment:**
	* PEEP is only ventilatory parameter consistently shown to improve respiratory function
	* Ideal PEEP value still unknown
	* Recruitment maneuvers + PEEP most effective for preventing postoperative atelectasis
	* These techniques more beneficial in obese vs. non-obese patients
	* Different recruitment methods exist:
	  * Pelosi method: series of three short (6 seconds) inflations with PCV, large tidal volume, inspiratory pressure 40-55 cmH₂O
	  * Higher-than-usual airway pressures may be needed to compensate for decreased chest wall compliance
	  * Achieve adequate transpulmonary pressure [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Ventilation|ⓘ]]
	* Benefits of recruitment + PEEP:
	  * Opens and maintains small airway patency
	  * Improves V/Q matching and oxygenation
	  * Leads to improved oxygenation, respiratory mechanics, reduced atelectasis
	  * Overall clinical outcome improvements remain unresolved
	* ⚠️ Recruitment maneuvers and PEEP can decrease venous return and cardiac output
	  * Usually adequately tolerated in normovolemic morbidly obese patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * **Oxygen concentration:**
	* FiO₂ should be titrated to minimum levels ensuring acceptable oxygenation
	* Avoid reabsorption atelectasis
	* Some experts recommend FiO₂ <0.8 in obese patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Ventilation|ⓘ]]

## 6.7. EMERGENCE AND EXTUBATION

* **Extubation strategy:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Extubation|ⓘ]]
  * Prompt but safe extubation reduces likelihood of ventilator dependence
	* Especially important in patients with underlying cardiopulmonary disease
  * Patient should preferably be extubated in semi-recumbent position
  * Recovery in sitting position provides more favorable respiratory mechanics than supine
  * Some institutions mandate two anesthesia providers for emergence/extubation of morbidly obese patients
  * Supplemental oxygen administration after extubation
  * Observation period of at least 5 minutes post-extubation before transport recommended
  * Risk of immediate postoperative hypoventilation with hypercapnia (with/without hypoxemia) has led to:
	* Development of non-invasive monitoring techniques
	* Increasing CPAP use [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Extubation|ⓘ]]
* **Patient transport:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Extubation|ⓘ]]
  * Lifting devices useful for safe transport:
	* HoverMatt (Patient Handling Technologies)
	* Patient transfer device (Pdf; Alimed)
	* Gantry-style mechanical lifting devices with slings
  * Pdf can be combined with Walter Henderson maneuver (Fig. 45-6) for safe, gentle transfer to postoperative beds [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Extubation|ⓘ]]

![Walter Henderson maneuver for patient transfer](BARIATRIC_image-5.png_c073a4.png)

**Bottom Line:** Intraoperative management of obese patients requires special consideration for equipment (appropriate OR tables, BP cuffs), positioning (ramped for intubation, head-up when possible), and airway management (adequate preoxygenation, preparation for difficult airway). Ventilation strategy should include lung-protective tidal volumes (6-8 mL/kg PBW), appropriate PEEP, and recruitment maneuvers. Anesthetic maintenance requires careful titration of agents (typically based on LBW), with short-acting agents preferred. Careful fluid management and monitoring are essential, with extubation performed when fully awake in semi-recumbent position.

# 7. ANESTHETIC TECHNIQUES

## 7.1. MONITORED ANESTHESIA CARE AND SEDATION

* **Respiratory considerations:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Monitoring adequacy of ventilation and oxygenation extremely important
  * Obese patients present higher risk of sedation-induced respiratory depression
  * Careful titration of benzodiazepines, opioids, and propofol mandatory to avoid hypercapnia/hypoxemia
  * Hypoxemia may require unplanned intubation
	* Thorough airway examination and preparation for unintended airway management critical even in MAC/sedation cases
  * Prevalence of closed claims related to adverse respiratory events during MAC increasing compared to general anesthesia
  * Obesity and suboptimal monitoring (pulse oximetry, end-tidal capnography) are significant factors in adverse MAC events [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

## 7.2. REGIONAL ANESTHESIA AND ANALGESIA

* **General considerations:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3 Local and Regional Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Neuraxial techniques (spinal, epidural, CSE) or peripheral nerve blocks can be used:
	* Alone
	* Combined with general anesthesia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3 Local and Regional Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * TAP blocks or wound infusions often effective for postoperative pain control
  * With proper planning, these techniques should be considered in anesthetic plan for appropriate candidates [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3 Local and Regional Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Advantages of regional techniques:**
	1. Minimal/reduced airway manipulation
	2. Fewer medications with cardiopulmonary depression
	3. Reduced PONV risk
	4. Better postoperative pain control
	5. Improved postoperative outcomes [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3 Local and Regional Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Combined techniques:**
	* When epidural anesthesia combined with general anesthesia, time to tracheal extubation may be reduced compared to general anesthesia alone
  * **Limitations:**
	* Increased risk of block failure in obese vs. normal-weight patients
	* Increased risk of complications
	* ⚠️ IV access should always be secured prior to block placement (risk of LAST or high spinal) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3 Local and Regional Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

## 7.3. NEURAXIAL ANESTHESIA

* **Physiologic changes with neuraxial anesthesia:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3 Local and Regional Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Can produce serious cardiopulmonary compromise requiring careful monitoring
  * **Pulmonary effects:**
	* Pulmonary mechanics, lung volumes, FRC, oxygenation, and ventilation already altered
	* Supine and Trendelenburg positioning during neuraxial anesthesia can worsen:
	  * Lung volumes
	  * Further FRC reductions
	* FRC may fall below closing capacity, promoting:
	  * Small airway collapse
	  * Atelectasis
	  * V/Q mismatch
	  * Hypoxia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3 Local and Regional Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
	* 💡 Helpful to measure oxygen saturation in sitting and supine positions to indicate pulmonary reserve before initiating neuraxial anesthesia
  * **Cardiovascular effects:**
	* Excess abdominal wall weight can compress vena cava, causing:
	  * Decreased cardiac preload
	  * Reflex tachycardia
	  * Decreased cardiac output
	* Early observations suggest spinal anesthesia in obese patients increases risk for:
	  * Hypotension
	  * Cardiac arrest
	* Supine positioning can contribute to these arrests [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3 Local and Regional Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Benefits versus risks:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3 Local and Regional Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Despite considerations, neuraxial techniques offer important advantages compared to general anesthesia alone
  * Parenteral opioid administration hazards:
	* Increased sensitivity to opioids
	* Risk of hypoxemia
	* High incidence of sleep apnea
	* Increased incidence of adverse respiratory events post-surgery
  * ASA guidelines for OSA patients recommend regional techniques to reduce/eliminate systemic opioid requirements [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3 Local and Regional Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Positioning and placement:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3 Local and Regional Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Positioning importance:**
	* Critical for successful neuraxial anesthetic placement
	* Sitting position assists with midline identification
	* Back should be parallel to bed edge to prevent lateral needle deviation
	* Lateral deviation increases depth to epidural/spinal spaces
	* Can result in block failure and increased risk for intraoperative conversion to general anesthesia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3 Local and Regional Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Landmark identification:**
	* Anatomic landmarks often obscured
	* If spinal processes not palpable with deep palpation:
	  * Draw line from cervical vertebral spinal process to uppermost portion of gluteal cleft
	  * This approximates midline over vertebral column
	* Ultrasound imaging helpful for identifying spinal processes
	  * Significantly reduces needle passes
	  * Decreases time for spinal block placement
	* If iliac crests difficult to appreciate:
	  * Use patient's skin folds to draw perpendicular line to vertical line
	  * Intersection point serves as reasonable needle insertion guide [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3 Local and Regional Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Technical challenges:**
	* Particularly difficult with:
	  * Non-palpable bony landmarks
	  * Limited back flexion
	  * False losses of resistance due to fat deposition
	* Difficult to predict depth to epidural space (general correlation with BMI)
	* Prepuncture ultrasonography useful to facilitate epidural placement
	  * Helps predict depth to epidural space
	  * Limitations: image quality compromised by overlying fat, distance may be inaccurate if subcutaneous tissue compressed
	* 25-gauge needle can help identify spinous processes during local anesthetic infiltration
	* Patient feedback helpful ("Does it feel like I'm in the middle of your back?")
	* Obese women less likely to identify midline by pinprick vs. non-obese
	  * Both groups 99% correct in identifying right/left of midline stimulus [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3 Local and Regional Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Equipment considerations:**
	* Standard neuraxial needles (9-10 cm) usually sufficient with midline placement
	* Longer needles (16 cm) sometimes needed in extremely obese patients
	* ⚠️ Longer needles can cause serious overpenetration injury - use only after careful midline assessment and when standard needles inadequate [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3 Local and Regional Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Spinal anesthesia:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3 Local and Regional Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Considerations:**
	* Popular but concerns about:
	  * Technical difficulties
	  * Exaggerated local anesthetic spread
	  * Hypotension
	  * Inability to prolong block
	* Reasonable if:
	  * Normal airway examination
	  * No cardiopulmonary disease
	  * Surgery expected <90 minutes
	* Often easier to insert spinal needle when large-gauge stiff epidural needle used as guide [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S8 General Surgical Urological Gynaecological and Endoscopic Procedures|ⓘ]]
  * **CSF volume effects:**
	* Decreased CSF volumes confirmed in obese patients via MRI
	* Suggests reduced effective dose of spinal local anesthetic
	* Decreased CSF volume from soft tissue displacement into intervertebral foramen by increased abdominal pressure
	* Direct positive correlation between block height and obesity degree with same volume/dose of spinal bupivacaine in sitting position
	* Higher spinal anesthesia levels demonstrated in obese patients
	* Smaller bupivacaine volumes needed to achieve similar sensory levels
	* Large buttocks may elevate caudal vertebral column end, creating Trendelenburg effect and exaggerating cephalad spread
	* To avoid high block with hyperbaric bupivacaine:
	  * Place ramp under chest to elevate cervical/thoracic spine
	  * Normalizes Trendelenburg effect
	* Some reports show no clinical differences in sensory block height in obese vs. non-obese parturients
	* ⚠️ Perform spinal anesthesia with caution due to:
	  * Immediate consequences of extensive blockade
	  * Hazards of intraoperative general anesthesia induction if surgery prolonged [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S8 General Surgical Urological Gynaecological and Endoscopic Procedures|ⓘ]]
* **Epidural anesthesia:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S8 General Surgical Urological Gynaecological and Endoscopic Procedures|ⓘ]]
  * **Advantages over single-injection spinal:**
	* Titratable local anesthetic dosing
	* Ability to prolong block
	* Decreased risk of excessive motor block
	* More controllable hemodynamic changes
	* Availability for postoperative analgesia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S8 General Surgical Urological Gynaecological and Endoscopic Procedures|ⓘ]]
  * **Benefits and limitations:**
	* Incremental epidural local anesthetic dosing reduces hypotension and high block risk
	* Studies show epidural anesthesia fails more often than spinal or CSE techniques in laboring patients
	* Increased maternal BMI contributes to neuraxial technique failure rate [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S8 General Surgical Urological Gynaecological and Endoscopic Procedures|ⓘ]]
  * **Catheter management:**
	* Dislodgment potential problem in obese patients
	* Before securing catheter, patient should move from upright sitting to lateral position
	* In obese patients, catheter depth changes significantly with position changes
	  * Average maximum changes of 0.67-1.04 cm (BMI <25 and >30, respectively)
	  * Changes in epidural catheter depth significantly increased with BMI
	  * Maximum observed changes in distance to skin significantly increased with BMI (>4 cm in one obese patient)
	* Ligamentum flavum has mild grip on epidural catheter
	* Repositioning allows catheter to be pulled into subcutaneous fat, sometimes several centimeters
	* After repositioning, tape catheter without adjustment to reduce dislodgement and block failure [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S8 General Surgical Urological Gynaecological and Endoscopic Procedures|ⓘ]]
  * **Complications management:**
	* For inadvertent dural puncture:
	  * May thread catheter into subarachnoid space for continuous spinal analgesia
	  * Offers reliability and density consistent with single-injection spinal
	  * Converting to continuous spinal doesn't affect subsequent PDPH rate or epidural blood patch need
	  * Each case must be handled individually
	  * ⚠️ Intrathecal catheters must be clearly marked to prevent mistaking for epidural catheter
	  * Accidental epidural-dose administration through spinal catheter can cause immediate, disastrous high spinal:
		* Respiratory compromise
		* Loss of airway patency
		* Unconsciousness
	  * Higher spinal anesthesia levels may result from reduced CSF volume in obese patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S8 General Surgical Urological Gynaecological and Endoscopic Procedures|ⓘ]]
* **Combined spinal-epidural (CSE):** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S8 General Surgical Urological Gynaecological and Endoscopic Procedures|ⓘ]]
  * **Benefits and concerns:**
	* Alternative to conventional spinal or epidural anesthesia alone
	* Concerns include:
	  * Technically more complicated
	  * "Unproven" epidural catheter during spinal analgesia duration
	  * Risk of delayed conversion to general anesthesia if epidural nonfunctional
	* Large retrospective study (2,395 labor analgesia procedures) showed CSE technique didn't delay recognition of epidural catheter failures
	* Even without "spinal dose" during CSE placement, CSF return in spinal needle confirms correct midline placement
	* This increases likelihood of satisfactory epidural catheter placement:
	  * Bilateral block
	  * Improved sacral spread
	  * Faster anesthesia/analgesia onset [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S8 General Surgical Urological Gynaecological and Endoscopic Procedures|ⓘ]]

## 7.4. PERIPHERAL NERVE BLOCKS

* **Applications and challenges:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S8 General Surgical Urological Gynaecological and Endoscopic Procedures|ⓘ]]
  * Increasing use with/without general anesthesia for surgical procedures
  * Case reports describe oblique subcostal TAP catheters as epidural analgesia alternative after upper abdominal surgery [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S8 General Surgical Urological Gynaecological and Endoscopic Procedures|ⓘ]]
  * **Dosing challenges:**
	* Too large a dose risks:
	  * Hypotension
	  * Systemic toxicity
	  * Respiratory compromise (diaphragmatic hemiparesis)
	* Too small a dose risks block failure
	* ⚠️ Maximum safe local anesthetic dose should be based on IBW rather than actual weight due to LAST risk [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S8 General Surgical Urological Gynaecological and Endoscopic Procedures|ⓘ]]
* **Technical considerations:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S8 General Surgical Urological Gynaecological and Endoscopic Procedures|ⓘ]]
  * Blocks technically challenging in obese patients
  * Placement complications:
	* Difficult positioning
	* Obscure anatomic landmarks
	* Inadequate needle length
  * Increased failure rate compared to normal-weight patients
  * Early studies found block failure risk increased with BMI (using nerve stimulator technique)
  * Later retrospective review with ultrasound-guided continuous peripheral nerve blocks found no differences in:
	* Catheter insertion time
	* Success rates between obese and normal-weight patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S8 General Surgical Urological Gynaecological and Endoscopic Procedures|ⓘ]]
  * **Ultrasound guidance benefits:**
	* Helpful tool to increase block success and safety
	* Decreases procedure time
	* Decreases minimum effective local anesthetic dose
	* Greater penetration depth needed for visualizing target structures
	* Low-frequency transducers allow greater tissue penetration
	* High-frequency transducers produce best images
	* Image quality may be inferior to non-obese patients
	* Successful use requires training and experience
	* Several societies recommend education/training guidelines [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S14 Ambulatory Surgery|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Regional anesthesia offers significant advantages for obese patients, including minimal airway manipulation, reduced cardiopulmonary depression, better pain control, and improved outcomes. Successful neuraxial anesthesia requires careful positioning (sitting preferred), potentially longer needles, and awareness of altered CSF dynamics that may cause higher block levels. Epidural catheter migration is common with position changes. Peripheral nerve blocks are challenging but ultrasound guidance improves success rates. Monitored anesthesia care requires vigilant respiratory monitoring due to increased risk of sedation-induced respiratory depression.

# 8. POSTOPERATIVE CONSIDERATIONS

## 8.1. VENTILATORY EVALUATION AND MANAGEMENT

* **Respiratory challenges:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Postoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Increased incidence of atelectasis post-general anesthesia
  * Persists into postoperative period [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Postoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * **Interventions and management:**
	* CPAP or BiPAP initiation advocated
	* NIPPV doesn't seem to increase major anastomotic leakage risk after gastric bypass despite theoretical gastric insufflation risk
	* Useful adjuncts to avoid postoperative hypoventilation/atelectasis:
	  * Adequate analgesia
	  * Properly fitted elastic binder for abdominal support
	  * Early ambulation
	  * Deep breathing exercises
	  * Incentive spirometry [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Postoperative Management|ⓘ]]
	* Monitor pulse oximetry and arterial blood gases when indicated
	* Postoperative CPAP may improve oxygenation but doesn't facilitate CO₂ elimination [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Postoperative Management|ⓘ]]

## 8.2. POSTOPERATIVE ANALGESIA

* **Pain management approach:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Postoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Critical part of postoperative care plan
  * Goals:
	* Provide adequate analgesia
	* Ensure early mobilization
	* Ensure adequate respiratory function [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Postoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * **Importance of mobilization:**
	* Often difficult but critically important for complication prevention
	* Prevents:
	  * Pressure ulcerations
	  * Pulmonary emboli
	  * Deep venous thrombosis
	  * Pneumonia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Postoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * **Comprehensive plan components:**
	1. Multimodal analgesics
	2. Regional anesthesia/analgesia techniques
	3. Early mobilization
	4. Supplemental oxygen
	5. Elevation of head of bed [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Postoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Adequate postoperative monitoring required for safety alongside analgesic technique [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Postoperative Management|ⓘ]]
* **Special considerations:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Postoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * **Obstructive sleep apnea impacts:**
	* Increased likelihood of postoperative pulmonary complications
	* All complications more common in known OSA patients
	* Opioids increase central apnea risk
	* OSA patients at particular risk for opioid-induced apnea [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Postoperative Management|ⓘ]]
	* ⚠️ Due to hypoventilation/hypoxemia risk, postoperative pain management should include opioid-sparing multimodal techniques
	* ASA practice guidelines encourage regional techniques instead of systemic opioids (Table 45-4) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Postoperative Management|ⓘ]]

Table 45-4: Summary of Practice Guidelines for Perioperative Management of OSA Patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Postoperative Management|ⓘ]]

* Regional anesthetic techniques should be considered to reduce/eliminate systemic opioid requirements
* For neuraxial anesthesia, benefits/risks of opioid or opioid-local anesthetic mixture vs. local anesthetic alone must be considered
* If patient-controlled systemic opioids used, continuous background infusions should be avoided or used with extreme caution
* NSAIDs and other modalities should be considered to reduce opioid requirements
* Supplemental oxygen should be administered continuously to all increased-risk patients until able to maintain baseline saturation on room air
* Hospitalized patients at increased respiratory compromise risk should have continuous pulse oximetry monitoring after recovery room discharge [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Postoperative Management|ⓘ]]

## 8.3. MONITORING

* **Monitoring requirements:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Postoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Obese patients receiving neuraxial or parenteral opioids require careful postoperative monitoring
  * Routine ICU/high acuity unit admission unnecessary in most cases
	* Not shown to reduce pulmonary complications risk or change perioperative outcome [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Postoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * Patients with sleep apnea using noninvasive positive airway pressure should bring their equipment
	* Reduces respiratory depression risk [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Postoperative Management|ⓘ]]
  * All neuraxial opioid recipients should be monitored for ventilation adequacy using:
	* Respiratory rate
	* Depth of respiration (assessed without disturbing sleeping patient)
	* Oxygenation (e.g., pulse oximetry when appropriate)
	* Level of consciousness [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Postoperative Management|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Postoperative management of obese patients focuses on respiratory support, multimodal analgesia, and careful monitoring. Opioid-sparing techniques (regional anesthesia, NSAIDs) are preferred due to increased respiratory depression risk, especially in OSA patients. Respiratory adjuncts (CPAP, incentive spirometry, early mobilization) help prevent atelectasis. Continuous pulse oximetry monitoring is recommended for patients at high risk of respiratory complications, particularly those receiving opioids.

# 9. SPECIAL SITUATIONS

## 9.1. AMBULATORY ANESTHESIA

* **Patient selection considerations:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S14 Ambulatory Surgery|ⓘ]]
  * Outpatient surgery possible under certain circumstances
  * Identifying suitable candidates depends on:
	* Early identification of comorbidities
	* Procedure invasiveness
	* Planned anesthetic technique (local/regional with minimal sedation preferred)
	* Postoperative pain management plan
	* Surgeon and anesthesiologist skill [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S14 Ambulatory Surgery|ⓘ]]
* **Risk factors and outcomes:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S14 Ambulatory Surgery|ⓘ]]
  * American College of Surgeons NSQIP database identified perioperative risk factors within 72 hours post-ambulatory surgery:
	* Morbidity/mortality incidence only 0.1% in nearly 250,000 cases
	* Independent risk factors for perioperative morbidity:
	  * High BMI
	  * COPD
	  * Hypertension
	  * History of TIA/stroke
	  * Previous cardiac surgery
	  * Longer surgical times
	* Most common complications:
	  * Unplanned postoperative intubation
	  * Pneumonia
	  * Wound disruption [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S14 Ambulatory Surgery|ⓘ]]
  * Other studies found increased unplanned hospital admission risk with:
	* Increased BMI
	* ASA ≥3
	* Age >80 years
	* Surgery length >1 hour
  * Systematic review found BMI alone didn't increase perioperative complications or unexpected admission risk
	* Authors caution that super-obese patients (BMI ≥50 kg/m²) generally not ambulatory surgery candidates [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S14 Ambulatory Surgery|ⓘ]]
* **OSA considerations:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S14 Ambulatory Surgery|ⓘ]]
  * Morbidly obese patients with sleep-disordered breathing may be considered for ambulatory procedures if:
	* Comorbid conditions optimized
	* Postoperative pain control achievable with non-opioid techniques
  * Ambulatory surgical patients with OSA propensity demonstrate:
	* Increased laryngoscopy attempts
	* Difficult laryngoscopic grade views
	* Increased fiber-optic intubation use
	* Respiratory insufficiency soon after extubation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S14 Ambulatory Surgery|ⓘ]]
  * Large database examinations show increased risk for:
	* Emergency intubation
	* Respiratory failure
	* Mechanical ventilation
	* Aspiration pneumonia
	* Atrial fibrillation
	* ARDS
  * These patients more likely to suffer respiratory arrest with preoperative sedation or postoperative analgesia
	* Particularly sensitive to respiratory depressant effects of even small sedative/analgesic doses
  * OSA patients twice as likely to have respiratory complications vs. controls
  * Those with known preoperative OSA diagnosis and established CPAP usage less likely to experience cardiovascular complications vs. postoperatively diagnosed patients
  * OSA patients using CPAP at home should bring it on surgery day
	* May be needed immediately postoperatively
	* Should be used for several days afterward
  * Some recommend postoperative observation unit with close oxygen saturation monitoring or ICU for OSA patients
  * Discuss possibility of invasive monitoring, prolonged intubation, and postoperative mechanical ventilation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S14 Ambulatory Surgery|ⓘ]]

Table 45-5: Factors to Consider When Scheduling Obese Patients for Ambulatory Surgery [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S14 Ambulatory Surgery|ⓘ]]

**Appropriate for ambulatory surgery:**
* **Patient Factors:**
  * Usually BMI < 50 kg/m²
  * Good functional capacity
  * Comorbid conditions optimized (e.g., OSA treated with CPAP/NIV)
  * VTE prophylaxis
* **Anesthetic Factors:**
  * Adequate preoperative preparation
  * Regional anesthesia possible
  * Pain control easily achieved with non-opioid techniques
* **Surgical Factors:**
  * Non-invasive surgery
  * Adequate time scheduled
  * Adequate equipment
  * 23-hour observation PACU available
  * Experienced anesthesia team

**Inappropriate for ambulatory surgery:**
* **Patient Factors:**
  * Poor functional capacity
  * Metabolic syndrome
  * Inadequately treated HTN, cardiovascular disease
  * Symptomatic/untreated respiratory disease (untreated OSA, low O₂ saturation)
  * Previous VTE
* **Anesthetic Factors:**
  * High likelihood for admission due to inadequate pain control
* **Surgical Factors:**
  * Prolonged procedure

## 9.2. CRITICAL CARE AND RESUSCITATION

* **Critical care challenges:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Caring for critically ill obese patients poses same challenges as during surgery
	* Equipment
	* Monitoring
	* Multiple comorbidities [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Nutritional considerations:**
	* Nutritional status often paradoxical and difficult to address
	* Hyperglycemia from pre-existing diabetes or stress-induced state common
	* Should be corrected (associated with higher mortality) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Ventilatory management:**
	* Obese patients more likely to become ventilator dependent
	* BMI associated with increased ALI and ARDS risk
	* Effect on clinical outcomes still being explored
	* Relative contributions of obesity itself versus comorbidities (e.g., diabetes) unclear
	* Optimal ventilatory management increasingly using:
	  * Personalized respiratory system mechanics
	  * Esophageal manometry
	  * Novel imaging techniques (electrical impedance tomography) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Cardiopulmonary resuscitation:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C4 Trauma Resuscitation and Crisis Management|ⓘ]]
  * Should be anticipated when caring for morbidly obese critically ill patients
  * **Equipment and technical concerns:**
	* Chest compressions may not be effective
	* Mechanical compression devices may be required
	* Maximum 400 J energy on regular defibrillators sufficient (chest wall usually not much thicker)
	* Higher transthoracic impedance from fat may require several attempts
	* Airway management by conventional means may be very difficult
	* Direct visualization of anatomic landmarks extremely challenging in "can't intubate, can't ventilate" situation requiring emergency cricothyroidotomy
	* Time-consuming and technically difficult procedures should be final options performed by experienced practitioners:
	  * Tracheostomy
	  * Percutaneous cricothyrotomy
	  * Transtracheal jet ventilation
	  * Retrograde wire intubation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C4 Trauma Resuscitation and Crisis Management|ⓘ]]

## 9.3. MORBIDITY AND MORTALITY

* **Perioperative risk factors:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Cardiovascular system|ⓘ]]
  * Pre-existing disease impacts perioperative adverse event risk
  * Obesity and associated comorbidities increase risk for morbidity and mortality
  * Higher postoperative complication risk than non-obese patients makes outpatient surgery controversial
	* Should be individualized [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Cardiovascular system|ⓘ]]
  * NSQIP database analysis (2012-2016):
	* Over 1.3 million non-bariatric cases
	* Increasing BMI associated with increased risk of:
	  * Perioperative morbidity
	  * Infectious complications
	  * Critical care complications
	* Common postoperative adverse events:
	  * Respiratory complications (atelectasis, pneumonia)
	  * Vascular complications (thrombophlebitis, DVT)
	  * Wound complications (infection, dehiscence)
	* Higher risk of perioperative pulmonary complications:
	  * Unplanned intubation
	  * Ventilation >48 hours postoperatively
	  * Especially with pre-existing respiratory conditions (e.g., OSA) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Respiratory System|ⓘ]]
* **Preventive strategies:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Respiratory System|ⓘ]]
  * Sequential compression devices (SCDs)
  * Routine early ambulation
  * Careful perioperative anticoagulation decreases thromboembolism risk
  * Known infection risk factors:
	* Hyperglycemia/diabetes
	* Longer surgical procedures
	* Hypoperfusion/low tissue oxygen tension
	* Low tissue antibiotic concentration
  * Rhabdomyolysis more common in morbidly obese patients undergoing laparoscopic vs. equivalent open procedures
	* Especially with prolonged procedures
  * Patients with metabolic syndrome have significantly greater perioperative morbidity/mortality risk than obese patients without metabolic syndrome
  * NSQIP study reported relative odds of procedure-adjusted mortality:
	* 2.31 in super obesity (BMI 50.0-59.9 kg/m²)
	* 2.63 in super-super obesity (BMI ≥60.0 kg/m²) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Respiratory System|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Obese patients require careful selection for ambulatory surgery, with BMI <50 kg/m², optimized comorbidities, and good functional capacity being favorable factors. Morbidly obese patients with OSA need extra caution, with continuous monitoring and possibly extended stay facilities. In critical care settings, ventilatory management is challenging, with obesity increasing ventilator dependence risk. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation faces technical difficulties with chest compressions and airway management. Perioperative morbidity and mortality increase with BMI, particularly respiratory complications, thromboembolic events, and infectious complications.

# 10. DRUG DOSING IN OBESITY

## 10.1. BODY WEIGHT CONCEPTS FOR DOSING

* **Different weight measures:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]
  * **Total Body Weight (TBW):** Actual measured weight
  * **Ideal Body Weight (IBW):** Height in cm - 100 (men) or - 105 (women)
  * **Lean Body Weight (LBW):** TBW minus adipose tissue (~80% TBW in males, ~75% in females) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]
* **General dosing principles:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]
  * **Loading doses:**
	* Drugs distributed mainly to lean tissues: based on LBW
	* Drugs equally distributed between adipose and lean tissues: based on TBW [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]
  * **Maintenance doses:**
	* Drugs with similar clearance in obese and non-obese: based on LBW
	* Drugs with clearance increased with obesity: based on TBW [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Endocrine|ⓘ]]

## 10.2. SPECIFIC DRUG DOSING GUIDELINES

* **Induction agents:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Thiopental:** LBW (somewhat increased)
  * **Propofol:**
	* Induction: LBW (somewhat increased)
	* Maintenance: TBW [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Neuromuscular blocking agents:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Succinylcholine:** TBW (max 200mg)
  * **Rocuronium:** LBW
  * **Vecuronium:** LBW
  * **Atracurium:** LBW
  * **Cisatracurium:** LBW [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Opioids:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Fentanyl:** LBW
  * **Sufentanil:** LBW
  * **Remifentanil:** LBW [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Other agents:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Dexmedetomidine:** TBW
  * **Neostigmine:** TBW (max 5mg)
  * **Sugammadex:** Actual body weight (TBW) for both obese and non-obese patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Additional medications:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Atropine:** LBW
  * **Gentamicin:** IBW
  * **Noradrenaline:** IBW
  * **Adrenaline:** IBW [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

![Bariatric Drug Dosing](BARIATRIC_BARIATRIC_Bariatric_Drug_Dosing.001.png_a8ddaf.png_612250.png)

**Bottom Line:** Drug dosing in obese patients requires understanding pharmacokinetic alterations and choosing the appropriate weight descriptor (TBW, LBW, or IBW). Most anesthetic agents are dosed based on LBW, while some specific drugs (succinylcholine, neostigmine, dexmedetomidine) use TBW. Highly lipophilic drugs generally show increased volume of distribution and longer elimination half-lives. Careful individualization of dosing based on clinical response is essential to avoid over or underdosing.
