# 1. ELECTROLYTE REGULATION AND PHYSIOLOGY

## 1.1. WATER DISTRIBUTION AND FLUID COMPARTMENTS

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Body Fluids and Electrolytes|Part A: Body Water Compartments]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B: Perioperative Fluid Management]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C: Fluid & Electrolyte Integration]]

### 1.1.1. BODY WATER DISTRIBUTION

* **Water is the most abundant component of the human body** - on average, 60% of the body is composed of water (this value varies with sex, body habitus, and age)* Body water is distributed between two major compartments: intracellular and extracellular* For the average 70-kg man:
  * **Total body water** is 42 L (60% of 70 kg, where 1 kg of water has a volume of 1 L)
  * **Intracellular fluid (ICF)** comprises approximately two-thirds of body water (28 L)
  * **Extracellular fluid (ECF)** comprises approximately one-third of body water (14 L), which includes:
	* **Intravascular fluid** (plasma) - approximately a fifth of ECF (3 L)
	* **Transcellular fluid** - smaller proportion (around 1 L), including cerebrospinal fluid, ocular fluid, synovial fluid
	* **Interstitial fluid** - the remainder (around 10 L), which occupies spaces between cells and is where capillaries and cells exchange nutrients and waste products

### 1.1.2. MEASUREMENT OF BODY FLUID COMPARTMENTS

* Body fluid compartments are typically measured using an **indicator-dilution method**:
  * A known quantity of indicator substance is administered
  * Allowed to equilibrate across the body compartment of interest
  * Its concentration measured
  * Volume calculated based on dilution
* The key is understanding barriers between compartments and selecting the correct indicator with appropriate permeability properties:
  * **Plasma volume** measurement requires an indicator that cannot cross the capillary endothelium, such as radio-labeled albumin  * **ECF volume** measurement requires an indicator that can cross capillary endothelium but cannot enter ICF, such as thiosulfate  * **Total body water** measurement requires an indicator that can distribute across all compartments by crossing both capillary endothelium and cell membranes, such as deuterated water (²H₂O)* 💡 The volumes of ICF and interstitial fluid compartments cannot be directly measured but are calculated as differences between the other measured volumes
### 1.1.3. PLASMA VOLUME REGULATION

* Maintenance of intravascular volume is crucial for hemodynamic stability:
  * **Too low blood volume** results in reduced venous return, cardiac preload, cardiac output, and systemic hypotension, leading to organ ischemia  * **Too high blood volume** can stretch cardiac myocytes precipitating left ventricular failure and induce hypertension, damaging organs such as kidneys, heart, and retina* The body regulates ECF volume primarily through sodium management:
  * There are no water pumps in the body  * Na⁺ is the major cation of ECF; together with its conjugate anion, Na⁺ accounts for over 90% of the body's osmotic activity  * **ECF volume is regulated by controlling Na⁺ movement and thus water*** Extracellular Na⁺ concentration is a balance of:
  * Na⁺ intake (dietary or intravenous)
  * Extra-renal Na⁺ loss (sweating, feces)
  * Renal Na⁺ excretion
* The kidney is primarily responsible for plasma Na⁺ regulation:
  * Net renal Na⁺ loss = ECF volume reduction
  * Net renal Na⁺ gain = ECF volume expansion

### 1.1.4. OSMOLARITY AND OSMOLALITY

* **Osmolarity** is the number of osmoles per liter of solution, where osmoles denotes the number of moles of particles able to exert osmotic pressure* **Osmolality** is the number of osmoles per kilogram of solvent* **Difference from molarity** - 1 mol of NaCl yields 2 Osmol of osmotically active particles (Na⁺ and Cl⁻), while 1 mol of glucose yields only 1 Osmol* Plasma osmolarity can be estimated at the bedside by summing concentrations of common osmolytes:
  * **Plasma osmolarity = 2[Na⁺] + 2[K⁺] + [glucose] + [urea]** (mmol/L)
  * Na⁺ and K⁺ concentrations are doubled to account for conjugate anions
* Osmolality is preferred in rigorous discourse because it is independent of temperature and the weight of solute* Osmolality is measured in the laboratory by a method based on depression of the freezing point of the solution* **Osmolar gap** is the difference between measured osmolality and calculated osmolarity:
  * **Osmolar gap = osmolality – osmolarity**
  * High osmolar gap indicates presence of additional unmeasured osmotically active particles (e.g., alcohol intoxication, hypertriglyceridemia, methanol or ethylene glycol poisoning)
### 1.1.5. IMPORTANCE OF OSMOLARITY REGULATION

* Osmolarity must be tightly regulated as it affects **fluid tonicity** - the response of intracellular water to surrounding ECF osmolarity:
  * **Isotonic ECF**: Cells maintain normal size
  * **Hypertonic ECF**: Cells shrink due to osmotic water movement out of cells
  * **Hypotonic ECF**: Cells swell due to osmotic water movement into cells
* **Clinical implications of hypotonicity**:
  * Infusion of sterile water into a peripheral vein can cause red blood cell swelling and hemolysis
  * 💡 5% dextrose is used instead of sterile water as it's initially isotonic (278 mOsmol/L), but once glucose is metabolized, it effectively delivers free water without acute osmolarity changes
  * ⚠️ Severe hyponatremia with reduced plasma osmolarity can cause cerebral edema, raised intracranial pressure, seizures, altered consciousness, and brainstem herniation

**Bottom Line:** Water constitutes approximately 60% of body weight, distributed primarily between intracellular (two-thirds) and extracellular (one-third) compartments. Plasma volume is tightly regulated through sodium balance, while plasma osmolarity requires precise control to prevent cellular swelling or shrinking. Understanding these compartments and their regulation is fundamental for fluid management in clinical practice.

## 1.2. SODIUM REGULATION

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Body Fluids and Electrolytes|Part A: Sodium Homeostasis]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B: Electrolyte Management]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C: Electrolyte Integration]]

### 1.2.1. RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN SODIUM, OSMOLALITY, OSMOLARITY, AND TONICITY

* **Sodium** is the most abundant cation in the extracellular fluid (ECF) and the primary determinant of plasma osmolality  * Concentration maintained by Na-K ATPase pump (3:2 ratio at the cost of a single ATP) against concentration gradient* **Osmolality**: Concentration of osmoles dissolved per kilogram of plasma water  * Measurable via osmometer* **Osmolarity**: Concentration of osmoles per liter of plasma water* "Osmolality" is preferred in rigorous discourse* **Tonicity**: Concentration of **effective** osmoles that do not freely cross cell membranes  * With the exception of specialized epithelia in the distal nephron, intracellular and extracellular osmolality must be equal since cell membranes are freely permeable to water due to constitutive presence of aquaporins  * Accumulation of effective osmoles in either compartment provides a driving force for water movement into the compartment of greater tonicity to equalize concentration	* Hypotonic plasma: Water moves intracellularly → cellular swelling	* Hypertonic plasma: Cellular shrinkage  * Na and glucose are obligate extracellular solutes → effective osmoles → impact both osmolality and tonicity  * Urea: Increases osmolality without affecting tonicity (ineffective osmole)  * Plasma tonicity cannot be measured directly but is calculated by subtracting the contribution of urea from the measured plasma osmolality* While hypernatremia always indicates hyperosmolal and hypertonic plasma, hyponatremia can occur with low, normal, or elevated tonicity or osmolality  * Elevation of serum urea nitrogen (SUN) will increase serum osmolality without affecting tonicity  * Depending on the degree of azotemia, when concomitant water retention reduces tonicity, hypotonic hyponatremia with normal or even elevated serum osmolality can be observed
![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled.png_dac9e7.png_8f0a21.png)

**Bottom Line:** Sodium is the primary determinant of plasma osmolality and tonicity, with critical implications for fluid movement between compartments. While hypernatremia always indicates hypertonicity, hyponatremia can occur with various tonicity states, requiring careful evaluation beyond just the sodium level.

### 1.2.2. PITUITARY REGULATION OF TONICITY

* The pre-prohormone of ADH is synthesized in the hypothalamus and cleaved into ADH, neurophysin II, and copeptin. ADH is then stored in the posterior pituitary gland* **Plasma osmolarity is controlled by a feedback loop**:
  * **Sensors**: Osmoreceptors located in the organum vasculosum of the lamina terminalis and subfornical organ within the hypothalamus  * **Control center**: Hypothalamus, which interprets signals from osmoreceptors	* Osmoreceptors are extremely sensitive, detecting as little as 1% change in plasma osmolarity	* Normal set point of plasma osmolarity is 285-295 mOsmol/L  * **Effectors**: Hypothalamus responds to risen osmolarity by:
	* Stimulating thirst to increase oral water intake	* Reducing water excretion via antidiuretic hormone (ADH) release* **Hypertonic** extracellular environment → shrinkage of hypothalamic osmoreceptors → opens stretch-inactivated cation channels → membrane depolarization, intracellular signaling, and release of vasopressin (ADH) from posterior pituitary gland into systemic circulation  * ADH acts on collecting duct, increasing water reabsorption and decreasing serum tonicity* **Modest** decrease in perfusion → carotid baroreceptors → stimulation of pituitary vasopressin release, potentially at the expense of lowering serum tonicity* If plasma osmolarity falls, the hypothalamus reduces thirst sensation and inhibits ADH secretion, increasing water excretion by the kidney
**Bottom Line:** The pituitary gland regulates tonicity primarily through ADH release in response to hypothalamic osmoreceptor activation, with modulation by perfusion pressure via baroreceptors, allowing priority maintenance of circulation volume even at the expense of tonicity regulation.

### 1.2.3. RENAL REGULATION OF TONICITY

* ADH binds to vasopressin V2 receptors on the basolateral surface of principal cells in the collecting duct of the distal nephron* No ADH → collecting duct impermeable to water → dilute urine* ADH present → translocation of aquaporin 2-containing vesicles to apical membrane of principal cells → water flows into cell → exits into hypertonic medullary interstitium through constitutively active basolateral aquaporin channels → concentrated urine, lower serum osmolality  * If serum osmolality <280 mOsm/kg - expect urine osmolality of approximately 0 mOsm/kg
![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_1.png_4feb5e.png_3f6c85.png)

* In hypovolemic or hypotensive states, the relationship between plasma osmolality and vasopressin changes - at the same serum osmolality of 280 mOsm/kg, the body still produces ADH → hyponatremia in dehydration with low urine Na (<30 mmol/L)
![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_2.png_a01241.png_4c3b6c.png)

* Urine osmolality (UOsm) is a surrogate measure of vasopressin activity; high and low UOsm equate to high and low vasopressin levels, respectively* In healthy individuals: low plasma tonicity → low ADH; high plasma tonicity → ADH rises linearly → urine osmolality range from 50-1200 mOsm/kg H2O  * Concentration range narrowed in chronic kidney disease (CKD); more modest diluting effect
**High Medullary Osmolarity Generation**:
* The loop of Henle (LOH) generates a medullary interstitial osmolar gradient through a countercurrent multiplier mechanism:
  * In the proximal convoluted tubule (PCT), water is reabsorbed with ions, resulting in filtrate entering the LOH with osmolarity similar to plasma (300 mOsmol/L)  * The thick ascending limb of LOH moves Na⁺, K⁺, and Cl⁻ from filtrate to medullary interstitium via Na⁺/K⁺/2Cl⁻ co-transporters  * This makes the filtrate entering the distal convoluted tubule (DCT) hypo-osmolar and the renal medullary interstitium hyper-osmolar  * As filtrate enters the descending limb, water moves from filtrate to hyper-osmolar interstitium, increasing filtrate concentration  * In the thin ascending limb, ions move out of the tubule, further concentrating the interstitium  * This countercurrent system creates a gradient with the highest osmolarity (1200 mOsmol/L) at the tip of the LOH* The vasa recta (specialized blood vessels) have a hairpin arrangement that prevents washing away the osmotic gradient:
  * As they descend, electrolytes diffuse in and water diffuses out  * As they ascend, water diffuses in and solutes diffuse out  * Blood flows slowly enough for near-complete equilibration, preserving the osmotic gradient
**Sodium Handling and Diuretic Effects**:
* The kidney regulates Na⁺ excretion through:
  * Changes in glomerular filtration rate (GFR) - when plasma volume is high, GFR increases, delivering more Na⁺ to the nephron for excretion  * Changes in Na⁺ reabsorption - bulk reabsorption occurs in the PCT (60%) and LOH (30%), with fine-tuning in the DCT and collecting duct controlled by aldosterone* **Diuretic effects on sodium handling**:
  * Thiazide diuretics - block reabsorption of NaCl in distal nephron diluting site → impair urinary dilution - ~90% of cases of diuretic-associated hyponatremia are due to thiazides
  * Loop diuretics - inhibit Na reabsorption in loop of Henle → reduced renal medullary hypertonicity → reduce driving force for water reabsorption in the collecting duct	* As a result, loop diuretics—in contrast to thiazides—tend to hinder renal concentrating ability and can contribute to the development of hypernatremia

**Osmoregulation vs. Volume Regulation**:
* For absolute control, osmolarity and volume should be regulated independently, but regulatory mechanisms overlap* Osmoreceptors are sensitive to changes in osmolarity as small as 2-3%, whereas baroreceptors require 7-10% change in blood volume to trigger a response* Osmolarity is prioritized over volume initially - hypertonic saline infusion triggers ADH release despite volume expansion* With larger volume losses (>7%), volume regulation takes priority due to potential tissue ischemia
**Bottom Line:** Renal regulation of tonicity centers around ADH action on aquaporin channels in the collecting duct, with volume status significantly influencing this relationship. The medullary concentration gradient, established by the countercurrent multiplier system in the loop of Henle and preserved by the vasa recta, enables water reabsorption. Diuretics differentially affect water handling, with thiazides promoting hyponatremia while loop diuretics potentially contribute to hypernatremia.

## 1.3. CALCIUM PHYSIOLOGY

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Body Fluids and Electrolytes|Part A: Calcium Homeostasis]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B: Perioperative Calcium Management]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C: Electrolyte Clinical Integration]]

### 1.3.1. FUNCTION AND DISTRIBUTION

* Calcium (Ca²⁺) is a divalent cation that is predominantly (99%) stored in bone in the form of hydroxyapatite crystals, with the remainder found in the extracellular fluid (ECF)* Within serum, calcium is near evenly divided (45% to 55%) between:
  * Protein-bound calcium, largely to albumin  * Active ionized form (Ca²⁺)* A small portion of free calcium exists in serum as salts such as calcium phosphate or calcium citrate* Standard total calcium concentrations: 8.5 - 10.5 mg/dL, but clinical assessment is best done by measuring free ionized form which ranges from 1-1.25 mmol/L* 💡 Historically formulas have been used to "correct" total calcium concentrations for serum albumin levels, but these have proven unreliable clinically, especially in critically ill patients
**Key physiological functions:**
* Principal signaling cation for muscle contraction, synaptic transmission, cellular proliferation, ATP metabolism, cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) secondary messaging* Acts as a hormone and ligand for calcium-sensing receptors* **Cardiovascular functions:**
  * Myocyte contractility - calcium-induced calcium release at sarcoplasmic reticulum, driven by L-type Ca²⁺ channels  * Pacemaking in myocardial cells via T-type Ca²⁺ channels	* Class IV antiarrhythmics (e.g., diltiazem and verapamil) block Ca²⁺ action potential at SA and AV nodes → slow heart rate	* Hypocalcemia is associated with prolonged QTc and cardiac arrhythmias* **Hematologic functions:**
  * Triggers platelet adhesion - influx of intracellular platelet Ca²⁺ triggered by thromboxane-A2 release by nearby activated platelets → conformational changes → further activation of local platelets → platelet adhesion to damaged endothelium → further amplification  * Cofactor for activation of coagulation factor XII in intrinsic pathway; factors VII, IX, and X in extrinsic pathway; and factor II (thrombin - which activates fibrinogen to fibrin)  * Facilitates protein C and S activation
**Bottom Line:** Calcium exists primarily in bone with only 1% in circulation, divided between protein-bound and ionized forms. It serves critical functions in multiple physiological systems including muscle contraction, nerve transmission, cardiac function, and coagulation, explaining why calcium derangements can have wide-ranging clinical effects.

### 1.3.2. HOMEOSTASIS

* Three major hormones regulate calcium homeostasis:
  * **Parathyroid hormone (PTH)** - increases Ca²⁺ by:
	* Increasing skeletal bone resorption and calcium release	* Enhancing renal calcium reabsorption	* Stimulating renal 1-alpha-hydroxylase to convert 25-OH-vitamin D to active 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D  * **Vitamin D (1,25-dihydroxycholecalciferol)** - increases intestinal calcium absorption  * **Calcitonin** - promotes renal excretion of Ca²⁺ and inhibits osteoclastic bone resorption
![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_image_2.png_de9cd1.png_adcb8e.png)

**Bottom Line:** Calcium homeostasis is maintained by the coordinated actions of PTH, vitamin D, and calcitonin, which together regulate bone calcium release, intestinal absorption, and renal handling of calcium to maintain serum calcium within narrow limits.

## 1.4. PHOSPHATE PHYSIOLOGY

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Body Fluids and Electrolytes|Part A: Phosphate Physiology]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B: Perioperative Phosphate Management]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C: Electrolyte Clinical Integration]]

### 1.4.1. PHYSIOLOGIC ROLE

* Phosphate is important in the form of inorganic phosphate (Pi), notably as the anions HPO₄²⁻ and H₂PO₄⁻* **Biochemical and structural roles:**
  * Essential for cellular metabolism via ATP and NADP+ and creatine phosphate  * Constitutes the phosphodiesters that form nucleic acids in DNA and RNA  * Used in the enzymatic process of glycolysis and many intracellular signaling pathways such as cyclic-AMP (cAMP) and phospholipase-C  * Essential in regulating hemoglobin's oxygen-carrying capacity as red blood cell production of 2,3-diphosphoglycerate (2,3-DPG) results in shifts of the oxygen dissociation curve:
	* Increased 2,3-DPG → rightward shift	* Decreased 2,3-DPG → leftward shift
**Distribution in the body:**
* Vast majority (85% to 90%) exists in the form of calcium phosphate crystals (apatite) in bone* 10% to 15% found in soft tissues and organs* Less than 1% present in the extracellular fluid (ECF)* Within serum, phosphate is further divided:
  * 85% in the Pi anion (HPO₄²⁻ or PO₄⁻) form  * Approximately 10% protein-bound  * Remainder 5% in salt complexes* Serum concentrations typically range from 2.5 to 4.5 mg/dL
**Bottom Line:** Phosphate is essential for multiple cellular processes including energy metabolism, nucleic acid structure, and oxygen transport by hemoglobin. While primarily stored in bone, its small but critical presence in circulation impacts numerous physiologic functions.

### 1.4.2. REGULATION

* Pi reabsorption and excretion primarily occurs in the kidneys  * Pi is filtered freely at the glomeruli and reabsorbed in the proximal tubule  * Reabsorption is further regulated by:
	* Fibroblast growth factor-23 (FGF-23) - decreases reabsorption	* Parathyroid hormone (PTH) - decreases reabsorption	* Calcitriol (active vitamin D) - increases reabsorption
**Bottom Line:** Phosphate homeostasis is primarily maintained through renal handling, with the proximal tubule being the main site of regulation. FGF-23, PTH, and calcitriol coordinate the fine-tuning of phosphate reabsorption to maintain appropriate serum levels.

## 1.5. MAGNESIUM PHYSIOLOGY

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Body Fluids and Electrolytes|Part A: Magnesium Physiology]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B: Perioperative Magnesium Management]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C: Electrolyte Clinical Integration]]

### 1.5.1. PHYSIOLOGIC ROLE AND DISTRIBUTION

* Magnesium (Mg²⁺) is a divalent cation and the second most abundant intracellular cation* **Distribution:**
  * Largely stored in mineralized bone (50% to 65%)  * Remainder found in skeletal muscle (~30%)  * Soft tissue and organs (20%)  * Serum [Mg²⁺] is less than 1% of total body magnesium	* Majority in free ionized state (70%)	* Remaining 30% protein-bound, predominantly to albumin  * Normal serum [Mg²⁺] ranges from 1.7 to 2.1 mg/dL (0.75 to 1.10 mmol/L)
**Absorption and excretion:**
* Magnesium is absorbed in the gastrointestinal tract via a complex process involving:
  * PTH  * Calcitonin  * Insulin  * Vasoactive intestinal peptides* The majority of serum [Mg²⁺] (70%) is filtered through the renal glomerulus:
  * Approximately 30% is reabsorbed, mostly in the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle  * Remainder is renally excreted  * 💡 The loop of Henle is therefore where the majority of malabsorption can occur, as seen with chronic loop diuretic use
* Essential cofactor in over 600 enzymatic reactions
**Bottom Line:** Magnesium is predominantly stored in bone and muscle with only a small fraction in serum. Its homeostasis is regulated through intestinal absorption and renal handling, with the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle being the major site of reabsorption and thus a key target for magnesium-wasting medications.

### 1.5.2. FUNCTIONS

* **General role:** Calcium antagonist - competes with Ca²⁺ in ion-gated channels or at binding sites of Ca²⁺ transporters - this competitive antagonism has clinically relevant implications
**Neurological functions:**
* Mg²⁺ downregulates NMDA receptor excitability by occupying Ca²⁺ channels within the NMDA receptor, thus preventing glutamatergic excitatory signaling* Serum Mg²⁺ may decrease anesthetic requirements by competitive antagonism of presynaptic Ca²⁺ channels within the hippocampus, reducing CNS neurotransmitter release* Analgesic properties when administered intravenously, likely due to NMDA receptor antagonism secondary to its ability to compete and inhibit Ca²⁺ influx necessary for activation* 💡 MgSO₄ injected perineurally as an adjunct in regional anesthetic techniques promotes superior analgesia and prolonged duration of both sensory and motor blockade

**Cardiovascular functions:**
* Inhibitory role in L-type Ca²⁺ channels in phase II of the cardiac action potential* First-line agent for torsades de points
* Used in the management of supraventricular, ventricular, and digoxin toxicity-induced arrhythmias
* Useful to treat post-cardiopulmonary bypass atrial fibrillation due to its ability to function as a calcium channel antagonist, slowing L-type Ca²⁺ channel polarization and resulting in slower and more stabilized cardiac conduction

**Vascular functions:**
* Promotes vascular smooth muscle relaxation and vasodilation by:
  * Acting as an endogenous calcium channel blocker  * Increasing nitric oxide (NO) release* Used as a tocolytic agent in preterm labor by inducing systemic smooth muscle relaxation, systemic vasodilation, and uterine tone relaxation
* Maintains hemodynamic stability during pheochromocytoma resection and in pheochromocytoma crisis due to:
  * Vascular smooth muscle relaxation with resultant arteriolar vasodilation  * Direct inhibition of catecholamine release from the adrenal medulla

**Musculoskeletal functions:**
* Muscle contraction dependence on calcium-induced calcium release (CICR) from sarcoplasmic reticulum is impacted by Mg²⁺ concentration

![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_image_7.png_1d5e60.png_26e4ab.png)

**Bottom Line:** Magnesium serves as a physiologic calcium antagonist with multiple effects across organ systems, including NMDA receptor modulation, cardiac conduction regulation, smooth muscle relaxation, and vasodilation. These properties make it therapeutically valuable in various clinical scenarios from arrhythmias to preeclampsia.

**Bottom Line:** The approach to hyponatremia requires systematic evaluation of volume status, osmolality, and urine studies to determine etiology. Special caution is needed when initially hypovolemic patients develop dilute urine after volume repletion, as this signals impending autocorrection with risk of osmotic demyelination.

### 2.1.2. CLINICAL MANIFESTATIONS

**Symptoms vary with:**
* Degree of hyponatremia
* Duration of hyponatremia
* Rate of decline in plasma [Na⁺]
* Plasma volume state

**Clinical presentation:**
* Asymptomatic in most patients with mild hyponatremia
* Neurological manifestations mainly (due to cerebral edema):
  * Lethargy
  * Headache
  * Change in sensorium (e.g., confusion, decreased consciousness)
  * Muscle cramps
  * Seizures
* ⚠️ Severe symptoms such as seizure or diminished level of consciousness require urgent management

**Acute vs. Chronic Hyponatremia:**
* **Acute hyponatremia** (developing within 48 hours):
  * Water moves from hypotonic serum into cerebral neurons causing cerebral edema
  * Presentation includes neurological symptoms (headache, nausea/vomiting, altered mental status, seizures)
  * If emergency (<120 mmol/L) - hypertonic saline (3% NaCl) to increase serum Na by 4-8 mmol/L/hr
* **Chronic hyponatremia** (developing over >48 hours):
  * Cerebral neurons adapt with time by losing organic osmoles  * Presentation typically includes more subtle cognitive and gait changes
  * ⚠️ If corrected too rapidly → water moves via osmosis out of cells forming the blood-brain barrier → loss of integrity → allowing immune cells to enter → demyelination of certain pontine neurons → paralysis, cranial nerve palsies, locked-in syndrome → osmotic demyelination syndrome
  * The pathophysiology of osmotic demyelination syndromes is the destruction of myelin sheaths and oligodendrocytes, possibly through apoptosis as a stress response to rapid changes in intracellular tonicity and decreased cellular volume  * Do not increase Na⁺ >10 mmol/L in 24 hours
  * 💡 In the event of rapid overcorrection, hypertonic saline should be discontinued and replaced with hypotonic solutions. Administration of 2-4 mcg intravenous desmopressin every 6-8 hours can promote water reabsorption in the collecting duct with downstream reduction in serum [Na⁺]

**Risk factors for osmotic demyelination:**
* Serum Na⁺ <105 mmol/L
* Alcoholism
* Malnutrition
* Liver disease
* Hypokalemia

**Bottom Line:** Clinical manifestations of hyponatremia primarily affect the central nervous system and vary with severity, chronicity, and rate of development. Cerebral adaptation to chronic hyponatremia explains why rapid correction is dangerous, as it can lead to osmotic demyelination syndrome, necessitating cautious correction rates and vigilant monitoring.

### 2.1.3. DIAGNOSTIC EVALUATION

**1. Determine if it is true hyponatremia**
* Measure serum osmolality (<275 mOsm/kg indicates hypoosmolar state)
* **Hyperosmolar hyponatremia** (translocational) - osmoles raise serum osmolality and attract water from intracellular compartment, reducing serum Na⁺:
  * **Hyperglycemia** - glucose >3 mmol/L, Na⁺ <1 mmol/L → causes dilutional hyponatremia
	* **Corrected Na⁺ = measured Na⁺ + 0.3 (glucose [mmol/L] - 5.5)**
  * Mannitol (used for raised ICP)
  * Glycine (irrigation fluid in urologic surgery)

  ![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_4.png_6f176a.png_27a856.png)

* **Isoosmolar hyponatremia** (pseudohyponatremia - lab measurement artifact due to reduced plasma water in these conditions) → **use iSTAT (doesn't dilute sample)**:
  * Severe hyperlipidemia - look at lipemic index
  * Hyperproteinemia (e.g., myeloma)

  ![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_5.png_09c64f.png_5dac57.png)

**2. Determine if immediate correction is required**
* Rapid correction of hyponatremia can be done if acute (<48 hours) - no time for cerebral neurons to adapt → reduced risk of osmotic demyelination
* Severe symptoms (coma and seizures) - prompt use of hypertonic saline (aiming for Na⁺ increase of 4-6 mmol/L in 6 hours)

**3. Determine etiology**
* Classify by volume status or biochemically
* **Overt hypovolemia:** Any history of recent vomiting or diarrhea or diuretic use - GI or Renal losses
* **Overt hypervolemia:** Clinically fluid overloaded state can be caused by low effective blood volume from the underlying cause of fluid overload
* **If clinically euvolemic** → assess urine osmolality:
  * **Low urine osmolality (<100 mOsm/kg)** - suppressed ADH secretion:
	* Psychogenic polydipsia - psychiatric disease with excessive free water intake (exceeding renal excretory capacity) → diluted serum osmoles
	* Low solute intake - malnourished alcoholics (beer potomania) or low salt, low protein diet ("tea and toast")
	* Reset osmostat syndrome - Maintenance of sodium balance at a chronically stable Na⁺ level - normal response to fluids by suppressing ADH and excreting diluted urine
	* ⚠️ (Caution) treated hypovolemic hyponatremia with impending autocorrection:
	  * Fluid repletion → corrects hypovolemia, removing stimulus for ADH release → diluted urine
	  * Mimics psychogenic polydipsia but is a **dangerous** scenario
	  * Once ADH action ceases → dilute urine secreted in large amounts → rapid and perilous Na⁺ rise (autocorrection)
	  * Consider this if urine osmolality measured after fluid repletion is low
  * **High urine osmolality (>100 mOsm/kg), low urine Na⁺ (<30 mmol/L)** - presence of ADH effect with active sodium reabsorption via stimulated RAAS (activated with low effective arterial blood flow):
	* **Reduced ECF** - hypovolemic (depletional) hyponatremia:
	  * GI losses - diarrhea, vomiting, stoma losses
	  * Renal losses - Remote diuretic use (prescribed or covert)
	* **Increased ECF (fluid overload state)** - hypervolemic:
	  * Cardiac failure, renal failure, cirrhosis, or nephrotic syndrome - low effective arterial blood volume due to movement of fluids from intravascular spaces into third spaces → pedal edema, pulmonary congestion, elevated JVP
  * **High urine osmolality (>100 mOsm/kg), High urine Na⁺ (>30 mmol/L)** - indicates renal sodium loss, ADH effect without RAAS activation:
	* Consider:
	  * Active diuretic use - inhibits tubular sodium absorption (Thiazides in particular) → Do serum creatinine assessment
	  * Renal disease - impairment of free water excretion in advanced kidney disease (eGFR <20 ml/min), recovery from AKI, renal tubular acidosis, other tubulopathies
	  * Adrenal insufficiency - causes hyponatremia via hypovolemia or via removal of ADH inhibition by cortisol → Do 8am cortisol, short synacthen test
	  * Severe hypothyroidism - causes hyponatremia (mechanism unclear). Mildly abnormal thyroid function in hospitalized patients (sick euthyroid) should not preclude a diagnosis of SIADH → Do thyroid function tests
	* If the above investigations are unrewarding:
	  * **SIADH** (hypotonic hyponatremia, euvolemic) - Schwartz criteria → fluid restrict, high protein diet, avoid further ADH stimulus (pain, nausea)

	  * **3 Cs - Cerebral, chest, C drugs**:
		* Tumors - small cell lung cancer, lymphoma, squamous cell cancers and others
		* Pulmonary - pneumonia, TB
		* Neurological - meningitis, intracranial hemorrhage, tumors, trauma
		* Drugs - psychiatric drugs (SSRI, antipsychotics, tricyclics), ecstasy, carbamazepine, others

		* Workup - Thorax and brain scans

### 2.1.4. MANAGEMENT

**Decision tree:**
1. **Is hyponatremia an emergency?**
   * Typically serum Na⁺ <120 mmol/L
   * Presence of severe neurological symptoms (seizures or impending respiratory arrest)
   * **ESCALATE TO SENIOR**
   * Management: 150 ml hypertonic saline (3% NaCl) infusion over 20 min OR 2 ml/kg (whichever is lower; max 150 ml; up to 2 boluses):
	 * To increase serum Na⁺ by 4-5 mmol/L in 1 hour (enough to reduce ICP by 50%)
	 * Check serum Na⁺ after 20 min and can repeat 1 more bolus
	 * Watch for clinical response
	 * 💡 1 ml/kg of 3% NaCl expected to raise serum Na⁺ by 1 mmol/L

2. **Is hyponatremia acute or chronic?**
   * **Acute hyponatremia** (<2 days):
	 * Correction can be done quickly without significant danger to the patient
   * **Chronic hyponatremia** (>2 days):
	 * ⚠️ Do not increase serum Na⁺ by more than 10 mmol/L in 24 hours
	 * Risk of osmotic demyelination if correction is excessive or too rapid

3. **Treat the cause of hyponatremia:**
   * **Hypertonic hyponatremia** (due to hyperglycemia):
	 * Translocational hyponatremia should improve with lowering of blood glucose level
	 * Aggressive volume repletion with isotonic fluid is typically required to correct volume depletion from glucosuria-driven osmotic diuresis and make up for intracellular translocation of water with glucose in response to insulin
   * **Hypotonic hyponatremia**:
	 * 💡 Correcting potassium will raise sodium too
	 * ⚠️ Beware of placing urinary catheter - it may induce sudden diuresis which will rapidly increase serum Na⁺

	 * **In hypovolemic hyponatremia:** Replace salt and water deficit using saline infusion or salt tablets

	 ![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_11.png_a9c7fc.png_3c5db8.png)

	   * Na⁺ deficit (mmol) = 0.6 × BW (kg) × (desired serum [Na⁺] – current serum [Na⁺])
	   * ⚠️ **Because restoration of euvolemia is accompanied by suppression of nonosmotic vasopressin release, this may lead to the abrupt onset of a brisk water diuresis and the consequent risk for overly rapid correction of [Na⁺]**
		 * Close observation of hourly urine output is mandatory in severe cases, and frequent measurement of urine osmolality in this setting is useful
		 * Slow correction of hypovolemia (if shock is absent and the patient is not compromised by hypoperfusion) may be beneficial in preventing this complication
	 * **In euvolemic hyponatremia (e.g., SIADH):** Fluid restriction, treat underlying cause, increase solute intake, consider vaptans
	   * 💡 Fluid restriction alone is unlikely to be successful in many cases of SIADH when the underlying cause is persistent (especially if urine Na⁺ > serum Na⁺)
		 * Consider adding NaCl tablets or solutes (osmolytes to clear free water) or high protein diet if fluid restriction not helping
		 * The higher the urine osmolality, the less the patient can drink
		 * The less the person eats, the less the patient can drink as well
	   * Monitor intake/output and weight for negative balance, mental status, hemodynamics
	   * The degree of water restriction that will mitigate hyponatremia can be estimated using the ratio of the sum of urinary sodium and potassium concentrations to the serum [Na⁺]:
		 * A ratio < 1 allows for more liberal limits (i.e., <1 L/day)
		 * A ratio > 1 may call for prohibitively strict measures (<500 mL/day) and predicts worsening of hyponatremia with IV isotonic saline administration because the sodium chloride is excreted in a smaller volume of more highly concentrated urine with the remaining electrolyte-free water retained
	   * **If severely symptomatic** (e.g., headache, vomiting - signs of impending herniation) - correct relatively fast - 100 ml of 3% saline over 15 min
	 * **In hypervolemic hyponatremia:**
	   * **Hypoalbuminemia:** IV albumin with furosemide, vaptans
		 * ⚠️ Caution is indicated before using vaptans in patients with cirrhosis due to their potential to cause splanchnic vasodilation (conivaptan) or hepatotoxicity (tolvaptan). In practice, these medications are nonetheless used in patients awaiting liver transplantation to optimize volume status and increase [Na⁺] to a level acceptable for transplantation listing
	   * **Cardiac failure or renal failure:** Fluid restriction, diuretics, consider vasopressin antagonist (2nd line; vaptans - tolvaptan, conivaptan)
		 * 💡 Because worsening volume overload may exacerbate a low cardiac output state in decompensated heart failure and in turn potentiate nonosmotic vasopressin release, **loop diuretics should be considered first-line therapy for hyponatremia attributed to heart failure.** Loop diuretics tend to impair free-water reabsorption. They can be cautiously used in cirrhosis as well
	 * **For severely symptomatic patients** with marked alterations in sensorium or seizures, rapid elevation of the [Na⁺] (irrespective of presumed duration of hyponatremia) by just 4-6 mEq/L in 1-2 hours can significantly reduce brain swelling and abort seizures or seizure risk:
	   * This can be accomplished by administration of up to three 100-mL boluses of hypertonic saline solution given 10 minutes apart
	   * Consider hypertonic saline solution:
		 * Given concerns for phlebitis associated with prolonged peripheral infusion of hyperosmolar solution, some centers require that continuous infusion of hypertonic saline solution be given through a central venous catheter, though data for adverse outcomes associated with peripheral IV infusion are lacking
		 * 💡 Each 1 mL/kg/h of 3% saline solution administered can be expected to increase [Na⁺] by 1 mEq/L/h
		 * Serial lab measurements of Na⁺ every 1-2 hours during active correction

**Bottom Line:** Management of hyponatremia depends on symptoms, severity, chronicity, and underlying etiology. For severe symptoms, hypertonic saline is used to achieve rapid partial correction. For chronic cases, correction should not exceed 10 mmol/L/24h. Treatment should address the underlying cause - fluid restriction for SIADH, cautious volume repletion for hypovolemic states, and diuretics for hypervolemic states, while carefully monitoring correction rates to avoid complications.

### 2.1.5. COMPLICATIONS OF TREATMENT

* While a brain cell depleted of its organic osmolytes resists swelling in a hypotonic environment, it may dehydrate and shrink if its surrounding tonicity increases faster than it can reaccumulate effective osmoles
**Osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS):**
* Irreversible neurological sequelae including dysphagia, dysarthria, spasticity, behavioral disturbance, cognitive impairment, delirium, seizures, quadriparesis, coma, and "locked-in" syndrome
* Neurological deterioration develops over days
* 💡 ODS is unlikely to complicate treatment of patients with an initial [Na⁺] >125 mEq/L
* Risk factors among patients with chronic hypotonic hyponatremia:
  * Presenting [Na⁺] <105 mEq/L
  * Alcoholism
  * Advanced liver disease
  * Malnutrition
  * Hypokalemia (because potassium is exchangeable with sodium, repletion of potassium deficits leads to translocation of sodium out of cells and a further increase in [Na⁺])
* 💡 Because normalization of [Na⁺] in acute hyponatremia carries no risk for ODS, the rate of increase need not be constrained when the duration of hyponatremia is known to be less than 48 hours

**Prevention and management of overcorrection of hyponatremia:**
* Monitor serum Na⁺ every 2-4 hours during active correction
* In thiazide-induced and hypovolemic hyponatremia, aquaresis may follow the suppression of nonosmotic vasopressin release that occurs after volume repletion, resulting in a rapid increase in [Na⁺]:
  * Because the increase will be preceded by an increase in urine output and decrease in urine osmolality, urine output should be monitored hourly and urine osmolality should be measured frequently in this setting
* If overcorrection occurs:
  * Calculate urine electrolyte-free water excretion
  * Administer enteral water or IV 5% dextrose in water:
	* Volume = urinary electrolyte-free water loss + estimated extrarenal and insensible water losses (to prevent further increases in Na⁺)
	* Rate = 3 ml/kg/hr
  * If urine output remains high - consider desmopressin
  * In patients at high risk for over-rapid correction due to aquaresis, some authorities recommend preemptive administration of desmopressin, 1-2 μg, IV or subcutaneously every 6 hours to prevent urine electrolyte-free water loss, with 3% saline infused at a rate calculated to effect the desired rate of increase in [Na⁺]

**Use of vaptans and urea:**
* **Vaptans**:
  * **Conivaptan** - non-selective vasopressin V1 and V2 receptor inhibitor
	* Potent CYP3A4 inhibitor with many drug-drug interactions
	* Only available as IV formulation for short-term use
  * **Tolvaptan** - selective V2 inhibitor
	* Costly
	* Significant risk of liver injury (used in treatment of PKD at higher doses)
  * As these drugs were developed, the initial expectation was that their ability to antagonize excessive vasopressin would make them highly efficacious and widely used. However, neither agent can be used long-term, which has limited their use even for short-term treatment, as clinicians are reluctant to start a treatment for a chronic condition that can only be used briefly
  * Adverse effects: Thirst, dry mouth, polyuria
  * ⚠️ Contraindications: Do not use with hypertonic saline (case reports of ODS)
* **Urea**:
  * Less expensive alternative to vaptans in the treatment of SIADH
  * Mechanism of action: Excreted in urine → increasing urinary solute content and electrolyte-free water clearance
	* Retrospective studies: Administration of 7.5 to 90 g/day of urea is associated with an approximately 6 mEq/L increase in [Na⁺] over 4-5 days
  * Not associated with sodium overcorrection
* Neither vaptans nor urea have been well studied in severe symptomatic hyponatremia. Patients with [Na⁺] <120 mEq/L and attributable neurologic symptoms were excluded from vaptan trials. The effect of urea is slow
* Accordingly, at present, without further data, vaptans or urea should be considered for use only in patients with euvolemic or hypervolemic hyponatremia with mild to moderate symptoms, and not in circumstances where [Na⁺] must be increased emergently

**Bottom Line:** The principal complication of hyponatremia treatment is osmotic demyelination syndrome, particularly when correction occurs too rapidly in chronic, severe hyponatremia. Prevention requires careful rate control, frequent monitoring, and readiness to counteract overcorrection with desmopressin and hypotonic fluids. Vaptans and urea offer alternative treatment options for specific scenarios but should not replace hypertonic saline for emergency correction.

### 2.2.2. CELLULAR ADAPTATION

**Bottom Line:** In response to hypernatremia, cells initially lose water and shrink, then adapt by accumulating organic osmolytes through TonEBP activation. This adaptation, while protective, creates an abnormal intracellular environment with molecular crowding and oxidative stress, leading to ongoing cell damage unless hypernatremia is corrected.

### 2.2.3. CLINICAL FEATURES

* Symptoms generally not apparent until serum Na⁺ >160 mmol/L
* Doughy feel to skin in hypernatremic dehydration
* **Neurological manifestations** primarily:
  * Restlessness, irritability, lethargy, muscular twitching, hyperreflexia, tremulousness, ataxia, seizures, stupor, coma
  * Subcortical or subarachnoid hemorrhage due to tearing of cerebral vessels with shrinkage of brain cells in severe cellular dehydration
* **Expected physiological response:**
  * **CNS:**
	* Thirst response. If absent, look for CNS condition impairing thirst response
  * **Renal:**
	* Excretion of small amount of concentrated urine (Uosm >1000 mOsm/kg H₂O)
* **Two factors to evaluate:**
  * Sodium gain
  * Water loss (most common)
* **Prerequisite - inadequate water intake:**
  * Alert patients with intact thirst mechanism - access to water should be able to compensate for water losses
  * Seen in: Delirium or cognitive impairment, hospitalized patients given inadequate free water, impaired thirst mechanism (e.g., some cases of central diabetes insipidus)
* **Contributory factors:**
  * **Renal water losses:** Glycosuria or use of osmotic diuretics (mannitol) causing osmotic diuresis in diabetes; diabetes insipidus (reduced vasopressin release); age-related reduced renal concentrating ability
  * **Extra-renal water losses:** Diarrhea and vomiting, sweating and burns
  * **High salt intake without adequate water:** Salt poisoning and sea water ingestion
* Two distinct patterns when comparing hospital-acquired vs. community-acquired hypernatremia:
  * **Patients with hypernatremia on admission:**
	* Were older
	* Often came from long-term care facilities where access to fluid may have been impaired
	* Mostly had preserved urinary concentrating capacity
  * **Patients who developed hypernatremia in the hospital:**
	* Had an age similar to the overall hospital population
	* 86% lacked free access to water
	* 74% had enteral water intake <1 L/day
	* Virtually all received <1 L/day of electrolyte-free water during the period when hypernatremia developed
	* Renal concentrating ability was impaired in 89% due principally to diuretics or osmotic diuresis
  * In this adult population, hypernatremia acquired **outside the hospital** was principally a **geriatric** disorder, whereas **hospital-acquired hypernatremia** was due to an insufficient fluid prescription, i.e., **iatrogenic**
* **Diabetes insipidus:**
  * Among central diabetes insipidus (CDI) causes, pituitary trauma is characterized by a classic triphasic response: initial period of DI followed by SIADH due to uncontrolled release of vasopressin from necrotizing pituitary cells, followed by sustained DI
  * Lithium is the most common cause of acquired nephrogenic DI
  * Diagnosis via overnight water deprivation testing
	* Direct measurement of vasopressin is difficult; recently using prohormone copeptin to differentiate between primary polydipsia, central DI, and nephrogenic DI
  * ⚠️ Confusion may arise if a patient with polyuria due to primary polydipsia is mistakenly assumed to have DI and undergoes diagnostic water deprivation:
	* Long-term polydipsia can wash out the medullary interstitial concentration gradient and downregulate aquaporin 2, leading to impaired urinary concentration and falsely suggesting nephrogenic DI
	* These patients will not have a history of hypernatremia

![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_image.png_4c664e.png_f0e88e.png)

**Bottom Line:** Clinical features of hypernatremia are predominantly neurological and generally appear when serum Na⁺ exceeds 160 mmol/L. Two major patterns exist: community-acquired hypernatremia (primarily in elderly with impaired access to water) and hospital-acquired hypernatremia (iatrogenic due to inadequate free water provision). Diabetes insipidus requires careful diagnostic evaluation to differentiate central from nephrogenic forms.

### 2.2.4. DIAGNOSTIC EVALUATION

* Etiology of hypernatremia is often apparent from clinical history
* Check urine osmolality (appropriate renal response is to concentrate urine):
  * If high (>600 mOsm/kg) - hypernatremia is not due to renal water loss → either extra-renal water loss or high salt intake
  * If low (<300 mOsm/kg) despite high serum osmolality (>295 mOsm/kg) → renal water loss
* **Diabetes insipidus** (large amounts of dilute urine despite hypernatremia):
  * Desmopressin administration to distinguish between central and nephrogenic DI

**Bottom Line:** Diagnostic evaluation focuses on differentiating water loss (renal vs. extra-renal) from sodium gain, with urine osmolality being a key discriminator. In patients with inappropriate urinary dilution, desmopressin response helps distinguish central from nephrogenic diabetes insipidus.

### 2.2.5. MANAGEMENT

![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_17.png_41b0c9.png_a66140.png)

* ⚠️ To protect against brain shrinkage as hypernatremia develops, brain cells take up solute. Dissipating those added osmoles during correction takes time. Rapid correction can result in neuronal water uptake and cerebral edema
* If hypernatremia develops rapidly (<48 hours), rapid correction is appropriate, with correction to normal over 24 hours. How quickly to correct hypernatremia of longer duration is uncertain:
  * NUH Pediatrics - aim for decrease in serum Na⁺ by not more than 10 mmol/L in 24 hours
* Absent definitive data, our approach is to correct chronic hypernatremia over a 48-hour period:
  * Na⁺ correction >0.5 mEq/L per hour (compared with approximately 0.35–0.45 mEq/L per hour) in neonates with [Na⁺] >150 mEq/L
  * Hypernatremia <48 hours (acute): correction rate up to 1 mEq/L per hour
  * Hypernatremia >48 hours (chronic): correction rate up to 0.5 mEq/L per hour
  * Newer corroborating observations show a higher mortality rate with slow correction of <0.25 mEq/L per hour in adults with [Na⁺] >155 mEq/L
* **For hypervolemic or euvolemic hypernatremia:**
  * Primary goal is to promote net deficit of Na⁺
  * Diuretics (furosemide) to promote natriuresis with careful infusion of hypotonic solutions (NOT pure dextrose solution)
* **For hypovolemic hypernatremia:**
  * Primary goal is volume correction with isotonic fluids; water repletion should commence after volume stabilization
  * Free water deficit = 0.6 × BW (kg) × (1 - [Na⁺]/140)
  * ⚠️ Of importance, existing formulas perform poorly in predicting the expected [Na⁺] change with a given volume of water administration. It is therefore imperative to monitor serum [Na⁺], often every 2 hours initially, to control the speed of correction
  * Note choice of IV fluids: 5% dextrose in 0.45% saline, 5% dextrose in 0.23% saline, 5% dextrose in 0.12% saline
	* ⚠️ Do not use pure dextrose - Na⁺ will decrease too fast

	![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_18.png_876596.png_d92169.png)

	![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_19.png_68d185.png_87938d.png)

	![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_20.png_817817.png_3e5989.png)

	![Predicting sodium change](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_21.png_976332.png_cf5a4a.png)

  * Fluid of choice: start with hypotonic solution (NOT pure dextrose solution) - should be with dextrose in saline but change to dextrose in saline with lower sodium concentration if rate of correction is too fast
  * **Secondary goal** is treatment of specific defect:
	* Insulin for hyperglycemia
	* Symptomatic management for GI symptoms
	* DDAVP for central DI
	* For lithium-induced nephrogenic DI:
	  * Once-daily lithium dosing
	  * Adding amiloride (impedes distal tubular lithium uptake via epithelial sodium channels)
	  * Adding thiazide to augment (AVP-independent) aquaporin 2 expression (which also enhances proximal tubular lithium absorption, requiring lithium dose reduction by approximately 1/3)
	  * Adding indomethacin to suppress prostaglandin production (limited by risk of AKI)
	* Correct hypercalcemia or hypokalemia if present

**Bottom Line:** Hypernatremia correction requires careful attention to chronicity, rate, and volume status. In chronic cases (>48h), correct at ≤0.5 mEq/L/hour using hypotonic fluids (not pure dextrose). For hypovolemic cases, begin with isotonic volume resuscitation before addressing free water deficit. Address underlying causes such as diabetes insipidus concurrently. Regular monitoring of serum sodium is crucial as predictive formulas are unreliable.

# 3. POTASSIUM DISORDERS

## 3.1. HYPERKALAEMIA

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Body Fluids and Electrolytes|Part A: Hyperkalemia Pathophysiology]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B: Hyperkalemia Management]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C: Hyperkalemia Emergency Management]]

### 3.1.1. DEFINITION AND SYMPTOMS

* Defined as serum potassium >5.5 mmol/L
  * Note that the upper limit of normal in preterm infants and young infants may be as high as 6.5 mmol/L
  * Severe hyperkalemia (serum [K⁺] >7 mmol/L) is a serious medical emergency that requires immediate attention
* Expected physiological response: Urine potassium excretion of >100 mmol/L

**Clinical manifestations:**
* Often asymptomatic until severe
* **Neuromuscular:** Paresthesia, flaccid paralysis which ascends symmetrically toward trunk and may affect respiratory muscles
* **Cardiovascular:** Arrhythmias
* **ECG changes:** Peaked T waves, prolonged PR interval, prolonged QRS complex, sine wave pattern, etc.
  * ECG progression: Tall tented T waves → flat P waves → widened QRS → ventricular fibrillation → sinusoidal pattern

![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_hyperkalemia6.png_644e71.png_634ed2.png)

**Bottom Line:** Hyperkalemia is defined as serum potassium >5.5 mmol/L and can be life-threatening at levels >7.0 mmol/L. While often asymptomatic until severe, it can cause progressive ECG changes (peaked T waves, prolonged PR and QRS, loss of P waves) and neuromuscular symptoms (ascending paralysis that may affect respiratory muscles).

### 3.1.2. DIAGNOSTIC APPROACH

1. **Rule out pseudohyperkalemia:**
   * Pseudohyperkalemia (measured K⁺ > actual serum K⁺) can occur due to hemolysis during blood collection (most labs report hemolysis index)
   * ⚠️ Do not discount profoundly elevated potassium due to mild hemolysis - repeat if hemolyzed sample shows borderline elevation

2. **Risk stratify hyperkalemia:**
![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_23.png_623ddb.png_1586fe.png)

**Bottom Line:** First confirm true hyperkalemia by excluding pseudohyperkalemia from hemolysis, then risk-stratify based on potassium level, ECG changes, and rate of rise to determine urgency of management. Repeat testing is warranted for borderline elevations in hemolyzed samples.

### 3.1.3. MANAGEMENT

![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_24.png_c22984.png_6c9468.png)

![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_image_1.png_c35179.png_7b4036.png)

**Treatment algorithm:**
1. **Is hyperkalemia real?**
   * Check for hemolysis during blood collection, high blood cell count in CBC (e.g., leukemias more prone to lysis)
2. **Is this an emergency?**
   * Serum [K⁺] >7 mmol/L
   * ECG changes (not just peaked T waves)
   * Rapid rise in potassium
3. **Emergency management takes precedence over diagnostic evaluation:**
   * Cardiac monitoring
   * Treatment

* 💡 Furosemide is the preferred treatment for fluid overload and hyperkalemia (e.g., in glomerulonephritis) but may not be used in dehydration or shock with hyperkalemia
* Consider sodium polystyrene sulfonate (resonium) for lower potassium levels and long-term use (watch for sodium load); may not work acutely. Other chronic hyperkalemia agents listed below:

* Monitor blood glucose hourly for next 6-12 hours and repeat potassium measurements regularly
* **Two newer agents for hyperkalemia treatment:**
  * Zirconium cyclosilicate
  * Patiromer

**Bottom Line:** Hyperkalemia management follows a stepwise approach based on severity. For cardiotoxicity, use calcium gluconate as a membrane stabilizer. For rapid potassium reduction, employ insulin/glucose, beta agonists, and sodium bicarbonate which shift potassium intracellularly. For removal, use loop diuretics, potassium binders, or dialysis. Monitor potassium and glucose regularly during treatment.

### 3.1.4. ETIOLOGY

![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_28.png_e09597.png_5a2744.png)

![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_29.png_f3f8f8.png_fb1ae4.png)

**Major causes of hyperkalemia:**
1. **Impaired excretion (renal)**:
   * **Decreased GFR:** Acute kidney injury, **chronic kidney disease**
   * **Hypoaldosteronism:** **Addison's disease** and **Type 4 renal tubular acidosis (RTA)**
	 * Adrenal insufficiency (primary - **Addison's**, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, or secondary) → hyperkalemia, hyponatremia, and hypotension
	   * Consider 8am cortisol, synacthen test, renin-angiotensin system testing
	 * Hyperkalemic **Type 4 RTA** - relative aldosterone deficiency reduces K⁺ and H⁺ excretion (hyperkalemia and normal anion gap metabolic acidosis)
	   * Occurs in diabetes mellitus with mild nephropathy (mild volume expansion inhibits aldosterone release) and with drugs like NSAIDs (inhibiting prostaglandin-dependent renin secretion)
	   * Rare genetic syndromes - pseudohypoaldosteronism, Gordon syndrome
		 * Consider if patient is young
   * **Drugs:** RAAS inhibitors (**ACE inhibitors, ARBs**, spironolactone), drugs impairing kidney function (NSAIDs), direct impairment of tubular excretion (cyclosporine and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole)

2. **Release of intracellular K⁺ into ECF**:
   * **Cell lysis**:
	 * Rhabdomyolysis - crush injury, heat stroke
	 * Tumor lysis syndrome - chemotherapy
	 * Burns
   * **Insulin deficiency** - insulin drives cellular uptake of K⁺; absence of insulin (e.g., in DKA) → hyperkalemia
   * **Inorganic acidosis** - excess H⁺ enters cells to buffer acidosis but in exchange for K⁺
   * **Increase in serum osmolality** - IV hypertonic mannitol, IVIG, hyperglycemia - water efflux out of cells draws potassium along
   * **Drugs:** Succinylcholine (depolarizes muscle, causing K⁺ efflux), digoxin overdose and fluoride poisoning - inhibit K⁺ uptake via Na/K pumps

3. **Increased intake**:
   * Large volume red cell transfusion or over-replacement of KCl
   * ⚠️ High dietary intake of K⁺ alone rarely causes hyperkalemia, except in patients with CKD and impaired K⁺ excretion

**Bottom Line:** Hyperkalemia etiology can be categorized into three mechanisms: impaired renal excretion (most common, particularly in renal dysfunction, hypoaldosteronism, or due to medications), shift of potassium from intracellular to extracellular space (cell lysis, insulin deficiency, acidosis), or excessive intake (rarely a sole cause unless renal function is impaired).

## 3.2. HYPOKALAEMIA

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Body Fluids and Electrolytes|Part A: Hypokalemia Pathophysiology]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B: Hypokalemia Management]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C: Hypokalemia Patient Safety]]

### 3.2.1. APPROACH AND SYMPTOMS

![Other aldosterone low causes: Syndrome of apparent mineralocorticoid excess (defect in 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2, mineralocorticoid receptor mutation)](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_30.png_836ba5.png_7f7988.png)

**Clinical manifestations:**
* Generally asymptomatic in the range of 3-3.5 mmol/L but needs replacement in patients with cardiovascular disease, on diuretics, or taking digoxin
* **Neuromuscular:** Weakness, flaccid paralysis, paresthesia, rhabdomyolysis (in profound hypokalemia), constipation, paralytic ileus, urinary retention
* **Renal:** Polyuria, nocturia, polydipsia (nephrogenic diabetes insipidus), interstitial nephritis (in prolonged hypokalemia)
* **Growth:** Growth retardation (in chronic potassium wasting)
* **Cardiovascular:** Arrhythmias
  * Hyperpolarization → ventricular escape, re-entrant phenomena, ectopic tachycardias, conduction delay
  * Premature atrial contractions, premature ventricular contractions
  * Supraventricular tachycardias (especially atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter)
* **Metabolic:** Metabolic alkalosis

**Bottom Line:** Hypokalemia affects multiple organ systems beyond just cardiac function, with neuromuscular, renal, and metabolic manifestations depending on severity and chronicity. While mild hypokalemia (3.0-3.5 mmol/L) may be asymptomatic in healthy individuals, patients with heart disease, on digoxin, or receiving diuretics require correction even at these levels.

### 3.2.2. ECG CHANGES

![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_31.png_ab86ec.png_e13adb.png)

![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_32.png_f29a24.png_e1e33b.png)

**ECG changes in hypokalemia:**
* Increased P wave amplitude
* Prolongation of PR interval
* Widespread ST depression and T wave flattening/inversion
* Prominent U waves (best seen in the precordial leads)
* *Apparent* long QT interval due to fusion of T and U waves (= long QU interval)

**With worsening hypokalemia:**
* Frequent supraventricular and ventricular ectopics
* Supraventricular tachyarrhythmias: Atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, atrial tachycardia
* Potential for life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias, e.g., ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation, and Torsades de Points

![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_33.png_b8f09f.png_e36f59.png)

![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_34.png_856848.png_5e91ca.png)

* 💡 Hypokalemia creates the illusion that the T wave is "pushed down," resulting in T-wave flattening/inversion, ST depression, and prominent U waves
* 💡 In hyperkalemia, the T wave is "pulled upwards," creating tall "tented" T waves, and stretching the remainder of the ECG to cause P wave flattening, PR prolongation, and QRS widening

**Bottom Line:** ECG changes in hypokalemia include T wave flattening, ST depression, prominent U waves, and PR prolongation, reflecting altered cardiac repolarization. As hypokalemia worsens, the risk of both supraventricular and potentially fatal ventricular arrhythmias increases. Recognizing the contrast between hypokalemic and hyperkalemic ECG patterns (T waves "pushed down" vs. "pulled up") aids in rapid visual diagnosis.

### 3.2.3. DIAGNOSTIC EVALUATION

**1. Identify if cause is apparent from history:**
* Exclude pre-analytical artifacts - blood drawn from infusion arm, pseudohypokalemia
* **Intracellular-extracellular shifts:**

  ![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_35.png_b9efd7.png_b47e01.png)

  * Intensive **insulin** therapy for DKA
  * Increased **beta-adrenergic** activity - bronchodilator therapy (acute asthma), stress-induced catecholamine release (MI), excess caffeine, pheochromocytoma
  * (Congenital) **periodic paralysis** - hypokalemic (due to **genetic calcium channel mutation**) or **thyrotoxic** (acquired)
	* Episodes of marked generalized weakness associated with low potassium, occurring after exercise, stress, high-carbohydrate meal
  * Transient, idiopathic: Replacing B12 deficiency with B12 supplementation (e.g., in anemia) → can cause true hypokalemia
  * Leukemia
* **Poor potassium intake:**
  * **Refeeding syndrome** - severe hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, and hypophosphatemia when malnourished patients with low body potassium stores are fed → stimulates insulin release
	* The risks of refeeding are greatest in the most malnourished patients (especially chronic alcoholics), but even those who have gone without food for 5 days can be at risk. Restitution of feeding should always be done slowly, with careful monitoring of serum potassium, phosphate, and magnesium in the first 3–5 days
	* Release of insulin triggers shifting of potassium, phosphate, and magnesium into cells (with water following the osmotic gradient), causing potentially fatal shifts of fluids and electrolytes from the extracellular to the intracellular compartment. Rapid depletion of (already low) **thiamine** exacerbates the condition
	* Strategies: Correct electrolytes aggressively, feed slowly, give thiamine
  * Chronic poor nutrition - mild chronic hypokalemia common - rarely the only cause of hypokalemia
  * Anorexia nervosa
* **GI potassium loss:** Diarrhea, vomiting, ileostomy losses

**2. If cause not apparent from history, determine if renal potassium loss is present:**
* Urinary potassium should be suppressed in a hypokalemic patient
* **Low urine potassium (24-hour K⁺ <15 mmol/day or spot urine K⁺/creatinine <1.5)** - **extrarenal loss → evaluate above causes again**
  * Appropriate renal conservation of potassium
  * Look for cause of potassium loss, transcellular shift, or inadequate intake
  * ⚠️ Intermittent surreptitious diuretic use can present with low urinary potassium between doses
* **High urine potassium (24-hour K⁺ >15 mmol/day, spot urine K⁺/creatinine >1.5)** - **renal loss**
  * Renal potassium wasting
  * May be occult
* Check magnesium - hypomagnesemia is associated with refractory hypokalemia

**3. Workup for renal loss causing hypokalemia:**
Assess: a) **BP and ECF volume status** and b) **presence of acidosis or alkalosis** → measure BP, serum bicarbonate, and urine chloride

* **Hypertension, hypervolemia + metabolic alkalosis:**
  * Suggests RAAS activation driving hypokalemia
  * **High aldosterone, low renin:**
	* Primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn's syndrome - aldosterone overproduction by adrenal tumor or hyperplasia)
	  * Workup: Plasma aldosterone:renin ratio (high aldosterone:low renin), confirmatory salt loading or saline suppression test, CT adrenal gland for adrenal tumor
	* Mineralocorticoid excess
  * **High renin/aldosterone:**
	* Renal artery stenosis
	  * Decreased renal perfusion → juxtaglomerular apparatus stimulation → RAAS activation
	  * Workup: Renal artery Doppler ultrasound - decreased flow
	* Malignant hypertension
	* Renin-secreting tumor
	* Pheochromocytoma
  * **Low renin/aldosterone:**
	* Steroid excess (secondary hyperaldosteronism)
	  * Including exogenous steroid ingestion (take history) and Cushing's syndrome
	  * Workup: Low-dose dexamethasone suppression test
	* Pseudohyperaldosteronism (cortisol normal)
	  * Genetic syndromes - **Liddle's syndrome**, syndrome of apparent mineralocorticoid excess (rare hereditary disorder of increased activity of epithelial sodium channels → excessive potassium excretion but sodium and water retention), congenital adrenal hyperplasia (increase in non-aldosterone mineralocorticoid), and others
	  * **Chronic licorice ingestion** (mimics aldosterone effects but with suppressed renin and aldosterone levels)
	* Cortisol low (17-alpha hydroxylase deficiency)
	* Liddle syndrome → treat with amiloride

	![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_36.png_316835.png_ca3f65.png)

* **Normal/hypotension, eu/hypovolemia + metabolic acidosis:**
  * Most causes of hypokalemia produce alkalosis
  * Apart from diarrhea…
  * Renal tubular acidosis (hyperchloremic)
	* **Type 1** (distal) or **Type 2** (proximal) - inability to acidify urine → congenital or acquired (associated with autoimmune disease, myeloma, or certain drugs)
	  * Hypokalemic
	* Type 4 - resistant to aldosterone - most commonly from diabetes mellitus
	  * Hyperkalemic
	* Potassium loss occurs because of increased sodium delivery enhancing potassium excretion
* **Normal/hypotension, eu/hypovolemia + metabolic alkalosis → urine chloride helpful:**
  * **High urine chloride (>20 mmol/L):**
	* Magnesium deficiency
	  * Required in distal nephron to block potassium wasting via ROMK channel
	  * Test and treat hypomagnesemia (hypokalemia often refractory until magnesium is replaced)
	  * Other causes of hypomagnesemia: Aminoglycoside therapy, cisplatin
	* Active diuretic therapy - inhibits potassium and chloride reabsorption
	* Renal tubular genetic defects - mimic effect of diuretics → **Bartter syndrome (loop diuretic-like effect) and Gitelman syndrome (thiazide-like effect)**
	  * Young patients, possibly developmentally delayed, with polyuria, polydipsia (potassium loss), calciuria (urine calcium:urea ratio >1 in Bartter, decreased in Gitelman)
  * **Low urine chloride (<20 mmol/L):**
	* Vomiting or nasogastric tube suction
	  * Loss of potassium-rich upper GI secretions
	  * Vomiting also → metabolic alkalosis → reabsorption of H⁺ in exchange for K⁺ in kidneys → hypokalemia
	* Non-reabsorbable anions - e.g., ketoanions and salicylate → excretion with accompanying cations (Na⁺ and K⁺)

**Bottom Line:** Diagnostic evaluation of hypokalemia involves first excluding pre-analytical errors, considering redistribution phenomena, and assessing intake. If the cause isn't apparent, determining renal versus extrarenal potassium loss is key, followed by evaluating acid-base status, volume status, and urinary electrolytes to pinpoint specific diagnoses ranging from endocrine disorders to tubular defects.

### 3.2.4. MANAGEMENT

![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_37.png_3379f9.png_b0776f.png)

**Management approach:**
1. **Is this an emergency?**
   * Depends on whether patient is symptomatic (usually only if K⁺ <2.5 mmol/L): Cardiac arrhythmias, paralysis, severe muscle weakness

2. **Amount to give:** Potassium deficit (approximate) = **0.6 × BW (kg) × (4 - K⁺)** while keeping in mind the underlying cause
   * For transcellular shift (e.g., periodic paralysis) - don't administer excessive amounts

   ![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_38.png_3d89b4.png_eb4521.png)

3. **Choose the route of replacement:**
   * Oral route almost always preferred: Safer and equally effective
   * IV indicated in emergency:
	 * IV 7.45% KCl infusion:
	   * ⚠️ IV potassium should not exceed >0.2 mmol/kg/hour or be infused at too high a concentration (>40 mmol/L via peripheral vein)
	   * Concentrated potassium infusion: Requires ECG monitoring and ICU setting with central venous access
	   * Maximum infusion rate in ICU: 0.4 mmol/kg/hour

	 ![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_39.png_765b57.png_96dba7.png)

   * Remember to check sodium and potassium, fluid status, input/output charts, blood pressure

4. **Choose the type of potassium replacement:**
   * PO Slow-K: 600 mg tablet contains 8 mmol K⁺ (600 mg twice daily = 16 mmol/day)
   * PO KCl mixture: 10 ml contains 13 mmol K⁺ (10 ml twice daily = 26 mmol/day)
   * IV premixed (used when K⁺ <3 mmol/L):
	 * 10 mmol in 100 ml normal saline over 1 hour - will correct by approximately 0.1 mmol/L - usually done ×3 cycles
	 * 20 mmol premix only in ICU/high-dependency unit (requires ECG monitoring)
	 * ⚠️ Be careful if renal function is impaired
   * Potassium phosphate
   * Potassium citrate - only give if patient has concomitant acidosis - citrate is an alkalinizing agent

5. **Additional precautions:**
   * ⚠️ Avoid glucose infusion without KCl in hypokalemia
   * ⚠️ Avoid rapid correction of concurrent metabolic acidosis
   * Check serum magnesium in resistant hypokalemia cases

**Bottom Line:** Management of hypokalemia depends on severity, with oral replacement preferred for non-emergent cases. When IV replacement is necessary, strict infusion rates and monitoring are essential for safety. The degree of replacement should be calculated based on the estimated deficit, and concomitant magnesium deficiency must be addressed to prevent refractory hypokalemia. Avoid giving glucose alone or rapidly correcting acidosis when treating hypokalemia.

# 4. CALCIUM DISORDERS

## 4.1. HYPERCALCAEMIA

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Body Fluids and Electrolytes|Part A: Hypercalcemia Pathophysiology]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B: Hypercalcemia Management]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C: Hypercalcemia Patient Safety]]

### 4.1.1. SYMPTOMS

* Moderate to severe hypercalcemia causes:
  * **Stones** - Nephrolithiasis (causing renal tubular impairment → hyperuricemia and hyperchloremia)
  * **Bones** - Osteopenia, erosions, fractures
  * **Groans** - Abdominal pain, constipation, nausea/vomiting due to depression of gut motility
  * **Moans** - Depression, lethargy, fatigue
  * **Cardiac effects** may be present - **short QT interval** and arrhythmias
* In asymptomatic patients - it may herald malignancy, so take seriously
* Can present as an acute emergency - severe hypercalcemia and dehydration

**Bottom Line:** Hypercalcemia manifests with the classic "bones, stones, groans, and moans" presentation, affecting multiple systems including renal (stones), skeletal (bone pain/fractures), gastrointestinal (constipation, nausea), neurological (lethargy, altered mental status), and cardiac (short QT, arrhythmias). Even asymptomatic hypercalcemia warrants investigation as it may indicate underlying malignancy.

### 4.1.2. CAUSES

![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_40.png_e1147b.png_8f8933.png)

**Bottom Line:** Major causes of hypercalcemia include primary hyperparathyroidism (most common in outpatients), malignancy (most common in hospitalized patients), medication effects (thiazides, lithium, vitamin D), endocrine disorders (thyrotoxicosis, adrenal insufficiency), and granulomatous diseases (sarcoidosis, tuberculosis). Less common causes include immobilization, familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia, and milk-alkali syndrome.

### 4.1.3. DIAGNOSTIC APPROACH

* Calculate corrected Ca = Ca **(mmol/L)** + 0.02 (40-albumin)
  * Measured calcium includes protein-bound and free calcium; only free calcium is biologically active → FALSE LOW TOTAL CALCIUM IN HYPOALBUMINEMIC STATE
  * When using calcium in mg/dL, the equation uses 0.8 as the modifier with albumin in g/dL
* Measure ionized calcium when patient is critically ill, post-transfusion (due to citrate), or an ESRF patient on dialysis
* Classify into 3 clinical pictures based on **serum creatinine and PTH levels**:
  * Hypercalcemia in chronic kidney disease (CKD)
  * Hypercalcemia with normal/increased PTH and normal kidney function
  * Hypercalcemia with decreased PTH and normal kidney function

  ![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_41.png_74b31f.png_26a641.png)

**Hypercalcemia and CKD:**
* eGFR <30 mL/min → tertiary hyperparathyroidism
* Calcium initially low in CKD - deficient hydroxylation of vitamin D to active vitamin D
* Phosphate retention due to reduced renal clearance
* Low calcium, high phosphate stimulates PTH secretion (SECONDARY HYPERPARATHYROIDISM)
* Over time, PTH secretion becomes autonomous and decoupled from calcium levels → if hypercalcemia develops → tertiary hyperparathyroidism
* Other causes of hypercalcemia - especially multiple myeloma - remain possible; consider if there are atypical features (e.g., PTH is suppressed)

![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_42.png_8459a1.png_2fa78f.png)

![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_43.png_07089d.png_cf097c.png)

**Hypercalcemia and normal/high PTH (no CKD):**
* Hypercalcemia should suppress PTH → if PTH is elevated or inappropriately normal, PTH is driving hypercalcemia
* **PTH adenoma - primary hyperparathyroidism**:
  * Workup: 99mTc-Sestamibi scan
  * Conservative management: High oral fluid intake to prevent renal stones, annual calcium biochemistry and renal function check + cinacalcet (calcimimetic - enhancing sensitivity of calcium-sensing receptor - reducing PTH - usually indicated in tertiary hyperparathyroidism and primary in patients unwilling or unfit for surgery)
  * Definitive treatment: Surgery (for those with clear-cut symptoms, or asymptomatic with significant hypercalcemia - corrected >2.85 mmol/L) + post-op vitamin D supplementation (because of risk of hypocalcemia post-op)
  * Acute management in life-threatening hypercalcemia with dehydration: IV fluids with bisphosphonates → urgent parathyroidectomy if unstable
* **Lithium therapy** - can stimulate PTH secretion
* **Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia (FHH)**:
  * Genetic mutation in the calcium-sensing receptor → calcium maintained at higher than normal set point
  * Benign condition
  * Workup: **Low 24-hour urine calcium** (<100 mg/day) and low calcium/creatinine ratio
  * Diagnosis: Screening family members for hypercalcemia and/or identifying inactivating mutation in gene

**Hypercalcemia and low PTH (no CKD):**
* Suppression of PTH by extrinsic cause of hypercalcemia - usually malignancy:
  * Bone invasion
  * Production of PTH-related protein (PTHrP) as a paraneoplastic syndrome
  * High vitamin D levels in lymphoma
* Rapid clinical course - features of malignancy will soon develop, but if hypercalcemia has been present >1 year without symptoms - tends not to be due to cancer
* **Look for malignancy** (cancer of unknown primary):
  * Clinical features - symptoms and physical examination
  * Imaging - CT chest, abdomen, bone scans (squamous cell cancers - lungs, head and neck, renal, urogenital sites are prone to causing hypercalcemia)
  * Hematological anomalies
	* Multiple myeloma (consider serum protein electrophoresis)
  * Consider direct endoscopic visualization if age-appropriate screening not yet done - Pap smear, colonoscopy
* Other differential diagnoses - if cancer is excluded:
  * Vitamin D intoxication - chronic overdose of vitamin D supplements (also elevated in lymphoma)
  * Granulomatous disease - vitamin D activation and hypercalcemia → imaging and biopsy
  * High bone turnover - hyperthyroidism, Paget's disease, other causes of high bone turnover

**Bottom Line:** The diagnostic approach to hypercalcemia begins with calculating corrected calcium based on albumin levels, then classifying by PTH level and renal function. In CKD, tertiary hyperparathyroidism is common. With normal renal function, high/normal PTH suggests primary hyperparathyroidism or FHH, while suppressed PTH raises concern for malignancy. The evaluation should include comprehensive biochemical testing, imaging, and consideration of duration and severity of hypercalcemia.

### 4.1.4. MANAGEMENT

**Broad principles:**
* **Volume expansion with 0.9% NaCl**:
  * NaCl recommended given the sodium-linked calcium diuresis that occurs in the proximal tubule because of the higher [Na⁺] of 0.9% saline compared to balanced crystalloid solutions
  * 1 to 2 L 0.9% saline should be administered as bolus for volume expansion, followed by infusion of 75 to 300 mL/hr to promote calciuresis, ideally targeting 100 to 150 mL/hr urinary output
  * Historically when euvolemia was reached, loop diuretics were added to further aid in calcium excretion, but loop diuretics are no longer recommended given concerns for iatrogenic electrolyte derangements and lack of evidence-based support, and therefore should be used with caution unless there are concurrent concerns for volume overload
* **Calcitonin for rapid correction** - increases renal excretion and inhibits osteoclastic bone resorption - has tachyphylaxis after 48 hours
* **Hemodialysis** if refractory or life-threatening

**Treatment based on underlying cause:**
* **Glucocorticoids** - decrease intestinal absorption of the active form of vitamin D, calcitriol - effective for granulomatous diseases, vitamin D toxicity, and malignancies such as multiple myeloma or lymphoma
* **Bisphosphonates / Denosumab** (if bisphosphonate refractory) for moderate-severe hypercalcemia / malignancy-induced osteolysis
* **Primary hyperparathyroidism** - cinacalcet (calcimimetic) or surgical parathyroidectomy

**Bottom Line:** Management of hypercalcemia begins with aggressive saline hydration to enhance renal calcium excretion, followed by targeted therapy based on etiology: bisphosphonates or denosumab for malignancy-related hypercalcemia, glucocorticoids for vitamin D excess or granulomatous disease, and calcimimetics or surgery for hyperparathyroidism. Calcitonin offers rapid but temporary reduction, while dialysis remains an option for severe, refractory cases.

## 4.2. HYPOCALCAEMIA

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Body Fluids and Electrolytes|Part A: Hypocalcemia Pathophysiology]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B: Hypocalcemia Management]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C: Hypocalcemia Patient Safety]]

### 4.2.1. SYMPTOMS

* Mild hypocalcemia - asymptomatic
* Causes neuromuscular excitability affecting:
  * **Sensory nerves** (circumoral numbness, paresthesia)
  * **Muscles** (muscle cramps, twitching, dystonias)
  * **Brain** (irritability, psychosis)
  * **Heart** (long QT, arrhythmias)
* More severe - tetany (increased excitability of nerves, children more susceptible, triad of carpopedal spasm, stridor, and convulsions)
  * Latent tetany - induced by Trousseau's sign (inflate BP cuff > systolic BP - carpal spasm) or Chvostek's sign (tapping over facial nerve emerging from parotid gland → twitching of facial muscles)
  * Can also cause papilledema and prolonged QT interval → ventricular arrhythmias
* If severe → seizures and laryngospasm → respiratory arrest

**Bottom Line:** Hypocalcemia primarily manifests through increased neuromuscular excitability, ranging from mild paresthesias to tetany with carpopedal spasm. Severe cases can cause seizures, laryngospasm, and cardiac arrhythmias due to QT prolongation. Physical examination signs include positive Trousseau's and Chvostek's signs, which demonstrate latent tetany.

### 4.2.2. DIAGNOSTIC APPROACH

* Calculate corrected calcium as described previously
  * Albumin is a negative acute-phase reactant - falls in acute illness
  * In hypoalbuminemia - measured total calcium falls because of decreased albumin-bound calcium, although ionized calcium may be normal
* Normal values:
  * Normal corrected calcium: Total 2.1-2.55 mmol/L, Ionized 1.0-1.2 mmol/L
* Diagnostic approach:
  * Look for overt causes of calcium loss from circulation
  * Measure PTH, phosphate, and magnesium
  * Three clinical patterns:
	* Hypocalcemia with **overt calcium loss**
	* Hypocalcemia with **high PTH**
	* Hypocalcemia with **low PTH**

![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_44.png_4afc3a.png_799bd3.png)

![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_45.png_2be36f.png_b78b26.png)

* Most common cause: Low serum albumin with normal ionized calcium concentration or conversely ionized calcium low with normal total calcium in patients with alkalosis (e.g., hyperventilation)
* Can occur due to hypomagnesemia - consider in malabsorption, diuretic use, PPI therapy, those with alcohol excess history - impairs ability of parathyroid to secrete PTH
* Prolonged hypocalcemia and hyperphosphatemia (hypoparathyroidism) → calcification of basal ganglia, grand mal epilepsy, psychosis, and cataracts
* Hypocalcemia associated with hypophosphatemia → vitamin D deficiency → rickets in children and osteomalacia in adults

![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_46.png_b4a729.png_63f31d.png)

**Bottom Line:** Diagnostic evaluation of hypocalcemia begins with calculating corrected calcium based on albumin level, and measuring ionized calcium in critically ill patients. Further workup includes PTH, phosphate, and magnesium levels to categorize patients into overt calcium loss, PTH-related issues (high or low), or hypomagnesemia. This classification guides both further investigation and therapeutic approaches.

### 4.2.3. ETIOLOGY

**Hypocalcemia from Calcium Sequestration:**
* Clinically overt causes:
* **Acute pancreatitis:**
  * Released pancreatic enzymes autodigest fat → free fatty acids bind calcium (saponification) → calcium deposition causes hypocalcemia (variable PTH depending on time elapsed)
  * Degree of hypocalcemia is a severity marker for pancreatitis
* **Cell lysis** - release of intracellular phosphate → calcium phosphate deposition → fall in calcium:
  * Tumor lysis syndrome
  * Rhabdomyolysis
* **Osteoblastic metastases** - widespread osteoblastic metastases (e.g., prostate or breast cancer) can cause hypocalcemia due to calcium deposition around the tumor
* **Extensive transfusions** - citrate in blood products chelates calcium:
  * In anesthesia - relevant during cardiopulmonary bypass and massive transfusion:
	* Components of the bypass machine such as the large volume, acidity, and protein content of priming fluid, as well as large doses of heparin necessary for CPB are associated with hypocalcemia
	* Massive transfusion historically has been defined by the administration of ≥10 units of packed red blood cells (pRBCs), or the transfusion of more than the patient's blood volume in a 24-hour period
  * The mechanism is driven by calcium sequestration in the setting of large citrate loads:
	* A unit of pRBCs is stored in 3 g of citrate, similarly fresh whole blood being stored in 1.66 g citrate; with the citrate serving as an anticoagulant to prevent coagulation during storage
	* In normal physiologic circumstances, the liver can metabolize 3 g of citrate every 5 minutes, but unfortunately in times of systemic stressors such as major trauma, especially with concurrent acidosis or hypothermia, hepatic citrate metabolism is greatly reduced
	* In the setting of ongoing massive transfusion, the citrate load from blood product administration can exceed hepatic metabolism, allowing for increased circulating citrate concentrations to freely bind with ionized Ca²⁺, resulting in progressive hypocalcemia
  * Incorporated in the "Diamond of Death":

  ![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_image_3.png_c378ed.png_5045c9.png)

**Hypocalcemia with High PTH:**
* Response from parathyroid glands is appropriate, but PTH is ineffective in increasing serum calcium
* **Chronic kidney disease:**
  * Impaired 1-hydroxylation of vitamin D hinders calcium absorption → hypocalcemia and secondary hyperparathyroidism
* **Vitamin D deficiency:**
  * Measured vitamin D and phosphate are low (vitamin D required for both calcium and phosphate absorption)
  * Occurs with poor sunlight exposure, nutritional deficiency, gastrointestinal disease (malabsorption of fat-soluble vitamins)
* **Genetic vitamin D or PTH resistance** (rare):
  * Vitamin D resistance (vitamin D-resistant rickets) - vitamin D deficiency picture (low calcium, low phosphate) WITH HIGH VITAMIN D levels
  * PTH resistance (pseudohypoparathyroidism) - apparent hypoparathyroidism (low calcium, high phosphate) BUT WITH HIGH PTH
	* May have phenotypic features of Albright's hereditary osteodystrophy - short stature, round facies, obesity, short 4th metacarpal bones, developmental delay
* **Hungry bone syndrome:**
  * Longstanding vitamin D deficiency or hyperparathyroidism (which stimulates calcium release from bone) depletes bone calcium
  * If corrected → rapid uptake of calcium by bone may lead to hypocalcemia
* **Drugs:**
  * Inhibitors of bone resorption used in osteoporosis (bisphosphonates and denosumab)
* Biochemical distinction - serum creatinine, phosphate, vitamin D levels (Vitamin D raises both calcium and phosphate, PTH raises calcium, lowers phosphate)

![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_47.png_872feb.png_ca0f26.png)

**Hypocalcemia with Low PTH:**
* Low PTH is inappropriate in hypocalcemia → failure to respond to hypocalcemia
* **Hypoparathyroidism** - various causes:
  * Inadvertent parathyroid gland damage from thyroid surgery
  * Autoimmune disease (Polyglandular autoimmune syndrome)
  * Infiltration (hemochromatosis)
  * Congenital syndromes
* **Hypomagnesemia** - impairs PTH secretion

**Bottom Line:** Hypocalcemia etiologies are diverse, including calcium sequestration (acute pancreatitis, tumor lysis, massive transfusion), ineffective PTH action (vitamin D deficiency, CKD, medication effects, genetic resistance), and insufficient PTH production (surgical hypoparathyroidism, autoimmune destruction). Hypomagnesemia is an important reversible cause that impairs both PTH secretion and action, resulting in refractory hypocalcemia until magnesium is repleted.

### 4.2.4. MANAGEMENT

**Indications for IV calcium correction:**
* Acutely symptomatic patient (tetany, papilledema, seizures, QT prolongation, arrhythmias)
* Asymptomatic: Corrected calcium <1.8 mmol/L or ionized calcium <0.8 mmol/L

**Calcium preparations and dosing:**
* An ampoule of 10 mL of calcium gluconate 10% = 2.3 mmol calcium = 90 mg elemental calcium - this dose will raise the serum calcium for only 2-3 hours
  * Bolus doses of intravenous calcium will only result in transient rises in serum ionized [Ca²⁺] and therefore should be followed by continuous infusions of elemental calcium at 0.5 to 1.5 mg/kg per hour
* Calcium gluconate preferred because it is less likely to cause tissue necrosis compared to calcium chloride

**Treatment protocols:**
* **If symptomatic:**
  * 10 mL calcium gluconate 10% slow IV bolus over 10 minutes, followed by an infusion at 0.5–1.5 mg/kg/hr
  * ⚠️ In emergency, undiluted solution CAN be administered as slow bolus over minimum 3 minutes through a large vein during emergency
  * PO option: 500–1,000 mg calcium carbonate every 6–8 hours
* **If asymptomatic:**
  * Corrected calcium <1.8 mmol/L, Ionized calcium <0.8 mmol/L - 10-20 mL calcium gluconate 10% (dilute 10 mL of calcium gluconate 10% to 100 mL normal saline or D5W and infuse over 1 hour)
  * Corrected calcium 1.8-1.9 mmol/L, Ionized calcium 0.8-0.9 mmol/L - up to ICU/treating physician's discretion whether to correct
* **Calcium continuous IV infusion preparation:**
  * Every 10 mL of calcium gluconate 10% in compatible diluent (incompatible with sodium bicarbonate) to make final volume of 100 mL
	* E.g., for 6.9 mmol of calcium requires 300 mL total volume
  * Consider use of 0.22 micron filter for continuous IV infusion due to potential presence of particulates and lack of stability data from manufacturer

**Monitoring:**
* **Watch for hypomagnesemia** (causes refractory hypocalcemia):
  * IV magnesium sulfate 1–2 g/hr over 3–6 hours, followed by an infusion at 0.5–1 g/hr
* Monitor ionized calcium/corrected calcium 6-12 hours after replacement
* Monitor ionized calcium more frequently during massive transfusion protocol (MTP)

**Bottom Line:** Management of hypocalcemia prioritizes IV calcium for symptomatic patients or severe biochemical deficits. Calcium gluconate is preferred over calcium chloride for peripheral administration. Continuous infusions are often necessary after initial bolus due to the transient effect of bolus therapy. Concomitant hypomagnesemia must be corrected to ensure effective calcium replacement, and frequent monitoring is essential, particularly during massive transfusion protocols.

# 5. PHOSPHATE DISORDERS

## 5.1. APPROACH TO PHOSPHATE DERANGEMENTS

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Body Fluids and Electrolytes|Part A: Phosphate Disorders]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B: Phosphate Management]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C: Phosphate Patient Safety]]

![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_48.png_290c40.png_323d87.png)

* **Acute hypophosphatemia** (Serum [Pi] <2.5 mg/dL) → ATP depletion → impaired oxidative cellular metabolism
  * → Neuromuscular symptoms (limb or cranial nerve weakness, altered mental status), rhabdomyolysis, cardiac dysfunction
* **Chronic hypophosphatemia** → bone pain, osteomalacia, rickets
* **Hyperphosphatemia** - may be asymptomatic or present with symptoms associated with hypercalcemia or hypocalcemia
* Isolated phosphate imbalances are rare - phosphate homeostasis closely tied to potassium and calcium balance
* **Clinical patterns** (check electrolytes including calcium):
  * Phosphate disorder in renal impairment
  * Phosphate disorder with concomitant potassium derangement
  * Phosphate disorder with concomitant calcium derangement
  * Phosphate disorder with normal potassium, calcium, and creatinine - consider renal phosphate wasting

**Bottom Line:** The approach to phosphate disorders requires assessment of associated electrolytes, particularly calcium and potassium, along with renal function. Acute hypophosphatemia impairs energy metabolism, leading to neuromuscular and cardiac dysfunction, while chronic deficiency affects bone health. Hyperphosphatemia symptoms often manifest through associated calcium derangements.

## 5.2. HYPOPHOSPHATEMIA

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Body Fluids and Electrolytes|Part A: Hypophosphatemia]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B: Hypophosphatemia Management]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C: Hypophosphatemia Patient Safety]]

### 5.2.1. ETIOLOGY

* Serum [Pi] <2.5 mg/dL
* Three major mechanisms leading to hypophosphatemia:
  1. **Poor intestinal absorption**:
	 * Chronic diarrhea or steatorrhea
	 * Chronic phosphate-binding antacid use
	 * Vitamin D deficiency
  2. **Increased urinary excretion**:
	 * Hyperparathyroidism
	 * Diuretic use
	 * Fanconi syndrome
	 * Chronic alcoholism
  3. **Internal redistribution** (most common):
	 * Refeeding syndrome
	 * Recovery from diabetic ketoacidosis
	 * Respiratory alkalosis
* Certain chemotherapy classes, notably ones with renal toxicity, such as alkylating agents (e.g., cyclophosphamide), tyrosine kinase inhibitors, or high-dose estrogen therapy, have also been associated with induced hypophosphatemia

![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_image_4.png_f60e00.png_940329.png)

**Bottom Line:** Hypophosphatemia results from decreased intestinal absorption, increased urinary excretion, or internal redistribution. The most common clinical scenarios include refeeding syndrome, recovery from DKA, respiratory alkalosis, certain medications, and malnutrition states. Understanding these mechanisms helps direct appropriate investigation and management.

### 5.2.2. MANAGEMENT

* **Severe hypophosphatemia**:
  * IV potassium phosphate or sodium phosphate 0.2-0.68 mmol/kg (5–16 mg/kg) over 12 hours
	* Potassium phosphate (93 mg/mL of phosphate)
	* Sodium phosphate (93 mg/mL of phosphate)
	* ⚠️ Slow administration (not >1-3 mEq/hr - risk for induced hypocalcemia from calcium phosphate precipitation → calciphylaxis)
* **Mild-moderate hypophosphatemia**:
  * Oral replacement over 3-10 days
* Monitor phosphate, potassium, and calcium levels for iatrogenic electrolyte imbalances

**Bottom Line:** Management of hypophosphatemia depends on severity, with IV replacement reserved for severe cases (<1.0 mg/dL) or symptomatic patients. Careful administration rates are crucial to prevent iatrogenic hypocalcemia. Oral supplementation is appropriate for mild-moderate cases with regular monitoring of phosphate, calcium, and potassium.

## 5.3. HYPERPHOSPHATEMIA

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Body Fluids and Electrolytes|Part A: Hyperphosphatemia]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B: Hyperphosphatemia Management]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C: Hyperphosphatemia Patient Safety]]

### 5.3.1. ETIOLOGY

* Serum [Pi] >4.5 mg/dL
* Major causes:
  * **Chronic kidney disease/Reduced eGFR**
  * **Increased intestinal absorption** via dietary or supplemental intake
  * **Pathologic redistribution from intracellular stores**:
	* Tumor lysis syndrome
	* Hemolysis
	* Trauma
	* Sepsis
	* Rhabdomyolysis

**Phosphate Derangements in Renal Impairment:**
* Hyperphosphatemia occurs due to impaired phosphate excretion → drives secondary hyperparathyroidism
* Other factors may contribute to hyperphosphatemia → commonly ill-advised Fleet enema (high phosphate load)
* Hypophosphatemia can occur when excess phosphate binders are used or phosphate is rapidly removed via dialysis (usually in those newly initiated on dialysis)

**Phosphate Derangements with Deranged Potassium:**
* Phosphate usually moves in the same direction as potassium
* **Hypokalemia with hypophosphatemia**:
  * Increased excretion:
	* GI or renal losses - distinguished by 24-hour urinary phosphate (inappropriately low <100 mg/day in GI loss, but high in renal losses >100 mg/day)
	* Proximal tubular defect i.e., Fanconi syndrome → renal wasting of potassium, phosphate, bicarbonate (renal tubular acidosis), glucose, and amino acids
  * Increased cellular uptake:
	* Insulin therapy (DKA)
	* Beta-agonist therapy
  * Refeeding syndrome
* **Hyperkalemia with hyperphosphatemia**:
  * Release of intracellular ions - rhabdomyolysis, tumor lysis, severe hemolytic anemia, etc.

**Phosphate Derangements with Deranged Calcium:**
* Work through causes of hypocalcemia/hypercalcemia

![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_Untitled_49.png_683f1f.png_2afb86.png)

**Primary Renal Phosphate Wasting Syndromes:**
* Inappropriately high 24-hour urinary phosphate despite low serum phosphate
* E.g., Hereditary hypophosphatemic rickets (genetic)
* Oncogenic osteomalacia (excess fibroblast growth factor 23 production by tumor)
* Rare disorders

**Bottom Line:** Hyperphosphatemia most commonly results from reduced renal excretion (CKD) or cellular release during tissue destruction syndromes. Associated electrolyte disturbances, particularly potassium and calcium abnormalities, often provide clues to the underlying mechanism. Primary renal phosphate wasting syndromes are rare genetic or paraneoplastic disorders characterized by inappropriate urinary phosphate loss.

### 5.3.2. MANAGEMENT

* Treatment approach is often referred to as the "3D's of hyperphosphatemia management: diet, drugs, and dialysis."
* **Normal eGFR** - dietary modification/restriction
* **CKD**:
  * Identify and treat underlying cause:
	* Hyperparathyroidism (secondary): vitamin D and calcimimetics - decrease PTH and bone resorption
  * Decrease intake and absorption - phosphate binders (calcium carbonate, sevelamer)
  * Increase phosphate excretion - volume expansion, loop or thiazide diuretics, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors
* **Dialysis** when severe or refractory

![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_image_5.png_a831c7.png_df25d3.png)

**Bottom Line:** Management of hyperphosphatemia follows the "3D" approach: dietary restriction, drugs (phosphate binders, treatments for secondary hyperparathyroidism), and dialysis in severe cases. In CKD, a comprehensive approach includes addressing the underlying cause, limiting phosphate absorption, and enhancing excretion when possible.

# 6. MAGNESIUM DISORDERS

## 6.1. HYPOMAGNESEMIA

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Body Fluids and Electrolytes|Part A: Hypomagnesemia]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B: Hypomagnesemia Management]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C: Magnesium Patient Safety]]

### 6.1.1. ETIOLOGY

**Clinical features parallel calcium derangements:**
* **Hypomagnesemia** - neuromuscular irritability affecting peripheral nerves (tetany, weakness, tremor), brain (seizure, vertigo, ataxia, delirium), cardiac muscle (tachyarrhythmias and long QT)
* Magnesium homeostasis closely related to potassium and calcium balance - many overlapping causes
* ⚠️ Hypomagnesemia is associated with refractory hypokalemia (renal potassium wasting via ROMK channel) and hypocalcemia (PTH resistance and decreased PTH secretion)

![](ELECTROLYTES_ELECTROLYTES_image_8.png_363a46.png_91bdcc.png)

**Hypomagnesemia with hypokalemia:**
* **Increased excretion**:
  * GI or renal losses - diuretics, genetic defects mimicking diuretics, proximal tubular defects - Fanconi syndrome
  * 24-hour urinary magnesium low in GI losses, high in renal losses:
	* Urine Mg²⁺ of ≥10 to 30 mg/day in a patient with normal renal function is indicative of excessive renal wasting
	* Urine Mg²⁺ <10 mg/day is suggestive of either inadequate gastrointestinal absorption or excessive losses
* **Increased cellular uptake** - insulin or beta-agonist therapy
* **Reduced intake** - alcoholism, starvation, refeeding syndrome, use of proton pump inhibitors (PPIs):
  * Chronic PPI use can cause PPI-induced inhibition of the transient receptor potential melastatin-6 and 7 (TRPM6/7) channels that are critical for Mg²⁺ absorption

**Hypomagnesemia with hypocalcemia:**
* Deposition - acute pancreatitis
* Vitamin D deficiency
* Hungry bone syndrome

**Hypomagnesemia with normal potassium and calcium:**
* Rare genetic tubular defects

**Bottom Line:** Hypomagnesemia often presents alongside hypokalemia and hypocalcemia, which may be refractory until magnesium is repleted. Etiologies include GI losses, renal wasting (particularly with diuretics), reduced intake (alcoholism, malnutrition), inadequate absorption (chronic PPI use), and increased cellular uptake. Distinguishing renal from non-renal causes through urinary magnesium measurement guides management.

### 6.1.2. CLINICAL FEATURES

**Early symptoms:**
* Nausea and anorexia
* Muscle cramps and weakness
* Weight loss
* Lethargy

**Late manifestations:**
* Convulsions and tetany
* Stridor and muscle spasm
* Hypertension
* Pulmonary edema
* PR and QT prolongation
* ST depression and flattened T waves
* Trousseau's and Chvostek's signs
* Hypocalcemia due to reduced PTH production and increased renal excretion

**Bottom Line:** Hypomagnesemia manifests initially with non-specific symptoms like fatigue, anorexia, and muscle cramps, progressing to more severe neurological and cardiovascular features including tetany, seizures, QT prolongation, and arrhythmias. Physical examination may reveal positive Trousseau's and Chvostek's signs similar to hypocalcemia, which often coexists.

### 6.1.3. MANAGEMENT

* **Mild cases** - dietary correction:
  * Oral repletion can be accomplished with 60 to 90 mEq/day magnesium oxide
* **Symptomatic/Severe** - IV 1-2 g/hr over 3-6 hours with further repletion guided by serum magnesium levels:
  * ⚠️ With the exception of pulseless torsades de points, more rapid repletion should not be considered given the theoretical risk of inducing cardiac conduction abnormalities
* Patients should also have deep tendon reflexes (DTR) assessed frequently during ongoing repletion, as loss of DTR may reflect a transition to hypermagnesemia

**Bottom Line:** Magnesium replacement is guided by severity - oral supplementation for mild cases and IV therapy for symptomatic or severe deficiency. Infusion rate should not exceed 1-2 g/hr except in pulseless torsades de points. Regular assessment of deep tendon reflexes helps monitor for iatrogenic hypermagnesemia. Addressing the underlying cause is essential for preventing recurrence.

## 6.2. HYPERMAGNESEMIA

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Body Fluids and Electrolytes|Part A: Hypermagnesemia]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B: Hypermagnesemia Management]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C: Magnesium Patient Safety]]

### 6.2.1. ETIOLOGY

* **Hypermagnesemia** - neuromuscular blockade (weakness, coma, areflexia, bradycardia, heart block), vasodilation (hypotension)
* Hypermagnesemia defined as serum [Mg²⁺] >2.5 mg/dL
* 💡 As Mg²⁺ acts as an endogenous Ca²⁺ antagonist, increasing serum [Mg²⁺] will mimic hypocalcemia
* Main causes are a subset of causes of hyperkalemia:
  * **Reduced excretion** - renal impairment
  * **Release of intracellular magnesium** - rhabdomyolysis, tumor lysis, trauma, burns
  * **Increased intake** - Mg²⁺-containing antacids, laxatives, iatrogenic administration of IV Mg²⁺
* → Look at potassium and calcium levels for further workup

**Bottom Line:** Hypermagnesemia typically occurs in the setting of renal insufficiency with excessive intake (from antacids, laxatives, or iatrogenic IV administration) or cellular release during tissue destruction syndromes. Its clinical manifestations reflect magnesium's role as a physiologic calcium antagonist, with neuromuscular and cardiovascular effects predominating.

### 6.2.2. CLINICAL FEATURES

**Early symptoms:**
* Vomiting and headache
* Diarrhea
* Muscle weakness (above 4 mmol/L)
* Reduced muscle tone

**Late manifestations:**
* Respiratory depression
* Hypotension
* Bradycardia
* Wide QRS complex
* Cardiac arrest (above 10 mmol/L)

**Bottom Line:** Hypermagnesemia causes a progression of symptoms from GI disturbance and mild muscle weakness at lower levels to more serious neuromuscular inhibition, respiratory depression, cardiac conduction abnormalities, and severe hypotension at higher levels. Cardiac arrest may occur at levels exceeding 10 mmol/L.

### 6.2.3. MANAGEMENT

* **Severe hypermagnesemia** - when neuromuscular or cardiac toxicity is observed:
  * IV calcium (5 to 10 mEq) should be used to antagonize Mg²⁺ as a temporizing measure, in addition to ongoing supportive care
  * Consider dialysis for severe or refractory cases
* **All cases:**
  * Stop all magnesium supplementation
  * Focus care on promoting maximal renal excretion, either by volume expansion, administering loop diuretics, or both

**Bottom Line:** Management of hypermagnesemia focuses on discontinuing magnesium intake, enhancing renal excretion through volume expansion and diuretics, and administering IV calcium to antagonize magnesium's effects in severe cases. Dialysis is reserved for critical or refractory hypermagnesemia, particularly in patients with renal failure.
