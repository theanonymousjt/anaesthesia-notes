# 1. HEPATIC FUNCTION IN HEALTH

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|Part A: Liver Physiology]] • [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B: Perioperative Medicine]] • [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C: Medical Knowledge]]

**The liver is the largest internal organ and the body's metabolic headquarters, playing crucial roles in metabolism, synthesis, detoxification, and immune function.**

# 2. LIVER ANATOMY AND BLOOD SUPPLY

## 2.1. BASIC STRUCTURE

* **Weight:** 1.5 kg or about 2% of total body weight in adults
* **Functional unit:** The lobule - approximately 1 mm × 2 mm structure
* **Lobule organization:**
  * Plates of hepatocytes arranged radially around central vein
  * Afferent blood supply (portal vein and hepatic arteriole) enters at periphery
  * Bile flows from hepatocytes → canaliculi → bile ducts at lobule periphery

## 2.2. DUAL BLOOD SUPPLY SYSTEM

* **Total hepatic blood flow:** Approximately 25% of cardiac output
* **Portal vein contribution:**
  * Supplies 75% of hepatic blood flow (~1 L/min)
  * Drains gastrointestinal tract
  * Lower oxygen content
* **Hepatic artery contribution:**
  * Supplies 25% of hepatic blood flow
  * Higher oxygen content
  * Due to higher oxygen content, each vessel provides roughly 50% of hepatic oxygen supply

## 2.3. HEPATIC CIRCULATION PHYSIOLOGY

* **Portal vein pressure:** 8-10 mmHg (normal)
* **Hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG):** 3-5 mmHg (normal)

  * > 5 mmHg = abnormal and defines portal hypertension

* **Sympathetic innervation:** T3-T11 controls hepatic venous resistance
* **Hepatic arterial buffer response:** Reciprocal relationship between portal and arterial flow
  * When portal flow decreases, hepatic artery can increase flow up to 100%
  * Maintains hepatic oxygen delivery

## 2.4. HEPATIC MICROCIRCULATION

**Zone 1 (Periportal):**
* Receives oxygen-rich blood from portal vein and hepatic artery
* Less susceptible to hypoxic injury

**Zone 2 (Intermediate):**
* Intermediate oxygen content
* Transitional metabolic characteristics

**Zone 3 (Pericentral):**
* Oxygen-poor blood around central vein
* Greater quantity of cytochrome P450 enzymes
* Site of anaerobic metabolism
* Most susceptible to hypoxia and reactive metabolic intermediates

## 2.5. STORAGE FUNCTIONS

* **Blood reservoir:** Can store up to 1 L of blood, capable of accepting or releasing blood based on volume status
* **Vitamin storage:**
  * Vitamin B12: 1-year supply
  * Vitamin D: 3-month supply  
  * Vitamin A: 10-month supply
* **Iron storage:** Apoferritin system serves as blood iron buffer

## 2.6. KUPFFER CELLS AND IMMUNE FUNCTION

* Reticuloendothelial cells lining venous sinusoids
* Macrophages that phagocytize bacteria entering from intestines
* <1% of bacteria entering liver pass through to systemic circulation
* Critical component of hepatic immune surveillance

**Bottom Line:** The liver receives 25% of cardiac output via dual blood supply (portal vein 75%, hepatic artery 25%), with complex zonal organization that determines susceptibility to injury and metabolic function.

# 3. METABOLIC FUNCTIONS

## 3.1. GLUCOSE HOMEOSTASIS

* **Glucose buffer function:** Primary mechanism for blood glucose regulation
* **Glycogen storage:** Stores glucose as glycogen during fed state
* **Gluconeogenesis:** Synthesizes glucose from:
  * Amino acids
  * Triglycerides (glycerol backbone)
  * Other carbohydrates (fructose, galactose)
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl:** In liver dysfunction, blood glucose can rise several-fold higher than normal postprandial levels

## 3.2. LIPID METABOLISM

* **Synthesis:**
  * Fat, cholesterol, phospholipids, and lipoproteins
  * Most fat synthesis from protein and carbohydrates occurs in liver
* **Metabolism:**
  * Converts fatty acids to acetyl coenzyme A (CoA)
  * Acetyl-CoA enters citric acid cycle for energy liberation
  * Excess acetyl-CoA packaged as acetoacetic acid for peripheral use
* **Cholesterol handling:**
  * Majority converted to bile salts and secreted in bile
  * Remainder distributed for cellular membrane formation

## 3.3. PROTEIN METABOLISM

* **Synthesis capacity:** 15-50 g protein per day
* **Albumin production:**
  * Major protein synthesized by liver
  * Primary determinant of plasma oncotic pressure
  * 3-week half-life
* **Other plasma proteins:** All except γ-globulins (produced by plasma cells)
* **Amino acid metabolism:**
  * Synthesizes nonessential amino acids from keto acids
  * Deamination of amino acids for energy production
  * Conversion of amino acids to carbohydrates or fats

## 3.4. AMMONIA DETOXIFICATION

* **Ammonia sources:**
  * Amino acid deamination
  * Intestinal bacterial production
* **Urea cycle:** Liver removes toxic ammonia by forming urea
* ⚠️ **Clinical Significance:** Hepatic dysfunction leads to hyperammonemia and encephalopathy

**Bottom Line:** The liver serves as the body's central metabolic processor, maintaining glucose homeostasis, synthesizing essential proteins including albumin, metabolizing lipids, and detoxifying ammonia through the urea cycle.

# 4. PROTEIN SYNTHESIS AND HEMOSTASIS

## 4.1. COAGULATION FACTOR SYNTHESIS

* **Liver-synthesized factors:** All except:
  * Factor III (tissue thromboplastin)
  * Factor IV (calcium) 
  * Factor VIII (von Willebrand factor)
* **Vitamin K-dependent factors:** II (prothrombin), VII, IX, and X
* **Factor clearance:** Liver also clears activated coagulation factors

## 4.2. HEMOSTATIC BALANCE IN HEALTH

* **Procoagulant factors:** Synthesized by liver
* **Anticoagulant factors:** Also synthesized by liver (protein C, protein S, antithrombin III)
* **Dynamic equilibrium:** Balance between proand anticoagulant forces determines effective thrombin generation
* 💡 **Key Concept:** Normal hemostasis depends on balanced production of both procoagulant and anticoagulant factors

**Bottom Line:** The liver synthesizes virtually all coagulation factors and maintains hemostatic balance through coordinated production of both procoagulant and anticoagulant proteins.

# 5. BILE PRODUCTION AND ENTEROHEPATIC CIRCULATION

## 5.1. BILE PRODUCTION

* **Daily production:** ~500 mL bile produced by hepatocytes
* **Storage:** Gallbladder concentrates and stores 35-50 mL bile
* **Composition:**
  * Bile salts (detergent function)
  * Bilirubin
  * Cholesterol

## 5.2. BILE FLOW REGULATION

* **Between meals:** High pressure in sphincter of Oddi diverts bile to gallbladder
* **During digestion:** Fat in duodenum stimulates cholecystokinin release
* **Cholecystokinin effects:**
  * Reaches gallbladder via circulation
  * Stimulates gallbladder contraction
  * Promotes bile release into duodenum

## 5.3. ENTEROHEPATIC CIRCULATION

* **Bile salt function:** Act as detergents, solubilizing fat into absorbable micelles
* **Recirculation:** Bile salts return to liver via portal vein
* **Clinical importance:** 
  * Essential for fat absorption
  * Cholestasis can result in steatorrhea and vitamin K deficiency

**Bottom Line:** The liver produces 500 mL bile daily, which is essential for fat absorption through its detergent action and undergoes efficient enterohepatic recirculation.

# 6. LIVER REGENERATION

## 6.1. REGENERATIVE CAPACITY

* **Extent:** Up to two-thirds of liver can be removed with complete regeneration
* **Timeline:** Regeneration occurs within weeks
* **Unique capability:** Liver has unparalleled regenerative capacity among solid organs

## 6.2. GROWTH FACTORS AND REGULATION

**Stimulatory factors:**
* Hepatocyte growth factor (produced by mesenchymal cells)
* Epidermal growth factor (EGF)
* Cytokines: TNF, interleukin-6

**Inhibitory factors:**
* Growth factor-β (inhibits hepatocyte proliferation)
* Halts regenerative process based on liver-to-body weight ratio

## 6.3. FACTORS AFFECTING REGENERATION

* **Inflammation:** Viral infections impair regeneration
* **Ratio sensing:** Process appears related to achieving appropriate liver-to-body weight ratio
* **Clinical relevance:** Understanding regeneration important for hepatic resection planning

**Bottom Line:** The liver possesses unique regenerative capacity allowing recovery from major tissue loss, regulated by growth factors and inhibited when appropriate liver mass is restored.

# 8. LABORATORY TESTS AND LIVER FUNCTION ASSESSMENT

## 8.1. TERMINOLOGY AND CLASSIFICATION

* **"Liver function tests" (LFTs):** Collective term for liver-related laboratory studies
* ⚠️ **Important distinction:** Many LFTs (AST, ALT) are markers of cellular injury, not function
* **True function tests:** Assess synthetic capacity (albumin, PT/INR)

## 8.2. MARKERS OF HEPATOCELLULAR INJURY

**Aspartate Aminotransferase (AST):**
* Formerly SGOT (serum glutamic oxaloacetic transaminase)
* Present in liver, heart, skeletal muscle, kidney, brain
* ⚠️ **Not liver-specific:** Elevations can be from non-hepatic sources

**Alanine Aminotransferase (ALT):**
* Formerly SGPT (serum glutamic pyruvic transaminase)  
* **Primarily liver-specific**
* More specific marker of hepatocellular injury than AST

## 8.3. INTERPRETATION OF TRANSAMINASE LEVELS

**Mild elevation (several-fold):**
* Fatty liver
* Chronic infections

**Moderate elevation:**
* Acute hepatitis

**Severe elevation (>50× normal):**
* Acute hepatic necrosis

**AST/ALT ratio clinical significance:**
* **>2:** Suggests alcoholic liver disease
* **<1:** Suggests viral hepatitis

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Declining enzyme levels may indicate either recovery OR lack of surviving hepatocytes - absolute levels must be interpreted in context.

## 8.4. MARKERS OF CHOLESTASIS AND BILE FLOW OBSTRUCTION

**Alkaline Phosphatase (AP):**
* Present in liver, bone, kidney, intestines, placenta, leukocytes
* Hepatic AP concentrated in bile canaliculi microvilli
* Elevated disproportionately to AST/ALT in bile flow obstruction

**5'-Nucleotidase (5'-NT):**
* Found in many tissues but elevations highly specific for hepatobiliary obstruction
* Reflects detergent action of bile salts on plasma membranes
* **Most specific test** for determining if elevated AP is hepatic in origin

**γ-Glutamyl Transferase (GGT):**
* Most sensitive indicator of biliary tract disease
* Less specific than 5'-NT
* Largely replaced by 5'-NT in clinical practice

## 8.5. BILIRUBIN ASSESSMENT

**Van den Bergh Reaction Classification:**
* **Indirect-reacting (unconjugated) bilirubin:** Lipid-soluble
* **Direct-reacting (conjugated) bilirubin:** Water-soluble

**Unconjugated bilirubin elevation indicates:**
* Excess bilirubin production (hemolysis)
* Decreased hepatic uptake/conjugation

**Conjugated bilirubin elevation indicates:**
* Impaired intrahepatic excretion
* Extrahepatic biliary obstruction

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Even with complete biliary obstruction, bilirubin rarely exceeds 35 mg/dL due to renal excretion of conjugated bilirubin.

## 8.6. TESTS OF HEPATIC SYNTHETIC FUNCTION

**Serum Albumin:**
* **Half-life:** 3 weeks
* **Limitation:** Not reliable for acute liver disease due to long half-life
* **Other causes of low albumin:** Protein losses, nephrotic syndrome, burns, malnutrition

**Prothrombin Time (PT) and International Normalized Ratio (INR):**
* **Sensitive indicators** of acute hepatic dysfunction
* Based on short half-life of factor VII
* **Vitamin K dependence:** PT prolonged in cholestasis despite preserved synthetic function
* **Other causes of prolonged PT/INR:**
  * Congenital factor deficiencies
  * Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)
  * Warfarin therapy
  * Factor Xa inhibitors (rivaroxaban, apixaban)

## 8.7. SPECIALIZED FUNCTION TESTS

**Indocyanine Green (ICG) Elimination:**
* Estimates hepatic blood flow and hepatocellular function
* High extraction ratio (>70%)
* Primarily research application in US

**MEGX Test:**
* Measures lidocaine conversion to monoethylglycinexylidide
* Assesses hepatic demethylation capacity
* Limited to research applications

## 8.8. DIAGNOSTIC SEROLOGY AND MARKERS

**Viral Hepatitis Serologies:**
* Anti-HAV IgM, HBsAg, anti-HBc IgM, anti-HEV, anti-HCV

**Disease-Specific Markers:**
* Antimitochondrial antibodies (primary biliary cirrhosis)
* Ceruloplasmin (Wilson disease)
* Ferritin (hemochromatosis)
* α1-antitrypsin (α1-antitrypsin deficiency)
* α-fetoprotein (hepatocellular carcinoma)

**Serum Ammonia:**
* Useful for monitoring hepatic encephalopathy
* Should be arterial sample when possible for accuracy

**Bottom Line:** Liver assessment requires understanding the distinction between injury markers (AST/ALT) and function tests (albumin, PT/INR), with specific patterns helping differentiate hepatocellular vs. cholestatic disease.

# 9. HEPATOBILIARY IMAGING MODALITIES

## 9.1. PLAIN RADIOGRAPHY

**Limited applications:**
* Calcified gallstones
* Chronic calcific pancreatitis
* Gas-containing liver abscesses
* Portal venous gas
* Emphysematous cholecystitis

## 9.2. ULTRASONOGRAPHY

**Primary screening test** for hepatic and biliary disease  
**Advantages:**
* Method of choice for detecting gallstones
* Excellent for detecting ascites
* Can identify portal or hepatic vein thrombosis
* Non-invasive and readily available

**Limitations:**
* Operator-dependent
* Cannot penetrate bone or bowel gas
* Limited by patient body habitus

## 9.3. COMPUTED TOMOGRAPHY (CT) SCANNING

**Supplements ultrasonography** with additional capabilities:
* Superior assessment of liver texture and parenchymal disease
* Excellent visualization of mass lesions
* Better resolution than ultrasonography
* Less operator-dependent
* **CT-guided procedures:** Enables biopsy of lesions

**Disadvantages:**
* Radiation exposure
* Higher cost than ultrasonography
* Requires contrast for optimal imaging

## 9.4. MAGNETIC RESONANCE IMAGING (MRI)

**Increasingly preferred** for hepatobiliary assessment:
* **Superior to CT** for malignant focal liver lesions
* Excellent for diffuse liver disease assessment
* **MR cholangiopancreatography (MRCP):** Non-invasive biliary imaging

**Advantages:**
* No radiation exposure
* Avoids contrast-induced nephropathy risk
* Superior soft tissue contrast

**Disadvantages:**
* Requires breath-hold sequences
* May need sedation/anesthesia for uncooperative patients
* Higher cost and longer acquisition times

## 9.5. RADIOISOTOPE SCANNING

**Limited current use:**
* Largely replaced by CT scanning
* Still used for suspected acute cholecystitis
* **HIDA scan interpretation:**
  * Gallbladder visualization = patent cystic duct
  * Biliary tree without gallbladder = cystic duct obstruction/cholecystitis

## 9.6. INVASIVE IMAGING PROCEDURES

**Percutaneous Transhepatic Cholangiography (THC):**
* Direct contrast injection into bile ducts
* **Uses:**
  * Determine site and cause of biliary obstruction
  * Assess cholangiocarcinoma resectability
  * Therapeutic interventions (balloon dilation, stent placement)

**Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography (ERCP):**
* Endoscopic visualization of ampulla of Vater
* Selective contrast injection into pancreatic and bile ducts
* **Advantages over THC:**
  * Does not require dilated biliary tree
  * Higher success rate
* **Therapeutic capabilities:**
  * Sphincterotomy and stone extraction
  * Biopsy and brushings
  * Balloon dilation and stent insertion

**Bottom Line:** Imaging selection depends on clinical question - ultrasonography for screening, CT/MRI for detailed assessment, and ERCP for therapeutic intervention with diagnostic capability.

# 10. LIVER BIOPSY TECHNIQUES

## 10.1. INDICATIONS AND ROLE

* **Primary method** for determining cause of liver damage:
  * Necrosis vs. inflammation vs. steatosis vs. fibrosis
* Remains important despite advances in non-invasive assessment
* **Essential for:**
  * Distinguishing NASH from simple steatosis
  * Staging of fibrosis
  * Diagnosis of specific conditions (PBC, autoimmune hepatitis)

## 10.2. CONTRAINDICATIONS AND CONSIDERATIONS

**Relative contraindications:**
* **Coagulopathy:** Bleeding risk assessment required
* **Thrombocytopenia:** Platelet count considerations
* **Ascites:** May complicate procedure

## 10.3. ALTERNATIVE APPROACHES

**Transjugular Liver Biopsy:**
* Performed when coagulopathy or thrombocytopenia present
* Access via jugular vein to hepatic vein
* Allows biopsy despite bleeding risk
* Can combine with hepatic venous pressure measurement

**Bottom Line:** Liver biopsy remains the gold standard for determining the nature and extent of liver damage, with transjugular approach available for high bleeding risk patients.

# 12. ACUTE LIVER FAILURE

## 12.1. DEFINITION AND CLASSIFICATION

**Acute Liver Failure (ALF):** Previously termed "fulminant hepatic failure"
* **Diagnostic criteria:**
  * Encephalopathy + coagulopathy (INR ≥1.5)
  * No previous history of liver disease
  * Illness duration <26 weeks
* **Incidence:** ~2,000 cases per year in United States

## 12.2. ETIOLOGY AND OUTCOMES

**Leading causes in descending order:**
1. **Drug-related toxicity (>50% of cases):**
   * Acetaminophen (>80% of drug-related cases)
2. **Idiopathic**
3. **Acute viral hepatitis**
4. **Autoimmune**
5. **Ischemic injury**

**Natural History in US:**
* **Spontaneous recovery:** ~45%
* **Liver transplantation:** ~25%
* **Death without transplantation:** ~30%

## 12.3. PROGNOSTIC FACTORS BY ETIOLOGY

**Better prognosis:**
* Acetaminophen overdose
* Ischemic injury  
* Hepatitis A

**Poorer prognosis:**
* Non-acetaminophen drug-induced liver injury
* Acute hepatitis B
* Wilson disease
* Autoimmune hepatitis

## 12.4. CLINICAL PRESENTATION AND DIAGNOSIS

**Essential History:**
* Potential infectious or toxic exposures
* Detailed medication review (including herbal/nutritional supplements)
* Timeline of symptom development

**Physical Examination:**
* **Key finding:** Encephalopathy (required for diagnosis)
* May otherwise be unrevealing
* ⚠️ **Important:** Evidence of chronic liver disease should NOT be present

**Laboratory Studies:**

## 12.5. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND COMPLICATIONS

**Cerebral Edema and Intracranial Hypertension:**
* Most serious complication and often proximate cause of death
* **Incidence correlates with encephalopathy grade:**
  * Stage I-II: Nearly negligible
  * Stage III: 25-35%
  * Stage IV: 75%

**Mechanisms of cerebral edema:**
* **Hyperammonemia:** Primary contributor
  * Brain lacks urea cycle capacity
  * Astrocytes synthesize glutamine from ammonia + glutamate
  * Glutamine is osmotically active → astrocyte swelling
* **Additional factors:**
  * Systemic inflammatory response
  * Loss of cerebral autoregulation → cerebral hyperemia

![Encephalopathy Grading](https://c7cfd6b7-3c34-4f3a-886b-08d1a3707c3a.ngrok-free.app/api/serve-image/c0654f4a-23ef-4c54-bd6b-e7a8f8d94e9d)

## 12.6. MANAGEMENT OF CEREBRAL EDEMA

**General Measures:**
* 30-degree head elevation
* Neutral head position (optimize venous return)
* Muscle relaxants after intubation (prevent ICP spikes from coughing/bucking)

**Osmotic Therapy:**
* **Mannitol 1 g/kg IV bolus**
  * May repeat if serum osmolarity <320 mOsm/kg
  * Limited utility in renal compromise
* **Hypertonic saline:**
  * Target serum sodium 145-155 mEq/L
  * Alternative when mannitol contraindicated

**Hyperventilation:**
* **Current recommendation:** Maintain normocarbia
* Reserve hyperventilation for acute ICP crises only
* **Target PCO2 for crisis:** 30-35 mmHg
* No evidence for benefit from chronic hyperventilation

**Metabolic Management:**
* **Barbiturates:** Decrease cerebral metabolism but may cause hypotension
* **Ammonia reduction strategies:**
  * ⚠️ **Note:** Limited evidence for lactulose/rifaximin in ALF setting
  * **Neomycin:** Specifically contraindicated (nephrotoxicity risk)
  * **Renal replacement therapy:** Effective for ammonia reduction, associated with reduced mortality

## 12.7. ICP MONITORING AND MANAGEMENT PROTOCOL

**Indications for ICP monitoring:**
* Stage III-IV encephalopathy
* No randomized trials support this practice
* Requires aggressive coagulopathy correction

![ICP Management Protocol](https://c7cfd6b7-3c34-4f3a-886b-08d1a3707c3a.ngrok-free.app/api/serve-image/7f25ab8e-7ef5-4e8b-af1d-be54a4c0e12b)

**Target Parameters:**
* **ICP:** <20 mmHg
* **Cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP):** 60-80 mmHg (CPP = MAP - ICP)

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** ICP monitoring protocols using rFVIIa for coagulopathy correction have achieved 95% response rate for ICP crises with no deaths from isolated cerebral edema.

## 12.8. PROGNOSTIC SCORING SYSTEMS

**King's College Hospital Criteria:**  
![King's College Criteria](https://c7cfd6b7-3c34-4f3a-886b-08d1a3707c3a.ngrok-free.app/api/serve-image/4e5a5f0e-7c5f-4bc7-85a1-5e3d8e40c6dd)

**Alternative Scoring Systems:**
* **MELD score**
* **Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA)**  
* **APACHE II**
* **Modifications:** Serum lactate levels may improve King's College criteria performance

⚠️ **Limitation:** Negative predictive value <50% for non-acetaminophen ALF in King's College criteria.

## 12.9. COAGULATION MANAGEMENT

**Key Principles:**
* ⚠️ **Spontaneous bleeding uncommon** despite abnormal INR
* **Conventional thresholds:**
  * Platelet count >50,000/mm³ for procedures
  * INR <1.5 for procedures
* **Modern understanding:** Conventional tests (INR, platelet count) poorly predict bleeding risk
* **Viscoelastic testing:** More accurate predictor of bleeding complications
* **rFVIIa or prothrombin complex concentrate:** Used for resistant INR or to avoid fluid overload
  * ⚠️ **Contraindication:** Hypercoagulable states (pregnancy, Budd-Chiari syndrome)

## 12.10. CIRCULATORY MANAGEMENT

**Causes of hypotension in ALF:**
* Gastrointestinal losses
* Poor oral intake
* Myocardial dysfunction
* Decreased arterial tone (liver necrosis progression)

**Assessment priorities:**
1. Volume status evaluation
2. Cardiac function assessment
3. Consider inotropes/vasopressors after volume optimization

**Vasopressor choices:**
* **Norepinephrine or dopamine:** Based on sepsis guidelines
* **Arginine vasopressin (AVP) and analogues:**
  * Previously avoided due to ICP concerns
  * Recent evidence: Increase cerebral blood flow without increasing ICP
  * Safe and effective option

**Bottom Line:** ALF is a rare but life-threatening condition requiring intensive monitoring and management of cerebral edema, coagulopathy, and circulatory failure, with liver transplantation as definitive therapy for appropriate candidates.

# 13. ACUTE VIRAL HEPATITIS

## 13.1. MAJOR HEPATITIS VIRUSES OVERVIEW

**Five identified viral hepatitides:** A, B, C, D (delta), and E
* **Vaccine available:** HAV and HBV
* **Declining incidence:** HAV and HBV due to vaccination
* **No vaccine:** HCV (declining due to better screening and universal precautions)
* **HDV:** Requires HBV helper function
* **HEV:** Epidemic potential in areas with poor sanitation

## 13.2. CLINICAL PRESENTATION

**Symptoms (often nonspecific):**
* Fatigue, poor appetite, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain
* Many infections subclinical

**Signs:**
* **Jaundice**
* **Serum-sickness-type presentation:**
  * Fever, arthralgia/arthritis, rash
  * Due to circulating hepatitis antigen-antibody complexes

**Incubation periods:** Several weeks to months
* ⚠️ **Perioperative consideration:** Patients may undergo surgery without awareness of illness
* **Differential diagnosis:** Consider viral hepatitis with any postoperative liver injury

## 13.3. HEPATITIS A VIRUS (HAV)

**Characteristics:**
* **Transmission:** Fecal-oral route, contaminated food/water
* **Picornavirus:** RNA virus
* **Clinical spectrum:** Asymptomatic (especially children) to ALF (<1%)
* **ALF outcomes:** 69% spontaneous recovery rate (best among hepatitis viruses)
* **No chronic disease state**

## 13.4. HEPATITIS B VIRUS (HBV)

**Characteristics:**
* **DNA virus**
* **Transmission:** Parenteral, cutaneous, or mucosal exposure to infected blood/body fluids
* **HBsAg (surface antigen):** Hallmark of active infection
  * Appears 1-10 weeks after exposure
  * Persistence >6 months = chronic infection

**Clinical outcomes:**
* **ALF:** <1% of acute infections
* **ALF survival:** 20% spontaneous recovery (poorer than HAV)
* **Chronic infection:** 2-5% of infected adults

## 13.5. HEPATITIS D VIRUS (HDV)

**Requirements:**
* **Depends on HBV** for virion assembly
* **Two patterns:**
  * Coinfection with acute HBV
  * Superinfection on chronic HBV
* **Prevalence:** ~5% of chronic HBV patients
* **Severity:** Both types cause severe illness, may cause ALF

## 13.6. HEPATITIS C VIRUS (HCV)

**Historical significance:**
* Previously "non-A, non-B hepatitis" until 1989 identification
* **Transmission:** Primarily parenteral
* **Blood products:** Risk eliminated by serologic screening
* **Most common risk factor:** Parenteral drug use

**Clinical significance:**
* **High chronicity rate:** 50-85% progress to chronic disease
* **Cirrhosis risk:** 5-25% over 25-30 years
* **No ALF association** in acute phase

**Bottom Line:** Viral hepatitis remains an important cause of acute liver injury, with HAV and HBV preventable by vaccination, while HCV has high progression to chronic disease but effective treatment now available.

# 14. ALCOHOLIC HEPATITIS

## 14.1. DEFINITION AND CLINICAL PRESENTATION

**Alcoholic hepatitis syndrome:**
* Development of jaundice and liver dysfunction in setting of heavy alcohol use
* **Encephalopathy presence:** Indicates severe disease with poorer prognosis

## 14.2. LABORATORY CHARACTERISTICS

**Transaminase pattern:**
* **Moderate elevations:** Typically <300 IU/mL
* **AST > ALT:** Classic pattern
* **AST:ALT ratio >2:** Present in ~70% of patients with alcoholic hepatitis

**Prognostic indicators:**
* ⚠️ **Elevated serum creatinine:** Particularly ominous
* May indicate impending hepatorenal syndrome

## 14.3. DIAGNOSTIC CONSIDERATIONS

**Alcohol use history:** Supportive but not definitive
* **Coexisting disease:** Up to 20% may have additional liver pathology
* **Liver biopsy:** Not required for diagnosis but helpful to exclude other causes

## 14.4. TREATMENT PRINCIPLES

**Primary intervention:**
* **Abstinence:** Key component of therapy
* Essential for any potential recovery

**Severe alcoholic hepatitis management:**
* **Nutritional therapy:** Addresses protein-calorie malnutrition and vitamin/mineral deficiencies
* **Consider medical therapy** for severe cases

**Bottom Line:** Alcoholic hepatitis requires careful assessment for severity, with abstinence as the cornerstone of treatment and attention to nutritional deficiencies common in this population.

# 15. DRUG-INDUCED LIVER INJURY (DILI)

## 15.1. CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE AND IMPACT

**DILI importance:**
* Significant cause of morbidity and mortality
* Often diagnosis of exclusion
* **Pharmaceutical industry impact:** Most common reason for regulatory actions
  * Failure of drug approval
  * Market removal
  * Indication restrictions

**ALF statistics:**
* **Non-acetaminophen DILI:** 11-13% of ALF cases
* **Survival with supportive care:** 20% (poorer than average)

## 15.2. MECHANISMS OF HEPATOTOXICITY

**Liver vulnerability:**
* Central role in drug metabolism
* Site of toxic metabolite generation

**Injury mechanisms:**
1. **Direct hepatotoxicity**
2. **Toxic metabolite generation:** Phase I metabolism via cytochrome P450

**Cellular injury pathways:**
* **Cell stress:**
  * Glutathione depletion
  * Reactive metabolite binding to intracellular structures
* **Mitochondrial injury:**
  * Uncoupling of mitochondrial respiration
  * ATP depletion
  * Reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation
* **Immune-mediated mechanisms:**
  * Reactive metabolites bind to cellular structures
  * Create antigenic entities
  * Antibody formation against modified cell structures

## 15.3. HALOTHANE HEPATITIS: HISTORICAL PERSPECTIVE AND LESSONS

**Background:**
* **Introduction:** 1956 - widely used due to non-flammability, potency, patient tolerance
* **Recognition:** Reports of postoperative liver injury appeared shortly after introduction
* **Scale:** >300 cases reported by 1963

**National Halothane Study findings:**
* **Study scope:** 856,000 anesthetics at 34 centers
* **Halothane cases:** ~255,000 exposures
* **Fatal hepatic necrosis:** 82 cases identified
* **Identifiable causes:** 63 cases had clinical factors explaining injury
* **Unexplained cases:** 19 (14 had received halothane)
* **Calculated incidence:** 1 in 35,000 (if association confirmed)

**Clinical manifestations:**  
**Mild form (up to 20% incidence):**
* Self-limited enzyme elevations
* No evidence of liver failure
* **Proposed mechanism:** Halothane degradation products + hypoxia from hepatic oxygen supply-demand imbalance

**Severe form (rare):**
* **Fulminant hepatitis:** Life-threatening
* **Evidence for immune mechanism:**
  * Association with repeated halothane exposure
  * Rash and eosinophilia
  * IgG antibodies against trifluoroacetyl (TFA)-modified liver proteins

## 15.4. MODERN VOLATILE ANESTHETIC SAFETY

**TFA metabolite production correlation:**
* **Halothane:** 20% metabolized (highest TFA production)
* **Enflurane:** 2%
* **Isoflurane:** 0.2%
* **Desflurane:** 0.01%

**Hepatitis risk correlation:**
* Risk appears to correlate with extent of metabolism
* Other agents (enflurane, isoflurane, desflurane) associated with very rare hepatitis cases

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Animal studies confirm tissue trifluoroacetylation correlates with metabolism extent: halothane >> enflurane > isoflurane > desflurane.

**Bottom Line:** DILI is a significant clinical problem often related to metabolite toxicity, with halothane hepatitis providing important lessons about immune-mediated liver injury from repeated anesthetic exposure.

## 16.1. OVERVIEW AND INCIDENCE

**Liver abnormalities in pregnancy:** 3-5% of pregnancies
* **Common causes:** Five acute, pregnancy-specific conditions
* **Differential diagnosis:** Must distinguish from underlying hepatic/biliary disease

## 16.2. FIVE PREGNANCY-RELATED CONDITIONS

1. **Hyperemesis gravidarum**
2. **Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy**
3. **Preeclampsia**
4. **HELLP syndrome**
5. **Acute fatty liver of pregnancy (AFLP)**

## 16.3. HYPEREMESIS GRAVIDARUM

**Timing:** First trimester  
**Clinical features:**
* Vomiting severe enough to warrant IV hydration
* **Risk factors:** Hyperthyroidism, molar pregnancy, multiple pregnancies

**Laboratory findings:**
* **Liver enzymes:** Elevated in ~50% (up to 20-fold)
* **Bilirubin:** Usually normal or minimally elevated
* **Differential diagnosis:** Exclude viral hepatitis, drug toxicity

**Management:**
* Primarily supportive care
* Usually resolves by second trimester

## 16.4. INTRAHEPATIC CHOLESTASIS OF PREGNANCY

**Timing:** Second to third trimester  
**Pathophysiology:** Interference with bile acid transport across canalicular membrane

**Clinical presentation:**
* **Primary symptom:** Pruritus
* **Laboratory findings:**
  * Modest bilirubin increase (usually <5 mg/dL)
  * Aminotransferases up to 20-fold elevation
  * **Serum bile acids:** Up to 100-fold elevation

**Management:**
* Primarily supportive (pruritus relief)
* **⚠️ Fetal concerns:**
  * Chronic placental insufficiency
  * Premature labor
  * Sudden fetal death
* **Pregnancy classification:** High risk

## 16.5. PREECLAMPSIA AND HELLP SYNDROME

**Preeclampsia triad:**
* Hypertension
* Edema  
* Proteinuria
* **Severe preeclampsia:** Elevated aminotransferases

**HELLP Syndrome (20% of severe preeclamptics):**
* **H:** Hemolysis (microangiopathic)
* **EL:** Elevated Liver enzymes
* **LP:** Low Platelets

**Pathophysiology:**
* Vascular endothelial injury
* Fibrin deposition and platelet consumption
* Hepatic infarction and hemorrhage

**Laboratory findings:**
* **Aminotransferases:** 10-20-fold elevation
* **Bilirubin:** Modest increases
* **Peripheral smear:** Schistocytes and burr cells
* **Platelet severity grading:**
  * Mild: 100,000-150,000/mm³
  * Moderate: 50,000-100,000/mm³
  * Severe: <50,000/mm³

**Complications and management:**
* **Imaging:** CT preferred for detecting hepatic complications
* **Conservative management:** Contained hemorrhage with volume/coagulopathy correction
* **⚠️ Surgical emergency:** Capsular rupture or rapid hematoma extension
* **Definitive therapy:** Delivery

## 16.6. ACUTE FATTY LIVER OF PREGNANCY (AFLP)

**Pathophysiology:**
* Rapid microvesicular fatty infiltration
* Acute portal hypertension and encephalopathy
* **Association:** Abnormalities in β-oxidation enzymes

**Clinical features:**
* **Similar to severe preeclampsia/HELLP** but additionally:
  * Hypoglycemia
  * Elevated ammonia
  * Asterixis
  * Encephalopathy

**Management:**
* **Urgent delivery** required for recovery
* **Recovery may be prolonged** in severely ill patients
* **Transplantation role:** For patients progressing to ALF after delivery

**Bottom Line:** Pregnancy-related liver diseases represent a spectrum from mild hyperemesis to life-threatening AFLP, with delivery being definitive therapy for conditions presenting in the third trimester.

# 18. CHRONIC HEPATOCELLULAR DISEASE

## 18.1. EPIDEMIOLOGY AND PUBLIC HEALTH IMPACT

**US Prevalence trends:**
* **1988-1994:** Not specified
* **2013-2016:** Almost 36% (25% increase)
* **Leading cause:** Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)

* **Hepatitis C:** 2.7%
* **Alcoholic liver disease:** 1.0%
* **Hepatitis B:** 0.35%

💡 **Key Trend:** The dramatic increase in chronic liver disease is almost entirely attributable to rising NAFLD prevalence, paralleling obesity and type 2 diabetes epidemics.

## 18.2. DISEASE CLASSIFICATION

**Anatomic classification:**
1. **Hepatocellular (parenchymal) disease:**
   * Evidence of cholestasis and synthetic dysfunction appear synchronously
2. **Biliary disease:**
   * Cholestasis precedes hepatocellular dysfunction
   * Hepatocellular function preserved until late in disease course

⚠️ **Transplant implication:** Late preservation of hepatocellular function in cholestatic disease disadvantages these patients on waiting lists.

## 18.3. MOST COMMON ACUTE CAUSES

1. **Drug toxicity** (leading cause)
2. **Infection**

## 18.4. PROGRESSION PATTERNS

**Acute liver disease outcomes:**
* Spontaneous resolution
* Progression to chronic disease
* Development of acute liver failure

**Bottom Line:** Chronic liver disease has reached epidemic proportions primarily due to NAFLD, with important distinctions between hepatocellular and cholestatic disease patterns affecting clinical presentation and transplant prioritization.

# 19. CIRRHOSIS AND PORTAL HYPERTENSION

## 19.1. DEFINITION AND PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

**Cirrhosis:** End product of chronic liver disease progression
* **Process:** Recurrent parenchymal inflammation and necrosis
* **Result:** Disruption of normal hepatic architecture
* **Structural changes:**
  * Fibrosis replacement of normal tissue
  * Regenerative nodule formation
  * Loss of normal hepatic lobule arrangement

**Portal hypertension development:**
* **Blood flow disruption:** Shunts between afferent (portal/arterial) and efferent (hepatic venous) vessels
* **Increased resistance** to hepatic blood flow
* **Definition:** HVPG >10-12 mmHg (severe portal hypertension)
* **Systemic impact:** Becomes multi-system illness affecting other organ systems

![NICV 46-1 Reference Image](https://c7cfd6b7-3c34-4f3a-886b-08d1a3707c3a.ngrok-free.app/api/serve-image/cirrhosis-pathophysiology)

## 19.2. HEMODYNAMIC CONSEQUENCES

**Portal circulation changes:**
* Formation of portosystemic collaterals
* Massive splanchnic vasodilation
* **Dual mechanism:**
  1. Impedance to hepatic flow (structural)
  2. Massive increase in splanchnic flow (functional)

**Bottom Line:** Cirrhosis represents the final common pathway of chronic liver injury, characterized by architectural distortion leading to portal hypertension with systemic hemodynamic consequences.

# 20. HEMOSTATIC ABNORMALITIES

## 20.1. COMPLEX HEMOSTATIC REBALANCING

**Traditional view vs. modern understanding:**
* **Old concept:** Cirrhotics have bleeding diathesis based on abnormal PT/PTT
* **Modern concept:** Rebalanced hemostasis with both proand anticoagulant factor deficiencies

## 20.2. COAGULATION FACTOR CHANGES

**Liver synthesis:** All factors except III (tissue thromboplastin), IV (calcium), VIII (von Willebrand factor)  
**Factor deficiencies in cirrhosis:**
* **Procoagulant factors:** Decreased levels
* **Anticoagulant factors:** Also decreased (protein C, antithrombin III)
* **Net effect:** Balanced reduction may maintain normal thrombin generation

## 20.3. EVIDENCE FOR REBALANCED HEMOSTASIS

**Thrombomodulin studies:**
* Adding thrombomodulin (protein C activator) to PT assays
* **Result:** Normal and cirrhotic patients generate equal amounts of thrombin
* **Implication:** Protein C deficiency in cirrhotics balances procoagulant factor deficiency

**Clinical correlation:**
* **Poor correlation:** PT/PTT abnormalities with actual bleeding complications
* **Invasive procedures:** PT/PTT poor predictors of bleeding risk

## 20.4. POTENTIAL PROTHROMBOTIC STATE

**Paradoxical findings:**
* **Reduced anticoagulant levels:** Protein C, antithrombin III
* **Increased procoagulant factors:** Factor VIII, von Willebrand factor
* **Clinical evidence:**
  * No protection against venous thromboembolism (VTE)
  * **Increased VTE risk** in both cirrhotic and non-cirrhotic liver disease

## 20.5. CHOLESTATIC DISEASE COAGULOPATHY

**Different mechanism** from hepatocellular dysfunction:
* **Vitamin K-dependent factors:** II, VII, IX, X (procoagulant)
* **Also affected:** Protein C, protein S (anticoagulant)
* **Cause:** Impaired bile secretion → vitamin K malabsorption
* **Treatment response:** Parenteral vitamin K effective (if liver can synthesize precursors)
* ⚠️ **Cannot correct:** Hepatocellular dysfunction coagulopathy

## 20.6. QUALITATIVE COAGULATION DEFECTS

**Dysfibrinogenemia:**
* **Incidence:** 70-80% of cirrhotics
* **Mechanism:** Excess sialic acid residues on fibrinogen
* **Effects:**
  * Abnormal thrombin enzymatic activity
  * Abnormal fibrin monomer polymerization
* **Clinical significance:** Serum fibrinogen levels may be adequate but function impaired

## 20.7. PLATELET ABNORMALITIES

**Thrombocytopenia prevalence:** 30-64% in chronic cirrhotics
* **Severe thrombocytopenia:** Platelet counts <30,000/mm³ rare

**Mechanisms:**
1. **Primary cause:** Splenic sequestration (portal hypertension)
   * Up to 90% of platelets may be sequestered
2. **Other factors:**
   * Decreased thrombopoietin production (liver origin)
   * Immunologic mechanisms
   * Bone marrow suppression
   * Consumptive processes (DIC)

**Compensatory mechanisms:**
* **Elevated von Willebrand factor:** Augments platelet-endothelial interaction
* **Thrombin generation threshold:** Platelet count >56,000/mm³ supports near-normal thrombin generation

💡 **Clinical guidance:** Platelet transfusion indicated for bleeding patients with counts in/below 56,000/mm³ range. Not indicated for non-bleeding patients.

## 20.8. FIBRINOLYTIC SYSTEM ABNORMALITIES

**Enhanced fibrinolysis:** 30-46% incidence in end-stage liver disease

**Mechanisms:**
* **Increased tPA levels:** Liver normally clears tissue plasminogen activator
* **Decreased inhibitors:** Reduced synthesis of plasmin inhibitors (PAI-1, TAFI)
* **Net effect:** Balance of proand anti-fibrinolytic factors determines outcome

**Assessment methods:**
* **Euglobulin clot lysis time (ECLT)**
* **Viscoelastic whole blood testing (VET):**
  * Clot lysis index = (amplitude at specific time)/(maximum amplitude)
  * Ratio less than normal range indicates accelerated fibrinolysis
  * Suggests need for antifibrinolytic agents (epsilon aminocaproic acid, tranexamic acid)

## 20.9. DISSEMINATED INTRAVASCULAR COAGULATION (DIC) CONTROVERSY

**Standard laboratory overlap:** DIC shares abnormalities with cirrhosis
* Cannot distinguish consumption from decreased synthesis

**Special assays for DIC diagnosis:**
* **Markers of excessive thrombin production:**
  * Prothrombin fragment F1+2
  * Fibrinopeptide A
  * Thrombin-antithrombin (TAT) complexes
* **Elevation indicates:** Consumption rather than underproduction

**Current consensus:**
* **Overt DIC:** Probably not feature of stable chronic liver disease
* **Accelerated intravascular coagulation and fibrinolysis (AICF):**
  * Low-grade consumptive process
  * <30% of cirrhotics
  * Primarily in severe, decompensated disease
  * **Risk factor:** For progression to DIC with stimuli (sepsis, SBP)

**Bottom Line:** Cirrhotic hemostasis represents a complex rebalanced state with both bleeding and thrombotic risks, requiring individualized assessment beyond conventional coagulation tests.

# 21. CARDIAC MANIFESTATIONS

## 21.1. HYPERDYNAMIC CIRCULATION

**Classic presentation:**
* **High cardiac output**
* **Low arterial blood pressure** (systolic 80s-90s)
* **Low systemic vascular resistance**
* **Physical appearance:** Warm, well-perfused despite hypotension

**Pulmonary hemodynamics:**
* **Mild elevation:** Pulmonary artery pressures
* **Normal range:** Pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR)
* **Explanation:** Elevated cardiac output proportional to transpulmonary gradient
  * PVR = (mPAP - PCWP) / Cardiac Output

## 21.2. VOLUME DISTRIBUTION ABNORMALITIES

* **Elevated intravascular volume:** Total body volume increased
* **Normal wedge pressure:** Volume sequestered in splanchnic bed
* **Reduced effective circulating volume:** Has consequences for other organ systems

## 21.3. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF HYPERDYNAMIC STATE

**Portal hypertension → local vasodilator production:**
* **Natriuretic peptides**
* **Vasoactive intestinal peptide**
* **Endotoxin**
* **Glucagon**
* **Nitric oxide (predominant)**

**Nitric oxide evidence:**
* Elevated production precedes hyperdynamic circulation development
* Inhibition of NO formation increases arterial pressure in cirrhotics
* Reduced circulatory responsivity to sympathetic stimulation

## 21.4. CIRRHOTIC CARDIOMYOPATHY

**Four key components:**
1. **Hyperdynamic circulation** (↑CO, ↓PVR)
2. **Systolic and diastolic dysfunction**
3. **Cardiac resistance to β-adrenergic stimulation**
4. **Electrophysiologic abnormalities**

**Historical misconception:**
* Hyperdynamic circulation previously thought to reflect "cardiac vigor"
* Few patients with overt cardiomyopathy attributed to alcohol

## 21.5. SYSTOLIC DYSFUNCTION (MASKED)

**Mechanism:** Elevated cardiac output due to profound afterload reduction  
**Revealed by stress:**
* **Exercise intolerance:** Inability to increase cardiac output appropriately
* **Ejection fraction paradox:** Cannot increase despite increased end-diastolic volume
* **Correlation:** Severity correlates with liver disease severity

## 21.6. DIASTOLIC DYSFUNCTION

**Clinical significance:** Renders patients sensitive to cardiac filling changes  
**Consequences:**
* Vulnerability to heart failure
* Prerenal insufficiency susceptibility
* Poor tolerance of volume shifts

## 21.7. AUTONOMIC DYSFUNCTION

**Prevalence:** 43% of cirrhotic patients  
**Testing methods:**
* Sustained handgrip
* Ice water hand submersion
* Valsalva maneuver
* Tilt table testing

**Manifestations:**
* Chronotropic incompetence
* Hemodynamic incompetence
* Poor response to physiologic challenges

## 21.8. ELECTROPHYSIOLOGIC ABNORMALITIES

**Prolonged QTc interval:**
* **Prevalence by Child class:**
  * Child A: 30%
  * Child C: 60%
* ⚠️ **Clinical consideration:** Use caution with QT-prolonging drugs

## 21.9. CORONARY ARTERY DISEASE IN CIRRHOSIS

**Emerging concern:** Liver transplantation expansion to older patients with comorbidities

**Risk factors (similar to general population):**
* Hypertension
* Dyslipidemia
* Age, gender, obesity

**NASH-specific considerations:**
* **Associated conditions:** Obesity, diabetes
* **Chronic inflammation:** Additional cardiac risk factor

**Diagnostic challenges:**
* Many patients cannot exercise (pharmacologic stress testing required)
* **Dobutamine stress echo:** Poor sensitivity, variable negative predictive value (75-89%)
* **Recommendation:** Consider coronary angiography for high-risk transplant candidates

**Bottom Line:** Cirrhotic cardiomyopathy is a complex condition with masked systolic dysfunction, diastolic abnormalities, and electrophysiologic changes that significantly impact perioperative management and outcomes.

# 22. RENAL DYSFUNCTION AND HEPATORENAL SYNDROME

## 22.1. OVERVIEW AND CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE

**Hallmarks of cirrhotic renal dysfunction:**
* Inappropriate avid sodium and water retention
* Renal hypoperfusion
* Decreased glomerular filtration
* **Extreme manifestation:** Hepatorenal syndrome (HRS)

**MELD score significance:** Renal function is one of only three variables, emphasizing mortality impact

## 22.2. DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS OF RENAL DYSFUNCTION IN CIRRHOSIS

**HRS is diagnosis of exclusion** - must rule out:
1. **Parenchymal renal disease:** Glomerulonephritis, diabetic nephropathy
2. **Sepsis-related:** Acute tubular necrosis
3. **Nephrotoxicity:** Drugs, contrast
4. **Hypovolemia:** GI bleeding, diuretics, diarrhea

## 22.3. COMORBID RENAL DISEASE

**Common associations:**
* **NAFLD:** Associated with type II diabetes → diabetic nephropathy
* **Hepatitis C:** Immune complex nephropathies
  * IgA nephropathy
  * Membranous proliferative glomerulonephropathy
* **Direct liver-kidney diseases:**
  * Amyloidosis
  * Systemic lupus erythematosus
  * Autoimmune hepatitis
  * Polycystic liver disease
  * Alagille syndrome

## 22.4. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF CIRRHOTIC RENAL DYSFUNCTION

**Circulatory response to portal hypertension:**
* **Sympathetic stimulation:** Response to effective volume loss
* **Renin-angiotensin-aldosterone activation**
* **Vasopressin system activation**
* **Combined effect:** Salt/water retention + reduced renal perfusion

**Protective mechanisms:**
* **Elevated renal prostaglandins:** Help maintain renal perfusion
* ⚠️ **Clinical implication:** Cirrhotics very sensitive to NSAIDs

**High-risk medications:**
* **NSAIDs:** Prostaglandin inhibition
* **Aminoglycosides:** Direct nephrotoxicity
* **ACE inhibitors/ARBs:** Reduced glomerular perfusion pressure
* **Contrast agents:** Surprisingly, no evidence for increased nephrotoxicity

## 22.5. ASSESSMENT CHALLENGES

**Volume status assessment difficulties:**
* Total body volume overloaded but intravascularly depleted
* Central filling pressures may reflect transmitted intra-abdominal pressure (ascites)
* Serum creatinine poor estimate of GFR (decreased muscle mass)

**Diagnostic approach:**
* Discontinue diuretics
* Volume expansion with albumin
* **Failure to improve:** Strongly suggests HRS

## 22.6. HEPATORENAL SYNDROME CLASSIFICATION (UPDATED)

**Previous terminology:** Type I and Type II HRS  
**Current terminology:** Based on improved understanding
* **Type I → HRS-AKI (acute kidney injury)**
* **Type II → HRS-CKD (chronic kidney disease)**

## 22.7. HRS-AKI (FORMERLY TYPE I)

**Definition:** Fulfills standard AKI criteria:
* Absolute ↑ serum Cr ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours, OR
* 1.5× baseline Cr within last 7 days
* **Advantage:** Earlier diagnosis than previous Type I (which required Cr ≥2.5 mg/dL)

**Precipitating factors:**
* Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP)
* Sepsis
* Gastrointestinal bleeding
* Surgical stress

**Management priorities:**
1. Address precipitating cause rapidly
2. Discontinue nephrotoxic drugs
3. Consider volume expansion trial
4. Initiate specific HRS therapy

## 22.8. HRS-CKD (FORMERLY TYPE II)

**Definition:** Estimated GFR <60 mL/1.73 m²/min for ≥3 months  
**Characteristics:**
* More indolent course
* Progressive activity of compensatory systems
* **Primary clinical problem:** Refractory ascites

## 22.9. HRS PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND THERAPY

**Proximate cause:** Profound renal vasoconstriction  
**Failed approaches:** Direct renal vasodilation
* Prostaglandins
* Dopamine agonists
* Endothelin antagonists

**Successful approach:** Target underlying pathology
* **Reduce portal hypertension**
* **Reduce splanchnic vasodilation**

## 22.10. EFFECTIVE VASOPRESSOR THERAPY

**Vasoconstrictors + volume expansion:**
* **Arginine vasopressin (AVP) and analogues**
* **Somatostatin and analogues**
* **α-agonists:** Norepinephrine, midodrine

**AVP/analogues advantages:**
* **V1 receptor interaction:** Mediates vasoconstriction
* **Splanchnic selectivity:** Well-represented in splanchnic circulation

**Terlipressin (not available in US):**
* Most studied vasopressor for HRS
* **Efficacy:** 40-60% of HRS-AKI patients respond
* **Definition of response:** Reduction in creatinine to 1.5 mg/dL
* **Recurrence:** Uncommon if therapy successful and withdrawn

## 22.11. TIPS FOR HRS

**Mechanism:** Decompress splanchnic circulation, return volume to central circulation  
**Efficacy:** Pilot studies show reversal of both HRS types  
**Limited application due to exclusion criteria:**
* Child-Pugh score >12
* Active infection
* Serum bilirubin >5 mg/dL
* Risk of hepatic encephalopathy development/worsening
* High shunt stenosis/migration rate

## 22.12. LIVER TRANSPLANTATION AND COMBINED TRANSPLANT

**Definitive therapy:** Liver transplantation for HRS  
**Bridge therapies:**
* Renal replacement therapy (typical)
* Terlipressin + albumin (where available)

**Combined liver-kidney transplant criteria:**
* **Recommendation:** Patients receiving dialysis ≥2× weekly for >6 weeks
* **Rationale:** HRS requiring RRT may not be reversible

**Bottom Line:** HRS represents the renal manifestation of cirrhotic circulatory dysfunction, requiring early recognition, treatment of precipitants, and targeted therapy with vasoconstrictors plus volume expansion.

# 23. PULMONARY COMPLICATIONS

## 23.1. OVERVIEW AND CLINICAL IMPACT

**Prevalence:** 50-70% of chronic liver disease patients report dyspnea  
**Significance:** Pulmonary abnormalities may overshadow underlying liver disease and influence transplant candidacy

## 23.2. COMMON MECHANISMS OF DYSPNEA

**Standard pulmonary causes:**
* Ventilation-perfusion abnormalities from obstructive airway disease
* Fluid retention and pleural effusions
* Decreased lung capacity from large-volume ascites
* **α1-antitrypsin disease:** Both lung and liver manifestations

## 23.3. UNIQUE PORTAL HYPERTENSION-RELATED ABNORMALITIES

**Two major vascular abnormalities:**
1. **Hepatopulmonary syndrome (HPS)**
2. **Portopulmonary hypertension (POPH)**

**Clinical significance:**
* Significant morbidity and mortality
* **May overshadow liver disease severity**
* **Influences transplant candidacy**

# 24. HEPATOPULMONARY SYNDROME (HPS)

## 24.1. DEFINITION AND PREVALENCE

**Triad components:**
1. Liver dysfunction
2. Otherwise unexplained hypoxemia
3. Intrapulmonary vascular dilatation (IPVD)

**Prevalence:** Up to 20% of liver transplant candidates

## 24.2. DIAGNOSTIC CRITERIA

**Oxygenation defect:**
* PaO2 <80 mmHg OR
* Alveolar-arterial oxygen gradient ≥15 mmHg (breathing ambient air)

**Pulmonary vascular dilatation:**
* Positive contrast-enhanced echocardiography OR
* Abnormal brain uptake (>6%) with radioactive lung-perfusion scanning

**Liver disease:**
* Portal hypertension (most common) with or without cirrhosis

## 24.3. TYPES OF INTRAPULMONARY VASCULAR DILATATIONS

**Type I lesions (more common):**
* Precapillary dilations at alveolar level
* **Functional shunts:** Not true anatomic communications
* **Mechanism:** Massive capillary diameter increase (8-15 μm → 50-500 μm)
* **Effect:** Insufficient time for oxygen diffusion through entire blood stream
* **Central stream:** Poorly oxygenated (functionally shunted)
* **Response to oxygen:** Easily correctable with increased FiO2

**Type II lesions:**
* Larger, centrally located dilations
* **True anatomic shunts**
* **Oxygen response:** Failure of 100% O2 to correct PaO2 to >150 mmHg suggests Type II

## 24.4. DIAGNOSTIC TESTING

**Contrast-enhanced echocardiography:**
* **Method:** Agitated saline contrast injection
* **Normal:** Microbubbles trapped by pulmonary capillaries
* **Abnormal (HPS):** Delayed appearance (>3 cardiac cycles) in left atrium

**Technetium-labeled microaggregated albumin (TcMAA):**
* **Normal:** Trapped by pulmonary capillaries
* **Abnormal (HPS):** Extrapulmonary uptake >5%

## 24.5. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND CLINICAL FEATURES

**Unique finding:** Orthodeoxia (positional oxygenation changes)
* **Mechanism:** IPVDs predominate in lung bases
* **Standing:** Worsens hypoxemia (increased basal blood flow)
* **Supine:** Improves oxygenation (redistributes blood to apices)

**Natural history:** Usually progressive hypoxemia

**Pathogenesis (poorly understood):**
* Nitric oxide involvement
* Splanchnic endotoxemia
* Decreased clearance of inflammatory mediators
* Angiogenesis factors
* Genetic contributions

**Treatment:** No effective medical therapy  
**Post-transplant:** Near-universal correction (may take up to 1 year)

# 25. PORTOPULMONARY HYPERTENSION (POPH)

## 25.1. DEFINITION AND DIAGNOSTIC CRITERIA

![POPH Criteria](https://c7cfd6b7-3c34-4f3a-886b-08d1a3707c3a.ngrok-free.app/api/serve-image/poph-criteria)

**Updated definition (2018):**
* **mPAP >20 mmHg** (lowered from ≥25 mmHg)
* **Concern:** New definition may include healthy individuals who don't benefit from treatment

## 25.2. SEVERITY CLASSIFICATION BY MPAP

* **Mild:** <35 mmHg
* **Moderate:** 35-50 mmHg  
* **Severe:** >50 mmHg

## 25.3. EPIDEMIOLOGY AND RISK FACTORS

**Prevalence:**
* **Portal hypertension population:** 2%
* **General population:** 0.13%
* **Liver transplant candidates:** 4-6%

**Risk factors:**
* **Increased risk:** Female gender, autoimmune hepatitis
* **Decreased risk:** Chronic hepatitis C
* **No correlation:** Severity of liver disease or portal hypertension

**Child classification distribution:**
* Child A: 51%
* Child B: 38%
* Child C: 11%

## 25.4. CLINICAL PRESENTATION AND DIAGNOSIS

**Symptoms (nonspecific):**
* Dyspnea
* Generalized weakness
* Decreased exercise tolerance

**Oxygenation:**
* **Contrast with HPS:** May have only modest PaO2 decrease
* **Coexistence:** HPS and POPH rarely coexist

**Screening:**
* **Best test:** 2D transthoracic echocardiography (TTE)
* **Method:** Estimate RVSP by tricuspid regurgitant jet velocity
* **Performance:** 97% sensitivity, 77% specificity for moderate-severe POPH

**Definitive diagnosis:** Right heart catheterization required

## 25.5. TREATMENT CONSIDERATIONS

**Conventional measures:**
* Diuresis for fluid overload

**Specific vasodilator therapy:**  
⚠️ **Contraindicated:** Calcium channel blockers
* **Reason:** Promote mesenteric vasodilation, worsen portal hypertension

**Evidence-based therapies:**
* **Prostanoids:** Epoprostenol (PgI₂)
* **Phosphodiesterase inhibitors:** Sildenafil
* **Endothelin antagonists:** Bosentan, ambrisentan

## 25.6. EPOPROSTENOL THERAPY

**Benefits:**
* Reduces pulmonary pressures
* Survival benefit documented
* Antiplatelet effects
* Promotes vascular remodeling

**Limitations:**
* **Administration:** Continuous IV infusion via central access
* **Little tolerance:** For infusion interruption
* **Side effects:** Splenomegaly, worsening thrombocytopenia

## 25.7. OTHER MEDICAL THERAPIES

**Sildenafil:**
* Reduces pulmonary pressures at 3 months
* **Limitation:** No sustained response at 12 months
* **Role:** Combination therapy or bridge to transplant

**Endothelin antagonists (bosentan, ambrisentan):**
* Sustained decrease in PVR
* Sustained increase in cardiac output
* **Evidence:** Case reports and series, no definitive studies

## 25.8. TRANSPLANT CONSIDERATIONS

**Unpredictable outcomes:**
* Some patients: Complete POPH resolution
* Some patients: No/incomplete resolution, continued medical therapy needed
* Some patients: Worsening POPH post-transplant

**Transplant candidacy criteria:**
* **mPAP <35 mmHg:** Expected to tolerate transplant well
* **mPAP ≥35 mmHg + elevated PVR:** Consider if respond to therapy
  * **Target:** mPAP <35 mmHg AND PVR <240 dynes/sec/cm⁵

**Bottom Line:** HPS and POPH are serious pulmonary complications of portal hypertension with distinct pathophysiology, requiring specific diagnostic approaches and treatments, with variable transplant outcomes.

# 26. HEPATIC ENCEPHALOPATHY

## 26.1. DEFINITION AND CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE

**Hepatic encephalopathy (HE):** Reversible neuropsychiatric complication of chronic and acute liver disease  
**Spectrum:**
* **Minimal HE:** Subclinical abnormalities (psychometric tests only)
* **Overt HE:** Clearly evident neurologic and behavioral derangements

## 26.2. CLINICAL IMPACT OF MINIMAL HE

**Functional impairments:**
* Employment maintenance difficulties
* Driving impairment
* Daily activity limitations
* Family interaction problems
* **Importance:** Frequently reversible, emphasizing need for identification and treatment

## 26.3. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

**Mechanism types:**
* **Type A:** Acute liver failure
* **Type B:** Portal-systemic shunting with normal hepatocellular function
* **Type C:** Chronic liver disease with cirrhosis

## 26.4. AMMONIA AND CENTRAL ROLE

**Traditional understanding:** Hyperammonemia from inadequate hepatic ammonia metabolism  
**Modern understanding:** Multifactorial process with ammonia as important but not exclusive factor

**Other contributing factors:**
* Other gut-derived neurotoxins
* GABA and endogenous GABA receptor agonists
* Oxidative stress
* Inflammatory mediators
* Hyponatremia
* Abnormal serotonin and histamine neurotransmission

## 26.5. AMMONIA EFFECTS ON ASTROCYTES

**Central mechanism:**
* Brain lacks urea cycle cells
* **Detoxification:** Astrocytes synthesize glutamine from ammonia + glutamate
* **Consequence:** Glutamine osmotically active → astrocyte edema
* **MRI findings:** Low-grade cerebral edema in chronic liver disease patients with HE
* **Distinction from ALF:** Intracranial hypertension rare in chronic disease

## 26.6. DIAGNOSTIC ASSESSMENT

**Neuropsychologic criteria:**
* Level of consciousness
* Attention and command-following ability
* Affect assessment

![Encephalopathy Grading](https://c7cfd6b7-3c34-4f3a-886b-08d1a3707c3a.ngrok-free.app/api/serve-image/encephalopathy-grades)

**Physical examination findings:**
* **Asterixis** (classic finding)
* Hyperreflexia (clonus, Babinski sign)
* Other focal findings (nystagmus, decerebrate posturing)

⚠️ **Important:** Focal neurologic findings warrant imaging to exclude structural lesions (ICH, ischemia, abscess, tumor)

## 26.7. DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS

**Must exclude other causes:**
* **Metabolic encephalopathies:** Uremia, glucose/electrolyte abnormalities, endocrinopathies
* **Sepsis:** Well-known precipitant via inflammatory mediators (TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6)
* **Structural/vascular CNS lesions**
* **CNS infections**
* **Drug-related encephalopathy:** Cirrhotics highly sensitive to sedatives with impaired metabolism

## 26.8. PRECIPITATING FACTORS

![Precipitating Factors](https://c7cfd6b7-3c34-4f3a-886b-08d1a3707c3a.ngrok-free.app/api/serve-image/he-precipitants)

**Management priority:** Systematic search for and treatment of precipitating factors

## 26.9. TREATMENT STRATEGIES

**First-line therapy:** Lactulose (nonabsorbable disaccharide)  
**Proposed mechanisms:**
1. **Acidification:** Anaerobic bacteria ferment lactulose to weak acids
   * Acidic colonic environment converts NH₃ to poorly absorbed NH₄⁺
2. **Cathartic effect:** Acid milieu promotes evacuation, reducing absorption time

**Protein intake considerations:**
* **Avoid protein restriction:** May be harmful due to poor nutritional reserve
* **Recommendation:** Normal protein intake (1-1.5 g/kg/d)
* **Prefer:** Plant-based over animal protein (higher calorie:nitrogen ratio)

**Zinc supplementation:**
* **Rationale:** Cofactor in urea cycle, potential dietary deficiency
* **Recommendation:** Consider chronic supplementation (unclear which patients benefit)

## 26.10. MANAGEMENT APPROACH

1. **Rule out other causes** of encephalopathy
2. **Identify and treat precipitating factors** (see table)
3. **First-line therapy:** Lactulose
4. **Nutritional support:** Adequate protein (avoid restriction)
5. **Consider adjuncts:** Zinc supplementation

**Bottom Line:** Hepatic encephalopathy is a complex, multifactorial neuropsychiatric syndrome requiring systematic evaluation for precipitants and treatment with lactulose as first-line therapy while maintaining adequate nutrition.

# 27. ASCITES MANAGEMENT

## 27.1. CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE AND PROGNOSIS

**Most common complication** leading to cirrhosis hospitalization  
**Prognostic milestone:**
* Marks threshold in liver disease progression
* **5-year mortality:** 70% once ascites develops
* **Recommendation:** Refer for liver transplant evaluation upon ascites development

## 27.2. DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS

**Hepatic ascites:** ~85% of cases  
**Non-hepatic causes (15%):**
* Malignancy
* Cardiac failure  
* Renal disease
* Pancreatitis
* Tuberculosis

## 27.3. DIAGNOSTIC EVALUATION

**Most expeditious study:** Paracentesis  
**Serum-ascites albumin gradient (SAAG):**
* **Calculation:** Simultaneous serum albumin - ascites albumin
* **SAAG ≥1.1 mg/dL:** Portal hypertensive ascites (97% accuracy)
* **SAAG <1.1 mg/dL:** Non-portal hypertensive causes

## 27.4. STANDARD TREATMENT APPROACH

**Initial therapy:**
* **Salt restriction:** 2 g/day
* **Diuretics:** Typically combination therapy

**Hyponatremia management:**
* **Common:** Among cirrhotic patients with ascites
* **Fluid restriction threshold:** Serum sodium <120-125 mEq/L
* ⚠️ **Osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS) risk:** Cirrhotics particularly vulnerable
* **Safe correction rate:** 4-6 mEq/L per day, not exceeding 8 mEq/L in 24 hours

## 27.5. FLUID AND BLOOD PRODUCT CONSIDERATIONS

**IV fluid choices:** Must consider sodium load
* **Blood products:** High obligate sodium load
* **Alternatives to reduce sodium:**
  * Prothrombin complex concentrate instead of FFP
  * 25% albumin instead of 5% albumin

## 27.6. REFRACTORY ASCITES

**Definition:** Immutable to:
* Sodium restriction
* Maximum diuretic doses
* Paracentesis

**Clinical significance:**
* Hallmark of HRS-CKD
* Indicates increased disease severity
* **6-month mortality:** 21%

**Treatment options:**
* Serial paracentesis
* Liver transplantation
* TIPS placement
* Peritoneovenous shunt (rarely used)

## 27.7. PARACENTESIS AND ALBUMIN REPLACEMENT

**Current practice:** Replace albumin with large-volume drainage  
**Evidence:** Not well-supported by randomized trials

**Theoretical rationales:**
* Prevent paracentesis-induced circulatory dysfunction
* Minimize electrolyte disturbances
* Minimize nutritional impact of albumin loss
* Prevent renal impairment

**Current recommendations:**
* **<5 L drainage:** No albumin replacement needed
* **Large volume (≥5 L):** Consider 6-8 g albumin per liter removed

## 27.8. TIPS FOR REFRACTORY ASCITES

**Expected benefits:**
* Improve quality of life
* Obviate need for serial paracentesis

**Considerations:**
* **Increased encephalopathy risk**
* **High shunt malfunction incidence**
* Balance quality of life improvement vs. complications

## 27.9. SPONTANEOUS BACTERIAL PERITONITIS (SBP)

**Recommendation:** Paracentesis for ALL hospitalized patients with ascites

**Diagnostic criteria:**
* **PMN count ≥250 cells/mm³** in ascitic fluid
* **Absence:** Other identifiable intra-abdominal source

**Pathogenesis:**
* **Primary:** Bacterial translocation from bowel
* **Secondary:** Nosocomial bacteremia from procedures

**Management urgency:**
* **Cell counts available faster** than culture results
* **Immediate treatment:** Broad-spectrum antibiotics
* **Rapid progression:** Sepsis can develop quickly
* **Associated risk:** SBP is recognized precipitant for HRS-AKI

**Empiric treatment indications:**
* **PMN ≥250 cells/mm³** (definitive)
* **Clinical suspicion** even if PMN <250:
  * Fever
  * Abdominal pain
  * Worsening hepatic function
  * Worsening renal function
  * Unexplained encephalopathy worsening

**Bottom Line:** Ascites marks a critical transition in cirrhosis with poor prognosis, requiring prompt evaluation for SBP, careful sodium management, and consideration for transplant evaluation.

# 28. VARICEAL BLEEDING

## 28.1. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND DEVELOPMENT

**Formation mechanism:**
* Portal hypertension → distorted hepatic architecture
* Impedance to splanchnic flow through liver
* **Portosystemic collateral formation:** Especially gastric and esophageal venous systems
* **Progressive vasodilation:** Massive splanchnic vasodilation from increased nitric oxide

**Dual problem:**
1. **Impedance to flow** (structural)
2. **Massive increase in flow** (functional)

## 28.2. PORTAL PRESSURE ASSESSMENT

**Wedged hepatic venous pressure (WHVP):**
* Correlates well with portal pressure (indirect measure)
* **Method:** Catheter advanced to wedge position in hepatic vein

**Hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG):**
* **Calculation:** WHVP - free hepatic venous pressure (or IVC pressure)
* **Correction:** For intra-abdominal pressure contribution from ascites
* **Normal HVPG:** 3-5 mmHg
* **Varix threshold:** HVPG ≥10-12 mmHg required for esophageal varices

## 28.3. VARIX PREVALENCE

**Correlation with liver disease severity:**
* **Child A:** 40% incidence
* **Child C:** 85% incidence

**Gold standard diagnosis:** Esophagogastroduodenoscopy

## 28.4. MANAGEMENT STRATEGIES

**Pharmacologic portal pressure reduction:**  
**Nonselective β-blockers mechanism:**
* **β1 blockade:** Decreased cardiac output
* **β2 blockade:** Splanchnic vasoconstriction
* **Net effect:** Reduced portal pressure

**Acute variceal bleeding management:**
1. **Volume resuscitation:** Avoid overresuscitation
2. **Coagulation correction:** Address severe abnormalities only
3. **Pharmacologic pressure reduction**
4. **Endoscopic variceal ligation**

## 28.5. RESUSCITATION PRINCIPLES

⚠️ **Avoid aggressive resuscitation:** Bleeding is pressure-related phenomenon  
**Targets:**
* **Hemoglobin:** 8 g/dL goal
* **Consider blood products:** For significant platelet count/INR abnormalities only
* **Rationale:** Aggressive volume replacement may cause resistant or recurrent bleeding

**Airway management:** Elective intubation often warranted for airway protection

## 28.6. PHARMACOLOGIC AGENTS

**Portal pressure reduction medications:**
* **Vasopressin and analogues**
* **Somatostatin and analogues**

⚠️ **Avoid β-blockers acutely:** Systemic pressure effects undesirable during bleeding

**Optimal approach:** Early endoscopic variceal ligation + pharmacotherapy

## 28.7. COMPLICATIONS AND PROGNOSIS

**High-lethality complication:** Rupture of high-pressure collaterals  
**Feared complication:** Historically very high mortality  
**Modern management:** Combination pharmacologic + endoscopic approach improves outcomes

**Bottom Line:** Variceal bleeding results from portal hypertension creating high-pressure portosystemic collaterals, managed with combined pharmacologic portal pressure reduction and endoscopic intervention while avoiding overresuscitation.

# 30. HEPATOCELLULAR CARCINOMA

## 30.1. EPIDEMIOLOGY AND GLOBAL IMPACT

**Worldwide significance:**
* **Second leading cause** of cancer-related deaths globally
* **Primary liver cancers:** HCC accounts for 75% of diagnoses

## 30.2. RISK FACTORS AND ASSOCIATIONS

**Major risk factors:**
* **Chronic viral hepatitis** (HBV, HCV)
* **Hemochromatosis**
* **Cirrhosis of any cause**

**Cirrhosis association:**
* **80-90% of HCC patients** have underlying cirrhosis
* **Non-cirrhotic HCC:** Almost always has underlying chronic hepatitis/necroinflammatory state

💡 **Key concept:** Chronic necroinflammatory state appears essential for HCC development

## 30.3. TREATMENT OPTIONS AND CANDIDACY

**Surgical resection:**
* **Optimal treatment** for patients with adequate hepatic reserve
* **US candidacy:** <5% due to underlying cirrhosis
* **Limitation:** Most patients unable to tolerate resection due to liver dysfunction

**Liver transplantation:**
* **Option for:** Non-resection candidates with appropriate tumor characteristics
* **Requirement:** Expected transplant outcomes similar to other indications

## 30.4. TRANSPLANT SELECTION CRITERIA

**Milan Criteria (earliest established):**
* **Single tumor:** No larger than 5 cm, OR
* **Multiple tumors:** Up to 3 tumors, none larger than 3 cm each

**Refined criteria:** Other systems manipulate:
* Tumor size limits
* Number of tumors allowed
* Associated tumor marker levels (e.g., AFP)

## 30.5. ALTERNATIVE TREATMENTS

**Non-surgical/non-transplant candidates:**
* **Radiofrequency ablation**
* **Chemoembolization**

**Bridge to transplant:**
* Some centers use these therapies to maintain transplant eligibility during waiting period
* Prevents tumor progression beyond selection criteria

**Bottom Line:** HCC is predominantly associated with cirrhosis, limiting resection candidacy to <5% in the US, with liver transplantation offering hope for selected patients meeting specific tumor criteria.

# 31. NONALCOHOLIC FATTY LIVER DISEASE

## 31.1. DISEASE SPECTRUM AND DEFINITION

**NAFLD encompasses:**
* **Simple steatosis:** Fat deposition only
* **NASH (steatohepatitis):** Fat + inflammation + hepatocellular necrosis

**Primary NAFLD associations:**
* **Insulin resistance** and metabolic syndrome components:
  * Obesity
  * Central adiposity
  * Type II diabetes
  * Arterial hypertension
  * Hypertriglyceridemia

## 31.2. CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE

**"Hepatic manifestation of metabolic syndrome"**
* **Most common cause:** Elevated liver enzymes in adults
* **US prevalence:** Estimated 3-5% for NASH specifically

## 31.3. DISEASE INTERACTIONS

**NAFLD may coexist with:**
* Chronic hepatitis C
* Hemochromatosis  
* Alcoholic liver disease
* **Effect:** Exacerbates damage when present together

## 31.4. DIAGNOSTIC GOLD STANDARD

**Liver biopsy required** to distinguish NASH from simple steatosis  
**Key histologic features:**
* Macrovesicular steatosis
* Lobular inflammation
* Hepatocyte ballooning
* Perisinusoidal fibrosis (often present)

## 31.5. CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE OF NASH VS. SIMPLE STEATOSIS

**Only NASH associated with:**
* Hepatocellular carcinoma risk
* Progression to cirrhosis
* **Simple steatosis:** Generally benign course

## 31.6. EPIDEMIOLOGIC IMPACT

**Relationship to NAFLD epidemic:**
* **Parallel increase:** NAFLD prevalence with obesity and diabetes
* **Future projection:** Likely to continue increasing
* **Public health burden:** Major contributor to chronic liver disease prevalence

**Bottom Line:** NAFLD represents the hepatic manifestation of metabolic syndrome, with NASH (the inflammatory form) carrying risk for cirrhosis and HCC, requiring liver biopsy for definitive diagnosis.

# 32. CHRONIC CHOLESTATIC DISEASE

## 32.1. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND CONSEQUENCES

**Biliary obstruction effects:**
* **Increased bile duct pressures**
* **Bile reflux:** Into liver sinusoids and vascular system
* **Laboratory changes:** ↑ bilirubin, ↑ bile salts, ↑ alkaline phosphatase (biliary epithelium synthesis)

**Infectious complications risk:**
* **Bacteria in bile + obstruction** = high risk for:
  * Ascending cholangitis
  * Hepatic abscess
  * Sepsis

## 32.2. RENAL COMPLICATIONS

**Cholestasis and kidney injury association:**
* **Increased AKI incidence** with cholestasis and hyperbilirubinemia
* **Mechanisms:**
  * Endotoxemia (sepsis + loss of bile salts to vascular space)
  * Induced diuresis from elevated bile salts
  * Impaired myocardial contractility from bile salt elevation

**Physiologic bile salt functions lost:**
* **Intestinal secretion normally:**
  * Prevents bacterial overgrowth
  * Binds endotoxin, preventing portal absorption
* **Loss with obstruction:** Portal and systemic endotoxemia

## 32.3. COAGULATION IN CHOLESTATIC DISEASE

**Hypercoagulability evidence:**
* Transplant patients with PBC and PSC show:
  * ↑ Portal venous thrombosis incidence
  * ↑ Thrombin-antithrombin (TAT) complexes
  * Thromboelastographic indices consistent with hypercoagulability
* **Contrast:** With hepatocellular disease coagulopathy

## 32.4. PRIMARY BILIARY CHOLANGITIS (PBC)

**Disease characteristics:**
* **Progressive destruction:** Small intrahepatic bile ducts
* **Portal inflammation:** Eventually leads to cirrhosis
* **Course:** 10-20 years to cirrhosis

**Laboratory hallmark:**
* **Antimitochondrial antibody:** Present in 95% of patients
* **Diagnosis confirmation:** Liver biopsy for histologic staging

**Treatment:**
* **Ursodeoxycholic acid:** Only proven therapy
  * May have immunomodulatory effects
  * Demonstrated to retard progression and improve survival
* **Liver transplantation:** Definitive therapy
  * **Recurrence rate:** 10-35%

## 32.5. PRIMARY SCLEROSING CHOLANGITIS (PSC)

**Disease characteristics:**
* **Progressive inflammatory disease:** Medium and large intraand extrahepatic bile ducts
* **Diagnostic imaging:** ERCP and MRCP
  * **Characteristic pattern:** "Beaded" biliary tree from multiple stenoses

**Associated conditions:**
* **Inflammatory bowel disease:** Extremely high association
  * Primarily ulcerative colitis
  * Occasionally Crohn disease
* **Other autoimmune diseases:**
  * Insulin-dependent diabetes
  * Psoriasis

## 32.6. PSC COMPLICATIONS AND PROGNOSIS

**Disease course:** Average 15-year progression  
**Major complications:**
* **Recurrent bacterial cholangitis**
* **Cholangiocarcinoma:** 15-30% over disease course
  * Very poor prognosis
  * May cause transplant ineligibility
* **Colon carcinoma:** Especially with coexisting IBD

**Treatment:**
* **Liver transplantation:** Most definitive therapy
* **Disease recurrence:** Occurs post-transplant
* **Medical therapy:** Limited effectiveness compared to PBC

**Bottom Line:** Chronic cholestatic diseases result from immunologic destruction of bile ducts, with PBC having effective medical therapy (ursodeoxycholic acid) while PSC has limited treatment options and high complication rates including cholangiocarcinoma.

# 34. PREOPERATIVE EVALUATION AND RISK ASSESSMENT

## 34.1. HEPATIC HISTORY AND RISK FACTOR ASSESSMENT

**History priorities:**
* **Prior jaundice episodes:** Especially related to surgery/anesthesia
* **Alcohol consumption:** Detailed quantification and timeline
* **Drug history:** Prescription, recreational, illicit, herbal products
* **Exposure risks:**
  * Tattoos, sexual promiscuity
  * Raw seafood consumption
  * Travel to hepatitis-endemic areas

**Symptom inquiry:**
* Fatigue, anorexia, weight loss
* Nausea, vomiting, dark urine
* Easy bruising, pruritus
* Biliary colic, abdominal distention
* Gastrointestinal bleeding

## 34.2. PHYSICAL EXAMINATION FINDINGS

**Signs of active liver disease:**
* **Cutaneous:** Icterus, palmar erythema, spider angiomas
* **Endocrine:** Gynecomastia, testicular atrophy
* **Abdominal:** Hepatosplenomegaly, ascites
* **Neurologic:** Asterixis
* **Hematologic:** Petechiae, ecchymoses

## 34.3. LABORATORY TESTING STRATEGY

**Asymptomatic patients:**
* **Routine LFTs NOT warranted**
* **False-positive rate:** 2.5% above upper normal (by definition)
* **Study findings:** 11/7,600 patients (0.1%) had abnormal LFTs; only 3 (0.04%) developed jaundice

**Abnormal results approach:**
* **Minor elevations (<3× normal):** May be clinically insignificant
* **Recommended approach:**
  1. Repeat testing
  2. **<3× upper normal:** Reasonable to proceed with surgery
  3. **≥3× upper normal:** Requires investigation

## 34.4. ACUTE VS. CHRONIC DISEASE IMPLICATIONS

**Acute hepatitis:**
* **Historical data (1960s-70s):** Prohibitive surgical risk
* **Series findings:** 36 patients with undiagnosed hepatitis undergoing laparotomy
  * **Nearly 1/3 died**
  * **All acute hepatitis patients** (viral or alcoholic) died
  * **Complications:** Peritonitis, wound dehiscence, hepatic failure

**Modern considerations:**
* Improved diagnostic capabilities reduce likelihood of undiagnosed acute hepatitis
* **Consensus opinion:** Postpone elective surgery in acute hepatitis

## 34.5. RISK STRATIFICATION IN CHRONIC LIVER DISEASE

**Child-Pugh Classification:**  
![Child-Pugh Score](https://c7cfd6b7-3c34-4f3a-886b-08d1a3707c3a.ngrok-free.app/api/serve-image/child-pugh-score)

**Historical Child-Pugh mortality data:**
* **Child A:** 10% mortality
* **Child B:** 17-30% mortality  
* **Child C:** 60-80% mortality

**Comparison with non-surgical mortality:**
* **Child A:** 4% at 3 months
* **Child B:** 14% at 3 months
* **Child C:** 51% at 3 months

## 34.6. MELD SCORE AND RISK ASSESSMENT

**MELD calculation:**  
MELD = 9.57 × ln(creatinine mg/dL) + 3.78 × ln(bilirubin mg/dL) + 11.2 × ln(INR) + 6.43

**MELD performance characteristics:**
* **C-statistic for 30-day mortality:** 0.72 (0.80 for abdominal surgery)
* **Mortality correlation:**
  * **Up to MELD 20:** Each point = +1% mortality
  * **MELD >20:** Each point = +2% mortality

**Risk stratification:**
* **MELD <11:** Low postoperative mortality, acceptable surgical risk
* **MELD 25-30:** 50% 30-day mortality after abdominal surgery
* **MELD ≥20:** High mortality risk contraindicates elective procedures until post-transplant

**Additional risk factors:**
* **Age >70:** Equivalent to +3 MELD points
* **ASA status >IV:** Equivalent to +5 MELD points

## 34.7. VOCAL-PENN SCORE (2021)

**Development:** Veterans Affairs collaboration with University of Pennsylvania  
**Dataset:** >4,700 surgeries in 3,700 patients  
**Components:** 9 variables including:
* Bilirubin (only MELD component retained)
* 8 additional variables including surgery category

**Performance:** C-statistic 0.86 (superior to previous systems)  
**Availability:** Online calculator at <www.vocalpennscore.com>

## 34.8. RISK FACTOR ANALYSIS

**Studies identify additional mortality predictors:**
* Male gender
* Ascites presence
* Cryptogenic cirrhosis (vs. other etiologies)
* Elevated creatinine
* Preoperative infection
* Higher ASA physical status
* Respiratory system surgery

**Risk factor accumulation:**
* **6 risk factors:** >80% 1-year mortality
* **2 risk factors:** ~30% 1-year mortality

## 34.9. MEDICAL OPTIMIZATION STRATEGIES

**Preoperative optimization targets:**
1. **Treat active infection**
2. **Minimize vasoactive infusions**
3. **Optimize central blood volume and renal status**
4. **Minimize ascites**
5. **Improve encephalopathy**
6. **Address coagulopathy**

⚠️ **Evidence limitation:** Little data supporting specific goal-directed targets

## 34.10. SYSTEM-BY-SYSTEM ASSESSMENT IN CHRONIC LIVER DISEASE

**Comprehensive preoperative evaluation framework for patients with chronic liver disease undergoing surgery:**

### 34.10.1. CARDIOVASCULAR SYSTEM

**Cirrhotic cardiomyopathy:**
* **Features:**
  * Impaired contractile response to stress
  * Diastolic dysfunction
  * Electrophysiological abnormalities (prolonged QT)
* **Clinical significance:** May only manifest under surgical stress
* **Assessment:** Consider stress testing or dobutamine stress echo in high-risk patients

**Hyperdynamic circulation:**
* **Characteristics:**
  * Increased cardiac output
  * Decreased systemic vascular resistance
  * Reduced response to vasopressors
* **Pathophysiology:** Splanchnic and systemic vasodilation
* **Implications:**
  * Baseline hypotension common
  * Exaggerated response to hypovolemia
  * May require higher vasopressor doses intraoperatively

**Portopulmonary hypertension (POPH):**
* **Definition:** Pulmonary arterial hypertension in setting of portal hypertension
* **Screening:** Echocardiography (RV systolic pressure >50 mmHg warrants further evaluation)
* **Diagnosis confirmation:** Right heart catheterization
  * Mean PAP ≥25 mmHg
  * Pulmonary vascular resistance >240 dynes·s·cm⁻⁵
  * PCWP <15 mmHg
* **Perioperative risk:** High mortality if mean PAP >50 mmHg
* **Management considerations:** May preclude surgery or require preoperative pulmonary vasodilator therapy

### 34.10.2. RESPIRATORY SYSTEM

**Hepatopulmonary syndrome (HPS):**
* **Triad:**
  1. Liver disease/portal hypertension
  2. Intrapulmonary vascular dilatations
  3. Impaired oxygenation (A-a gradient ≥15 mmHg on room air)
* **Clinical features:**
  * Platypnea (dyspnea worse when upright)
  * Orthodeoxia (O₂ desaturation when upright)
* **Diagnosis:** Contrast echocardiography (bubble study), Tc-99m MAA scan
* **Severity grading:**
  * **Mild:** PaO₂ ≥80 mmHg
  * **Moderate:** 60-79 mmHg
  * **Severe:** 50-59 mmHg
  * **Very severe:** <50 mmHg
* **Perioperative implications:**
  * Increased pulmonary complications
  * Consider lung-protective ventilation strategies
  * Severe HPS may preclude surgery

**Pleural effusions:**
* **Hepatic hydrothorax:** Transdiaphragmatic migration of ascites
* **Management:** Optimize ascites control preoperatively
* **Intraoperative:** Risk of respiratory compromise if large

**Restrictive physiology:**
* Ascites and hepatomegaly reduce functional residual capacity
* Increased work of breathing
* Postoperative atelectasis risk

### 34.10.3. RENAL FUNCTION

**Hepatorenal syndrome (HRS):**
* **Type 1 HRS (AKI-HRS):**
  * Rapid progressive renal failure (doubling creatinine to >221 μmol/L within 2 weeks)
  * Very poor prognosis without liver transplant
  * **Perioperative mortality:** >80%
  * **Contraindication** to elective surgery
* **Type 2 HRS (CKD-HRS):**
  * Slowly progressive renal impairment
  * Associated with refractory ascites
  * Better prognosis than Type 1
  * **High perioperative risk**

**Assessment:**
* Baseline creatinine may underestimate renal dysfunction (reduced muscle mass)
* Consider GFR estimation
* Urine sodium <10 mmol/L suggests HRS vs. ATN

**Optimization:**
* Avoid nephrotoxins
* Maintain adequate intravascular volume
* Consider terlipressin and albumin if HRS diagnosed
* Close fluid balance monitoring

### 34.10.4. CENTRAL NERVOUS SYSTEM

**Hepatic encephalopathy (HE) grading:**
* **Grade 0 (minimal HE):** Subclinical, detected only by psychometric testing
* **Grade 1:** Mild confusion, sleep disturbance, irritability
* **Grade 2:** Lethargy, disorientation, inappropriate behavior, asterixis
* **Grade 3:** Somnolence, severe confusion, incoherent speech
* **Grade 4:** Coma, unresponsive to painful stimuli

**Perioperative implications:**
* **Any grade of HE:** Increased postoperative encephalopathy risk
* **Grade ≥2:** Consider delaying elective surgery
* **Triggers to avoid:**
  * Hypovolemia
  * Infection
  * GI bleeding
  * Excessive protein load
  * Benzodiazepines and opioids (use cautiously)

**Optimization:**
* **Lactulose:** Target 2-3 soft stools/day
* **Rifaximin:** Adjunct to lactulose
* **Identify and treat precipitants:** Infection, constipation, GI bleed
* **Nutrition:** Maintain adequate protein intake despite concerns

### 34.10.5. HAEMATOLOGICAL SYSTEM

**Rebalanced hemostasis concept:**
* **Traditional view:** Cirrhotics are "auto-anticoagulated" (prolonged PT/INR, low platelets)
* **Modern understanding:** Balanced deficiency of pro- and anticoagulant factors
  * **Decreased:** Factors II, V, VII, IX, X, XI, protein C, protein S, antithrombin
  * **Increased/normal:** Factor VIII, von Willebrand factor
  * **Result:** May have normal or even increased thrombin generation despite abnormal PT

**Clinical implications:**
* **Bleeding risk:** Not accurately predicted by PT/INR alone
* **Thrombosis risk:** May be underappreciated
  * Portal vein thrombosis common in cirrhosis
  * Postoperative VTE risk exists despite coagulopathy
* **Laboratory assessment:**
  * Standard tests (PT, aPTT, platelet count) don't reflect overall hemostatic balance
  * Consider viscoelastic testing (TEG/ROTEM) for better functional assessment

**Platelet considerations:**
* **Thrombocytopenia mechanisms:**
  * Splenic sequestration (portal hypertension)
  * Decreased thrombopoietin production
  * Bone marrow suppression (alcohol, HCV)
* **Platelet function:** May be impaired even with adequate count
* **Transfusion triggers:** Individualized based on procedure and bleeding risk
  * Generally target >50 × 10⁹/L for major surgery
  * Higher targets (>80 × 10⁹/L) for neuraxial anesthesia

**Anemia:**
* **Multifactorial:** GI bleeding, folate deficiency, hypersplenism, chronic disease
* **Assessment:** Rule out active bleeding
* **Optimization:** Correct deficiencies, consider transfusion if Hb <70-80 g/L

### 34.10.6. METABOLIC CONSIDERATIONS

**Glucose metabolism:**
* **Hypoglycemia risk:** Impaired gluconeogenesis and glycogen stores
* **Intraoperative:** Monitor glucose, provide dextrose-containing fluids if needed
* **Postoperative:** Continue monitoring, especially if prolonged NPO

**Protein-energy malnutrition:**
* **Common in cirrhosis:** Sarcopenia, muscle wasting
* **Assessment:** Clinical evaluation, albumin (though affected by synthetic function)
* **Implications:** Poor wound healing, infections, prolonged recovery
* **Optimization:** Preoperative nutritional support if time permits

**Electrolyte abnormalities:**
* **Hyponatremia:** Common (dilutional from SIADH/water retention)
  * Avoid rapid correction (risk of osmotic demyelination)
  * Target gradual correction if symptomatic
* **Hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia:** From diuretics, renal losses
  * Correct preoperatively
  * Monitor and replace intraoperatively

**Acid-base:**
* **Respiratory alkalosis:** Common from hyperventilation (hepatic encephalopathy)
* **Metabolic acidosis:** May indicate poor perfusion or advanced disease

### 34.10.7. ADDITIONAL CONSIDERATIONS

**Chronic alcohol use:**
* **Acute withdrawal risk:** Peak 48-72 hours, potentially fatal
* **Prevention:** CIWA protocol, benzodiazepine prophylaxis if indicated
* **Nutritional deficiencies:** Thiamine (Wernicke's), folate, B12
* **Cardiomyopathy:** Alcoholic cardiomyopathy independent of cirrhosis
* **Postoperative delirium:** Higher risk

**Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) specific:**
* **Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP):** Tumor marker, trend over time
* **Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) staging:** Guides treatment approach
* **Performance status:** ECOG score affects surgical candidacy
* **Vascular invasion:** Portal vein tumor thrombus contraindicates some procedures
* **Extrahepatic disease:** Must be ruled out before resection

**Future liver remnant (FLR) assessment:**
* **Critical consideration:** Adequate remnant liver volume essential for survival
* **Calculation:** FLR volume as percentage of total liver volume or body weight
* **Minimum requirements:**
  * **Normal liver:** FLR ≥25-30%
  * **Chemotherapy-injured liver:** FLR ≥30-40%
  * **Cirrhotic liver:** FLR ≥40-50%
* **Imaging:** CT or MRI volumetrics preoperatively
* **Augmentation strategies if inadequate:**
  * Portal vein embolization (PVE)
  * Two-stage hepatectomy
  * Associating liver partition and portal vein ligation (ALPPS)

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The system-by-system approach ensures comprehensive risk assessment beyond traditional scoring systems, identifying potentially modifiable factors and absolute contraindications to surgery.

## 34.11. SURGICAL APPROACH CONSIDERATIONS

**Risk depends more on:**
* **Operative site** (evidenced by VOCAL-Penn score)
* **Degree of liver impairment**
* **Less on:** Anesthetic technique

**Laparoscopic surgery considerations:**
* **Child A and B:** Appears safe for laparoscopic procedures
* **Benefits:** Lower mortality, shorter hospital stay
* **Child C:** May benefit from less invasive approaches (e.g., percutaneous gallbladder drainage)

**Laparoscopic cholecystectomy data:**
* **Cirrhotic patients:** ~1:100 mortality
* **Non-cirrhotic patients:** ~1:2000 mortality
* **Meta-analyses show:** Less blood loss, shorter operative time, shorter hospitalization vs. open approach

## 34.12. TIPS FOR SURGICAL RISK REDUCTION

**Rationale:** Preoperative portal decompression for severe portal hypertension  
**Potential benefits:** May improve outcomes in severe portal hypertension patients  
**Considerations:**
* **Increases pulmonary artery pressure**
* **May worsen encephalopathy**
* **Case-by-case evaluation needed**

**Bottom Line:** Perioperative risk assessment requires comprehensive evaluation using validated scoring systems (Child-Pugh, MELD, VOCAL-Penn), with MELD <11 representing acceptable risk and MELD ≥20 contraindicating elective procedures until post-transplant.

# 35. INTRAOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT AND MONITORING

## 35.1. MONITORING CONSIDERATIONS

**Arterial pressure monitoring indications:**
* Preoperative systemic hypotension (vasodilation)
* Anticipated blood loss
* Need for intraoperative laboratory studies
* Coexisting disease and advanced age
* **Hepatic resection:** Strong consideration for arterial line

**Central venous pressure (CVP) monitoring:**
* **Fluid responsiveness:** Usefulness questioned in modern practice
* **Expert opinion:** Some abandon CVP monitoring for liver resection
* **Current practice:** Do not place CVP catheter exclusively for monitoring

**Pulmonary artery catheterization:**
* **Known/suspected pulmonary hypertension**
* **Low cardiac ejection fraction**

**Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE):**
* **Comprehensive assessment:** Preload, contractility, ejection fraction, regional wall motion, emboli
* **Safety in varices:** Studies confirm safety, although transgastric views avoided
* **Value:** Universally aided diagnosis in small series

**Viscoelastic coagulation testing:**
* **TEG/thromboelastometry:** Useful guidance if available
* **Advantage:** Reflects overall proand anticoagulant factor balance
* **Modern understanding:** May show normal thrombin generation despite abnormal PT

## 35.2. REGIONAL VS. GENERAL ANESTHESIA

**Neuraxial anesthesia effects:**
* **Hepatic blood flow:** Related to systemic blood pressure alterations
* **High neuraxial blocks (T-5):** Appear to reduce hepatic blood flow
* **Catecholamine correction:** May not fully restore hepatic blood flow

**Contraindications to neuraxial blockade:**
* **Coagulopathy** (common in advanced liver disease)
* **Thrombocytopenia**
* **Standard contraindications:** Apply with case-by-case benefit-risk assessment

**Alternative regional techniques:**
* **Peripheral nerve blocks:** May be appropriate when neuraxial contraindicated
* **Transversus abdominis plane (TAP) blocks**
* **Quadratus lumborum (QL) blocks**
* **Success:** Documented for hepatobiliary procedures

## 35.3. VOLATILE ANESTHETIC CONSIDERATIONS

**Hepatic blood flow effects (1 MAC):**
* **Isoflurane:** Minimal reduction
* **Sevoflurane:** Minimal reduction
* **Desflurane:** 30% reduction (more substantial)
* **Halothane:** Greatest reduction (cardiovascular depression)

**Higher concentrations:**
* **Isoflurane:** Dose-dependent hepatic blood flow reduction
* **Sevoflurane:** No dose-dependent reduction
* **Hepatic arterial buffer response:** Maintained by sevoflurane and isoflurane

**Reactive metabolite concerns:**  
**Halothane hepatitis lessons:**
* **TFA metabolite production:** Correlates with hepatotoxicity risk
* **Metabolism percentages:**
  * Halothane: 20% (highest risk)
  * Enflurane: 2.5%
  * Isoflurane: 0.2%
  * Desflurane: 0.02%

**Modern volatile agent safety:**
* **Little evidence:** For hepatic injury from current agents
* **Sporadic reports:** Rare cases after repeated exposure
* **Sevoflurane uniqueness:** Does not produce TFA metabolites
* ⚠️ **Recommendation:** Patients with prior halogenated anesthetic hepatotoxicity should avoid ALL fluorinated anesthetics

## 35.4. NITROUS OXIDE

**Hepatocellular injury:** No evidence in absence of hepatic hypoxemia  
**Mild alcoholic hepatitis:** Techniques with N₂O not associated with biochemical worsening  
**Potential effects:**
* **Sympathomimetic:** May decrease hepatic blood flow
* **Methionine synthase inhibition:** Occurs after brief exposures
* **Clinical significance:** Unclear, though prolonged/repeated exposure could cause B12 deficiency

## 35.5. INTRAVENOUS ANESTHETIC CONSIDERATIONS

**Short-duration procedures:**
* **Propofol, etomidate, midazolam:** No hepatic function alteration
* **Advanced liver disease:** Effects with prolonged infusions not well studied

**Propofol infusion syndrome:**
* **Rare syndrome:** Lactic acidosis, lipemia, rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalemia, myocardial failure, death
* **Risk factors:** Prolonged infusions (>48h), high dose (>5 mg/kg/h)
* **Monitoring:** Watch for worsening lactate, escalating vasopressor requirements
* **Management:** Discontinue propofol, consider continuous hemofiltration

**Opioids:**
* **Hepatic function:** No independent effect beyond hepatic blood flow impact
* **Sphincter of Oddi:** All opioids increase pressure
* **Morphine vs. meperidine:** Review failed to show differential sphincter effect; morphine preferred in pancreatitis due to lower seizure risk

## 35.6. PHARMACOKINETIC AND PHARMACODYNAMIC CHANGES

**Drug clearance alterations:**
* **Decreased hepatocyte mass**
* **Reduced hepatic blood flow** (portocaval shunts)
* **Result:** Reduced metabolism of hepatically cleared drugs

**Extraction ratio concepts:**  
**Low extraction ratio (<0.3):**
* **Restrictive hepatic clearance**
* **Affected by:** Protein binding, enzyme induction/inhibition, age, pathology
* **NOT significantly affected by:** Hepatic blood flow
* **Examples:** Benzodiazepines (long half-lives, e.g., diazepam t₁/₂ = 43h)

**High extraction ratio (>0.7):**
* **Extensive first-pass metabolism**
* **Significantly affected by:** Hepatic blood flow changes
* **Short elimination half-lives** (e.g., propranolol t₁/₂ = 3.9h)
* **Clinical relevance:** Affected by hemodynamic changes, hepatic inflow clamping

**Benzodiazepine considerations:**
* **Midazolam:** Conflicting cirrhosis effects (possibly due to protein binding changes)
* **Protein binding changes:** As synthesis declines, free drug fraction increases
* **Complex interactions:** May result in unpredictable effects

**Enhanced pharmacodynamic sensitivity:**
* **Encephalopathy patients:** Increased sensitivity to sedatives and analgesics
* **Mechanism:** Altered CNS responsiveness independent of pharmacokinetic changes

## 35.7. SPECIFIC DRUG CLASSES

**Opioids:**
* **Metabolism reduction:** Liver disease reduces clearance
* **Dosing strategy:** Increase intervals to avoid accumulation
* **Agent differences:** Morphine and meperidine more affected than shorter synthetic opioids
* **Meperidine concern:** Normeperidine metabolite clearance reduced → neurotoxicity risk
* **Remifentanil exception:** Ester hydrolysis independent of hepatic function and duration

**Induction agents:**
* **High extraction ratios:** Ketamine, etomidate, propofol, thiopental
* **Clearance similarity:** Despite liver disease, similar to normal patients
* **Pharmacodynamic enhancement:** More pronounced effects
* **Duration prolongation:** Possible with dexmedetomidine and benzodiazepines

**Neuromuscular blocking agents:**  
**Hepatic metabolism (prolonged in liver disease):**
* **Vecuronium, rocuronium:** Intermediate duration agents
* **Pancuronium:** Also prolonged

**Resistance phenomena:**
* **Initial dose resistance:** Due to elevated γ-globulins and increased volume of distribution
* **Organ-independent elimination:** Atracurium and cisatracurium unaffected
* **Laudanosine metabolite:** Hepatically eliminated but neurotoxicity not reported
* **Succinylcholine:** Altered metabolism (reduced pseudocholinesterase) but rarely clinically significant

## 35.8. VASOPRESSOR RESPONSE

**Reduced responsiveness to:**
* **Angiotensin II**
* **Arginine vasopressin (AVP)**
* **Norepinephrine**

**Mechanism:** Likely mediated by nitric oxide, prostacyclin, and endothelial-derived factors responding to humoral and mechanical stimuli

**Bottom Line:** Intraoperative management requires careful drug selection considering altered pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics, with preference for agents with organ-independent elimination and awareness of enhanced CNS sensitivity.

## 36.3. THREE MAJOR ALBUMIN INDICATIONS IN CIRRHOTIC PATIENTS

**1. Large volume paracentesis:**
* **Threshold:** 4-5 L drainage
* **Relevance:** Intra-abdominal surgery context
* **Dose:** 6-8 g albumin per liter removed (when indicated)

**2. Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis:**
* **Indication:** Prevent renal impairment
* **Specific criteria:**
  * Bilirubin >4 mg/dL, OR
  * Creatinine >1 mg/dL
* **Mechanism:** SBP frequently precipitates HRS-AKI

**3. HRS-AKI treatment:**
* **Combination therapy:** With splanchnic vasoconstrictors
* **Proven benefit** in conjunction with vasopressor therapy

## 36.4. PRACTICAL APPLICATION

**Intraoperative considerations:**
* **Large volume ascites drainage:** Consider albumin replacement
* **High-risk patients:** Those with elevated bilirubin/creatinine
* **SBP risk:** High-risk procedures or patients with evidence of infection

**Bottom Line:** Volume resuscitation follows standard principles with specific attention to albumin replacement in high-risk scenarios involving large volume losses or SBP prevention/treatment.

## 37.2. HEPATIC RESECTION

**Historical perspective:**
* **Early 1900s:** Liver surgery considered dangerous/impossible
* **1908:** Pringle described hilar vessel compression
* **1952:** Lortat-Jacob performed first right hepatectomy
* **1957:** Couinaud described segmental anatomy
* **1980s mortality:** >20%
* **Current mortality:** <5% (1-2% for normal livers)

**Modern outcomes:**
* **Major hepatectomy:** ≥3 segments resection
* **Mortality unchanged:** Despite decreasing percentage requiring major resection
* **Future improvements:** Likely from parenchyma preservation and infection prevention

## 37.3. BLOOD LOSS MANAGEMENT STRATEGIES

**Transfusion requirements:** <20% of current patients  
**Central venous pressure (CVP) management:**
* **Traditional approach:** Maintain low CVP (<5 cm H₂O)
* **Rationale:** Reduce hepatic venous bleeding during parenchymal transection
* **Specific targets:**
  * **CVP:** 0-5 mmHg during transection phase
  * **SVV (stroke volume variation):** <13%
* **Current debate:** Necessity questioned given reduced blood loss in modern era
* **Renal function concerns:** Whether low CVP deleterious uncertain
* **Evidence:** Two living donor series showed CVP not predictive of blood loss

**Hypovolaemic phlebotomy technique:**
* **Method:** Controlled blood withdrawal to achieve target CVP
* **Blood storage:** Autologous blood stored for later reinfusion
* **Timing:**
  * Implement during parenchymal transection phase
  * Restore volume after completion of resection
* **Benefits:** Reduces back-bleeding from hepatic veins while maintaining autologous blood availability

**Meta-analysis conclusions:**
* **Optimal CVP lowering methods:** Uncertain
* **Low CVP benefit:** Does not necessarily reduce morbidity
* **CVP fluid responsiveness:** Reliability questioned in liver surgery

**Vascular occlusion techniques:**
**Portal triad clamping (Pringle manoeuvre):**
* **Technique:** Compression of hepatoduodenal ligament containing portal vein and hepatic artery
* **Afferent vessels occluded:** Portal vein, hepatic artery
* **Tolerance:** Better hemodynamic tolerance compared to total vascular occlusion
* **Liver function:** Minimal impact if intermittent
* **Cycling protocols:**
  * **Standard intermittent:** 20 minutes occlusion, 5 minutes reperfusion
  * **Alternative:** 15 minutes occlusion, 5 minutes reperfusion (as mentioned in ischemic preconditioning)
  * **Maximum safe duration:** Up to 75 minutes continuous (with preconditioning)
* **Benefits:** Reduces blood loss during parenchymal transection
* **Monitoring:** Watch for hemodynamic changes during clamping and reperfusion

**Total vascular occlusion:**
* **Includes:** Afferent vessels + supra/infrahepatic vena cava
* **More blood loss reduction** but greater hemodynamic impact

**Ischemic strategies:**  
**Ischemic preconditioning:**
* **Protocol:** 10-min occlusion + 10-min reperfusion, then continuous clamping up to 75 min
* **Comparison:** Equally effective to intermittent clamping (15-min cycles with 5-min reperfusion)
* **Advantage:** Less blood loss than intermittent technique

**Parenchymal transection devices:**

**Cavitron Ultrasonic Surgical Aspirator (CUSA):**
* **Mechanism:** Ultrasonic vibration fragments hepatic parenchyma while preserving vessels
* **Function:** Selective tissue disruption allowing vascular and biliary structure identification
* **Advantages:** Precise dissection, reduced blood loss
* **Technique:** Emulsifies hepatocytes while leaving fibrous structures intact

**Harmonic scalpel:**
* **Mechanism:** Ultrasonic energy for cutting and coagulation
* **Temperature:** Lower than traditional electrocautery (reduces thermal injury)
* **Applications:** Vessel sealing and parenchymal division
* **Benefits:** Minimal lateral thermal spread, reduced collateral tissue damage

**LigaSure device:**
* **Mechanism:** Bipolar electrothermal energy for vessel sealing
* **Capacity:** Can seal vessels up to 7mm diameter
* **Applications:** Vascular control during dissection
* **Advantages:** Reliable vessel sealing without need for clips or ties

**Technique integration:**
* Combination of devices often used based on surgeon preference
* Energy devices complement traditional clamp-crush technique
* Selection depends on vessel size, tissue characteristics, and resection complexity

**Vasopressor effects:**
* **Direct splanchnic effects:** Reduce splanchnic pressure
* **Result:** Decreased blood loss during resection

**Blood conservation adjuncts:**

**Cell salvage in cancer surgery:**
* **Traditional concern:** Theoretical risk of reintroducing malignant cells into circulation
* **Historical practice:** Avoided in oncological liver resection
* **Emerging evidence:**
  * Growing body of literature suggests no increased recurrence rates
  * Leukocyte depletion filters may reduce circulating tumor cell risk
  * Cost-benefit analysis increasingly favors use in major resections
* **Current controversy:** Practice varies by institution
* **Considerations:** Balance theoretical risk against benefits of avoiding allogeneic transfusion

**Tranexamic acid (TXA) in liver resection:**
* **Mechanism:** Antifibrinolytic agent, inhibits plasminogen activation
* **Potential benefits:**
  * Reduced intraoperative blood loss
  * Decreased transfusion requirements
* **Concerns in liver surgery:**
  * Thrombotic complications in patients with pre-existing liver disease
  * Patients with cirrhosis may have baseline hypercoagulable state
  * Portal vein thrombosis risk
  * Hepatic artery thrombosis risk
* **Evidence base:**
  * Limited high-quality RCT data specific to liver resection
  * Some studies show blood loss reduction without increased thrombosis
  * Others report increased thromboembolic events
* **Current controversy:** Routine use not established; risk-benefit assessment needed
* **Practice variation:** Some centers use selectively in high-risk bleeding cases

## 37.4. COMPLICATIONS AND CONSIDERATIONS

**Air embolism:**
* **Higher risk scenarios:**
  * Large hepatectomy (e.g., right lobectomy)
  * Tumor near vena cava
  * Portal vessel involvement
* **Low CVP concern:** May increase venous air embolism risk (unsubstantiated)

**Postoperative coagulation changes:**
* **Normal preoperative profiles:** Can still develop abnormal INR and platelet count
* **Severity correlation:** With extent of resection
* **Timeline:** Peak day 1-2, resolution 5+ days
* **Pain management implications:** Affects neuraxial analgesia decisions

**Epidural considerations:**
* **Divergent approaches:**
  * Some avoid preoperative placement
  * Others recommend correcting abnormalities before removal
* **Alternative evidence:** Brief hypercoagulable period may exist despite prolonged PT
* **Database review:** >27,000 surgeries showed no epidural effect on mortality/morbidity but +0.5 day length of stay

## 37.5. ALTERNATIVE ANALGESIC APPROACHES

**Rationale for avoiding epidural:**
* Postoperative coagulopathy risk (develops after resection)
* Concerns about safe catheter removal timing
* Platelet count nadirs day 1-2 post-resection

### 37.5.1. INTRATHECAL MORPHINE

**Pharmacology:**
* **Lipid solubility:** Morphine is lipid soluble, allowing it to cross the dura mater
* **Distribution:** Enters cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and binds to opioid receptors in spinal cord
* **Mechanism:** Direct spinal cord analgesia via mu-opioid receptor activation
* **Spread:** Can provide multi-dermatomal analgesia from single injection
* **Duration advantage:** Prolonged CSF residence time compared to IV administration

**Dosing for hepatopancreatobiliary surgery:**
* **Typical range:** 100-300 mcg (0.1-0.3 mg)
* **Standard HPB dose:** 200 mcg for major hepatobiliary/pancreatic procedures
* **Timing:** Administered at induction of anesthesia
* **Route:** Single-shot intrathecal injection (usually L3-4 or L4-5 interspace)
* **Considerations:** Lower doses (100-150 mcg) in elderly or opioid-naive patients

**Duration of action:**
* **Analgesic effect:** Up to 24 hours post-administration
* **Peak effect:** 1-2 hours after injection
* **Offset:** Gradual decline over 18-24 hours
* **Advantage:** Covers critical immediate postoperative period

**Side effects and management:**

**Pruritus (most common):**
* **Incidence:** 50-100% of patients
* **Mechanism:** Central opioid receptor activation (not histamine-mediated)
* **Severity:** Usually mild to moderate
* **Management:**
  * Often self-limiting
  * Low-dose naloxone infusion (0.25-1 mcg/kg/hr) if severe
  * Antihistamines generally ineffective (not histamine-mediated)
  * Ondansetron may provide some relief

**Urinary retention:**
* **Incidence:** 15-90% depending on dose and patient factors
* **Mechanism:** Sacral parasympathetic inhibition
* **Management:**
  * Indwelling urinary catheter typically placed for major surgery
  * Monitor for retention after catheter removal
  * May require temporary recatheterization

**Respiratory depression:**
* **Timing:** Biphasic pattern
  * **Early phase:** 0-2 hours (rare, usually from co-administered systemic opioids)
  * **Late phase:** 6-12 hours (true intrathecal effect as morphine spreads rostrally)
* **Peak risk:** 8-12 hours post-injection
* **Incidence:** <1% with appropriate dosing
* **Risk factors:**
  * Advanced age
  * Concurrent systemic opioids
  * Sleep apnea
  * Doses >300 mcg
* **Monitoring requirements:**
  * Continuous pulse oximetry for 24 hours
  * Regular respiratory rate assessment (minimum 2-hourly)
  * Sedation scoring
  * Nurse-led monitoring protocols essential
* **Management:**
  * Supplemental oxygen as needed
  * Naloxone for clinically significant depression
  * Avoid excessive concurrent systemic opioids

**Nausea and vomiting:**
* **Incidence:** 30-60%
* **Mechanism:** Chemoreceptor trigger zone stimulation, delayed gastric emptying
* **Management:**
  * Multimodal prophylaxis (ondansetron, dexamethasone)
  * Low-dose naloxone if severe and refractory
  * Avoid in patients with high baseline PONV risk

**Clinical advantages for liver resection:**
* **Coagulopathy avoidance:** No indwelling catheter requiring removal
* **Excellent analgesia:** Comparable to epidural for first 24 hours
* **Opioid-sparing:** Reduces need for systemic opioids
* **Simplicity:** Single injection technique
* **Cost-effective:** No catheter materials or infusion pumps

**Contraindications:**
* Patient refusal
* Infection at injection site
* Coagulopathy at time of injection (note: different from epidural - no catheter to remove)
* Elevated intracranial pressure
* Known allergy to morphine

**Alternative intrathecal opioids:**
* **Fentanyl:** Shorter duration (3-4 hours), less side effects
* **Hydromorphone:** Intermediate duration (12-18 hours)
* Morphine preferred for major HPB surgery due to prolonged duration

### 37.5.2. OTHER ANALGESIC MODALITIES

**Local anesthetic infusion systems:**
* Continuous wound infiltration catheters
* May be less effective for deep visceral pain
* Useful adjunct to systemic analgesia

**Regional blocks:**
* **Erector spinae plane blocks:** Effective for somatic pain
* **Posterior quadratus lumborum blocks:** Equally effective for laparoscopic resection
* **Bilateral technique:** Often required for midline/bilateral incisions
* **Limitations:** May not adequately cover visceral pain component

## 37.6. EVOLVING SURGICAL TECHNIQUES

**Examples of newer approaches:**
* **Minimally invasive liver surgery**
* **Thermal ablation** of hepatic tumors
* **Portal vein embolization:** Induce remnant liver hypertrophy

**Complication rates:** Remain high (52% after hemihepatectomy)  
**Common complications:**
* Pleural effusions
* Biliary leakage
* Wound dehiscence
* Ascites
* Intra-abdominal abscess

**Risk factor:** Higher preoperative MELD score associated with complications

**Bottom Line:** Modern hepatic surgery has evolved significantly with improved outcomes, but requires careful attention to hemodynamic management, coagulation monitoring, and alternative pain management strategies due to procedure-related coagulopathy.

* **Persistent jaundice**
* **Severe enzyme elevations** (several times upper limit)

## 38.4. MECHANISMS OF POSTOPERATIVE HEPATIC DYSFUNCTION

## 38.5. MAJOR CAUSES OF POSTOPERATIVE JAUNDICE

**Blood product-related:**
* **Hematoma reabsorption:** Surgical or traumatic
* **Transfusion hemolysis:** 10% of transfused cells hemolyze within 24 hours
* **Bilirubin load:** 250 mg per unit transfused
* **Timeline:** Liver may require time to clear significant bilirubin load

**Hepatic hypoxemia mechanisms:**
* **Cardiopulmonary:** Pneumonia, atelectasis, heart failure
* **Hypoperfusion:** Secondary to shock
* **Anemia**
* **Fever**
* **Surgery itself:** Decreases hepatic blood flow

## 38.7. UNCONJUGATED HYPERBILIRUBINEMIA

**Suggests:**
* **Hemolysis**
* **Inherited disorders:** Gilbert syndrome, Crigler-Najjar syndrome

**Gilbert and Crigler-Najjar syndromes:**
* **Mechanism:** Absent or markedly decreased bilirubin glucuronyltransferase
* **Result:** Unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia
* **Perioperative significance:** Rarely cause surgical/anesthetic problems

**Other hemolytic causes:**
* **Hemoglobinopathies:** Sickle cell disease
* **Enzyme defects:** G6PD deficiency
* **Transfusion reactions**
* **Prosthetic heart valves**

## 38.8. CONJUGATED HYPERBILIRUBINEMIA (>50% CONJUGATED)

**Suggests hepatocellular dysfunction:** Requires investigation for:
* **Hypoxemia**
* **Toxic reactions**
* **Unsuspected preexisting liver disease**
* **Trauma**
* **Congenital disorders:** Dubin-Johnson, Rotor syndromes

**Dubin-Johnson and Rotor syndromes:**
* **Mechanism:** Defect in bilirubin excretion
* **Result:** Conjugated bilirubinemia
* **Perioperative effect:** Surgery can worsen these syndromes

## 38.9. PROCEDURE-SPECIFIC RISK FACTORS

**Surgical site importance:**
* **Abdominal surgery:** Significantly reduces hepatic blood flow
* **Mechanism:** Abdominal traction elevates prostaglandin levels
* **Risk hierarchy:** Procedure type affects postoperative liver failure risk

**Hepatic resection considerations:**
* **High risk:** For postoperative liver failure
* **Patient factors:** Many have HCC with underlying cirrhosis/chronic hepatitis
* **Functional reserve:** Difficult to estimate in cirrhotic liver

**Resection mortality data (>700 cases):**
* **Obstructive jaundice + malignancy:** 21% mortality
* **Cirrhosis:** 8.7% mortality
* **Normal liver:** 1% mortality

**Hepatorenal syndrome association:**
* **Renal failure + hyperbilirubinemia:** >50% mortality
* **Mechanism:** Low intestinal bile salts promote endotoxin absorption
* **Inflammatory mediators:** Precipitate renal hypoperfusion

## 38.10. CARDIAC SURGERY IN CIRRHOTIC PATIENTS

**Extremely poor tolerance:**
* **Overall perioperative mortality:** 31%
* **Child B cirrhosis:** 80% mortality
* **Mechanism:** Cardiopulmonary bypass exacerbates hepatic disease by unknown mechanisms

**Systematic review findings:**
* **1-year mortality by Child class:**
  * Child A: 27%
  * Child B: 66%  
  * Child C: 79%
* **Comparison:** Non-cirrhotic cardiac surgery mortality ~2%

## 38.11. RISK ASSESSMENT SUMMARY

**Key principle:** Nature and site of surgical procedure is important risk factor  
**High-risk procedures:**
* **Abdominal surgery** (especially upper abdomen)
* **Cardiac surgery** (especially in cirrhotics)
* **Hepatic resection**

**Patient factors:**
* **Preexisting liver disease severity**
* **Functional hepatic reserve**
* **Concurrent organ dysfunction** (especially renal)

**Bottom Line:** Postoperative liver dysfunction results from multiple mechanisms, with surgical site and preexisting liver disease being major risk factors, requiring systematic evaluation to distinguish between hemolytic, hepatocellular, and cholestatic causes.
