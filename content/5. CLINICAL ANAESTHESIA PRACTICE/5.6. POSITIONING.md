# 1. GENERAL PRINCIPLES OF PATIENT POSITIONING

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physiological Principles|Part A: Physiological Principles]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1: Airway, Oxygenation & Ventilation|Part B: C1]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C4: Trauma, Resuscitation & Crisis|Part B: C4]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Patient Care|Part C: Patient Care]]

**Exam Weighting:** High (Foundational concept for all anesthetic procedures)

**Patient positioning during anesthesia represents a balance between surgical requirements, physiological considerations, and patient safety concerns.**

## 1.2. GOALS OF OPTIMAL POSITIONING

* **Surgical requirements:** Positioning a patient for surgery is a compromise between the patient's physiological tolerance and surgical access needs
* **Facilitating optimal surgical access** to the target anatomy
* **Facilitating anesthesia management**
  * Airway access and management
  * IV access
  * Monitoring neuromuscular blockade and depth of anesthesia
* **Ensuring staff safety** during procedures
* **Patient protection**
  * Minimizing pressure on vulnerable points
  * Protection from X-ray exposure
  * Protection from laser exposure
* **Maintaining physiological homeostasis**
  * Cardiovascular stability
  * Respiratory function
  * Neurological integrity

## 1.3. POSITIONING RISK FACTORS

### 1.3.1. PATIENT-RELATED FACTORS

* **Age:** Elderly patients have less tissue padding and more fragile skin
* **Body habitus:**
  * Very high BMI - pressure distribution challenges
  * Very low BMI - less padding over bony prominences
* **Comorbidities:**
  * Diabetes - peripheral neuropathy predisposes to further nerve damage
  * Rheumatoid arthritis - joint instability and deformity
  * Pre-existing neuropathies
  * Vascular disease - compromised perfusion
* **Gender:** Men have a more exposed ulnar nerve at the elbow, and are more susceptible to ulnar neuropathies (70-90% of patients with this complication)

### 1.3.2. SURGICAL FACTORS

* **Duration of surgery** - prolonged procedures (>4 hours) increase risk
* **Use of tourniquet**
* **Procedures directly involving nerves**
* **High-risk positions** (prone, lateral, extreme positions)
* **Operations on high-risk areas** such as the neck

### 1.3.3. ANESTHETIC FACTORS

* **Hypotension** - reduces perfusion to compressed tissues
* **Hypoxia** - compounds ischemic injury
* **Hypothermia** - exacerbates tissue vulnerability
* **General anesthesia** eliminates protective reflexes and pain sensation
* **Anesthetic drugs** may promote systemic inflammation, potentially contributing to perioperative neuropathies

## 1.4. MECHANISMS OF POSITIONING INJURY

* **Direct tissue compression** causing ischemia and tissue damage
  * Most common at bony prominences and nerve pressure points
  * Severity correlates with pressure intensity and duration
  * Proper padding reduces but does not eliminate risk
* **Neural injury mechanisms**
  * **Direct trauma** to nerves during positioning
  * **Compression** causing local ischemia
  * **Stretch** beyond normal anatomical limits
	* ⚠️ Stretching nerves >5% beyond resting length can kink feeding arterioles and result in ischemia
  * **Ischemia** from vascular compromise
* **Inflammatory neuropathies**
  * 💡 Recent evidence suggests many perioperative neuropathies may have inflammatory rather than mechanical etiology
  * Microvasculitic neuropathies have been observed in patients with severe postoperative neuropathies
  * Perioperative factors like anesthetic drugs and blood transfusions may promote systemic inflammation
  * Immunosuppression during major procedures may allow viral activation in neural tissues

## 1.5. CLASSIFICATION OF NERVE INJURIES

* **Neuropraxia**
  * Mildest form of nerve injury
  * Temporary loss of nerve function
  * Complete recovery expected
* **Axonotmesis**
  * Substantial axon damage
  * Myelin sheath remains intact
  * Variable recovery
  * Nerves regenerate at approximately 2mm per day
* **Neurotmesis**
  * Most severe form
  * Complete transection of the nerve
  * Rarely yields full recovery
  * Often requires surgical intervention

## 1.6. DOCUMENTATION AND MEDICOLEGAL CONSIDERATIONS

* **Documentation importance:**
  * Careful description of positions used during anesthesia and surgery should be included in anesthesia records
  * Special protective measures (eye care, padding) should be documented
  * In complicated cases, a separate brief description in the patient's record is advisable
* **Medicolegal aspects:**
  * Positioning-related complications often lead to medicolegal entanglements
  * Anesthesia and operating room records may be absent or uninformative
  * Only through proper documentation can subsequent inquiries be properly answered
* **Perioperative assessment:**
  * Pre-existing conditions affecting positioning should be noted
  * 4 When possible, patients should be positioned while awake to verify comfort
  * Post-procedure assessment for positioning-related complications is recommended

**Bottom Line:** Patient positioning requires balancing surgical needs with physiological considerations. Multiple mechanisms can cause positioning injuries, including direct compression, stretch, and possibly inflammatory processes. Thorough documentation and preventive measures are essential for patient safety and medicolegal protection.

# 2. SUPINE POSITIONS

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physiological Principles|Part A: Physiological Principles]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1: Airway, Oxygenation & Ventilation|Part B: C1]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2: Cardiovascular System|Part B: C2]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Patient Care|Part C: Patient Care]]

**Exam Weighting:** High (Most common surgical position)

**The supine position places the patient on their back with various modifications for specific surgical access needs.**

## 2.2. VARIATIONS OF SUPINE POSITIONS

### 2.2.1. HORIZONTAL SUPINE

* **Basic configuration:**
  * Patient lies on back with a small pillow beneath the head
  * Arms are either alongside the trunk (padded and restrained) or abducted on well-padded arm boards
  * Arms may be extended ventrally with flexed forearm secured to an elevated frame
* **Positioning considerations:**
  * Bony contact points at occiput, elbows, and heels should be padded
  * Ensure brachial neurovascular bundle is neither stretched nor compressed at axilla
  * Avoid skin-to-metal contact to prevent electrical burns if cautery is used
  * Ensure adequate perfusion of hands
  * Lumbar spine may need padded support to prevent postoperative backache
* 💡 Though widely used, horizontal supine does not place hip and knee joints in neutral positions and is poorly tolerated for prolonged periods by awake patients

### 2.2.2. CONTOURED SUPINE (LAWN CHAIR)

* **Configuration:**
  * Operating table arranged so trunk-thigh hinge is angulated ~15 degrees
  * Thigh-knee hinge angulated a similar amount in opposite direction
  * Alternative: place rolled towel, pillow, or blanket beneath patient's knees
* **Benefits:**
  * Places hips and knees in gentle flexion
  * More comfortable for prolonged periods
  * Reduces lumbar spine stress

### 2.2.3. LATERAL UTERINE OR ABDOMINAL MASS DISPLACEMENT

* **Purpose:** Prevent aortocaval syndrome (supine hypotensive syndrome)
  * Large abdominal mass (e.g., pregnant uterus, very large tumor) can compress great vessels when supine
* **Technique:**
  * Roll compressive mass toward left hemiabdomen
  * Use leftward tilt of tabletop or wedge under right hip
  * Restores significant degree of perfusion

### 2.2.4. LITHOTOMY POSITIONS

#### 2.2.4.1. STANDARD LITHOTOMY

* **Configuration:**
  * Patient supine with arms extended laterally to <90 degrees on arm boards
  * Lower extremities flexed at hip and knee
  * Thighs flexed approximately 90 degrees on trunk
  * Knees bent to maintain lower legs nearly parallel to floor
* **Considerations:**
  * ⚠️ Hip flexion >90 degrees increases stretch of inguinal ligaments, risking lateral femoral cutaneous nerve impingement
  * Various leg-holding devices (e.g., "candy canes" or stirrups) must be fitted to patient's stature
  * Avoid compression of popliteal space or upper dorsal thigh
  * Compartment syndromes can result from prolonged use of this position

#### 2.2.4.2. LOW LITHOTOMY

* **Configuration:** Thigh elevation only ~30-45 degrees
* **Applications:**
  * Most urologic procedures
  * Procedures requiring simultaneous access to abdomen and perineum
* **Benefits:**
  * Reduces perfusion gradients to/from lower extremities
  * Improves lateral access to perineal surgical site

#### 2.2.4.3. HIGH LITHOTOMY

* **Configuration:**
  * Feet suspended from high poles
  * Legs almost fully extended on thighs
  * Thighs flexed 90 degrees or more on trunk
* **Considerations:**
  * Creates significant uphill gradient for arterial perfusion to feet
  * ⚠️ Requires careful avoidance of systemic hypotension
  * Significant variation in lower extremity perfusion pressure between patients
  * Less mobile patients may tolerate this posture poorly due to:
	* Angulation/compression of femoral canal contents by inguinal ligament
	* Stretch of sciatic nerve

#### 2.2.4.4. EXAGGERATED LITHOTOMY

* **Configuration:**
  * Pelvis flexed ventrally on spine
  * Thighs almost forcibly flexed on trunk
  * Lower legs aimed skyward
  * Long axis of pubic symphysis nearly parallel to floor
* **Applications:** Transperineal access to retropubic area
* **Risks:**
  * ⚠️ Stresses lumbar spine
  * Significant uphill gradient for foot perfusion
  * May restrict ventilation due to abdominal compression by bulky thighs
  * ⚠️ Very high frequency of lower extremity compartment syndrome
  * Pre-existing painful lumbar spine disease may be severely accentuated

## 2.3. COMPLICATIONS OF SUPINE POSITIONS

### 2.3.1. BRACHIAL PLEXUS NEUROPATHY

* **Epidemiology:** 56% of positioning-related perioperative peripheral nerve injuries involve brachial plexus
* **Risk factors:**
  * Arm out to side positioning
  * Multiple contributing factors (typically multifactorial)

### 2.3.2. ROOT INJURIES

* **Shoulder braces:**
  * ⚠️ When placed tight against base of neck can compress/injure brachial plexus roots with steep head-down positions
  * Less harmful when placed laterally over acromioclavicular joint
  * Use of shoulder braces should generally be discouraged
  * Similar risks with straps/tape from above shoulders to prevent cephalad sliding
* **Head positioning:**
  * Lateral displacement of head while supine can stretch brachial plexus roots on the side of obtuse head-shoulder angle
  * ⚠️ Risk increases if upper extremity is fixed to operating table at wrist while head moves laterally
  * Exaggerated rotation of head away from extended arm may be associated with brachial plexus injury

### 2.3.3. STERNAL RETRACTION RELATED INJURIES

* **Arm positioning during sternotomy:**
  * May have both arms padded and secured alongside torso, or both abducted
  * First-rib fractures and brachial plexus injuries can occur with median sternotomies
  * Injury extent related to retractor displacement amount
  * Studies suggest surgical manipulation more contributory than extremity positioning
  * Less slowing of somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEPs) when both arms are abducted vs. tucked

### 2.3.4. LONG THORACIC NERVE DYSFUNCTION

* **Presentation:** Serratus anterior muscle dysfunction and "winging" of scapula
* **Anatomy:**
  * Nerve arises from C5, C6, and C7
  * C5 and C6 fibers course through middle scalene muscle
  * Not routinely involved in brachial plexus stretch injuries
* **Etiology:** Relationship to intraoperative positioning remains speculative; may be coincidental neuropathy of viral or inflammatory origin

### 2.3.5. AXILLARY TRAUMA FROM THE HUMERAL HEAD

* **Mechanism:**
  * ⚠️ Abduction of arm on arm board to >90 degrees may thrust humeral head into axillary neurovascular bundle
  * When arm abducted >90 degrees, neurovascular bundle moves to extension side of joint
  * Bundle becomes both compressed and stretched
  * Can damage neural structures and compromise perfusion

### 2.3.6. RADIAL NERVE COMPRESSION

* **Anatomy:**
  * Arises from roots C6-8 and T1
  * Passes dorsolaterally around humerus in musculospiral groove
  * Vulnerable at lateral aspect of arm ~3 fingerbreadths proximal to lateral epicondyle
* **Compression risks:**
  * Pressure from vertical bar of anesthesia screen against lateral arm
  * Excessive cycling of automatic blood pressure cuff
  * Compression by sheets, towels, "toboggans" used to tuck arms
  * Arm boards and slings in lateral positions

### 2.3.7. MEDIAN NERVE DYSFUNCTION

* **Epidemiology:** Isolated perioperative injuries uncommon
* **Potential mechanisms:**
  * Iatrogenic trauma during venipuncture in antecubital fossa
  * Forced elbow extension after muscle relaxants in patients with limited elbow extension
  * Most common in men 20-40 years old who cannot easily extend elbows completely

### 2.3.8. ULNAR NEUROPATHY

* **Epidemiology:**
  * Most common perioperative neuropathy
  * Incidence of persistent perioperative ulnar neuropathy: 6.6 per 100,000 anesthetics
  * Increased with longer procedures
  * 70-90% of patients are men
* **Etiology:**
  * Unclear and likely multifactorial
  * Can develop in both surgical and medical patients
  * Many patients have contralateral ulnar nerve conduction dysfunction (suggesting pre-existing abnormalities)
  * Many patients do not notice symptoms until >48 hours after surgery
* **Mechanisms:**
  * **Elbow flexion:**
	* Compression by aponeurosis of flexor carpi ulnaris muscle and cubital tunnel retinaculum when elbow flexed >110 degrees
	* Anterior subluxation/dislocation of ulnar nerve over medial epicondyle (occurs in ~16% of people)
	* Recurrent mechanical trauma from nerve subluxation
  * **External compression:**
	* Can occur distal to medial epicondyle where nerve is relatively superficial
	* Ulnar nerve passes posteromedially to tubercle of coronoid process with minimal covering
* **Gender differences:**
  * Tubercle of coronoid process is ~1.5 times larger in men
  * Less adipose tissue over medial elbow in men
  * Men may have thicker cubital tunnel retinaculum
* **Clinical manifestations:**
  * Numbness, tingling, or pain in sensory distribution of ulnar nerve
  * Significant dysfunction can exist before symptoms appear
  * Many patients have bilateral symptoms
  * Timing of symptom recognition important for determining causation
  * Motor effects: Weakness of ring and little finger flexion, weak finger abduction/adduction, weak thumb adduction
  * Sensory effects: Medial surface of hand (dorsum and palm), little finger, medial half of ring finger
* **Preventive measures:**
  * During preanesthetic interview, inquire about history of ulnar neuropathies
  * Record findings and discuss possibility of postoperative recurrence
  * Consider assessing and documenting ulnar nerve function before PACU discharge

### 2.3.9. OTHER SUPINE POSITION PROBLEMS

#### 2.3.9.1. ARM COMPLICATIONS

* **Arm board security:**
  * ⚠️ Must be securely attached to prevent accidental release
  * Unsecured arm can slip over edge, resulting in:
	* Shoulder joint capsule injury from excessive dorsal humerus extension
	* Fracture of osteoporotic humeral neck
	* Ulnar nerve injury at elbow
* **Anterior interosseous nerve injury:**
  * ⚠️ Can occur with excessively tight strap across supinated forearm
  * Nerve and artery compressed against tough interosseous membrane
  * Resembles compartment syndrome in lower extremity
  * May require prompt surgical decompression

#### 2.3.9.2. BACKACHE AND PARAPLEGIA

* **Lumbar support:**
  * 4 Backache worsened by ligamentous relaxation during anesthesia
  * Loss of normal lumbar curvature in supine position is often the issue
  * Padding under lumbar spine before induction may help retain lordosis
  * ⚠️ Hyperlordosis should be avoided (especially >10 degrees at L2-3 apex)
  * Extreme hyperlordosis has resulted in paraplegia during pelvic procedures

#### 2.3.9.3. COMPARTMENT SYNDROME

* **Etiology in supine positions:**
  * Systemic hypotension and loss of extremity perfusion pressure (augmented by elevation)
  * Vascular obstruction by intrapelvic retractors, excessive joint flexion, or popliteal pressure
  * External compression by tight straps, leg wrappings, pressure from surgical assistant's arm
  * Tight arm straps or "draw sheets" may compress anterior interosseous bundle
* **Risk factors:**
  * Prolonged lithotomy posture in excess of 5 hours particularly high risk
  * Occurs in approximately 1 in 9,000 patients in lateral positions as well
  * Lateral position tends to affect arms; lithotomy affects lower extremities
* **Characteristics:**
  * Inadequate perfusion leads to ischemia, hypoxic edema, elevated tissue pressure
  * Extensive rhabdomyolysis
  * Potential for lasting damage to muscles and nerves in the compartment

**Bottom Line:** Supine positioning, while appearing straightforward, carries significant risks, particularly to peripheral nerves. The brachial plexus, ulnar, radial, and median nerves are especially vulnerable. Careful attention to arm positioning, avoidance of excessive joint angulation, and protection of pressure points are essential preventive measures. The etiology of perioperative neuropathies is likely multifactorial, involving both mechanical and possibly inflammatory mechanisms.

# 3. LATERAL POSITIONS

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physiological Principles|Part A: Physiological Principles]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C4: Trauma, Resuscitation & Crisis|Part B: C4]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Patient Care|Part C: Patient Care]]

**Exam Weighting:** Medium

**Lateral positioning involves placing the patient on their side, with various modifications for specific surgical requirements.**

## 3.2. GENERAL LATERAL POSITIONING CONSIDERATIONS

* **Venous pooling management:**
  * Compressive bandages for legs and thighs may help combat venous pooling
  * ⚠️ Marked flexion of lower extremities can obstruct venous return to IVC through:
	* Angulation of vessels at popliteal space and inguinal ligament
	* Thigh compression against abdomen (especially in obese patients)
* **Axillary protection:**
  * Small support placed just caudad to downside axilla
  * Lifts thorax enough to relieve pressure on axillary neurovascular bundle
  * Helps prevent disturbed blood flow to downside arm and hand
  * May decrease shoulder discomfort postoperatively
  * 💡 Support should only contact chest wall, not impinge on axilla

## 3.3. VARIATIONS OF LATERAL POSITIONS

### 3.3.1. STANDARD LATERAL POSITION

* **Configuration:**
  * Patient rolled onto one side on flat table surface
  * Stabilized by flexing the downside thigh
  * Downside knee bent to retain leg on table and improve trunk stabilization
  * Common peroneal nerve padded to minimize compression damage
  * Upside thigh and leg extended comfortably with pillows between lower extremities
  * Head supported with pillows or headrest for proper cervical/thoracic spine alignment
  * Small pad placed just caudad to downside axilla to raise chest wall
* **Arm positioning options:**
  * Extended ventrally and retained on single arm board with padding between them
  * Individually retained on padded two-level arm support
  * Both elbows flexed with arms on padding in front of patient's face
* **Stabilization techniques:**
  * Retaining tapes/straps across hip fixed to underside of tabletop
  * ⚠️ Hip tapes/straps must be between iliac crest and femoral head (not over femoral head)
  * Additional restraining tape/strap across thorax/shoulders if needed
  * "Bean bag" or vacuum-supported retention devices commonly used

### 3.3.2. SEMISUPINE AND SEMIPRONE

* **Purpose:** Allow surgical access to anterolateral (semisupine) and posterolateral (semiprone) trunk structures
* **Considerations for semisupine:**
  * Upside arm must be carefully supported
  * Avoid hyperextension and traction/compression of brachial/axillary neurovascular bundles
  * Supporting bar should be well wrapped to prevent electrical grounding
  * Sufficient noncompressible padding under torso and hip prevents rolling supine
  * Check restrained wrist pulse to ensure adequate circulation

### 3.3.3. FLEXED LATERAL POSITIONS

#### 3.3.3.1. LATERAL JACKKNIFE

* **Configuration:**
  * Downside iliac crest positioned over hinge between back and thigh sections
  * Table angulated at that point to flex thighs on trunk laterally
  * Table chassis tipped so uppermost surface of flank/thorax becomes horizontal
  * Results in feet below atrial level - may pool blood in leg vessels
* **Purpose:** Stretch upside flank and widen intercostal spaces for thoracotomy
* **Limitations:**
  * Significant physiologic impact due to:
	* Lumbar stress
	* Restriction of upside costal margin movement by taut flank
	* Blood pooling in depressed lower extremities
  * Once rib-spreading retractor is placed, position has reduced value

#### 3.3.3.2. KIDNEY POSITION

* **Configuration:**
  * Similar to lateral jackknife but adds elevated rest under downside iliac crest
  * Increases lateral flexion
  * Improves access to upside kidney under overhanging costal margin
* **Considerations:**
  * ⚠️ Strict stabilization needed to prevent caudad shifting
  * If patient shifts, elevated rest could relocate into downside flank and impede ventilation
  * Unlike lateral jackknife, no useful alternative exists for flank approach to kidney
  * Requires vigilant anesthesia management and hopefully rapid surgery

## 3.4. COMPLICATIONS OF LATERAL POSITIONS

### 3.4.1. EYE AND EAR INJURIES

* **Eye injuries:**
  * Unlikely if head properly supported during and after turning
  * ⚠️ If face turns toward mattress with unprotected eyes, corneal abrasions possible
  * Direct pressure on globe can:
	* Displace crystalline lens
	* Increase intraocular pressure
	* Cause ischemia (particularly with systemic hypotension)
* **Ear injuries:**
  * Weight of head can press downside ear against rough/wrinkled support
  * Padding with pillow or foam sponge usually provides sufficient protection
  * ⚠️ Check that external ear has not been folded over when placing head support

### 3.4.2. NECK INJURIES

* **Lateral neck flexion:**
  * Possible with inadequate head support
  * Can cause postoperative neck pain, especially with arthritic cervical spine
  * May intensify symptoms from cervical disk protrusion

### 3.4.3. SUPRASCAPULAR NERVE INJURY

* **Mechanism:**
  * Ventral circumduction of dependent shoulder rotates suprascapular notch away from neck root
  * Suprascapular nerve is fixed both paravertebrally and at notch
  * Circumduction stretches nerve, producing diffuse, dull shoulder pain
* **Diagnosis and treatment:**
  * Established by blocking nerve at notch and producing pain relief
  * May require resecting ligament over notch to decompress nerve
* **Prevention:**
  * Supporting pad under thorax caudad of axilla
  * Pad should be thick enough to raise chest off shoulder
  * Prevents circumduction stretch injury to the nerve

**Bottom Line:** Lateral positioning requires careful attention to stabilization and support of the dependent side. Key considerations include preventing axillary neurovascular compression, avoiding nerve stretch injuries (particularly suprascapular nerve), and providing adequate padding for eyes, ears, and bony prominences. The lateral jackknife and kidney positions add additional physiological challenges that require vigilant monitoring.

# 4. PRONE POSITIONS

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physiological Principles|Part A: Physiological Principles]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C4: Trauma, Resuscitation & Crisis|Part B: C4]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S3: Spine Surgery|Part B: S3]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Patient Care|Part C: Patient Care]]

**Exam Weighting:** High (Critical for spine surgery and increasing use in critical care)

**The prone position places the patient face-down, requiring special considerations for airway access, ventilation, circulation, and protection of vulnerable structures.**

## 4.2. FULL PRONE POSITION

* **Configuration:**
  * Trunk elevated off supporting surface so ventral abdominal wall is free of compression
  * Head and lower extremities typically below spine level
  * Table may be angulated at trunk-thigh hinge to remove lumbar lordosis
  * Table chassis can be rotated head up to level patient's back
* **Circulatory considerations:**
  * Significant perfusion gradient may develop between legs and heart
  * Wrapping legs in compressive bandages or full-length elastic hosiery minimizes blood pooling
  * ⚠️ When head is lower than heart, pressure gradient can cause venous and lymphatic stasis in head
	* May result in facial and airway edema (challenging extubation)
	* Associated with ischemic optic neuropathy and vision loss
* **Support systems:**
  * Various pelvic, abdominal, and chest supports available:
	* Parallel rolls of tightly packed sheets
	* Gels
	* Padded and adjustable metal frames
	* Four-pillar frames
  * ⚠️ Wilson frame particularly concerning as it places head lower than heart
  * Choice based on patient physique, surgical requirements, and availability
  * Frame use may produce opportunities for point pressure - careful padding needed
* **Head positioning options:**
  * **Patients with limited neck mobility, history of neck pain, or symptomatic cervical disk:**
	* Head retained in sagittal plane
	* Skull-pin head clamp or face rest
	* ⚠️ Avoid excessive periocular pressure with face rests
  * **Patients with normal neck mobility:**
	* Head can be turned laterally and supported
	* Prevent pressure on downside eye and ear
	* ⚠️ Avoid forced rotation of pronated head
	* 💡 Consider maintaining neutral head position for procedures >3 hours (reduces cervical neuropathy risk)
* **Arm positioning considerations:**
  * **Pre-procedure assessment:** Ask about limitations in raising arms overhead during preanesthetic interview
  * **Arms alongside head ("surrender" position):**
	* Extended ventrally at shoulder, flexed at elbow, abducted onto arm boards
	* Ensure shoulder musculature under no tension
	* Keep shoulders abducted <90 degrees
	* Pad ulnar nerves at elbows
	* Verify full wrist pulses
	* Consider anterior flexion of shoulders to reduce neurovascular tension
  * **Arms alongside torso:** Consider for patients with thoracic outlet symptoms
  * ⚠️ Patients with symptoms when arms raised overhead may develop severe postoperative pain with overhead arm placement

## 4.3. COMPLICATIONS OF PRONE POSITIONS

### 4.3.1. EYE AND EAR INJURIES

* **Eye protection:**
  * Eyelids should be closed
  * Eyes should be protected so lids cannot accidentally separate
  * Consider lubrication (though value debated)
  * ⚠️ Protect against head turning and pressure on globe
  * Check for monitoring wires/tubing beneath head
  * Verify eyes are free from compression with sagittal-plane head positioning
* **Conjunctival edema:**
  * Common when head at/below heart level
  * Usually transient and inconsequential
  * Resolves with normal supine position or slight head-up tilt
  * No connection to posterior ischemic optic neuropathy

### 4.3.2. BLINDNESS AND VISION LOSS

* **Epidemiology:**
  * 5 Permanent loss of vision can occur after nonocular surgical procedures
  * Especially associated with spine surgery in prone position
* **Risk factors:**
  * Use of Wilson surgical bed frame (head lower than heart)
  * Obesity (elevated intra-abdominal pressure)
  * Long anesthetic durations
  * Increased estimated blood loss
  * Male gender
  * Lower colloid administration
* **Etiology:**
  * Primarily related to ischemic optic neuropathy
  * Venous congestion in optic canal
  * Gravitational effect on suspended globe causing optic nerve stretch
* **Practice recommendations (ASA 2019):**
  * Review patient history for risk factors (pre-existing anemia, hypertension, diabetes, tobacco use, obesity)
  * Inform high-risk patients of this potential devastating complication
  * Maintain blood pressure close to baseline
  * Maintain ongoing communication with surgeon
  * Close attention to blood loss and fluid administration (monitor hemoglobin)
  * Use colloids in addition to crystalloids as indicated
  * Use vasopressors to correct hypotension
  * Position head level with or higher than heart when possible
  * Reduce intra-abdominal pressure
  * Carefully assess use of Wilson frame and other positioning devices
  * Regular eye checks for compression
  * Consider limiting duration or staging procedures >6 hours

### 4.3.3. NECK PROBLEMS

* **Mechanisms of injury:**
  * Anesthesia impairs reflex muscle spasm that protects against painful motion
  * Lateral rotation of head/neck can stretch relaxed muscles/ligaments
  * Can injure cervical vertebrae articulations
  * Result: postoperative neck pain and limited motion
* **Prevention:**
  * ⚠️ Keep arthritic neck in sagittal plane when prone
  * Avoid extremes of head/neck rotation which can interfere with:
	* Carotid flow
	* Vertebral system flow
	* Impaired cerebral perfusion

### 4.3.4. BRACHIAL PLEXUS INJURIES

* **Potential injury mechanisms:**
  * **Root stretch injuries:** Possible if shoulder held firmly caudad by wrist restraint contralateral to turned face
  * **Axillary bundle compression:** When arm placed on arm board, humeral head can stretch/compress neurovascular bundle
  * **Ulnar nerve compression:** Forearm naturally pronates with arm overhead, exposing ulnar nerve to compression by elbow weight
  * **Thoracic outlet syndrome:** Patients with history of symptoms with arms overhead should have arms alongside trunk

### 4.3.5. BREAST INJURIES

* **Injury mechanisms:**
  * Breasts can be stretched and injured along sternal borders if forced laterally/medially by chest and abdominal wall supports
  * Direct pressure can cause ischemia to breast tissue
  * ⚠️ Particular concern with breast prostheses
  * Multiple cases of breast tissue ischemia reported, sometimes requiring mastectomy and reconstruction

### 4.3.6. ABDOMINAL COMPRESSION

* **Physiological consequences:**
  * Compression by trunk weight can force diaphragm cephalad, impairing ventilation
  * If intra-abdominal pressure approaches/exceeds venous pressure, blood return from pelvis/lower extremities is reduced
  * Vertebral venous plexuses communicate directly with abdominal veins
  * Increased intra-abdominal pressure transmitted to perivertebral and intraspinal surgical field
  * Results in venous distention and increased surgical field bleeding
* **Prevention:** Proper use of supportive pads and frames to remove abdominal pressure

### 4.3.7. STOMA AND GENITAL INJURIES

* **Stoma risks:**
  * Drainage stoma at risk if compressed against supporting frame/pad
  * Compressive ischemia can cause stomal orifice to slough
* **Genital protection:**
  * Same issue for genitals, especially male penis and scrotum
  * Requires careful positioning and protection against compression

**Bottom Line:** Prone positioning creates unique challenges including airway access, circulatory changes, and risks to eyes, breasts, and pressure points. Particular attention must be paid to head positioning, eye protection, and avoiding compression of the abdomen. The risk of perioperative vision loss is a serious concern, particularly in prolonged spine surgeries, requiring specific preventive measures including maintaining the head at or above heart level when possible.

# 5. HEAD-ELEVATED POSITIONS

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physiological Principles|Part A: Physiological Principles]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C4: Trauma, Resuscitation & Crisis|Part B: C4]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S10: Neurosurgery|Part B: S10]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Patient Care|Part C: Patient Care]]

**Exam Weighting:** Medium

**Head-elevated positions are commonly used for neurological, ENT, and shoulder procedures, creating specific hemodynamic and ventilatory considerations.**

## 5.2. VARIATIONS OF HEAD-ELEVATED POSITIONS

### 5.2.1. SITTING POSITION

* **Configuration:**
  * Patient in semireclining posture on operating table
  * Legs elevated to approximately heart level
  * Head flexed ventrally on neck
  * ⚠️ Head flexion should not force chin into suprasternal notch (risk of midcervical tetraplegia)
  * Elastic stockings/compressive wraps reduce blood pooling in lower extremities
  * Head held by face rest or three-pin skull fixation frame

### 5.2.2. SUPINE—TILTED HEAD UP

* **Applications:**
  * Operations involving ventral and lateral aspects of head
  * Neck procedures
  * Occasionally for transcranial access to brain top (with neck flexed)
* **Configuration options:**
  * Back section of surgical table elevated for low sitting position
  * Entire table rotated head high with patient's extended legs on footrest
* **Purpose:**
  * Improves surgical target access
  * Drains blood and irrigation solutions away from wound
  * Creates small pressure gradients along vascular axis
	* May pool blood in lower extremities
	* Risk of air entrainment into vessels above heart level

### 5.2.3. BEACH CHAIR POSITION FOR SHOULDER SURGERY

* **Configuration:**
  * Head-elevated semisupine position
  * Upper trunk moved laterally until raised surgical shoulder extends beyond table edge
  * Torso supported with hips on table
  * Surgical shoulder off and above table edge
  * Head on pillow or headrest
* **Benefits:**
  * Provides access to both dorsal and ventral aspects of shoulder girdle
  * Surgical arm remains on front of torso, prepared and draped to be mobile

### 5.2.4. LATERAL—TILTED HEAD UP (PARK BENCH)

* **Applications:** Access to occipitocervical lesions
* **Configuration:**
  * Lateral position with head somewhat elevated
  * All standard lateral position stabilization requirements apply
  * Head may be in three-pin skull fixation holder or supported by pillows/padding
  * Head elevation typically <15 degrees
* **Considerations:**
  * Does not eliminate air embolization risk
  * Good anesthesiologist access to face and thorax for monitoring/resuscitation
  * ⚠️ Avoid neck vein compression (can increase intracranial pressure and cause tongue edema)

## 5.3. COMPLICATIONS OF HEAD-ELEVATED POSITIONS

### 5.3.1. POSTURAL HYPOTENSION

* **Mechanism:**
  * Normal protective reflexes inhibited by anesthetic drugs
  * Establishing head-elevated positions frequently reduces systemic blood pressure
* **Management considerations:**
  * Some recommend measuring mean arterial pressure at circle of Willis level to assess cerebral perfusion (controversial)

### 5.3.2. AIR EMBOLISM

* **Pathophysiology:**
  * Potentially lethal complication
  * Air migrates to heart creating compressible foam
  * Destroys propulsive efficiency of ventricular contraction
  * Irritates cardiac conduction system
  * Can obstruct pulmonary vessels, compromising gas exchange
  * Can cross patent foramen ovale to systemic circulation
* **Risk factors:**
  * Risk increases with degree of operative site elevation above heart
  * Most emboli are small, clinically silent, detectable only by Doppler or echocardiography
* **Management:**
  * Immediate detection of embolization
  * Careful search for entry portal
  * Prompt treatment of clinical effects

### 5.3.3. EDEMA OF FACE, TONGUE, AND NECK

* **Etiology:**
  * Severe postoperative macroglossia from venous/lymphatic obstruction
  * Caused by prolonged, marked neck flexion
  * May require postoperative tracheostomy
* **Prevention:**
  * Avoid placing chin firmly against chest
  * Use oral airway to protect endotracheal tube
  * Avoid extreme neck flexion/rotation (widely used for posterior fossa and cervical spine access)
  * Primary mechanism may be neurologically determined rather than vascular obstruction or local trauma
  * ⚠️ Similar problems reported with transesophageal echocardiography probes

### 5.3.4. MIDCERVICAL QUADRIPLEGIA

* **Mechanism:**
  * Can occur after hyperflexion of neck, with/without head rotation
  * Attributed to spinal cord stretching with vasculature compromise in midcervical area
  * Spondylosis may be a contributing factor
* **Presentation:** Paralysis below C5 vertebra level
* **Risk assessment:**
  * Most commonly reported with sitting position
  * Also occurs after prolonged, nonforced head flexion for intracranial surgery in supine position

### 5.3.5. SCIATIC NERVE INJURY

* **Mechanisms:**
  * Stretch injuries possible if hips markedly flexed without knee bending
  * Prolonged compression where nerve emerges from pelvis in thin seated patients
  * Inadequate buttock padding
* **Presentation:**
  * Foot drop (may be bilateral)
  * Can result from either sciatic or common peroneal nerve injuries

**Bottom Line:** Head-elevated positions create significant hemodynamic challenges and increase the risk of venous air embolism. Careful attention must be paid to neck positioning to avoid devastating complications like midcervical quadriplegia. Monitoring for and preventing air embolism is a critical consideration, as is maintaining adequate cerebral perfusion pressure despite the elevated position.

# 6. HEAD-DOWN POSITIONS

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physiological Principles|Part A: Physiological Principles]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C4: Trauma, Resuscitation & Crisis|Part B: C4]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S9: Pelvic Surgery|Part B: S9]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Patient Care|Part C: Patient Care]]

**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Increasing importance with robotic surgery)

**Head-down positions, including Trendelenburg, are increasingly used for robotic procedures, creating unique physiological challenges.**

## 6.2. GENERAL CONSIDERATIONS

* **Historical context:**
  * Increased use with introduction of robotic procedures
  * Initially for prostatectomies, colorectal, and gynecologic procedures in pelvis/lower abdomen
  * Early adopters requested steep head-down positions
  * As experience grows, less extreme positioning often sufficient
* **Physiological impact:**
  * Cephalad displacement of diaphragm by abdominal viscera
  * Increased work of spontaneous ventilation
  * Worsened ventilation-perfusion ratio (blood pools in poorly ventilated lung apices)
  * Higher inspiratory pressures needed for controlled ventilation
  * Cranial vascular congestion and increased intracranial pressure
  * ⚠️ Contraindicated in patients with known/suspected intracranial disease except when no alternative exists

## 6.3. COMPLICATIONS OF HEAD-DOWN POSITIONS

### 6.3.1. HEAD AND NECK INJURY

* **Sliding risks:**
  * ⚠️ Several patients have suffered severe injury from body shifts on steeply tilted tables
  * Medicolegal cases involving patients sliding off tables with resulting neck injuries
  * In one instance, a patient somersaulted heels over head off table and died from massive intracranial bleed
* **Recommendations:**
  * Steep head-down tilt rarely warranted and should be discouraged
  * Skilled operators typically need less steep positioning as they gain experience

### 6.3.2. BRACHIAL PLEXOPATHY

* **Mechanisms:**
  * Cephalad patient movement while arms/shoulders secured to table
  * Can stretch middle and lower brachial plexus divisions
  * If movement causes hyperabduction >90 degrees, plexus stretched around humeral head

### 6.3.3. COMBINED LITHOTOMY AND HEAD-DOWN POSITION

* **Physiological impact:**
  * Combines worst features of both positions
  * Abdominal visceral weight on diaphragm
  * Abdominal compression from flexed thighs (especially in obese patients)
  * Requires assisted/controlled ventilation
  * Creates uphill perfusion gradient to lower extremities
  * ⚠️ Increases risk of compartment syndrome in legs
  * Perfusion gradient often unpredictable and exaggerated

### 6.3.4. RESPIRATORY EFFECTS

* **Ventilatory challenges:**
  * Cephalad diaphragm displacement obstructs inspiratory movement
  * Increased work of spontaneous ventilation
  * Worsened ventilation-perfusion ratio (blood accumulates in poorly ventilated lung apices)
  * Higher inspiratory pressures needed with controlled ventilation

### 6.3.5. NEUROLOGICAL EFFECTS

* **Cerebral consequences:**
  * Cranial vascular congestion
  * Increased intracranial pressure
  * ⚠️ Use only when absolutely necessary in patients with intracranial disease
  * Keep duration as brief as possible

### 6.3.6. PATIENT STABILIZATION CHALLENGES

* **Preventing cephalad sliding:**
  * Bent knees sometimes used to retain patient position
  * Historically, shoulder braces, straps, or tape used
  * ⚠️ Shoulder supports best tolerated over acromioclavicular joints
  * Avoid forcing shoulder caudad (traps subclavian neurovascular bundle between clavicle and first rib)
  * Medial placement against neck root may compress neurovascular structures at scalene musculature
  * Steep head-down positioning should be limited to only essential portions of procedures

**Bottom Line:** Head-down positions create significant physiological challenges including impaired ventilation, increased intracranial pressure, and risks of brachial plexus injury. The steep Trendelenburg position commonly used in robotic surgery should be used judiciously and for the shortest duration necessary. Patient stabilization must be balanced against the risk of neurovascular compression from restraining devices.

# 7. SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS FOR LAPAROSCOPIC SURGERY

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physiological Principles|Part A: Physiological Principles]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C4: Trauma, Resuscitation & Crisis|Part B: C4]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S9: Pelvic Surgery|Part B: S9]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Patient Care|Part C: Patient Care]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Medical Knowledge|Part C: Medical Knowledge]]

**Exam Weighting:** High (Commonly performed procedures)

**Laparoscopic surgery presents unique positioning and physiological challenges due to pneumoperitoneum and often steep patient positioning.**

## 7.2. PATIENT FACTORS AND CONTRAINDICATIONS

* **Absolute contraindications:**
  * Known raised intracranial pressure
  * Severe hypovolemia
  * Right-to-left cardiac shunts
  * Patent foramen ovale

## 7.3. SURGICAL CONSIDERATIONS

* **Potential complications:**
  * Visceral and vascular damage (particularly with blind trocar insertion)
  * Venous gas embolism if trocar inserted into vessel or viscera
  * 💡 CO₂ embolism generally less serious than air embolism due to higher solubility

## 7.4. POSITIONING FACTORS

* **General considerations:**
  * Head up or down positioning depending on operation
  * Patient must be secured to table
  * Protect pressure points and eyes
* **Challenges of Trendelenburg position:**
  * Risk of cerebral and upper airway edema with prolonged use
  * Reduction of functional residual capacity
  * Ventilation-perfusion mismatch
  * ⚠️ Tracheal tube can migrate as lungs pushed cephalad
* **Well leg compartment syndrome:**
  * Extrinsic limb compression of raised legs
  * Venous compression by pneumoperitoneum
  * Reduced perfusion from head-down position
  * Can affect otherwise healthy legs
* **Risk factors:**
  * > 4 hour surgery
  * Large muscle mass
  * Obesity
  * Peripheral vascular disease
  * Hypotension
* **Prevention strategy:**
  * Some institutions flatten table every two hours
  * Allow adequate perfusion of lower limbs for several minutes before continuing

## 7.5. EFFECTS OF PNEUMOPERITONEUM

* **General effects:**
  * Intra-abdominal pressure from CO₂ insufflation
  * Reduced abdominal wall compliance
  * Compression of great veins and splanchnic circulation
* **Cardiovascular implications:**
  * Increased systemic vascular resistance
  * IVC compression reduces preload and cardiac output
  * Diaphragm displacement increases intrathoracic pressure and pulmonary vascular resistance
  * Head-up position can further reduce venous return
* **Respiratory implications:**
  * Diaphragm excursion reduced by intra-abdominal pressure
  * Reduced pulmonary compliance
  * Decreased functional residual capacity
  * Increased atelectasis
  * Ventilation-perfusion mismatch and hypoxia
  * Hypercarbia from:
	* Absorbed CO₂
	* Reduced ventilation
	* Ventilation-perfusion mismatch
* **Abdominal implications:**
  * Increased intra-abdominal pressure reduces organ perfusion
  * ⚠️ Pressures >20mmHg reduce GFR by 25%
  * Raised intra-abdominal pressure is an independent cause of acute kidney injury
* **Neurological implications:**
  * Increased intracranial pressure via inhibition of venous drainage into thorax
  * Can lead to cerebral edema, especially with prolonged head-down positioning

## 7.6. ANAESTHETIC FACTORS

* **Airway management:**
  * Endotracheal intubation and muscle relaxation with positive pressure ventilation most common
  * Allows tighter PaCO₂ control
  * ⚠️ Increased aspiration risk with supraglottic airways
  * Avoid facemask ventilation prior to intubation when possible
  * Consider nasogastric tube to improve surgical view and reduce gastric injury risk
* **Ventilation strategies:**
  * Challenges from increased intra-abdominal/intrathoracic pressure and positioning
  * Volume control may contribute to barotrauma
  * Pressure control modes recommended
  * Titrate PEEP to oxygenation and cardiac output
* **Analgesia and antiemetics:**
  * Pain typically reduced compared to open procedures
  * Regional techniques to consider:
	* Transversus abdominis plane block
	* Wound infiltration with local anesthetic
  * Dexamethasone may reduce postoperative opioid requirement
  * Laparoscopy is emetogenic - use multiple antiemetics:
	* Ondansetron
	* Dexamethasone
	* Cyclizine (also useful for preventing vagal stimulation-induced bradycardia)

**Bottom Line:** Laparoscopic procedures create a unique set of physiological challenges due to pneumoperitoneum and positioning requirements. Key considerations include managing increased intra-abdominal pressure effects on cardiovascular, respiratory, and neurological systems. Attention to ventilation strategies, positioning-related complications (especially compartment syndrome), and multimodal analgesia/antiemesis are essential for optimal outcomes.

# 8. PREVENTION OF POSITIONING INJURIES

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physiological Principles|Part A: Physiological Principles]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C4: Trauma, Resuscitation & Crisis|Part B: C4]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Patient Care|Part C: Patient Care]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Systems-Based Practice|Part C: Systems-Based Practice]]

**Exam Weighting:** High (Critical safety concept)

**Preventing positioning injuries requires understanding of risk factors, proper positioning techniques, and vigilant monitoring.**

## 8.2. PREOPERATIVE ASSESSMENT

* **Risk factor identification:**
  * Pre-existing neuropathies or nerve entrapment syndromes
  * Peripheral vascular disease
  * Diabetes
  * Arthritis or joint mobility limitations
  * History of neck or back problems
  * Obesity or cachexia
  * Advanced age
* **Patient-specific planning:**
  * 4 When possible, position patients while awake to verify comfort
  * Document pre-existing deficits or limitations
  * Discuss positioning concerns with surgical team
  * Plan modifications for high-risk patients

## 8.3. GENERAL PROTECTIVE PRINCIPLES

* **Padding and pressure distribution:**
  * 3 Use padding to widely disperse point pressure on body parts and soft tissues
  * Various materials (gel, foam pads, blankets) are acceptable - none proven significantly better than others
  * Avoid creating new pressure points by wrapping/securing padding too tightly
* **Joint positioning:**
  * Maintain joints in neutral or slightly flexed positions when possible
  * Avoid extreme joint angles
  * Ensure adequate support for all extremities
* **Nerve protection principles:**
  * 2 ⚠️ Avoid stretching nerves >5% beyond resting length - can kink feeding arterioles and result in ischemia
  * Protect common compression sites (ulnar nerve at elbow, peroneal nerve at fibular head)
  * Verify that restraining straps don't create pressure over vulnerable areas
* **Circulation considerations:**
  * Check peripheral pulses after positioning
  * Maintain normotension, especially during position changes
  * Consider cycling compression devices for lengthy procedures in lithotomy position

## 8.4. POSITION-SPECIFIC PROTECTIVE MEASURES

### 8.4.1. SUPINE POSITION PROTECTIONS

* **Arm positioning:**
  * If extended on arm boards, keep abduction ≤90°
  * Ensure arm boards securely attached
  * Avoid external rotation and extension
  * If tucked, ensure adequate padding and avoid compression
  * Check that chest straps don't compress axilla
* **Ulnar nerve protection:**
  * Pad medial aspect of elbow
  * Avoid excessive pressure on cubital tunnel
  * Consider neutral forearm position
  * Particularly important for male patients
* **Back support:**
  * Consider lumbar support to maintain normal lordosis
  * ⚠️ Avoid hyperlordosis (>10° at L2-3 apex)

### 8.4.2. LATERAL POSITION PROTECTIONS

* **Axillary support:**
  * Place chest support (not axillary roll) just caudad to downside axilla
  * Support should only contact chest wall, not impinge on axilla
  * Helps prevent shoulder discomfort and vascular compression
* **Head and neck alignment:**
  * Support head to maintain neutral cervical spine alignment
  * Avoid lateral neck flexion
  * Check ears for proper positioning and padding
* **Dependent arm management:**
  * Avoid circumduction of dependent shoulder
  * Support arm to prevent stretch on suprascapular nerve

### 8.4.3. PRONE POSITION PROTECTIONS

* **Eye protection:**
  * Close eyelids
  * Consider lubrication
  * Ensure no direct pressure on globes
  * Check eyes after final positioning
  * Monitor for vision loss risk factors in spine surgery
* **Head positioning:**
  * Consider neutral position for procedures >3 hours
  * If head turned, avoid excessive rotation
  * Confirm adequate perfusion to ears
* **Abdomen decompression:**
  * Ensure abdomen is free from compression
  * Properly position chest and pelvic supports
  * Maintain adequate ventilation
* **Prevention of vision loss:**
  * 5 Maintain blood pressure close to baseline
  * Monitor hemoglobin levels
  * Consider colloid administration
  * Position head level with or higher than heart when possible
  * Consider staging procedures >6 hours

### 8.4.4. HEAD-ELEVATED POSITION PROTECTIONS

* **Hemodynamic management:**
  * Prepare for position-related hypotension
  * Consider measuring perfusion pressure at brain level
  * Maintain adequate preload
* **Air embolism precautions:**
  * Monitor for air embolism (precordial Doppler, end-tidal CO₂, TEE if available)
  * Prepare for management (lower surgical field, flood with fluid, aspiration via central line)
* **Neck positioning:**
  * ⚠️ Avoid extreme flexion that forces chin to chest
  * Prevent excessive rotation
  * Be vigilant for signs of tongue/facial edema

### 8.4.5. HEAD-DOWN POSITION PROTECTIONS

* **Patient stabilization:**
  * Secure patient safely without compressing neurovascular structures
  * If using shoulder supports, place over acromioclavicular joints, not neck base
  * Consider bent knees for stabilization
* **Ventilation management:**
  * Adjust ventilation parameters for diaphragmatic displacement
  * Monitor for hypoxemia and hypercarbia
* **Duration limitation:**
  * Limit steep head-down positioning to essential portions of procedure
  * Consider temporary repositioning for prolonged cases

## 8.5. MONITORING AND DOCUMENTATION

* **Intraoperative assessments:**
  * Regular checks of pressure points
  * Verification of extremity positioning after any table movement
  * Documentation of position changes
* **Documentation recommendations:**
  * Record specific positions used
  * Note any special protective measures
  * Document padding, support devices, and position changes
  * In complicated cases, consider separate detailed note
* **Postoperative assessment:**
  * Check for positioning-related symptoms in PACU
  * Consider documenting assessment of vulnerable nerves (e.g., ulnar) before PACU discharge

**Bottom Line:** Prevention of positioning injuries requires thorough preoperative assessment, position-specific protective measures, and vigilant monitoring. Understanding the unique physiological challenges of each position allows for optimal management of risk factors. Comprehensive documentation is essential both for patient safety and medicolegal protection. While traditional focus has been on mechanical factors, emerging evidence suggests inflammatory mechanisms may contribute to some perioperative neuropathies.
