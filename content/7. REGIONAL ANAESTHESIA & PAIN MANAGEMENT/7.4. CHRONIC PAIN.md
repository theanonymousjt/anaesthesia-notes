# 1. ANATOMY, PHYSIOLOGY, AND NEUROCHEMISTRY OF SOMATOSENSORY PAIN PROCESSING

## 1.1. NOCICEPTIVE PATHWAY OVERVIEW

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|Part A: 1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|Part B: Pain Medicine]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Medical Knowledge|Part C: Medical Knowledge]]

**Exam Weighting:** High (Fundamental neurophysiological concept)

**Basic Structure of Pain Pathway**

* Three-neuron, dual-ascending system with descending modulation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* *Ascending pathways* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Anterolateral system (spinothalamic tract)
	* Dorsal column-medial lemniscal system
* *Descending modulation* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]: From cortex, thalamus, and brainstem
* *Nociceptors* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]: Free nerve endings located in skin, muscle, bone, connective tissue
	* Cell bodies located in dorsal root ganglia (DRG)

**Pain Definition and Purpose**

* An unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with actual or potential tissue damage or described in terms of such damage [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* Subjective experience subject to significant modulation by higher centers [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* Evolutionary purpose: To inform the organism of tissue damage [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* No discrete Cartesian pain "pathway" - the system exhibits plasticity with functional changes occurring in response to tissue damage or inflammation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]

**Classification of Pain**

* **Functional basis** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
  * **Nociceptive pain**: Experience resulting from activation of peripheral nociceptors
  * **Neuropathic pain**: Dysfunction or injury to the nervous system itself with or without tissue damage
  * *Treatment implications*: Nociceptive pain generally more opioid-sensitive; neuropathic pain typically responds better to anticonvulsants and antidepressants (though this division is not absolute) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* **Based on location** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
  * **Superficial pain**: From skin and mucosa of mouth and anus; well-localized and sharp
  * **Deep pain**:
	* **Somatic**: From somatic structures; dull and aching but localizable within segmental nerve supply
	* **Visceral**: From visceral structures; poorly localized, vague, and deep aching in nature

**Bottom Line:** The pain pathway consists of a three-neuron circuit with dual ascending systems and descending modulation that processes nociceptive input from free nerve endings with cell bodies in the dorsal root ganglia. Pain is both a sensory and emotional experience that serves to inform of actual or potential tissue damage.

## 1.2. PRIMARY AFFERENTS AND PERIPHERAL STIMULATION

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|Part A: 1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|Part B: Pain Medicine]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Medical Knowledge|Part C: Medical Knowledge]]

**Exam Weighting:** High (Core neuroanatomical concept)


### 1.2.1. NOCICEPTIVE STIMULI TYPES

* Mechanical, thermal, electrical, or chemical stimuli can result in pain sensation and perception [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* Pain information is transmitted by receptors and neurons often distinct from those carrying innocuous sensory information [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]

### 1.2.2. PRIMARY AFFERENT FIBER CLASSIFICATION

* Four main groups of afferent fibers differentiated by [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Anatomy (myelination, diameter)
	* Rate of transmission (conduction velocity)
	* Sensory modality transduced

### 1.2.3. A-BETA (AΒ) FIBERS

* *Structure* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Heavily myelinated, large-diameter (12-20 μm)
	* Conduction velocity: >40-50 m/s
	* Lloyd-Hunt classification: Group II
* *Receptors* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]: Specialized encapsulated nerve endings
* *Function* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]: Transduce innocuous/low-threshold mechanical stimulation
* *Clinical significance* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Contribute to pain relief mechanism with TENS
	* May adopt C-fiber-like "phenotype" after injury
	* May participate in allodynia development

### 1.2.4. A-DELTA (AΔ) FIBERS

* *Structure* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Lightly myelinated, medium-diameter (1-4 μm)
	* Conduction velocity: 10-40 m/s
	* Lloyd-Hunt classification: Group III
* *Receptors* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]: Free nerve endings
* *Function* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]: Transduce noxious/high-threshold thermal, mechanical, and chemical stimulation
* *Pain type* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]: Mediate "first pain" (epicritic)
	* Well-localized pain
	* Sharp or stinging sensation
	* Rapid conduction
	* Less affective/emotional component
* *Primary neurotransmitter* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]: Glutamate

### 1.2.5. C FIBERS

* *Structure* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Unmyelinated, small-diameter (0.5-1.5 μm)
	* Conduction velocity: <2 m/s
	* Lloyd-Hunt classification: Group IV
* *Receptors* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]: Free nerve endings
* *Function* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]: Transduce noxious/high-threshold thermal, mechanical, and chemical stimulation
* *Pain type* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]: Mediate "second pain" (protopathic)
	* Follows epicritic pain temporally
	* Burning, aching, throbbing sensation
	* Poorly localized/diffuse
	* Slow conduction
	* Strong emotional component
* *Primary neurotransmitters* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]: Glutamate, substance P, CGRP

💡 **Clinical Pearl**: Erlanger-Gasser classification (AB/AS/C, based on axon size) and Lloyd-Hunt classification (II/III/IV, based on conduction velocity) are often used interchangeably due to relationship between size, myelination and conduction velocity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]

### 1.2.6. NOCICEPTOR CHARACTERISTICS

* Most Aδ and C nociceptors are polymodal (respond to multiple stimulus types) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* Located in multiple tissues [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Muscle
	* Fascia
	* Adventitia of blood vessels
	* Knee joint
	* Dura
	* Viscera
* Response pattern [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Slow adaptation
	* Residual firing after stimulus withdrawal

**Tissue-Specific Differences**

* **Cutaneous nociceptors** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]: All three types of nociceptors present in skin, subcutaneous tissue, and fascia
  * C-polymodal nociceptors account for 95% of C fibers in skin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* **Muscle nociceptors** [A:5-6]:
  * Supplied by both A𝛿 and C fibers
  * Higher threshold to mechanical stress compared to skin
  * Most muscle pain mediated by unmyelinated C fiber endings
  * Particularly responsive to ischemic muscle injury [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* **Periosteum** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
  * Innervated by both A𝛿 and C fibers
  * Low threshold for noxious stimulation compared with other structures
* **Joint nociceptors** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
  * Supplied with A𝛿 and C fiber afferents
  * Some end in specialized receptors for torque
  * Majority form a plexus of free nerve endings in joint cavity

**Bottom Line:** Primary afferents are classified into three main types with distinct characteristics: A-Beta (heavily myelinated, innocuous sensation), A-Delta (lightly myelinated, "first"/sharp pain), and C fibers (unmyelinated, "second"/burning pain), each with specific roles in somatosensory processing. Different tissues have varying distributions and characteristics of these nociceptors, which explains the diverse quality of pain experienced from different body structures.

## 1.3. PAIN PROCESSING: FOUR KEY ELEMENTS


**Exam Weighting:** High (Fundamental pain pathway concept)

**Competency Focus:**,

![image.png](CHRONIC_CHRONIC_image.png_7995d5.png_04554e.png)

### 1.3.1. TRANSDUCTION

* *Conversion of noxious stimuli into action potentials* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* Stimuli types: Thermal, Chemical, Mechanical [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* Occurs at nociceptor free nerve endings [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]

**Molecular Basis of Transduction**

* Primary afferent peripheral terminals express specific transducer channels [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* Sensitive over range of stimulus intensities [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* When activated by appropriate stimulus → activate voltage-sensitive cation channels (Nav, Cav) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* Result: Action potential initiation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]

**Key Molecular Components**

* **Sodium Channels** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Nav1.7 isoform (SCN9A gene):
		* Nonsense mutations → congenital insensitivity to pain
		* Impaired inactivation mutations → paroxysmal hyperexcitability (erythromelalgia)
	* 🔑 HY: Nav1.7 is a critical target for analgesic development
* **Transient Receptor Potential (TRP) Channels** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Primary molecular mechanism of sensory pain processing
	* TRPV1 (capsaicin receptor):
		* Responds to capsaicin and vanilloid compounds
		* Activated by acid and heat
		* Localized to nociceptors
		* Integrates multiple sensory modalities in single neuron
		* Knockout mice: Deficient thermal response but normal mechanical response
	* ⚠️ Despite clear involvement in nociception, drug development targeting TRP channels has not yet produced successful analgesics

### 1.3.2. TRANSMISSION

* *Conduction of action potential through nervous system* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* Via three-neuron pathway [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* First-order neurons (cell bodies in dorsal root ganglion)
	* Second-order neurons (cell bodies in dorsal horn)
	* Third-order neurons (cell bodies in thalamus)

### 1.3.3. MODULATION

* *Alteration of afferent neural transmission along pain pathway* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* Dorsal horn is primary site for pain pathway modulation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* Can involve either inhibition or augmentation of pain signals [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* **Inhibitory spinal modulation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* **Intrinsic spinal mechanisms**: Release of inhibitory neurotransmitters (GABA, Glycine)
	* **Descending efferent pathways** from:
		* Motor cortex
		* Hypothalamus
		* Periaqueductal gray matter
		* Nucleus raphe magnus
	* **Neurotransmitters involved**: Norepinephrine, Serotonin, Endorphins
* **Augmentative spinal modulation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Results in central sensitization due to neuronal plasticity
	* **"Wind-up" phenomenon**: Specific example from repetitive C-fiber stimulation

### 1.3.4. PERCEPTION

* *Final common pathway of pain processing* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* Integration of painful input into somatosensory cortex and limbic cortex [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* Allows localization and emotional response to pain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]

**Therapeutic Implications of Four Elements**

* ⚠️ Traditional analgesic therapies only target pain perception [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* 🔑 **HY**: Multimodal approach should target all four elements of pain-processing pathway [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: Understanding the cellular mechanisms explains why multimodal analgesia targeting different receptors (opioid, NMDA, COX) is more effective than single-agent therapy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

📋 **MNEMONIC: TTMP** - "The Totally Manageable Pain" - for remembering the four elements of pain processing: Transduction, Transmission, Modulation, Perception [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Pain processing involves four key elements: transduction (converting stimuli to action potentials), transmission (conducting signals through the nervous system), modulation (altering signals through inhibitory or augmentative mechanisms), and perception (the conscious experience of pain). Effective pain management should target multiple points in this pathway.

## 1.4. NEUROCHEMISTRY OF PERIPHERAL NERVE AND DORSAL ROOT GANGLION


**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Foundational concept for pain treatment)

**Competency Focus:**,

**Receptor Types on Peripheral Nerve Endings**

* Glutamate receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* Opioid receptors (μ-, δ-, and κ-opioids) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* Substance P receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* Somatostatin receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* Vanilloid receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]

**Dorsal Root Ganglia (DRG)**

* Contains cell bodies of all primary afferents [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* Located outside spinal cord within bony foramen [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* Regardless of innervation structure, all afferent cell bodies reside here [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]

**Neurotransmitters and Neuromodulators**

* *Primary neurotransmitter*: Glutamate [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
	* Acute activation via AMPA-type glutamate receptors
	* AMPA activation → brief depolarization → augments NMDA receptor activation
* *Additional transmitters from primary afferents* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Substance P
	* Calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP)
	* Adenosine triphosphate
	* Adenosine
	* Galanin
	* Somatostatin
	* Brain-derived nerve growth factor

**Bottom Line:** The peripheral nervous system uses a complex interplay of receptors and neurotransmitters, with glutamate as the primary excitatory transmitter. This diversity of neurochemical targets provides multiple intervention opportunities for pain management.

## 1.5. DORSAL HORN PROCESSING


**Exam Weighting:** High (Crucial for understanding central pain processing)


**Afferent Fiber Entry and Organization**

* Fibers enter through dorsal root entry zone [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* **Heavily myelinated fibers (Aα, Aβ)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Enter through dorsal roots
	* Traverse across dorsal horn top (Lissauer tract)
	* Ascend ipsilaterally in dorsal column
	* Provide collateral branches into dorsal horn gray matter
* **Small-diameter fibers (Aδ, C fibers)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Enter Lissauer tract
	* May ascend rostrally or descend caudally before innervating adjacent spinal levels
	* Innervate spinal cord gray matter

**Synaptic Activity**

* First-order neurons synapse on second-order neurons [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
	* Primary locations: Laminas I, II, and V
	* Release excitatory amino acids and neuropeptides
	* Some fibers travel in Lissauer tract before terminating

**Second-Order Neurons: Two Main Types**

* **Nociceptive-specific neurons** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Located primarily in lamina I
	* Respond only to noxious stimuli
	* Involved in sensory-discriminative aspects of pain
* **Wide dynamic-range (WDR) neurons** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Located predominantly in laminae IV, V, and VI
	* Respond to both nonnoxious and noxious input
	* Involved with affective-motivational component of pain
	* ⚠️ Critical for development of "wind-up" phenomenon and central sensitization

**Spinal Cord Gray Matter Organization**

* 10 laminae based on histologic appearance [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* Dorsal horn: Laminae I-VI (primary sensory component)
* Ventral horn: Laminae VII-IX (somatic motor function)
* Lamina X: Autonomic function

**Laminar Structure and Function**

* *Lamina I (outer marginal layer)* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Contains interneurons
	* Projection neurons to brainstem and midbrain
	* Contains nociceptive-specific neurons
* *Lamina II (substantia gelatinosa)* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Excitatory and inhibitory interneurons
	* Fewer projection neurons
	* Primary termination site for C fibers
* *Laminae III-IV* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Interneurons
	* Second-order neurons forming dorsal column pathways
	* Relay non-nociceptive sensory and proprioceptive information
* *Laminae IV-VI* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Interneurons
	* Nociceptive projection neurons to brainstem and thalamus
	* Contains wide dynamic-range neurons
* *Visceral nociceptive processing areas* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Laminae III, IV, V, VII, and X

**Detailed Organization of Dorsal Horn Circuitry**

* Large and small diameter fibers are initially randomly situated in peripheral nerves [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* As axons pass through the dorsal root, small (C) fibers become mostly situated on the lateral aspect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* In the dorsal root entry zone, small fibers on the medial side cross to join lateral fibers and enter the dorsolateral (Lissauer's) tract [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* Larger A fibers travel more medially in the dorsal column [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* Primary nociceptive afferent axons may also enter the cord via ventral nerve roots (approximately 12%) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
  * 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: This alternative entry route may explain the failure of dorsal rhizotomy in some pain treatment cases [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]

**Visceral Pain Processing**

* Visceral C fiber afferents: Broader termination pattern [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
	* Ipsilateral laminae I, II, V, X
	* Contralateral laminae V and X
	* Can terminate ≥5 segments rostrally/caudally
	* 💡 Clinical Pearl: This wide branching pattern explains why visceral pain is less localizable than somatic pain

**Additional Visceral Pain Characteristics**

* Poor localization, vagueness, and deep aching nature [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* Often referred to somatic areas due to convergence of visceral and cutaneous afferent fibers onto second-order neurons in laminae V-VIII [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* Possible mechanisms for referral [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
  * Brain unable to distinguish between visceral and somatic inputs
  * Visceral afferent activity facilitates passage of somatic sensations perceived as pain
  * May also be produced at thalamic level
* Visceral nociception caused by [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
  * Distension, stretching, and tearing of hollow viscera
  * Traction and compression
  * Inflammation
  * Ischemia (mediator release)
* Visceral afferent impulses conveyed with sympathetic afferent fibers via [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
  * Cervicothoracic ganglia (thoracic viscera)
  * Coeliac plexus (abdominal viscera)
  * Inferior hypogastric plexus (pelvic viscera)
* Afferent fibers from viscera constitute <10% of fibers in dorsal roots and converge over multiple segments [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** The dorsal horn is a complex processing center with a laminar organization that serves as the first central relay for nociceptive information. Its architecture explains many clinical pain phenomena including central sensitization, visceral pain referral, and targets for spinal analgesic administration. The wide termination pattern of visceral afferents across multiple segments explains the poor localization of visceral pain compared to somatic pain.

## 1.6. DORSAL HORN NEUROTRANSMITTERS AND RECEPTOR COMPONENTS


**Exam Weighting:** High (Critical for understanding analgesic mechanisms)

**Competency Focus:**,

**Pain Transmitter Classes in Dorsal Horn**

* **Excitatory amino acids**: Glutamate and aspartate [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
	* Fast-acting chemical messengers
* **Excitatory neuropeptides**: Substance P and neurokinin A [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
	* Slower, longer-lasting messengers
* **Inhibitory amino acids**: Glycine and GABA [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
	* Chemicals that dampen pain signals

**Pain Receptors in Dorsal Horn**

* **NMDA receptors** - important for ongoing, persistent pain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
	* Calcium channel dependent - normally blocked by Magnesium
	* Only activated following prolonged depolarization
	* Substance P removes magnesium block on NMDA receptor channel
	* Allows glutamate access to NMDA receptor
	* *Critical for developing chronic pain*
* **AMPA receptors** - handle quick pain signals [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
	* Sodium channel dependent
	* Essential for fast synaptic afferent input
* **Kainate receptors** - also involved in quick pain transmission [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* **Metabotropic receptors** - affect pain signaling indirectly [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]

![image.png](CHRONIC_CHRONIC_image_1.png_8c272b.png_caaa6e.png)

**Cellular Mechanisms in Dorsal Horn**

![image.png](CHRONIC_CHRONIC_image_2.png_b1ec79.png_af3c09.png)

* **Primary afferent C fibers release** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Peptides (substance P [sP], calcitonin gene-related peptide [CGRP])
	* Excitatory amino acids (glutamate)
	* Nitric oxide (NO) via nitric oxide synthase (NOS)
* **Synaptic transmission** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Glutamate → direct monosynaptic excitation via non-NMDA receptors (AMPA)
	* ⚠️ Acute primary afferent excitation of WDR neurons is NOT mediated by NMDA or NK-1 receptors
* **Dorsal horn neuron activation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Excited by afferent barrage
	* Induce excitation in second-order neurons via NMDA receptor
	* Pathway: NMDA activation → ↑ intracellular Ca²⁺ → activation of kinases and phosphorylating enzymes
	* Result: Formation and release of:
		* Prostaglandins (PGs) via cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2)
		* NO via NOS
* **Retrograde transmission** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* PGs and NO diffuse extracellularly
	* Facilitate transmitter release from primary and nonprimary afferent terminals
	* Mechanism:
		* Direct cellular action (e.g., NO)
		* Interaction with specific receptors (e.g., EP receptors for prostanoids)
* **Neuroinflammatory component** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Activated astrocytes and microglia as nonneuronal sources of prostaglandins
	* Stimulated by circulating cytokines
	* Cytokines released secondary to peripheral nerve injury and inflammation
* **Terminal modulation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Terminal excitability altered by various receptors on sensory terminal
	* Includes receptors for μ-, δ-, and κ-opioids
		* All three receptor types → Decrease terminal excitability
		* *Clinical relevance*: Target for analgesic medications
* 🏥 **Clinical Practice**: COX-2 inhibitors provide analgesia not just through peripheral action but also by reducing central sensitization in the dorsal horn [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** The dorsal horn contains diverse neurotransmitter systems and receptor types that mediate both excitatory and inhibitory pain signaling. NMDA receptor activation is particularly critical for central sensitization and chronic pain development, while opioid receptors provide important inhibitory control at multiple levels.

## 1.7. NEUROBIOLOGY OF ASCENDING PATHWAYS


**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Important for understanding pain localization)


![image.png](CHRONIC_CHRONIC_image_3.png_aaeb62.png_84e589.png)

**Dual Pathway System**

1. **Dorsal Column Tracts** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
	* Contains:
		* Axons of second-order spinal cord projection neurons
		* Ascending axons of primary afferent neurons
	* Relays:
		* Touch
		* Pressure
		* Vibratory sensation
		* Noxious visceral stimulation (via second-order cells around lamina X)
	* Some thalamic cells receive converging input from both visceral and somatic structures
2. **Spinothalamic Tract (STT)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
	* *Function*: Primary relay for nociceptive input from spinal cord to supraspinal levels
	* *Pathway*:
		* STT cell axons cross midline through anterior white commissure
		* Ascend primarily in contralateral anterolateral tracts
		* Terminate in posterior thalamic complex (ventral posterior lateral and ventral posterior medial nuclei)
	* *Cell location*:
		* Somatic nociceptive input: Predominantly lamina I and lateral dorsal horn (laminae IV-V)
		* Mixed somatic/visceral input: Scattered throughout deep dorsal horn, intermediate region (including lamina X), and lamina VII
	* *Organizational principle*:
		* Noxious and non-noxious sensory input from same body region converges on same thalamic neurons
		* Creates somatotopic encoding for localization
		* Maps onto cortical representation of specific body region

**Detailed Ascending Pathway Structures**

* **Spinothalamic tract** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
  * Main nociceptive pathway
  * Cell bodies located in laminae I, V-VIII, and IX
  * Those projecting to ventrobasal and posterior thalamus concentrated in laminae I and V
  * Those projecting to medial thalamus found in VI-IX with some in I
  * Axons cross within one or two segments through ventral white commissure
  * Fibers are thick, oligosynaptic, and rapidly transmitting
  * Division produces hypoalgesia on contralateral side (80-90% effectiveness)
  * Topographic representation with sacral segments laterally
  
* **Spinoreticular tract** [A:8-9]:
  * Cell bodies likely in laminae VII and VIII
  * Axons cross midline and ascend medial to spinothalamic tract
  * Synapse with brainstem reticular formation nuclei
  * Project to smaller intralaminar nuclei of thalamus
  * Fibers are thin, slowly conducting, and polysynaptic
  * Part of the phylogenetically older system concerned with reflex responses, endocrine responses, and triggering fear/suffering

**Spinohypothalamic, Limbic, and Cortical Connections**

* *Function* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Mediate affective component of pain (fear, anxiety, depression)
	* Trigger autonomic responses (↑HR, ↑BP, endocrine stress response)
	* Contribute to antinociception
* *Origin*: Ascending axonal projections primarily from laminae I and X [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]

**Projection to Third-Order Neurons**

* Located in thalamus [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* Project to somatosensory cortex [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* Allow nociceptive input to be perceived as pain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]

**Clinical Pathway Distinctions**

* **Lateral spinothalamic tract** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
  * Larger in humans than primates
  * Somatotopically organized with rapid transmission
  * Projects to somatosensory cortex
  * Concerned with quantitative analysis and spatial discrimination
  
* **Spinoreticular and spinomesencephalic tracts** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
  * Project to phylogenetically older brain parts
  * Concerned with reflex responses, endocrine responses, motivational drive, fear, and suffering
  
* **Cranial nerve nociception** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
  * Travels in bulbothalamic tract
  * Distributed with spinothalamic tract through trigeminal nerve nucleus
  * Descends to C2-C3 vertebral level

**Bottom Line:** Pain signals are transmitted through two main ascending pathways: the spinothalamic tract (primarily nociceptive) and dorsal column pathways. These systems process both sensory-discriminative aspects (location, intensity) and affective-motivational aspects (emotional response) of pain, which explains the complex nature of pain perception.

## 1.8. NEUROBIOLOGY OF DESCENDING PATHWAYS


**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Important for understanding endogenous analgesia)

**Competency Focus:**,

![image.png](CHRONIC_CHRONIC_image_4.png_c93795.png_fa4abb.png)

**Key Components of Descending Pain Inhibition System**

* The "triad" [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	1. Periaqueductal Gray (PAG)
	2. Rostral Ventral Medulla (RVM)
	3. Dorsolateral Pontine Tegmentum (DLPT)

**Periaqueductal Gray (PAG)**

* *Function*: Important site for antinociception production [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* *Activation methods* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Electrical stimulation
	* Chemical stimulation
	* Opioid receptor agonist injection
* *Neurotransmitters*: Contains enkephalin (endogenous opioid) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* *Receptors*: All opioid receptor subtypes present [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* *Projections*: Dense connections to RVM, locus coeruleus, and A7 nuclei [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]

**Rostral Ventral Medulla (RVM)**

* *Dual role* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Relay nucleus for midbrain structures (including PAG)
	* Primary role in suppressing nociceptive transmission at spinal cord level
* *Pathway* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* RVM neuron axons descend in dorsolateral funiculus
	* Terminate bilaterally in laminae I, II, V, VI, and VII
	* Terminate coincident with dorsal horn interneurons related to nociceptive transmission
* *Physiologic effects* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Inhibits nociceptive-specific neurons in dorsal horn
	* Selectively inhibits nociceptive responses of wide-dynamic range neurons

**Dorsolateral Pontine Tegmentum (DLPT)**

* *Composition*: Contains all noradrenergic neurons projecting to RVM and spinal cord [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* *Mechanism*: Electrical stimulation produces spinal cord α2-adrenergic receptor-mediated analgesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]

**Cortical Modulation of Pain**

* PAG receives input from multiple higher centers [A:9-10]:
  * Frontal cortex
  * Other cognitive areas
  * Limbic system
  * Thalamus
  * Hypothalamus
  
* **Mechanisms of pain suppression during stress** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
  * Stimulation of the PAG by electrical currents can produce intense analgesia
  * Intense excitement, distraction, or motivation can abolish pain perception
  * Endogenous opioid molecules (endorphins) likely responsible
  * Opioid receptors and endorphins found in high concentrations in the PAG
  
* **Psychological factors affecting pain perception** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
  * Anxiety and depression increase pain perception
  * Possibly related to decreased discharge in descending inhibitory pathways
  * May also involve altered function in cortical processing areas

**Three Descending Inhibitory Pathways**

1. **PAG-Medullary-Spinal Pathway** [A:10-11]:
   * Ascending nociceptive impulses input into medullary/mesencephalic nuclei
   * Particularly into nucleus raphe magnus, reticular formation, and PAG
   * From PAG, inhibitory pathways project via pons to dorsal horn or via nucleus raphe magnus
   * Descend in dorsolateral funiculus to synapse in dorsal horn laminae I, IIo, and V

2. **Direct Hypothalamo-Spinal System** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Anatomy|ⓘ]]:
   * Descends directly to medullary dorsal horn and spinal cord
   * Terminals concentrated at laminae I and X

3. **Direct PAG-Spinal System** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Anatomy|ⓘ]]:
   * Bypasses basal nuclei
   * Projects to medullary horn and laminae I, IIo, V, and X

**Neurotransmitters in Descending Control**

* Main neurotransmitters: Serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine) and norepinephrine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Anatomy|ⓘ]]
* These pathways also demonstrate functional plasticity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Anatomy|ⓘ]]

**Gate Control Theory of Pain**

* Developed by Melzack and Wall to explain variable relationship between stimulus and pain perception [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Anatomy|ⓘ]]
* Key components [A:11-12]:
  * Large and small fibers input into spinal cord transmission cells and substantia gelatinosa
  * Transmission is controlled by a spinal gating system influenced by relative activity in fiber types
  * Activity in large fibers tends to inhibit transmission ("closes the gate")
  * Activity in small fibers tends to facilitate transmission ("opens the gate")
  * Large-diameter fibers are not necessarily nociceptive afferents
  * Flooding dorsal horn with touch/pressure sensation may close gate for nociceptive input
  * System includes excitatory and inhibitory links from substantia gelatinosa to transmission cells
  * Incorporates descending inhibitory control from brainstem

**Bottom Line:** Descending pain modulation involves a complex interplay between brainstem structures that can inhibit nociceptive transmission at the spinal level. This system uses endogenous opioids and monoamines (norepinephrine, serotonin) and provides targets for analgesic medications including opioids and antidepressants. The gate control theory explains how activation of large non-nociceptive fibers can inhibit pain transmission at the spinal level.

## 1.10. NEUROPLASTICITY AND CENTRAL SENSITIZATION [PART A/B]


**Exam Weighting:** High (Critical for understanding chronic pain development)

**Competency Focus:**,

**Key Concept: Pain Adaptation**

* Unlike other sensory modalities, pain does not rapidly adapt to prolonged stimulation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* 🔑 **HY: Pain pathway is not "hard wired"** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
	* Nociceptive input is not passively transmitted from periphery to brain
	* Tissue injury causes neuroplastic changes within nervous system
	* Results in both peripheral and central sensitization

**Altered Pain States**

* *Allodynia* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Previously innocuous stimuli perceived as painful
	* Example: Light touch of towel painful after sunburn
	* *Painful response to typically nonpainful stimulus*
* *Hyperalgesia* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Previously noxious stimulus perceived as more painful
	* *Exaggerated pain response to normally painful stimulus*
	* Result of peripheral nociceptor sensitization

**Central Sensitization ("Wind-Up")**

* *Mechanism* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Persistent C fiber (not Aβ fiber) stimulation with tissue injury/inflammation
	* Specific example of central plasticity
	* Results from repetitive C-fiber stimulation of WDR neurons in dorsal horn
	* Leads to progressively increasing response to same stimulus
* *Effects* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Enhanced response to subsequent stimulation
	* Augmented receptive field size of dorsal horn neurons
	* Afferent input from adjacent dermatomes produces neuronal excitation
	* Non-noxious stimulation increasingly activates pain neurons
* *Clinical manifestation* [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Explains allodynia and hyperalgesia surrounding injury site
	* Present in acute, subacute, and chronic pain phases
	* Secondary hyperalgesia (increased pain response outside injury area)

![image.png](CHRONIC_CHRONIC_image_5.png_dbe85e.png_80ac5c.png)

**Detailed Central Sensitization Process**

* **Increased afferent input** from tissue damage or inflammation increases spinal cord excitability [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Central sensitization definition**: Increased activation and lowered excitation threshold of central nervous system neurons [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Functional consequences** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]:
  * Increases signal "gain"
  * Normally non-painful stimuli start to produce pain
  * Early phase reflects changes in synaptic connections
  * Late phase involves changes in protein production
  
* **Changes in descending control systems** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]:
  * Alterations in midbrain and brainstem pathways
  * Changes in areas involved with affective, autonomic, and aversive aspects of pain
  * These systems primarily utilize serotonin as neurotransmitter
  
* **Neuroplasticity concept** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]:
  * Reversible physiological and pharmacological changes ("plasticity")
  * Leads to altered relationship between input stimuli and perceived sensation
  * Fundamental to development of persistent pain

**Bottom Line:** Neuroplasticity fundamentally alters the pain pathway following injury, leading to central sensitization where the nervous system becomes more responsive to both noxious and non-noxious stimuli. This mechanism underlies many chronic pain conditions and partially explains why pain can persist after tissue healing.

## 1.11. PERIPHERAL SENSITIZATION AND HYPERALGESIA [PART A/B]


**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Important for understanding inflammatory pain)

**Competency Focus:**,

**Chemical Mediators After Tissue Damage**

* Released by inflammatory cells [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Macrophages
	* Mast cells
	* Lymphocytes
	* Platelets
* Antidromic release of substance P and glutamate from nociceptive afferents causes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Vasodilation
	* Extravasation of plasma proteins
	* Stimulation of inflammatory cells to release algogenic substances

![image.png](CHRONIC_CHRONIC_image_7.png_786c7d.png_29bb2c.png)

**Types of Hyperalgesia**

* **Primary hyperalgesia** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* *Exaggerated response to pain at the site of injury*
	* Caused by peripheral sensitization of:
		* Polymodal C fibers
		* High-threshold mechanoreceptors
* **Secondary hyperalgesia** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* *Increased pain response evoked by stimuli outside the area of injury*
	* Results from central sensitization in dorsal horn

**Neuroplastic Changes After Nerve Injury**

* *Neuroma formation* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Altered ion channel complement
	* Increased sodium channels
	* Decreased potassium channels
	* Net result: Increased neuronal excitability and nociceptive transmission
* *Sodium channel changes* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Increased expression in neuroma and DRG
	* Nav1.8 and 1.9 subtypes primarily on C fiber DRG cells
	* Nav1.8 genetic knockout: No effect on baseline pain thresholds but reverses nerve injury-evoked nociception
* *Potassium channel changes* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Reduced currents after nerve damage
	* K+ channel antagonists: Increase ectopic firing after nerve injury
	* K+ channel agonists: Decrease ectopic firing after nerve injury

**Neurochemical Changes in Nerve Injury**

* *Altered sensitivity to humoral factors* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Cytokines
	* Prostaglandins
	* Catecholamines
* *Tumor Necrosis Factor-α (TNF-α)* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Anatomy|ⓘ]]:
	* Short-term: Decreases K+ conductance → increases neuronal excitability
	* Long-term: Activates second messenger systems → alters protein production
	* Application to peripheral nerve → hyperalgesia
	* TNF-α antibodies or binding protein → reduces neuropathic pain
* *Prostaglandins* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Anatomy|ⓘ]]:
	* Released from inflammatory cells after tissue/nerve damage
	* Enhance opening of Nav1.8 channels via afferent terminal receptors

**Phenotypic Switch in Aβ Fibers**

* *Normal state*: Aβ fibers don't express substance P (unlike C fibers) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Anatomy|ⓘ]]
* *After injury*: Aβ fibers can express substance P [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Anatomy|ⓘ]]
* *Implication*: Aβ fiber involvement in noxious transmission [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Anatomy|ⓘ]]
* *Supporting evidence*: Aβ fiber blockade reduces light-touch-evoked allodynia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Anatomy|ⓘ]]
* *Therapeutic potential*: Target for intervention if Aβ fibers involved in noxious vs. non-noxious transmission can be differentiated [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Peripheral Sensitization Mechanisms**

* When tissue damage and inflammation occur, C fibers become sensitized by inflammatory mediators [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
  * Prostanoids
  * Bradykinin
  * Serotonin
  * Neural growth factor
  * Cytokines
  
* **Transduction protein effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
  * These chemicals reduce threshold for stimulation by affecting transduction proteins
  * Transduction proteins convert stimulus into electrical energy
  * Provides mechanisms for:
	* Tenderness after injury
	* Development of peripheral sensitization
	* Persisting afferent input into cord leading to central hyperexcitability
  
* **Temporal changes** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
  * Early phase: Changes to existing proteins
  * Later phase: Alteration of proteins produced (e.g., dramatically altering ion channel activity)

**Bottom Line:** Peripheral sensitization involves complex changes in nociceptor excitability, ion channel expression, and neurochemical signaling following injury. These mechanisms explain primary hyperalgesia at the injury site and contribute to the development of chronic pain states, particularly with nerve injury.

	* Chronic neuropathic pain briefly extinguished by peripheral nerve blocks
	* Post-stroke neuropathic pain reduced by anesthesia of peripheral nerves serving pain location
* *Contributing factors* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Anatomy|ⓘ]]:
	* Ongoing peripheral input → phenotypic changes from gene transcription/expression in DRG
	* Non-neuronal cell responses (immune cells, glial cells, keratinocytes, cancer cells, stem cells)
	* Result: Chronic neuroinflammation
* 🔑 **HY**: These factors provide additional targets for future therapies to reduce acute-to-chronic pain transition [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** The transition from acute to chronic pain involves complex interactions between peripheral and central mechanisms, with ongoing peripheral input and neuroinflammation playing key roles. Understanding these mechanisms is crucial for developing strategies to prevent chronic pain development.

## 1.13. HIGH-YIELD CLINICAL CONCEPTS [PART A/B/C]


**Exam Weighting:** High (Synthesis of core concepts)

**Competency Focus:**,,

**Key High-Yield Points**

* Pain is processed through 4 key steps: transduction, transmission, modulation, and perception [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Anatomy|ⓘ]]
* Central sensitization and neuroplasticity explain many clinical pain syndromes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Anatomy|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Dorsal horn is a critical site for pain modulation (both inhibitory and facilitatory) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Anatomy|ⓘ]]
* WDR neurons play a key role in central sensitization phenomenon of "wind-up" [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Anatomy|ⓘ]]
* Multimodal analgesia targeting different parts of the pain pathway is more effective than single-agent approach [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* NMDA receptor activation is critical for development of central sensitization [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Anatomy|ⓘ]]

**Clinical Mnemonics**

* 📋 **MNEMONIC: TTMP** - "The Totally Manageable Pain" - for remembering the four elements of pain processing: Transduction, Transmission, Modulation, Perception [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Anatomy|ⓘ]]
* 📋 **MNEMONIC: TRAP** for remembering pain sensitization types [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Anatomy|ⓘ]]:
	* T: Transduction changes (altered receptor sensitivity)
	* R: Receptive field expansion (larger areas become painful)
	* A: Amplification of signals (hyperalgesia)
	* P: Phenotypic switch (Aβ fibers expressing substance P)

**Bottom Line:** Understanding the neurobiological basis of pain is essential for effective pain management. Key concepts include the four stages of pain processing, central and peripheral sensitization mechanisms, and the rationale for multimodal analgesia targeting different points in the pain pathway.

## 1.14. TERMINOLOGY REFERENCE [PART A/B]


**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Important for precise communication)

**Competency Focus:**, [COMP: ICS]

**Pain and Neurological Terminology**

* *Paresthesia*: Abnormal sensation due to neural insult (severe symptoms may be termed dysesthesia) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* *Hyperalgesia*: Higher severity of pain than anticipated due to noxious stimulus [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* *Allodynia*: Pain due to non-noxious stimulus [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* *Radicular pain*: Pain in distribution of a dermatome [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Radiculitis*: Inflammation of nerve root; symptoms vary but follow dermatomal distribution [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Radiculopathy*: Sensory or motor deficit in distribution of a dermatome [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Spondylosis*: Degenerative change in spine [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Spondylolysis*: Fracture in osseous bridge between adjacent spine segments (traumatic or degenerative) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Spondylolisthesis*: Shift of vertebral body out of alignment with adjacent spinal segments (requires spondylolysis) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Pain Classification Systems**

* **Functional basis** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
  * **Nociceptive pain**: From activation of peripheral nociceptors; generally more opioid-sensitive
  * **Neuropathic pain**: From nervous system dysfunction or injury; typically more responsive to anticonvulsants and antidepressants
  
* **Location basis** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
  * **Superficial pain**: From skin and mucosa; well-localized and sharp
  * **Deep somatic pain**: From somatic structures; dull and aching but localizable within segmental nerve supply
  * **Visceral pain**: From visceral structures; poorly localized, vague, and deep aching

**Bottom Line:** Precise terminology is essential for accurate description and diagnosis of pain conditions. Understanding these terms and classification systems facilitates clear communication among healthcare providers and guides appropriate treatment selection.

## 2.1. SPINE PAIN: RADICULAR PAIN SYNDROMES


**Exam Weighting:** High (Common clinical problem)

**Competency Focus:**,

**Overview of Spine Pain**

* **Most common location**: Low back [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Potential causes** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Radicular pain/radiculopathy (herniated disc, spinal/foraminal stenosis)
	* Spondylosis
	* Internal disc disruption
	* Myofascial pain syndrome
	* Sacroiliac joint (SIJ) syndrome and piriformis syndrome (typically buttock pain but can mimic radicular pain)
	* Metastases
	* Vertebral body fractures

**Herniated Disc Pathophysiology**

* **Mechanism**: Nerve root irritation → inflammatory processes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Important epidemiology** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Up to 36% of general population have asymptomatic herniated discs
	* Up to 53% of pregnant women have asymptomatic herniated discs
* **Clinical presentation** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Spine pain with radicular symptoms in distribution of involved nerve root
	* Paresthesia
	* Loss of sensation
	* Reduced muscle strength
	* Diminished reflexes in affected dermatomal distribution
* **Natural history** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Often shows spontaneous regression
	* Symptoms may resolve without surgical intervention
	* May have absence of symptoms despite additional abnormalities

**Inflammatory Mediators**

* **Key inflammatory process**: Herniated nucleus pulposus → local release of cytokines and inflammatory mediators → chemical radiculitis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* **TNF-α role** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Expressed by disc cells
	* When applied to spinal nerve roots, causes changes similar to disc material application
	* 🔍 *Research note*: Selective TNF-α inhibition may reduce intraneural edema
	* ⚠️ Evidence limitations: RCT showed TNF-α inhibitor injection into pain-generating disc did not reduce pain or disability scores
* **Disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs)** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Limited evidence for spine/radicular pain
	* Studies are low quality or show inconclusive/short-term benefits only

**First-Line Treatments**

* **Effective non-interventional options** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Physical therapy
	* NSAIDs
	* Acetaminophen
	* Short courses of muscle relaxants
* **Medications with limited efficacy** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Antiepileptics: Not effective for low back pain or lumbosacral radicular pain
	* Duloxetine: Superior to placebo for neuropathic component of chronic low back pain

**Bottom Line:** Spine pain, particularly low back pain with radicular symptoms, is extremely common and often has a favorable natural history. Initial management should focus on conservative approaches including physical therapy and analgesics, with interventional treatments reserved for those who don't respond to first-line treatments.

## 2.2. EPIDURAL STEROID INJECTIONS (ESIS)


**Exam Weighting:** High (Common interventional procedure)

**Competency Focus:**,,

**Clinical Indications**

* **When to consider**: For spine pain not responding to non-interventional management [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Appropriate use context** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Should be a component (not sole modality) of nonsurgical management
	* Consider natural history of spine pain (typically improves over time)

**Mechanism of Action**

* **Anti-inflammatory effects**: Phospholipase A2 inhibition [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* **Analgesic properties**: Methylprednisolone blocks transmission of C fibers (not Aβ fibers) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]

**Efficacy Evidence**

* **For radicular pain** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Moderate-term efficacy in most controlled studies
	* May decrease need for operative intervention
* **Benefits** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Can minimize need for opioids and NSAIDs (reducing related side effects)
* **For spinal stenosis** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* When combined with local anesthetic, efficacy may last up to 3 months
	* Evidence for epidural local anesthetic alone (without steroid) is low

**Procedural Technique**

* **Image guidance** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Fluoroscopy should be used to guide placement
	* Ensures correct vertebral level
	* Documents appropriate flow of injectate
* **Approaches** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* **Interlaminar (IL)**: Deposits steroid into posterior epidural space
	* **Transforaminal (TF)**: Traverses intervertebral foramina to access anterolateral epidural space, deposits steroid closer to nerve root
	* **Comparative efficacy**: Similar moderate-duration efficacy
		* Paramedian IL approach deposits steroid in lateral posterior epidural space (closer to site of nerve insult than midline approach)

![image.png](CHRONIC_CHRONIC_image_8.png_5da23e.png_7450bc.png)

**Injection Protocol**

* **Assessment**: Reassessment 2-3 weeks after initial injection [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Number of injections** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* 🚫 Multiple injections with short intervals not advised
	* If no response to initial injection: Can repeat once (some patients require second injection)
	* If partial response: Up to three injections may be performed

**Optimal Dosing**

* **Lowest effective doses** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Lumbar IL ESI: 40 mg methylprednisolone
	* TF ESI: 10 mg triamcinolone acetate or 4 mg dexamethasone phosphate
* **Recommendation**: Use lowest effective steroid dose (or pharmacologic equivalent) to reduce adverse effects [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]

**Complications**

* **Technique-related** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Needle trauma
	* Vasospasm / occlusion from agents
	* Infection
	* *Note*: More common with cervical/thoracic than lumbar/sacral injections
* **Agent-related side effects** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* **Metabolic**: Reduces hypoglycemic effect of insulin, interferes with blood glucose control in diabetes
	* **Endocrine**: Dose-related HPA axis suppression (resolves in 1-2 weeks for most, can persist >3 weeks)
	* **Bone health**:
		* May reduce bone density in patients >40 years (cumulative effect, increases vertebral fracture risk)
		* Significant decrease in bone mineral density in postmenopausal women associated with cumulative methylprednisolone dose >200 mg over 1 year or >400 mg over 3 years
* **Serious CNS injury risk** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Due to proximity to vertebral and radicular arteries
	* Can occur from trauma or unintentional injection causing occlusion/vasospasm
	* Can occur with injection of local anesthetic or dye (without steroid)
	* 2021 report: Similar incidence of adverse events for particulate and non-particulate steroids
* **Steroid Characteristics** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* **Particulate steroids**:
		* Methylprednisolone acetate: Largest particles (theoretically highest risk)
		* Triamcinolone: Marginally smaller particles
		* Betamethasone: Smallest particles
	* **Non-particulate steroids**:
		* Dexamethasone: No identifiable particles (ideal for TF ESIs but easily washed out from epidural space)
		* Similar efficacy to particulate steroids but may require more injections

**2015 Safety Recommendations for ESIs**

1. **IL ESIs**: Use image guidance with appropriate views (anteroposterior, lateral, or contralateral oblique) and contrast media [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
2. **Cervical IL ESI**: Perform at C6-C7 or C7-T1 (not at more superior levels) [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
3. **TF ESIs**: Inject contrast medium under real-time fluoroscopy and/or digital subtraction imaging, using anteroposterior view, before injection [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
4. **Steroid selection** [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]:
	* Do not use particulate steroids in cervical TF ESIs
	* Particulate steroids may be used in specific situations for lumbar TF ESIs

**Clinical Pearls**

* 💡 **Patient selection**: ESIs more effective in patients with acute radicular symptoms [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* 💡 **Ineffective scenarios**: Not effective in patients with chronic lumbar radiculopathy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* 💡 **Documentation**: Appropriate clinical assessment and documentation of benefits needed before repeating ESIs [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
* 💡 **Timing**: Adequate interval should be observed before repeating ESI [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Epidural steroid injections can provide moderate-term relief for acute radicular pain when performed with fluoroscopic guidance using appropriate technique. They should be part of a comprehensive treatment approach, with careful attention to dosing and safety considerations to minimize complications.

## 2.3. LOW BACK PAIN: FACET SYNDROME


**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Common source of spinal pain)

**Competency Focus:**,

**Clinical Presentation**

* **Pain pattern**: Low back pain radiating to ipsilateral posterior thigh (usually terminates at knee) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Physical exam findings** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Paraspinal tenderness
	* Pain reproduction with extension-rotation maneuvers of the back

**Diagnostic Approach**

* **Diagnostic triad** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	1. Patient history
	2. Physical examination findings
	3. Positive response to prognostic medial branch blocks (MBBs) or facet joint injections
* **MBB technique recommendation** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Use local anesthetics with different durations (e.g., lidocaine and bupivacaine)
	* Correlate duration of relief with known duration of effect
	* *Benefits*: Reduces false-positive outcomes
	* *Limitations*: Increases false-negative rate
	* 🔑 **HY**: Better predictive value for radiofrequency ablation (RFA) candidacy

![image.png](CHRONIC_CHRONIC_image_9.png_1eaebb.png_41dd3a.png)

**Injection Therapy**

* **Facet joint injections** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* **Minimum effective dose per joint**:
		* 10-20 mg methylprednisolone or triamcinolone
		* 3 mg betamethasone
		* 2 mg dexamethasone
	* **Duration of response**: Up to 6 months
	* **Best responders**: Patients with acute facet joint inflammation (e.g., positive SPECT imaging)
	* 💡 **Clinical pearl**: Wait for pain relief to diminish to <50% before performing RFA

![image.png](CHRONIC_CHRONIC_image_10.png_6d21b8.png_3f3dbe.png)

**Treatment Selection**

* **Guidelines recommendation**: MBBs rather than intra-articular facet injections as predictive tool before lumbar/cervical RFA [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Rationale** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Better face validity
	* High failure rate for intra-articular injections
	* Studies show better outcomes when MBBs used to identify RFA candidates

**Radiofrequency Ablation (RFA) Efficacy**

* **Lumbar medial branches** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Controlled studies show improvements lasting 3-12 months
* **Cervical facet syndrome** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* 2016 systematic review: Majority pain-free at 6 months, >1/3 pain-free at 1 year
* **Cochrane review evidence** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Moderate evidence supporting facet joint RFA over placebo (short-term)
	* Low-quality evidence supporting RFA over steroid injections

**Bottom Line:** Facet joint pain is a common cause of axial spine pain. Accurate diagnosis requires a combination of clinical presentation and response to diagnostic blocks. Medial branch blocks are preferred for diagnostic purposes prior to considering radiofrequency ablation, which can provide moderate-duration pain relief.

## 2.4. BUTTOCK PAIN


**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Common pain conditions)

**Competency Focus:**,

### 2.4.1. SACROILIAC JOINT (SIJ) SYNDROME

**Clinical Presentation**

* **Pain location** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Affected SIJ region and medial buttock
	* May radiate to groin, posterior thigh, occasionally below knee
* **Physical examination** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Tenderness over sacroiliac sulcus
	* Reduced joint mobility
	* Pain reproduction when affected SIJ is stressed

**Diagnostic Tests**

* **Provocative tests** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* FABER (flexion, abduction, external rotation)/Patrick's test
	* Gaenslen's test
	* Yeoman's test
	* Iliac (gapping) distraction
	* Iliac compression
	* Thigh thrust (posterior SIJ shear)
	* Sacral thrust
	* Gillet (stork) test
* **Diagnostic criteria** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Symptoms and physical exam findings suggestive of SIJ syndrome
	* Pain on three provocative tests
	* Positive response to SIJ injection

**Treatment Options**

* **Conservative approaches** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Physical therapy
	* Manipulation
	* *Note*: Widely used but lacks large outcome studies validating efficacy
* **Interventional procedures** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* **Intra-articular injections**:
		* Steroid (40-60 mg methylprednisolone or other depot steroid) + local anesthetic
		* Duration of relief: 2-3 months
		* Guidance: Fluoroscopy more accurate than landmark-based or ultrasound techniques (especially in obese patients)
		* 💡 **Combined approach**: Intraand extra-articular injections more effective (possibly due to extra-articular nociceptors)
		* **Dose**: 40 mg appears to be lowest effective intra-articular dose
		* **Technique pearl**: Cranial spread (observed with dye study) improves short-term efficacy
		* **Long-term relief**: May last 9 months when 2-3 intra-articular injections given in 1 year
	* **Local anesthetic blockade**:
		* Target: Sensory innervation of dorsal SIJ portion (primary dorsal ramus of L5 and lateral branches of dorsal rami S1-S3)
		* Duration: Relief may last weeks to months when combined with physical therapy
	* **Radiofrequency procedures**:
		* **Thermal RF lesioning**: For more lasting relief of lateral branches
		* **Bipolar RF strip lesions**: Created along dorsal border of SI joint in "leapfrog" manner for lower portion denervation
		* **Multi-lesion probe** (Simplicity II, NeuroTherm): Placed along posterior sacral plate lateral to foramina
			* Effective for ≥6 months in ~50% of patients
		* **Water-cooled RF technique**: Produces larger lesions capable of capturing variable innervation along lateral border of sacral foramina
	* **Surgical approach**: Joint fusion

### 2.4.2. PIRIFORMIS SYNDROME

**Overview**

* **Epidemiology**: Comprises 5% of patients referred for back and leg pain treatment [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Etiology** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Trauma
	* Surgery
	* Infection
	* Compression of sciatic nerve components (between two divisions of piriformis muscle)

**Clinical Presentation**

* **Pain pattern** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Buttock pain with/without radiation to ipsilateral leg
	* Usually extends from sacrum to greater trochanter of femur
	* Sciatic nerve irritation causes buttock pain radiating to ipsilateral leg
* **Aggravating factors** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Prolonged sitting (driving, biking)
	* Rising from sitting position
	* Hip flexion, adduction, and internal rotation
* **Neurologic findings** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Typically negative exam
	* Possible leg numbness with sciatic nerve irritation
	* Straight-leg test may be normal or limited

**Diagnostic Signs**

* **Three confirmatory signs** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	1. **Pace sign**: Pain and weakness on resisted hip abduction when seated with hip flexed
	2. **Lasègue sign**: Pain on flexion, adduction, and internal rotation of hip when supine
		* *Note*: Some clinicians classify pain on straight-leg raise as Lasègue sign
	3. **Freiberg sign**: Pain on forced internal rotation of extended thigh
* **Functional note**: Piriformis is internal rotator of flexed hip and external rotator of extended hip [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Anatomy|ⓘ]]
* **Diagnostic approach** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Primarily clinical
	* Electromyography may detect myopathic and neuropathic changes
	* H-reflex delay in affected leg in flexed, adducted, internally rotated (FADIR) position compared to normal anatomic position

**Treatment Approaches**

* **Conservative management** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Physical therapy
	* Muscle relaxants
	* Anti-inflammatory drugs
	* Analgesics (to reduce spasm, inflammation, and pain)
* **Interventional procedures** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* **Local anesthetic and steroid injections**:
		* Target: Piriformis muscle (to break pain/muscle spasm cycle)
		* Guidance techniques: CT, nerve stimulator, or combined fluoroscopy-nerve stimulator
		* Comparison: Similar efficacy between fluoroscopy and ultrasound-guided injections
		* Injectate: No difference between lidocaine alone vs. lidocaine with betamethasone
	* **Botulinum toxin A**:
		* Indication: Transient relief from local anesthetic
		* Dosage: 100 units in 2-3 mL of local anesthetic
	* **Perisciatic injection**: Steroid addition to piriformis muscle injection when pain along sciatic nerve

**Bottom Line:** Buttock pain can arise from sacroiliac joint dysfunction or piriformis syndrome. SIJ syndrome responds to intra-articular injections and radiofrequency denervation, while piriformis syndrome typically responds to local injections of anesthetic, steroid, or botulinum toxin combined with physical therapy.

## 2.5. MYOFASCIAL PAIN SYNDROME AND FIBROMYALGIA


**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Common pain conditions)

**Competency Focus:**,,

### 2.5.1. MYOFASCIAL PAIN SYNDROME

**Clinical Characteristics**

* **Definition**: Painful regional syndrome characterized by active trigger point(s) in skeletal muscle [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Physical findings** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Palpable taut band
	* Trigger point manipulation (digital pressure or needle penetration) may induce twitch response
	* Point tenderness in taut band
	* Pressure on tender nodule induces recognizable pain pattern
	* Possible limitation to full passive range of motion of affected muscle

**Management Approaches**

* **Physical modalities** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Repeated cold spray application over trigger point (in line with involved muscle fibers)
	* Gentle massage of trigger point
	* Stretching of affected muscle
* **Physical therapy components** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Posture improvement
	* Body mechanics
	* Relaxation techniques
	* Trigger point massage
	* Post-isometric relaxation
	* Reciprocal inhibition
* **Interventional procedures** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* **Local anesthetic injection/dry needling**:
		* Studies show dry needling may be as effective as local anesthetic injection
		* Local anesthetic decreases procedural pain
		* Local anesthetic alone as effective as local anesthetic + steroid
		* Several injections at 2-3 week intervals + physical therapy may provide long-term benefit
	* **Botulinum toxin injections**: Suggested but clinical study results not uniform

### 2.5.2. FIBROMYALGIA

**Diagnostic Criteria (American College of Rheumatology, 2016)**

1. Generalized pain in at least 4 of 5 regions [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
2. Symptoms present at similar level for ≥3 months [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
3. Widespread pain index (WPI) ≥7 and symptom severity scale (SSS) score ≥5 OR WPI of 4-6 and SSS score ≥9 [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
4. Valid diagnosis irrespective of other diagnoses (doesn't exclude other clinically important illnesses) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Etiology and Pathophysiology**

* **Genetic factors**: Strong genetic and familial component [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Anatomy|ⓘ]]
* **Family predisposition**: Family members more likely to have [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Irritable bowel syndrome
	* Temporomandibular disorders
	* Headaches
	* Other regional pain syndromes
* **Neurochemical findings** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Anatomy|ⓘ]]:
	* **Opioidergic activity**: Normal or increased
	* **CSF enkephalins**: ~2x higher than healthy controls
		* Explains clinical observation that opioids are ineffective
	* **Norepinephrine metabolite** (3-methoxy-4-hydroxyphenethylene glycol): Lower in CSF
		* May explain efficacy of drugs raising serotonin and norepinephrine levels

**Treatment Approaches**

* **Optimal approach**: Multifaceted program with pharmacologic and nonpharmacologic therapy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Effective medications** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Serotonin and norepinephrine receptor inhibitors (SNRIs): Duloxetine, milnacipran
	* Pregabalin
	* Amitriptyline
	* Gabapentin
	* Gamma-hydroxybutyrate (sodium oxybate)
* **Effective nonpharmacologic treatments** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Patient education
	* Supportive therapy
	* Exercise programs (specifically low-intensity, low-impact)
	* Cognitive-behavioral therapy
	* Operant-behavioral therapy
* 🔑 **High-Yield**: Opioids are generally ineffective in fibromyalgia due to already increased opioidergic activity in these patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Myofascial pain syndrome is a regional condition characterized by trigger points that responds well to physical approaches and trigger point injections. Fibromyalgia is a widespread pain condition with central sensitization that requires a multidisciplinary approach including medications targeting serotonin/norepinephrine systems, physical therapy, and psychological interventions. Opioids are ineffective for fibromyalgia.

# 3. NEUROPATHIC PAIN SYNDROMES





## 3.1. HERPES ZOSTER AND POSTHERPETIC NEURALGIA


**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Common neuropathic condition)

**Competency Focus:**,

**Acute Herpes Zoster**

* **Prodromal phase**: Dermatomal pain may occur before skin eruptions [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Pain characteristics*: Usually moderate severity, manageable with analgesics [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Pain typically subsides with healing of the rash [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Antiviral therapy** hastens rash healing, reduces viral shedding, decreases PHN incidence [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Options: Acyclovir, famciclovir, valacyclovir
	* Selection factors: Administration frequency and cost (rather than efficacy differences)
* **Vaccination**: Decreases PHN incidence [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Recent recombinant vaccine: Increased efficacy, less decline over time vs. live attenuated virus vaccine

**Postherpetic Neuralgia (PHN)**

* *Definition*: Pain persisting >3 months after resolution of rash [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Incidence**: 10-15% overall, increases to 30-50% in elderly patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Risk factors** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Increased pain during acute stage
	* Greater severity of skin lesions
	* Older age
	* Presence of prodrome

**Interventional Management**

* Most studies on neuraxial/peripheral nerve blocks: Retrospective or case series [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Prospective controlled studies: Conflicting results [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* For maximum effectiveness: Perform blocks within 2 weeks of rash onset [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Sympathetic blocks: Role in reducing acute zoster pain and preventing PHN not well established [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Intrathecal therapy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Methylprednisolone with lidocaine (weekly for 4 weeks): Mixed evidence
	* Conflicting efficacy studies and procedural risks make intrathecal steroids not conclusively recommended
* Spinal cord stimulation (SCS): Some benefit shown in retrospective studies (no control groups) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Pharmacologic Management** 🔑 HY

* **First-line agents** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* **Antiepileptics**: Gabapentin, pregabalin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
		* Side effects: Somnolence, dizziness, peripheral edema
	* **Antidepressants** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]:
		* TCAs: Effective but limited by side effects in elderly
		* Nortriptyline preferred over amitriptyline (equal efficacy, better tolerated)
		* Venlafaxine (SNRI): Fewer side effects but less effective than TCAs
* **Combination therapy** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Gabapentin + controlled-release morphine
	* Gabapentin + nortriptyline
	* More effective and requires lower dosages than monotherapy
* **Opioids and tramadol** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Can be effective
	* Concerns: Tolerance, dependence, regulatory issues
* **Topical options** for allodynia and localized pain [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Lidocaine patch
	* High-concentration capsaicin

💡 **Clinical Pearl**: When considering both efficacy and side effects/quality of life, gabapentin/pregabalin may be preferred first-line agents despite antidepressants showing higher efficacy in studies [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Interpersonal and Communication Skills|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Postherpetic neuralgia is a common complication of herpes zoster, especially in elderly patients. First-line pharmacotherapy includes antiepileptics (gabapentin/pregabalin) and antidepressants (TCAs, SNRIs), with combination therapy showing greater efficacy. Early antiviral therapy and vaccination are important preventive measures.

## 3.2. DIABETIC PAINFUL NEUROPATHY (DPN)


**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Common neuropathic condition)

**Competency Focus:**,

**Epidemiology**

* Present in ~65% of patients with insulin-dependent diabetes [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Most common form: Distal symmetric polyneuropathy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Risk factors**: Increased duration of diabetes, age, degree of hyperglycemia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Typically develops after several years of hyperglycemia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Pathophysiology**

* Multiple mechanisms [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]:
	* Polyol pathway
	* Microvascular changes
	* Glycosylation end-product formation
* All pathways → Chronic nerve ischemia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

**Management Approach**

* **Non-pharmacologic** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Tight blood glucose control
	* Lifestyle modification
	* Control of dyslipidemia (associated with DPN development)
* **Pharmacologic therapy**: 🔑 HY
	* **Antiepileptics** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
		* Gabapentin and pregabalin: Effective [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
		* Enhanced efficacy when gabapentin combined with controlled-release morphine or nortriptyline
	* **Antidepressants** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
		* TCAs: More effective but more side effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
		* SNRIs (duloxetine, milnacipran): Better tolerated [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
		* 📋 **EXPERT RECOMMENDATIONS**: European Federation of Neurological Sciences Task Force and IASP NeuPSIG recommend SNRIs as first-choice for DPN
	* **Opioids**: Efficacy of tramadol, morphine, and hydromorphone not well established [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* **Interventional**: SCS requires further study [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Diabetic painful neuropathy is a common complication of diabetes mellitus requiring both glycemic control and symptomatic pain management. First-line medications include SNRIs (particularly duloxetine), gabapentin, and pregabalin, with TCAs as a cost-effective alternative when tolerated.

## 3.3. HIV NEUROPATHY


**Exam Weighting:** Low (Specialized neuropathic condition)

**Competency Focus:**,

**Epidemiology**

* Symptomatic neuropathy: 10-35% of HIV-seropositive patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Pathologic abnormalities: Present in almost all AIDS patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Types**

* **Distal sensory polyneuropathy**: More common, related to viral infection [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Antiretroviral toxic neuropathy (ATN)**: Secondary to treatment [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Clinical Features**

* Painful allodynia and hyperalgesia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Gradual onset, typically starting in lower extremities [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Progression: Distal → proximal [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Minimal motor involvement (mostly limited to intrinsic foot muscles) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Management**

* ⚠️ No FDA-approved medication specifically for HIV-related sensory neuropathy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Approach**: Symptomatic treatment and optimization of metabolic/nutritional status [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Effective options** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* **Lamotrigine**: 300 mg/day for both HIV sensory neuropathy and ATN [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
	* **Gabapentin**: 1,200-3,600 mg/day [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
	* **Topical therapies**: Lidocaine, high-dose capsaicin cream [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
	* **Cannabis**: Some efficacy but limited duration (5 days) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** HIV neuropathy presents as a painful distal symmetric polyneuropathy, either from direct viral effects or as a medication toxicity. Treatment focuses on symptom management with lamotrigine, gabapentin, and topical agents showing the best evidence for efficacy.

## 3.4. PHANTOM PAIN


**Exam Weighting:** Low (Specialized pain condition)

**Competency Focus:**,

**Characteristics**

* Nearly all amputees experience nonpainful phantom sensations [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Onset**: May be immediate but commonly within first few days post-amputation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Course**: ~50% experience decreased pain over time, ~50% report no change or increased pain [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Pathophysiology**

* **Peripheral mechanisms** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
	* Neuromas
	* Increased C fiber activity
	* Sodium channel activation
* **Central mechanisms** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
	* Abnormal firing of spinal internuncial neurons
	* Supraspinal involvement (new synaptic connections in cerebral cortex)

**Prophylactic Measures** (variable success)

* Perioperative epidural infusions (opioids, local anesthetics, clonidine) [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
* Continuous brachial plexus blockade with memantine [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]

**Treatment Options**

* **Pharmacologic** (evidence for effectiveness) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Gabapentin
	* Morphine
	* Tramadol
	* Intramuscular botulinum toxin
	* Epidural ketamine
* **Non-pharmacologic** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* TENS
	* Spinal cord stimulators
	* Biofeedback
	* Peripheral nerve stimulation (PNS) implant: 60-day placement shown to provide relief for up to 12 months

🏥 **Clinical Practice**: Most effective approach combines pharmacologic treatment with physical, psychological, or behavioral interventions [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Phantom limb pain involves both peripheral and central mechanisms. Preventive strategies include perioperative regional anesthesia, while treatment options include gabapentin, opioids, neurostimulation techniques, and multidisciplinary approaches combining pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions.

## 3.5. COMPLEX REGIONAL PAIN SYNDROME (CRPS)


**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Important neuropathic condition)

**Competency Focus:**,,

**Classification**

* Now considered nociplastic in nature (ongoing inflammation and tissue damage) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Drugs for Management of Monitored Parameters|ⓘ]]
* **CRPS type I**: Previously termed reflex sympathetic dystrophy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **CRPS type II**: Represents causalgia, identical to type I but with preceding nerve injury [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Risk Factors**

* Previous trauma [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Nerve injury (for causalgia/CRPS II) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Previous surgery [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Work-related injuries [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Female sex [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Clinical Features**

* Spontaneous pain [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Hyperalgesia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Allodynia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Trophic, sudomotor, vasomotor abnormalities [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Active and passive movement disorders [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Diagnosis**

* Clinical diagnosis using Budapest criteria [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* High sensitivity and specificity when criteria followed correctly [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Treatment Options**

* **Oral medications** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Gabapentin
	* Memantine (NMDA blocker)
	* Alendronate
* **Intravenous infusions** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Ketamine:
		* 4-5 day infusion at 1-7 μg/kg/min, OR
		* 4 hours daily for 10 days at 0.35 mg/kg/hr
		* ⚠️ Liver enzymes should be serially monitored
	* Bisphosphonates
* **Other medications** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Intramuscular/subcutaneous calcitonin
* **Interventional procedures** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* **Sympathetic blocks**:
		* Provide short-term relief
		* Facilitate physical therapy
		* Consider series if >50% relief from first block
		* Phenol neurolysis of lumbar paravertebral sympathetic ganglia: As effective as thermal RF
		* Botulinum toxin A (76 IU) into L2/L3 sympathetic ganglia: Greater limb temperature increase and pain reduction vs. levobupivacaine
		* Note: Temperature increase correlates with magnitude of sympathetic block, not pain relief
	* **Spinal cord stimulation (SCS)**: Consider if patient doesn't respond to other treatments

🔑 **HY**: CRPS is diagnosed clinically - temperature increase during sympathetic blocks doesn't correlate with pain relief but with block magnitude [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** CRPS is a complex pain syndrome with sensory, autonomic, and motor disturbances requiring multimodal management. Treatment includes physical therapy, oral medications (gabapentin, memantine), intravenous therapies (ketamine, bisphosphonates), sympathetic blocks, and spinal cord stimulation for refractory cases.

# 4. CANCER PAIN MANAGEMENT





## 4.1. OVERVIEW OF CANCER PAIN


**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Important subspecialty area)

**Competency Focus:**,,

**Classification by Origin**

* **Somatic pain** (responds to opioids, NSAIDs, neural blockade) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Visceral pain** (responds to sympathetic nerve blocks) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Neuropathic pain** (responds to antiepileptics, TCAs, SNRIs) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Multifaceted Approach Components**

1. Tumor-specific antineoplastic therapy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
2. Multiple classes of pharmacologic analgesics [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
3. Interventional procedures [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
4. Behavioral and psychological interventions [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
5. Palliative care [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Treatment Backbone**

* **Mainstay**: Opioids are backbone of cancer pain treatment [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Most patients have positive response with appropriate guidelines
	* Advanced interventional options include neurolytic procedures and intrathecal opioids

**Bottom Line:** Cancer pain management requires a comprehensive approach that addresses pain based on its underlying mechanism. While opioids remain the foundation of treatment, a multimodal strategy incorporating interventional procedures, adjuvant medications, and psychosocial support optimizes outcomes.

## 4.2. NEUROLYTIC BLOCKS FOR VISCERAL CANCER PAIN


**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Important interventional procedures)

**Competency Focus:**,

### 4.2.1. CELIAC PLEXUS BLOCK

**Anatomic Target**

* Collection of ganglia innervating all abdominal viscera except left colon and pelvis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
	* Location: Anterior to diaphragm crura, surrounding abdominal aorta, celiac and superior mesenteric arteries
	* Innervation sources: Greater, lesser, least splanchnic nerves (sympathetic) and vagus nerve (parasympathetic)
	* Vertebral level: Splanchnic nerves at T12-L1 retroperitoneally

**Block Approaches**

* Antecrural injection [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Retrocrural injection [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Percutaneous options: Posterior peri-aortic, posterior transaortic, transdiscal, anterior peri-aortic [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Guidance Techniques**

* Fluoroscopy (needle directed toward L1 vertebra body) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* CT guidance [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Ultrasound guidance [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Neurolytic Procedure**

* Diagnostic block: Local anesthetic to predict efficacy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Neurolytic agents: 50-100% alcohol or 3-12% phenol [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Dosing [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Antecrural space: 30-50 mL
	* Retrocrural space: 15-30 mL
	* Splanchnic nerve blockade: 10-15 mL each side
* Superior results: Local anesthetic combined with phenol [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Efficacy Notes**

* No difference in efficacy between approaches [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Splanchnic neurolysis superior for pancreatic body/tail cancer [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* ⚠️ Strong endorsement: European Association for Palliative Care (EAPC) and American Society of Pain and Neuroscience (ASPN) for pancreatic cancer pain [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Complications**

* Serious: Retroperitoneal hematoma, reactive pleurisy, abdominal aortic dissection, transient motor paralysis, paraplegia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Common side effects: Back pain, diarrhea, orthostatic hypotension [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Paraplegia mechanism: Possibly from spasm of lumbar segmental arteries, direct vascular/neurologic injury, or retrograde spread [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 4.2.2. SUPERIOR HYPOGASTRIC PLEXUS BLOCK

**Indications**

* Pelvic pain from cancer and chronic nonmalignant conditions [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Anatomic Target**

* Retroperitoneal plexus extending bilaterally from lower L5 to upper S1 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

**Procedure Technique**

* Guidance: Fluoroscopy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Approach options [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Two 7-cm needles directed medially and caudally to L5-S1 disc space
	* Alternative: Single needle via transdiscal approach
	* Ultrasound-guided anterior approach (effective for elderly and bladder cancer)
* Agents [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Diagnostic: 6-8 mL local anesthetic
	* Neurolytic: Phenol or alcohol

**Efficacy**

* EAPC and ASPN endorse for intractable cancer-related pelvic pain (though large prospective controlled studies lacking) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 4.2.3. GANGLION IMPAR BLOCK

**Indications**

* Perineal pain associated with malignancies [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Anatomic Target**

* Solitary retroperitoneal structure anterior to sacrococcygeal junction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Innervates: Perineum, distal rectum, anus, distal urethra, vulva, distal third of vagina [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

**Procedure Technique**

* Trans-sacrococcygeal approach through sacrococcygeal ligament [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Needle tip placement: Just anterior to anterior portion of sacrum [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Agents [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Diagnostic: 4-8 mL local anesthetic
	* Neurolytic: 6-10% phenol or 50% alcohol

**Efficacy**

* Considered effective/safe despite lack of controlled studies; ASPN recommends for perineal cancer pain [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Neurolytic blocks targeting sympathetic ganglia can provide significant relief for visceral cancer pain. The celiac plexus block is particularly effective for upper abdominal pain (especially pancreatic cancer), while the superior hypogastric plexus block and ganglion impar block are valuable for pelvic and perineal pain, respectively.

### 4.3.3. OPIOID USE INDICATION AND EVIDENCE IN PAIN

**Cancer Pain**
* Common use [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Long-acting as mainstay, short-acting for breakthrough
	* Rarely successful as monotherapy (adjuvants and procedures usually added)

**Acute/Short-Term Pain (<3 months)**
* Well-accepted, literature supported [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Chronic Non-Cancer Pain (>3 months)**
* Controversial [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* No controlled studies establish efficacy beyond 3 months
	* Likely effective for small subset
	* Third-line for neuropathic pain
	* Meta-analysis: No reduced pain in low back pain vs placebo/non-opioid control
	* Short-term efficacy for acute low back pain (with NSAIDs, muscle relaxants)
	* Long-term efficacy (≥16 weeks) unclear, not recommended

**Fibromyalgia**
* Only tramadol, tapentadol, or tramadol/acetaminophen combination shown more effective than placebo [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Pure opioid agonists should not be used

### 4.3.4. RISK ASSESSMENT WHEN PRESCRIBING OPIOIDS

**Opioid Risks** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
* Tolerance
* Physical dependence
* Substance use disorder (SUD)
* Opioid misuse

**Risk Statistics** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
* Aberrant drug-related behaviors: 6-37%
* Opioid abuse: 8-16%
* SUD development: 2-14%

**Risk Factors for Misuse** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
* Personal/family history of SUD
* History of sexual abuse
* Younger age
* Major depression
* Psychotropic medication use

**Note**: Some patients self-medicate with opioids for depression independent of pain [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Opioids remain the foundation of cancer pain management with morphine, hydromorphone, oxycodone, and fentanyl frequently used. While strong evidence supports opioid use for cancer and acute pain, their role in chronic non-cancer pain is controversial with limited evidence for long-term efficacy. Risk assessment is essential before initiating opioid therapy.

## 4.4. PHARMACOLOGIC TREATMENT OF NEUROPATHIC PAIN [PART A/B]


**Exam Weighting:** High (Essential knowledge for pain management)

**Competency Focus:**,

**Treatment Tier Recommendations**

* **First-line**: Antiepileptics, SNRIs, TCAs [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

## 4.5. ANTIEPILEPTICS [PART A/B]


**Exam Weighting:** High (First-line agents for neuropathic pain)

**Competency Focus:**,

**Mechanism Relevance**

* Neuropathic pain involves functional changes in sodium/calcium channel subunit expression [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
	* Chronic nerve injury results in redistribution/alteration of channel subunits
	* Causes spontaneous firing at ectopic sites along sensory pathway

**Mechanism Categories**

* **Sodium channel blockers** (most antiepileptics) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
	* Inhibit spontaneous activities at neuromas, DRG, dorsal horn
* **GABAA system modulators** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]:
	* Receptor agonists: Topiramate, felbamate
	* Transaminase blockers: Vigabatrin
	* Transport blockers: Tiagabine
* **Glutamate receptor antagonists**: Felbamate, topiramate [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Calcium channel blockers** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]:
	* Direct blockers: Lamotrigine
	* T-type calcium channels: Topiramate, zonisamide
	* α2-delta subunits: Gabapentin, pregabalin

**Evidence Base**

* Controlled studies show efficacy in [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Trigeminal neuralgia
	* Postherpetic neuralgia (PHN)
	* Diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN)
	* HIV polyneuropathy
	* Phantom limb pain
	* Spinal cord injury (SCI) pain
	* Central poststroke pain

### 4.5.1. GABAPENTIN

* Effective for [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Neuropathic pain (PHN, DPN, SCI)
	* Multiple sclerosis pain
	* Neuropathic cancer pain
	* Fibromyalgia
* Characteristics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]:
	* Wide therapeutic range
	* Considerable bioavailability variation between individuals
	* Up to 50% of patients experience adverse effects without benefit
* Dosing: 900-3,600 mg/day, three times daily [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 4.5.2. PREGABALIN

* Compared to gabapentin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]:
	* More linear pharmacokinetic profile
	* More consistent therapeutic dose range
	* Identical pharmacodynamics → similar indications
* Common side effect: Drowsiness (mitigated by slow uptitration) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Safety note: Can increase overdose risk if prescribed with opioids [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Dosing: 150-600 mg/day, twice daily [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 4.5.3. LAMOTRIGINE

* Effective for [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* HIV polyneuropathy
	* SCI pain
	* Trigeminal neuralgia
	* Central poststroke pain
* Safety concern: Risk of Stevens-Johnson syndrome [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Most common side effect: Rash [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Dosing: 50-150 mg/day [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 4.5.4. TOPIRAMATE

* Effective for: Migraine prophylaxis (similar to divalproex) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Dosing: 50-200 mg/day, twice daily [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 4.5.5. OXCARBAZEPINE

* Similar to carbamazepine but with fewer side effects [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Effective for: Trigeminal neuralgia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Note: Rapid pain relief (24-48 hours) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Dosing: 300-900 mg/day, twice daily [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 4.5.6. ANTIEPILEPTIC SIDE EFFECTS

* Gabapentin/pregabalin: Dizziness, fatigue, somnolence, weight gain (peripheral edema) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Lamotrigine: Rash [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Topiramate: Paresthesia, cognitive effects, weight loss [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Oxcarbazepine: Hyponatremia, low thyroid concentrations [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Antiepileptic drugs are first-line agents for neuropathic pain through multiple mechanisms, primarily sodium and calcium channel modulation. Gabapentin and pregabalin, targeting calcium channel α2-delta subunits, have the strongest evidence base for various neuropathic pain conditions with manageable side effect profiles.

## 4.6. ANTIDEPRESSANTS [PART A/B]


**Exam Weighting:** High (First-line agents for neuropathic pain)

**Competency Focus:**,

### 4.6.1. TRICYCLIC ANTIDEPRESSANTS (TCAS)

**Mechanisms**

* **Serotonergic effect**: Interfere with serotonin reuptake, alter serotonin binding [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Noradrenergic effect**: Interact with α-receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Opioidergic effect** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **NMDA receptor complex blockade** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Adenosine uptake inhibition** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Sodium/calcium channel blockade** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Possible anti-inflammatory effect** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

**Side Effects from Receptor Inhibition**

* Histaminic, cholinergic, muscarinic, nicotinic receptor inhibition [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Results in: Sedation, dry mouth, urinary retention [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Clinical Notes**

* Commonly used: Amitriptyline, nortriptyline [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Long history of neuropathic pain treatment [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Minimal supporting evidence [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**NNT Values**

* PHN: 2.1-2.8 [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* DPN: 1.3-3.4 [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Central pain: 1.7 [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Driving Impact**

* Impaired during first week or dose escalation; not impaired with long-term use (>3 years) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Weight Effect**

* More likely to cause weight gain compared to SNRIs [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Dosing (mg/day)** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

* Amitriptyline: 10-300
* Doxepin: 30-300
* Nortriptyline: 50-150
* Desipramine: 25-300

### 4.6.2. SEROTONIN AND NOREPINEPHRINE REUPTAKE INHIBITORS (SNRIS)

**General Characteristics**

* Antidepressant action plus antinociceptive effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Improved side-effect profile vs. TCAs [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Free of cholinergic, histaminic, α-adrenergic effects
	* Less potential for drug interactions

**Types of SNRIs**

**Venlafaxine**

* Serotonergic predominance at lower doses [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Greater noradrenergic activity above 215 mg/day [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Side effects: May cause hypertension, mania, exacerbate seizures [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Discontinuation: Gradual decrease needed [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Dosing: 37.5-300 mg/day [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Duloxetine**

* Increased selectivity for serotonin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Preferential noradrenergic effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Half-life: 12 hours [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* No active metabolites [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Effective for: DPN, fibromyalgia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Discontinuation: Gradual decrease to prevent agitation, anxiety, confusion, hypomania [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Dosing: 60-120 mg/day, once or twice daily [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Milnacipran**

* Preferential noradrenergic effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Half-life: 8 hours [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* No active metabolites [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Effective for: Moderate pain relief in fibromyalgia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Dosing: 100-200 mg/day, 50-100 mg twice daily [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Combination Therapy Note**

* Most effective treatment of fibromyalgia achieved with pregabalin + duloxetine [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 4.6.3. SEROTONIN SPECIFIC REUPTAKE INHIBITORS (SSRIS)

* Minimal benefit in neuropathic pain [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Examples: Citalopram, paroxetine [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Dosing (mg/day) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Fluoxetine: 5-40
	* Paroxetine: 20-40

**Bottom Line:** Antidepressants are valuable analgesics for neuropathic pain through multiple mechanisms. TCAs (particularly nortriptyline) have strong efficacy but significant side effects, while SNRIs (duloxetine, milnacipran) offer a better side effect profile with good efficacy. SSRIs have minimal benefit for pain management.

## 4.7. TOPICAL ANALGESICS AND OTHER AGENTS [PART A/B]

### 4.7.1. LIDOCAINE 5% PATCH

**Mechanism**

* Local sodium channel blockade (not systemic effects) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

**Application**

* Content: 700 mg lidocaine in adhesive vehicle [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Maximum: Three patches for 12 hours/day [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Extended use if needed: 16-18 hours for continuous relief [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Pharmacokinetics**

* Only 3% of total dose absorbed systemically [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Maximum plasma concentration on second day [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Concentrations well below cardiotoxic threshold [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

**Clinical Response**

* Typically occurs within days [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* For some, 2-week trial needed [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Some experience continued relief between applications [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 4.7.2. CAPSAICIN

**Mechanism**

* Defunctionalizes TRPV1 nerve endings, reduces epidermal nerve fiber density [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

**Effective For**

* PHN [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* DPN [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* HIV neuropathy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**High-Concentration (8%) Patch**

* Superior to placebo and low-concentration (0.04%) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* NNT: 12 for 30% relief [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Analgesia persists up to 12 weeks after one application [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Application Protocol**

* Apply local anesthetic one hour before patch (reduces burning sensation) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* 60-minute application (as effective as 90-minute, better tolerated) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Note: Pain increases 20-30 minutes after application, resolves 2-3 hours after removal [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Maximum: Four doses at 12-week intervals [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 4.7.3. MEXILETINE

**Recommended Dose**

### 4.7.5. BUPRENORPHINE-NALOXONE THERAPY

* In patients with opioid dependence/SUD: Shows some efficacy for chronic pain [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

💡 **Clinical Pearl**: When determining a treatment plan for cancer pain, begin with classifying the pain type (somatic, visceral, neuropathic) to guide initial therapy selection [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Patient Care and Procedural Skills|ⓘ]]

🔑 **HY**: For visceral abdominal pain from cancer, celiac plexus block has strong evidence and endorsement from multiple professional societies, especially for pancreatic cancer [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

📋 **MNEMONIC: CANCER PAIN APPROACH** - "VINMO" [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

* **V**isceral (blocks)
* **I**nterventional procedures
* **N**europathic agents
* **M**ultiple drug classes
* **O**pioids (mainstay)

**Bottom Line:** Topical analgesics provide targeted pain relief with minimal systemic effects. Lidocaine 5% patches and high-concentration capsaicin are effective for localized neuropathic pain, while mexiletine offers an oral sodium channel blocker option. Cannabinoids show limited evidence for pain management, and buprenorphine-naloxone may have a role in patients with both pain and substance use disorders.

# 5. INTERVENTIONAL PAIN PROCEDURES





## 5.1. CONTRAST AGENTS FOR INTERVENTIONAL PROCEDURES


**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Important procedural knowledge)

**Competency Focus:**,

**Iodinated Contrast Media (ICM)**

* Primary purpose: Injected before pain medications to confirm correct needle placement and exclude off-target delivery [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Usually first-line choice for interventional procedures [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Gadolinium-Based Contrast Agents (GBCAs)**

* *When to use*: Alternative when patient has history of hypersensitivity to iodinated contrast [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Types* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]:
	* Linear GBCAs: Less stable, more easily release gadolinium
	* Macrocyclic GBCAs: More stable structure
* ⚠️ **Important adverse events associated with GBCAs** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Nephrogenic systemic fibrosis
	* Gadolinium brain deposition/retention
	* Encephalopathy/death from unintentional intrathecal injection
* *Clinical pearls*:
	* 💡 Adverse events linked to gadolinium ion (toxic lanthanide element) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
	* Use with caution in patients with renal insufficiency (especially linear agents) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Gadolinium brain deposition possible after two intravenous MRIs [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* 🔑 HY: Clinical sequelae from brain retention not established [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* 2021 multisociety practice advisory recommends against GBCA use in IL and TF epidural injections [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Systems-Based Practice|ⓘ]]

**Hypersensitivity Reactions**

* *Clinical pearl*: 💡 Unintentional use of same ICM in patients with previous reaction rarely causes repeat reaction [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Why*: Related to [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Extravascular injection of small volumes
	* Concomitant injection of steroid

**Bottom Line:** Iodinated contrast media are preferred for interventional pain procedures to confirm needle placement and distribution of injectate. Gadolinium-based contrast agents should be avoided for epidural injections due to safety concerns and used cautiously in patients with renal insufficiency due to risk of nephrogenic systemic fibrosis.

## 5.2. THERMAL ANNULAR PROCEDURES


**Exam Weighting:** Low (Specialized interventional procedures)

**Competency Focus:**,

**Intradiscal Electrothermal Therapy (IDET)**

* *Technique*: Thermal resistance catheter placed percutaneously in posterolateral disc [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Mechanism*: Heat causes collagen of annulus fibrosis to contract [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* *Evidence* [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Two randomized sham-controlled studies with conflicting results:
		* One showed significant improvements in pain scores and Oswestry Disability Index
		* One found no significant differences in pain or functional outcomes
	* 🏥 Clinical Practice: Not approved by U.S. Centers for Medicare/Medicaid Services → dramatic reduction in use

**Biacuplasty**

* *Technique*: Bilateral cooled RF approach [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Development rationale*: Addresses difficulty navigating IDET catheter in degenerated discs [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Evidence* [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Prospective study showed efficacy vs. sham control at 6 and 12 months
	* Open-label trial showed superiority to conventional medical management
	* 🏥 Clinical Practice: Limited third-party insurance coverage

**Basivertebral Nerve Ablation**

* *Target*: Basivertebral nerve innervating trabecular bone of vertebral bodies [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Rationale* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]:
	* Vertebral bodies (particularly endplates) may be source of axial low back pain
	* Endplate fractures → increased communication with avascular disc
	* Upregulation of cyclooxygenase, CGRP, prostaglandins in degenerated discs
* *Evidence* [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Industry-sponsored randomized trials show >1-year pain reduction and functional improvement compared to standard care and sham neurotomy
	* 💡 Clinical Pearl: Challenges remain in generalizing to real-world populations and identifying appropriate candidates

**Bottom Line:** Thermal annular procedures target discogenic pain through heat application to the annulus fibrosis or vertebral body innervation. While IDET has conflicting evidence and limited insurance coverage, newer approaches like biacuplasty and basivertebral nerve ablation show promise but require further research on patient selection and generalizability.

## 5.3. MINIMALLY INVASIVE LUMBAR DECOMPRESSION (MILD)


**Exam Weighting:** Low (Specialized interventional procedure)

**Competency Focus:**,

**Clinical Context: Spinal Stenosis**

* *Definition*: Narrowing of spinal canal with nerve root compression (demonstrated on MRI/CT) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Types* [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Central canal narrowing
	* Lateral recess narrowing
	* Intervertebral foramina narrowing
* *Causes* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]:
	* Hypertrophy/buckling of ligamentum flavum
	* Disc protrusions
	* Spondylolisthesis
	* Facet joint hypertrophy
* *Typical pain presentation*: Low back, buttocks, thighs, legs with neurogenic claudication [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Worse with erect posture (smaller spinal canal diameter)
	* Worse with ambulation
* *Natural history*: 3-year follow-up of nonsurgical treatment shows [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* One-third improve
	* 50% show no change
	* 10-20% worsen

**MILD Procedure**

* *Indication*: Low back pain and neurogenic claudication with imaging evidence of central canal stenosis from ligamentum flavum hypertrophy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Technique* [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Small percutaneous laminotomy
	* Thinning of ligamentum flavum to increase critical diameter of stenosed canal
	* Usually 1-2 level decompression
* *Comparison to surgical decompression* [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Surgical: Complete resection of ligamentum flavum and bony posterior elements
	* MILD: Only partially debulks ligamentum flavum
* *Evidence* [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Randomized studies show superiority to ESIs
	* Effective as add-on therapy to conventional medical management
	* 🏥 Clinical Practice: Limited utilization due to insurance payment restrictions

**Bottom Line:** Minimally invasive lumbar decompression (MILD) is a percutaneous procedure that partially debulks the ligamentum flavum to address central canal stenosis. It shows superiority to epidural steroid injections and may be effective as an intermediate option between conservative management and open surgical decompression.

## 5.4. VERTEBROPLASTY AND KYPHOPLASTY


**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Common interventional procedures)

**Competency Focus:**,

**Vertebral Compression Fractures (VCFs)**

* *Common causes*: Usually secondary to osteoporosis in elderly patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Clinical impact*: Associated with significant morbidity and mortality [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Pain characteristics* [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Most healed VCFs are asymptomatic
	* Acute/subacute VCF pain: Deep back pain with bending, lifting, prolonged sitting/standing, or rising from seated position
	* May include radiculitis and/or paravertebral muscle spasm
* *Imaging* [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Radiography: Shows osteopenia/decreased bone mass
	* 🔑 HY: MRI is imaging modality of choice as it detects bony edema associated with acute fracture (primary indication for vertebral augmentation)

**Vertebroplasty**

* *Technique*: Injection of polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) into affected vertebral body [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Clinical pearl*: 💡 Entire vertebral body does not need to be filled with cement to achieve pain relief [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Kyphoplasty**

* *Technique* [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Insertion of balloon prior to cement injection
	* Balloon inflation creates potential space
	* Space filled with PMMA
* *Advantages over vertebroplasty* [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Allows use of more viscous PMMA
	* May restore some vertebral height
	* May strengthen vertebral body
	* Potentially lower rate of cement extravasation due to:
		* Higher PMMA viscosity
		* Lower injection pressure
		* Inflatable bone trap sealing leakage pathways
	* 💡 Clinical Pearl: Some argue this may limit cement spread to affected areas

**Procedural Details (Both Techniques)**

* Performed under fluoroscopic guidance [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Vertebral body access via unior bipedicular approach [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

![image.png](CHRONIC_CHRONIC_image_16.png_2641c4.png_e5c32b.png)

**Complications**

* *PMMA leakage* [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Contributing factors: Level of injection, fracture severity, amount of cement injected
	* *Neurologic complications*: Radiculopathy, spinal claudication, paraplegia
* *Other complications* [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Increased risk of adjacent segment fractures
	* Infection
	* Bleeding
	* Allergic reactions

**Evidence**

* *Vertebroplasty* [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Controlled studies found either no significant differences between vertebroplasty and sham, or only small clinically questionable benefit
* *Kyphoplasty* [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Two reports showed superiority for VCFs compared to nonsurgical care in osteoporosis or cancer patients
	* 💡 Clinical Pearl: Studies criticized for industry funding and author affiliation
* *Comparative evidence (2018 systematic review)* [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* No significant differences in pain scores or function between vertebroplasty and kyphoplasty
	* Kyphoplasty associated with:
		* Increased vertebral body height
		* Decreased kyphotic wedge angle
		* Less cement leakage

**Bottom Line:** Vertebroplasty and kyphoplasty involve injection of bone cement into fractured vertebrae with the goal of pain relief and structural support. Evidence for vertebroplasty is conflicting, while kyphoplasty may offer superior height restoration and less cement leakage. MRI evidence of acute fracture with bone edema is the best indicator for patient selection.

## 5.5. SPINAL CORD STIMULATION (SCS)


**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Important neuromodulation technique)

**Competency Focus:**,,

**Mechanisms of Action**

* *Gate control theory*: Increased input of large nerve fibers "closes gate" at substantia gelatinosa of dorsal horn [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* *Neurochemical changes* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]:
	* Altered local neurochemistry of dorsal horn
	* Decreased hyperexcitability of wide dynamic range neurons
	* Increased levels of inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA
	* Decreased levels of excitatory neurotransmitters (glutamate, aspartate)
* *For ischemic pain*: Alteration of sympathetic tone → improved oxygen supply-demand balance [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* Additional mechanisms: Antidromic activity, supraspinal mechanisms [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]

**FDA-Approved Indications (U.S.)**

* Failed back surgery syndrome [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Intractable low back and leg pain [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Complex regional pain syndrome [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Diabetic neuropathy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Additional Approved Indications (Europe)**

* Refractory angina [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Limb ischemia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Procedure**

* *Trial phase*: 5 days to 4 weeks to assess temporary pain relief [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Permanent implantation*: For patients who [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Failed conservative therapy
	* Experience temporary relief during trial
	* Have no psychological contraindications
* *Typical conversion rates*: 50-85% [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Technique*: SCS electrodes placed via Touhy needle [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

![image.png](CHRONIC_CHRONIC_image_17.png_b6f574.png_8080b3.png)

**Contraindications**

* Systemic infection [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Local site infection [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Abnormal coagulopathy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Complications**

* Nerve and spinal cord injury [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Infection [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Hematoma [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Lead breakage or migration [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Evidence**

* Generally supports efficacy for persistent back and leg pain [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* 💡 Clinical Pearl: Wide range of outcomes, with industry-sponsored studies showing superior results [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *For neuropathic pain*: Conflicting evidence vs. placebo, low-to-moderate evidence for effectiveness vs. conventional medical management [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *For diabetic neuropathy*: Systematic review of 2 randomized trials found superiority vs. medical management [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *For failed back surgery syndrome*: Large database review (>550,000 patients) found no or only small, clinically questionable reductions in chronic opioid therapy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* 🔑 HY: More favorable for peripheral neuropathic pain than for nociceptive, central, or mixed pain conditions [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**SCS Stimulation Parameters**

* *Conventional SCS*: Low frequency (40-130 Hz) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *High-frequency SCS (10 kHz)* [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Superior pain relief in back and leg pain vs. conventional SCS in long-term follow-up
	* Paresthesia-free (theoretically better for placebo-controlled trials)
* *Burst SCS* [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Pulse trains of 5 consecutive waves at 500 Hz
	* Improved back and leg pain control vs. tonic stimulation
	* Mostly paresthesia-free
* *Closed-loop SCS* [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Adjusts stimulation intensity based on measured vs. target-evoked compound action potentials
	* Superior to conventional stimulation for back and leg pain

**SCS for CRPS**

* Evidence: Retrospective case series, prospective clinical studies, two randomized controlled trials [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *First RCT*: Superiority at 2 years for pain (not function) vs. conventional medical management, no significant benefit afterward [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Second RCT*: Short-term superiority vs. placebo, no differences between various types/frequencies [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* 💡 Clinical Pearl: RCT limitations include robust placebo effect for invasive procedures and fact that most SCS patients already failed medical management [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**SCS for Ischemic Pain**

* Some randomized trial evidence for modest effects on [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Pain relief
	* Relief of angina
	* Limb salvage

**Bottom Line:** Spinal cord stimulation is an FDA-approved neuromodulation technique for failed back surgery syndrome, CRPS, and diabetic neuropathy. Different stimulation parameters (conventional, high-frequency, burst, closed-loop) offer options for optimizing therapy. Evidence is strongest for peripheral neuropathic pain conditions with some support for ischemic pain syndromes.

## 5.6. PERIPHERAL NERVE STIMULATION (PNS)


**Exam Weighting:** Low (Specialized neuromodulation technique)

**Competency Focus:**,

**Types**

* *Open PNS* [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Circumferential (cuff) electrode
	* Requires surgical exposure of target nerve
	* High-frequency (10-kHz) alternating current → reversible complete neuronal conduction block
* *Percutaneous PNS* [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Cylindrical percutaneous lead near target nerve
	* Requires target specificity
	* Risk of lead migration and unwanted motor stimulation in mixed nerves
* *Peripheral nerve field stimulation* [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Lead placed in subcutaneous area supplied by target nerve
	* Less target specific but technically less challenging

**Trial Options**

* Electrical stimulation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Local anesthetic nerve block [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Pulsed RF [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Clinical Pearl**

* 💡 Temporary devices may provide sustained relief in some patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Presumed mechanism: Reversal of peripheral and central sensitization [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]

**Evidence**

* Small industry-sponsored placebo-controlled trials and comparative-effectiveness studies show effectiveness in [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Post-stroke shoulder pain
	* Postamputation pain
	* Low back pain
	* Post-traumatic neuropathy
* *For occipital nerve stimulation in migraine*: Systematic review found large effect size but substantial heterogeneity across three sham-controlled trials, with highest-quality study negative for primary outcome [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Peripheral nerve stimulation offers targeted neuromodulation for focal neuropathic pain conditions. Different approaches (open, percutaneous, field stimulation) provide options based on anatomical considerations and technical complexity. Emerging evidence supports efficacy for specific conditions like post-stroke shoulder pain and post-amputation pain.

## 5.7. DORSAL ROOT GANGLION (DRG) STIMULATION


**Exam Weighting:** Low (Specialized neuromodulation technique)

**Competency Focus:**,

**Technique**

* Special percutaneous leads introduced initially in IL epidural space [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Directed toward adjacent target DRG [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Advantages vs. Conventional SCS**

* Can target unique dermatomes [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Better for difficult-to-target areas (e.g., foot) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Minimal-to-no positional changes in paresthesia intensity [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Evidence**

* Comparative-effectiveness study (152 CRPS patients) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* DRG stimulation vs. paresthesia-based SCS
	* Significantly greater proportion achieved ≥50% pain relief with DRG at 12-month follow-up

**Bottom Line:** Dorsal root ganglion stimulation offers precise targeting of specific painful dermatomes, particularly advantageous for areas difficult to cover with conventional SCS like the foot. It has demonstrated superior efficacy compared to conventional SCS for complex regional pain syndrome.

## 5.8. INTRATHECAL DRUG DELIVERY (IDD)


**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Important advanced pain management technique)

**Competency Focus:**,,

**Mechanisms**

* Direct deposition of medications near spinal cord receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* Bypass of blood-brain barrier and first-pass metabolism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* For spinal opioids: Release of adenosine into CSF [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]

**Clinical Utility**

* Valuable for chronic pain patients who [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Have not responded to other modalities
	* Find oral/transdermal medications ineffective at reasonable doses
	* Experience unacceptable side effects (GI dysfunction, sedation)
* Allows efficient delivery of medications with [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Limited blood-brain barrier permeability
	* Target receptors in spinal canal
* *Equianalgesic ratios* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]:
	* Morphine oral:intrathecal = 300:1
	* Baclofen oral:intrathecal = 100:1

**FDA-Approved Medications for Pain**

* Preservative-free morphine [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Ziconotide (synthetic peptide, not available in other formulations) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Medication Selection Guidelines (Polyanalgesic Consensus)**

* *First-line*: Ziconotide and morphine [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Less desirable first-line*: Fentanyl (with/without bupivacaine) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Second-line*: Hydromorphone (with/without bupivacaine) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Third-line or later*: Sufentanil, clonidine, baclofen, other adjuvants [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *For spasticity*: Baclofen (FDA-approved, limited role in pain applications) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Patient Selection Considerations**

* Age (younger patients at higher risk of tolerance/hyperalgesia) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Comorbidities [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Pain location (well-localized pain may be better for IDD due to limited medication spread) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Pain type and frequency [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Baseline opioid consumption [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Response to drug trial [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Intrathecal Trials**

* Options: Single-shot or longer epidural/intrathecal administration [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* 💡 Clinical Pearl: Several day intrathecal trials have higher face validity, but consensus guidelines neutral regarding preference [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Tolerance and Side Effects**

* Patients develop tolerance similar to systemic opioids [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Side effects more common with intrathecal vs. systemic administration [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Pruritus
	* Urinary retention
	* Myoclonus
	* Peripheral edema

**Intrathecal Catheter Tip Granulomas**

* *Risk*: 8% unique to intrathecal opioid infusion [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Composition*: Sterile inflammatory masses (fibroblasts, macrophages, neutrophils, monocytes) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* *Risk factors* [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Higher doses/concentrations of morphine and hydromorphone (not fentanyl)
	* Not mediated through opioid receptors
	* May relate to dural mast cell degranulation (occurs with morphine/hydromorphone, not fentanyl)
* *Signs of development* [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Loss of analgesia
	* Increased pain
	* Loss of sensation
	* Other neurologic symptoms in catheter tip dermatome
	* Later stages: Paraplegia, loss of bowel/bladder function

**Complications**

* *Procedure/device-related* [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Wound dehiscence
	* Skin erosion over device
	* Infection
	* Bleeding
	* CSF leak
	* Seroma/hygroma collection
	* Pump malfunction
	* Catheter kinks/shearing
	* Overdose from "pocket" fills
* *Medication-related* [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* **Opioids**: Androgen deficiency, decreased testosterone, hypogonadism (increased risk of bone mineral deficiency), peripheral edema (morphine/hydromorphone), pruritus, urinary retention, intrathecal granuloma
	* **Bupivacaine**: Sensorimotor deficits, urinary retention
	* **Clonidine**: Hypotension
* *Withdrawal risk*: From failure to refill pump on time (opioids, clonidine, baclofen) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* ⚠️ Fatal outcomes reported from baclofen withdrawal

**Non-Cancer Pain Considerations**

* Risk-benefit analysis critical due to chronic nature and longer therapy duration [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Higher risks of tolerance, hyperalgesia, and granuloma formation compared to cancer pain [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Evidence*: Small randomized controlled trial (15 people) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Higher pain scores and higher dropout rate in patients with 20% weekly dose reduction over 10 weeks vs. maintained dose
	* 💡 Clinical Pearl: Limited generalizability due to rapid tapering and short-term follow-up

**Procedural Technique**

* Intrathecal needle placement under fluoroscopy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Catheter connected to programmable pump [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* CSF confirmation prior to pump pocket placement [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Adjuvant Considerations**

* Bupivacaine may be added for individuals with lower body pain [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Intrathecal drug delivery provides targeted medication delivery directly to the spinal cord, offering a valuable option for patients with refractory pain who have failed or cannot tolerate systemic medications. Morphine and ziconotide are first-line options, with careful patient selection and monitoring required for complications including granuloma formation and withdrawal syndromes.

## 6.1. THE BIOPSYCHOSOCIAL MODEL

**Exam Weighting:** High (Foundation of chronic pain management)

The biopsychosocial model, conceptualised by **George Engel in 1977**, describes pain and disability as a multidimensional, dynamic integration of biological, psychological, and social factors that reciprocally influence each other. [Source: PMC6067990; EFIC 2023]

* **Key principle:** Psychological and social factors are not secondary reactions to pain - they form an interactive complex with biological processes
* **Clinical application:** Provides foundation for comprehensive pain assessment and tailored multidisciplinary treatment
* **ICD-11 recognition:** By including emotional distress and function in chronic primary pain definition, ICD-11 formally endorses the biopsychosocial model

### 6.1.1. THE THREE DOMAINS

**BIOLOGICAL FACTORS:**
* Genetics and family predisposition to pain conditions
* Tissue injury, damage, or disease
* Magnitude of nociception
* Peripheral and central sensitisation
* Neurochemistry and neurobiology (neurotransmitter levels)
* Hormonal influences (menstrual cycle, HPA axis)
* Effects of medications
* Sex differences in pain processing (threshold, tolerance)
* Nervous system characteristics (predisposition to sensitisation)
* Immune function and neuroinflammation

**PSYCHOLOGICAL FACTORS:**
* **Cognitions:** Beliefs about pain, expectations of outcome, catastrophizing, attention to pain
* **Emotions:** Depression, anxiety, anger, frustration, fear, perceived injustice
* **Behaviours:** Avoidance, activity modification, coping strategies, health-seeking behaviour
* Fear-avoidance beliefs
* Self-efficacy (perceived ability to manage pain)
* Acceptance vs resistance
* Prior trauma history (childhood abuse, PTSD)

**SOCIAL FACTORS:**
* Family dynamics and spousal/partner influence
* Social support networks (quality and availability)
* Workplace factors (job satisfaction, physical demands, support)
* Socioeconomic status
* Healthcare system interactions
* Cultural factors and beliefs about pain
* Benefits, compensation, and litigation
* Health literacy

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Depression, anxiety, and distress are among the most potent predictors of transition from acute to chronic pain - more so than pain intensity alone.

## 6.2. FLAG SYSTEM FOR RISK STRATIFICATION

### 6.2.1. RED FLAGS - SERIOUS PATHOLOGY

Require urgent investigation/referral:
* Fracture (trauma, osteoporosis)
* Malignancy (unexplained weight loss, history of cancer)
* Infection (fever, immunosuppression)
* Cauda equina syndrome (saddle anaesthesia, bladder dysfunction)
* Inflammatory arthritis (morning stiffness, multiple joints)

### 6.2.2. YELLOW FLAGS - PSYCHOLOGICAL RISK FACTORS

The four psychosocial "yellow flags" most strongly predicting transition to chronic pain and disability: [Source: PMC6067990]

1. **Pain beliefs** - catastrophizing, negative expectations, belief that pain indicates harm
2. **Psychological distress** - depression, anxiety, emotional disturbance
3. **Locus of control** - external (fate, others) vs internal control
4. **Self-efficacy** - low perceived ability to manage or influence pain

**Additional yellow flags:**
* Fear of pain and movement (kinesiophobia)
* Passive coping strategies (rest, medication reliance)
* Previous treatment failures with negative healthcare experiences
* Somatisation (multiple unexplained symptoms)
* Anger and perceived injustice

### 6.2.3. BLUE FLAGS - WORKPLACE/OCCUPATIONAL FACTORS

* Belief that work is harmful or should be avoided
* Unsupportive work environment or manager
* Lack of job satisfaction
* Poor return-to-work expectations
* High physical job demands
* Lack of workplace modifications available
* Compensation/litigation involvement

### 6.2.4. BLACK FLAGS - SYSTEM/CONTEXTUAL OBSTACLES

* Healthcare system barriers (waiting times, access to services)
* Insurance policy restrictions
* Legal/compensation system processes
* Employer policies (lack of modified duties)
* National disability benefit policies
* Geographic barriers to care

### 6.2.5. ORANGE FLAGS - PSYCHIATRIC FACTORS

Require specialist psychiatric input:
* Clinical depression requiring specialist management
* Personality disorder
* Substance use disorder
* Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)
* Abnormal illness behaviour
* Severe anxiety disorders

⚠️ **Safety Warning:** Early-life trauma increases chronic pain risk 2-3 fold. Childhood abuse confers 97% increased risk for painful somatic syndromes. PTSD mediates the relationship between trauma and chronic pain.

## 6.3. ASSESSMENT TOOLS AND QUESTIONNAIRES

### 6.3.1. AFFECTIVE ASSESSMENT

| Tool | Domain | Items | Notes |
|------|--------|-------|-------|
| **HADS** | Anxiety + Depression | 14 | Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale; validated in pain populations |
| **PHQ-9** | Depression | 9 | Patient Health Questionnaire; monitors severity |
| **GAD-7** | Anxiety | 7 | Generalised Anxiety Disorder scale |
| **BDI** | Depression | 21 | Beck Depression Inventory |

### 6.3.2. PAIN CATASTROPHIZING

**Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS):**
* 13 items across 3 domains:
   * **Rumination** (4 items) - "I can't stop thinking about how much it hurts"
   * **Magnification** (3 items) - "I wonder whether something serious may happen"
   * **Helplessness** (6 items) - "There's nothing I can do to reduce the intensity"
* Total score 0-52; clinically significant ≥30
* 🔑 **High-Yield:** Catastrophizing is "the single most important pre-treatment risk factor impairing effectiveness of pain-relieving interventions"

### 6.3.3. FEAR-AVOIDANCE

**Fear-Avoidance Beliefs Questionnaire (FABQ):**
* Measures beliefs about physical activity and work in relation to pain
* Two subscales: FABQ-Physical Activity, FABQ-Work
* Higher scores predict poorer outcomes

**Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia (TSK):**
* 17 items measuring fear of movement/(re)injury
* Scores ≥37 indicate high kinesiophobia

### 6.3.4. COPING AND SELF-EFFICACY

**Coping Strategies Questionnaire (CSQ):**
* 6 cognitive strategies: diverting attention, reinterpreting pain, coping self-statements, ignoring pain, praying/hoping, catastrophizing
* 2 behavioural strategies: increasing activity, increasing pain behaviours
* Active coping correlates with better outcomes; passive coping with worse

**Pain Self-Efficacy Questionnaire (PSEQ):**
* 10 items measuring confidence in performing activities despite pain
* Higher scores predict better functional outcomes

### 6.3.5. COMPREHENSIVE SCREENING TOOLS

**STarT Back Screening Tool:**
* 9 items for risk stratification in low back pain
* Classifies into low, medium, high risk for chronicity
* Guides treatment intensity (matched care)

**Örebro Musculoskeletal Pain Screening Questionnaire (ÖMPSQ):**
* 21 items (short form 10 items)
* Predicts work disability and chronic pain development
* Score ≥105 (long form) or ≥50 (short form) indicates high risk

### 6.3.6. NEUROPATHIC PAIN SCREENING TOOLS

<!-- EPA-12:K3:neuropathic_pain EPA-12:S1:clinical_assessment -->

**Rationale for Screening:**
* Neuropathic pain requires different treatment strategies than nociceptive pain
* Early identification enables timely initiation of appropriate therapies (anticonvulsants, SNRIs)
* Standard analgesics (paracetamol, NSAIDs) typically ineffective for neuropathic component
* IASP grading system: Possible → Probable → Definite neuropathic pain

**IASP Grading System for Neuropathic Pain Diagnosis:**

| Grade | Criteria | Requirements |
|-------|----------|--------------|
| **Possible** | History + pain distribution neuroanatomically plausible | Suggestive symptoms alone |
| **Probable** | Possible + clinical examination confirmation | Sensory signs in same distribution |
| **Definite** | Probable + diagnostic test confirmation | Nerve conduction, imaging, biopsy |

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Screening questionnaires identify "possible" neuropathic pain - clinical examination and investigations are required to upgrade to "probable" or "definite" diagnosis.

---

#### 6.3.6.1. DN4 (Douleur Neuropathique 4 Questions)

**Overview:**
* 10-item clinician-administered tool combining interview and examination
* Developed by French Neuropathic Pain Group (2005)
* Most widely validated screening tool globally
* Available in self-completed interview version (DN4-Interview, 7 items)

**Components:**

**Interview Questions (7 items):**

| Item | Question | Response |
|------|----------|----------|
| 1 | Does the pain have burning characteristics? | Yes/No |
| 2 | Does the pain have a cold/painful sensation? | Yes/No |
| 3 | Does the pain have electric shock characteristics? | Yes/No |
| 4 | Is the pain associated with tingling? | Yes/No |
| 5 | Is the pain associated with pins and needles? | Yes/No |
| 6 | Is the pain associated with numbness? | Yes/No |
| 7 | Is the pain associated with itching? | Yes/No |

**Examination Items (3 items):**

| Item | Test | Response |
|------|------|----------|
| 8 | Hypoesthesia to touch (cotton wool) | Yes/No |
| 9 | Hypoesthesia to pinprick | Yes/No |
| 10 | Brush-evoked pain (allodynia) | Yes/No |

**Scoring:**
* Each "Yes" = 1 point; Total score: 0-10
* **Cut-off ≥4/10 indicates neuropathic pain**
* DN4-Interview (7 items): Cut-off ≥3/7

**Performance Characteristics:**
* Sensitivity: 83% (range 71-100%)
* Specificity: 90% (range 72-97%)
* Cronbach's alpha: 0.60-0.97
* Highest sensitivity for central neuropathic pain and polyneuropathies
* Lower sensitivity for trigeminal neuralgia

**Strengths:**
* Quick to administer (~5 minutes)
* Validated in >15 languages
* Combines subjective symptoms with objective examination
* High diagnostic accuracy in diabetic neuropathy (AUC 0.94)

**Limitations:**
* Examination component requires clinician training
* Pain intensity may influence results (optimal at VAS ≥40mm)
* Original French version shows superior properties to some translations

---

#### 6.3.6.2. LANSS (Leeds Assessment of Neuropathic Symptoms and Signs)

**Overview:**
* First validated neuropathic pain screening tool (Bennett, 2001)
* 7 items: 5 symptom questions + 2 examination items
* Self-completed version (S-LANSS) eliminates examination component

**Components:**

**Symptom Questions (5 items with weighted scoring):**

| Item | Question | Score |
|------|----------|-------|
| 1 | Does your pain feel like strange, unpleasant sensations (pins and needles, tingling, pricking)? | 5 |
| 2 | Does your pain make the skin in the painful area look different (mottled, more red/pink)? | 5 |
| 3 | Does your pain make the affected area abnormally sensitive to touch (uncomfortable to wear clothes, unpleasant light touch)? | 3 |
| 4 | Does your pain come on suddenly in bursts without apparent reason (electric shocks, jumping, bursting)? | 2 |
| 5 | Does your pain feel as if the skin temperature is abnormal (burning sensation)? | 1 |

**Examination Items (2 items):**

| Item | Test | Score |
|------|------|-------|
| 6 | Allodynia: Stroking with cotton wool produces pain | 5 |
| 7 | Altered pinprick threshold: Pinprick feels blunted or painful | 3 |

**Scoring:**
* Maximum score: 24 points
* **Cut-off ≥12 indicates neuropathic mechanism**
* S-LANSS cut-off: ≥10 (interview), ≥12 (unaided)

**Performance Characteristics:**
* LANSS Sensitivity: 82-91%
* LANSS Specificity: 80-94%
* S-LANSS Sensitivity: 62-89%
* S-LANSS Specificity: 76-95%
* Cronbach's alpha: 0.64-0.96

**Strengths:**
* First validated tool - extensive literature base
* Weighted scoring may improve diagnostic accuracy
* S-LANSS suitable for postal surveys and research
* Validated in chronic pain populations

**Limitations:**
* Examination component impractical in some settings
* Variable performance across language versions
* Lower sensitivity in some translations (Swedish 35.7%)

---

#### 6.3.6.3. painDETECT (PD-Q)

**Overview:**
* Self-administered questionnaire developed in Germany (2006)
* Originally designed for chronic low back pain neuropathic screening
* Validated across multiple pain conditions
* Does NOT require clinical examination

**Components:**

**Section 1 - Pain Severity (3 questions, 0-10 scale):**
* Current pain intensity
* Strongest pain in last 4 weeks
* Average pain in last 4 weeks

**Section 2 - Pain Course Pattern:**
* Pattern A: Persistent pain with slight fluctuations
* Pattern B: Persistent pain with pain attacks
* Pattern C: Pain attacks without pain between
* Pattern D: Pain attacks with pain between

**Section 3 - Pain Radiation:**
* Does pain radiate to other body regions? (+2 if yes)
* Body diagram for marking location and radiation

**Section 4 - Sensory Symptoms (7 items, 0-5 scale: never to very strongly):**

| Item | Symptom |
|------|---------|
| 1 | Burning sensation |
| 2 | Tingling or prickling |
| 3 | Light touch painful |
| 4 | Sudden pain attacks/electric shocks |
| 5 | Cold or heat painful |
| 6 | Numbness |
| 7 | Slight pressure painful |

**Scoring:**
* Final score: -1 to 38
* **<12: Neuropathic component unlikely (≤15% probability)**
* **12-19: Uncertain/possible (indeterminate)**
* **>19: Neuropathic component likely (>90% probability)**

**Performance Characteristics:**
* Sensitivity: 85% (range 67-95%)
* Specificity: 80% (range 55-98%)
* Cronbach's alpha: 0.76-0.86
* Good test-retest stability for longitudinal monitoring

**Strengths:**
* Purely self-administered - no examiner required
* Captures temporal patterns unique to neuropathic pain
* Includes spatial radiation assessment
* Validated for mixed pain syndromes (low back pain, osteoarthritis)
* Can be used for treatment monitoring

**Limitations:**
* Three-tier scoring creates indeterminate zone (12-19)
* Variable performance across language versions
* Lower specificity in some populations
* Weaker performance in cancer pain with mixed mechanisms

#### 6.3.6.4. OTHER VALIDATED SCREENING TOOLS

**NPQ (Neuropathic Pain Questionnaire):**
* 12 items addressing pain quality (10) and sensitivity changes (2)
* Scored 0-100 per item with discriminant function calculation
* Sensitivity: 50-84%; Specificity: 64-100%
* Highest specificity (100%) in spinal cord injury patients
* NPQ-SF (short form): 3 items - tingling, numbness, touch sensitivity

**ID-PAIN:**
* 6 self-administered items designed for primary care
* Score -1 to 5 (cut-off ≥2)
* Sensitivity: 77-98%; Specificity: 67-85%
* Simple and brief but lower internal consistency (α 0.32-0.76)
* Probability categories: ≥4 (likely), 2-3 (probable), 1 (possible), ≤0 (improbable)

**NPS (Neuropathic Pain Scale):**
* Only tool specifically validated for central neuropathic pain
* Measures 10 pain qualities on 0-10 scale
* Used for monitoring treatment response

#### 6.3.6.6. CLINICAL APPLICATION GUIDELINES

**When to Use Screening Tools:**
* Chronic pain with atypical features
* Pain disproportionate to tissue damage
* Pain in neurological distribution
* Suspected mixed pain syndrome
* Pre-treatment assessment for analgesic selection
* Research and epidemiological studies

**Tool Selection by Setting:**

| Setting | Recommended Tool | Rationale |
|---------|-----------------|-----------|
| Primary care (brief) | ID-PAIN, painDETECT | Self-completed, no examination |
| Primary care (thorough) | DN4-Interview | Good accuracy without examination |
| Pain clinic | DN4, LANSS | Comprehensive with examination |
| Research/epidemiology | S-LANSS, painDETECT | Standardized, self-completed |
| Diabetic neuropathy | DN4 | Highest AUC (0.94) in this population |
| Chronic low back pain | painDETECT, S-DN4 | Originally designed/validated for LBP |
| Treatment monitoring | painDETECT, NPS | Good test-retest reliability |

**Important Caveats:**
* **Screening tools are NOT diagnostic** - they identify possible neuropathic pain
* Clinical examination and investigations required for diagnostic confirmation
* 10-20% of clinically diagnosed neuropathic pain escapes screening detection
* Mixed pain conditions remain challenging - tools designed for pure distinction
* Use two different tools in uncertain cases to improve reliability
* Original language versions generally superior to translations

⚠️ **Safety Warning:** A negative screening result does NOT exclude neuropathic pain. Clinical judgment should override questionnaire scores when examination findings suggest neuropathic mechanism.

**Bottom Line:** DN4, LANSS, and painDETECT are the most widely validated neuropathic pain screening tools. DN4 offers the best balance of sensitivity and specificity when examination is feasible. painDETECT is optimal for self-screening without clinical examination. All tools support but do not replace clinical assessment - they identify "possible" neuropathic pain requiring confirmation through examination and investigation.

### 6.4.3. PSYCHOLOGICAL THERAPIES FOR CHRONIC PAIN

<!-- EPA-12:K1:psychological_therapies EPA-12:S2:multidisciplinary_management -->

#### 6.4.3.1. OVERVIEW AND RATIONALE

Psychological therapies represent an essential component of multimodal chronic pain management. The 2020 Cochrane Review of psychological therapies for chronic pain (excluding headache) provides the most comprehensive evidence synthesis, analysing 75 studies. These interventions target behaviour, thoughts, and emotions to enable patients to live more satisfying lives despite ongoing pain. [Source: Cochrane Library 2020]

**Key Principles:**
* **Shift from cure to management:** Psychological approaches move the clinical agenda away from pain elimination toward functional restoration
* **Active patient participation:** Patients are taught self-management skills rather than receiving passive treatments
* **Process-based intervention:** Focus on how patients relate to pain rather than pain intensity alone
* **Integration with biopsychosocial model:** Addresses psychological and social factors alongside biological mechanisms

**Evidence Quality Summary (Cochrane 2020):**

| Therapy | Studies | Quality | Effect on Pain | Effect on Disability | Effect on Distress |
|---------|---------|---------|----------------|---------------------|-------------------|
| **CBT** | 59 | Moderate | Small (negligible) | Small | Small |
| **ACT** | 5 | Low | Insufficient | Insufficient | Insufficient |
| **BT** | 8 | Low | Small | Small | Small |

---

#### 6.4.3.2. COGNITIVE BEHAVIOURAL THERAPY (CBT)

**Theoretical Framework:**
* Based on the cognitive model: thoughts influence emotions and behaviours
* Pain-related **catastrophizing** and **fear-avoidance beliefs** are key cognitive targets
* Aims to modify maladaptive thoughts and behavioural patterns perpetuating disability
* Supported by the American Psychological Association as having "strong research support" for chronic pain

**Core Treatment Components:**

1. **Psychoeducation**
   * Pain neuroscience education
   * Explaining the biopsychosocial model
   * Distinction between hurt and harm
   * Understanding central sensitisation

2. **Cognitive Restructuring**
   * Identifying negative automatic thoughts
   * Challenging cognitive distortions
   * Developing balanced, realistic appraisals
   * Targeting catastrophizing specifically

3. **Behavioural Activation**
   * Graded activity and pacing strategies
   * Activity-rest cycling
   * Goal setting for meaningful activities
   * Reducing avoidance behaviours

4. **Relaxation Training**
   * Progressive muscle relaxation
   * Diaphragmatic breathing
   * Guided imagery
   * Applied relaxation techniques

5. **Problem-Solving Training**
   * Identifying and defining problems
   * Generating solutions
   * Evaluating outcomes
   * Building adaptive coping repertoire

6. **Relapse Prevention**
   * Anticipating setbacks
   * Maintenance strategies
   * Long-term goal planning

**Evidence Summary (Cochrane 2020):**
* **59 RCTs** included in meta-analysis
* **Pain:** Negligible positive effect at end of treatment
* **Disability:** Small but robust improvement (SMD -0.22 at follow-up)
* **Distress:** Small improvement maintained at follow-up
* Effects generally maintained at 6-12 month follow-up
* **Recommendation:** CBT is the first-line psychological intervention for chronic pain

**Typical Treatment Structure:**
* 8-12 sessions (individual or group)
* 60-90 minutes per session
* Weekly frequency
* Homework assignments between sessions

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** CBT has the strongest evidence base of all psychological therapies for chronic pain. While effect sizes are small, benefits are consistent and maintained at follow-up. The diversity of "talking therapies" is bewildering - but not all are equally effective.

---

#### 6.4.3.3. ACCEPTANCE AND COMMITMENT THERAPY (ACT)

**Theoretical Framework:**
* "Third-wave" cognitive behavioural therapy extending earlier approaches
* Core concept: **Psychological flexibility** - "the capacity to persist or change behaviour, including conscious and open contact with discomfort, guided by goals and values"
* Rather than prioritising symptom reduction, ACT focuses on reducing pain's dominance in patients' lives
* Uses exposure-based methods, metaphor, paradox, and mindfulness exercises

**The Psychological Flexibility Model (Hexaflex):**

Six interconnected therapeutic processes organised into three components:

**OPEN:**
* **Acceptance** - Embracing unwanted experiences connected to goals; willingness to experience pain without avoidance or struggle
* **Cognitive Defusion** - Differentiating thoughts from actual events; creating distance from unhelpful thoughts ("I notice I'm having the thought that...")

**AWARE:**
* **Present Moment Awareness** - Connecting with moment-to-moment experience; mindful attention to current sensations without judgment
* **Self-as-Context** - Experiencing distinction between observed thoughts and the observing self; "observer perspective"

**ENGAGED:**
* **Values Clarification** - Freely chosen directional aims; identifying what matters most across life domains (family, work, health, relationships, personal growth)
* **Committed Action** - Persisting in value-aligned behaviour despite obstacles; concrete goals serving values

**Key Therapeutic Techniques:**

1. **Creative Hopelessness**
   * Collaboratively examining previous pain-management strategies
   * Demonstrating limitations of control-based approaches
   * Creating openness to alternative approaches

2. **Values Clarification Exercises**
   * Identifying meaningful life directions across six domains
   * Distinguishing values from goals
   * Assessment guides all subsequent interventions

3. **Defusion Methods**
   * Repeating thoughts rapidly until they lose meaning
   * Changing vocal qualities (singing thoughts)
   * Reframing thoughts as suggestions rather than commands
   * "Thanking your mind" for the thought

4. **Mindfulness Practice**
   * Present-moment awareness training
   * Non-judgmental observation of sensations
   * Integration throughout treatment

5. **Committed Action Planning**
   * Behaviour aligned with values despite pain
   * Organised exposure to pain-eliciting situations
   * Strengthening values-directed behaviour through direct experience

**Evidence Summary:**

*Meta-analysis findings (2023):*

| Outcome | Effect Size (SMD) | 95% CI | Interpretation |
|---------|-------------------|--------|----------------|
| Pain acceptance | 0.67 | 0.48-0.87 | Medium |
| Pain-related functioning | -0.88 | -1.14 to -0.63 | Large |
| Depression | -0.74 | -0.98 to -0.50 | Medium |
| Pain intensity | -0.45 | -0.62 to -0.27 | Small |
| Anxiety | -0.35 | -0.54 to -0.15 | Small |
| Quality of life | 0.43 | 0.29-0.57 | Small |

[Source: J Pain 2023; PLOS ONE 2024]

**Follow-up Effects:**
* **3 months:** Reduced depression, psychological inflexibility; improved functioning
* **6 months:** Improved mindfulness, functioning, pain acceptance, quality of life
* **6-12 months:** Reduced pain catastrophizing; improved pain-related functioning

**Cochrane Review Caution (2020):**
* Only 5 RCTs met inclusion criteria
* Evidence quality rated as **low**
* "ACT methods have been widely adopted, often replacing CBT, but the evidence found does not support this"
* High-quality, adequately powered trials needed

**Dropout Rates:**
* ACT: 15.8% overall (12.1% for chronic pain specifically)
* CBT: 25.3% overall
* Better retention may reflect patient preference for ACT approach

**Validated Assessment Tools:**

| Instrument | Items | Measures |
|------------|-------|----------|
| **CPAQ** (Chronic Pain Acceptance Questionnaire) | 20 (or 8) | Activity engagement, pain willingness |
| **PIPS** (Psychological Inflexibility in Pain Scale) | Variable | Avoidance, cognitive fusion |
| **CAQ-8** (Committed Action Questionnaire) | 8 | Persistence toward goals |
| **CFQ** (Cognitive Fusion Questionnaire) | 7 | Fusion with pain-related thoughts |

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** ACT shows promising results, particularly for pain acceptance and functioning. However, enthusiasm has outpaced the evidence. Reserve for patients who have not responded to CBT or express preference for values-based approach.

#### 6.4.3.4. MINDFULNESS-BASED STRESS REDUCTION (MBSR)

**Background:**
* Developed by Jon Kabat-Zinn in 1979 at University of Massachusetts
* Originally for stress management; evolved to encompass chronic pain
* Standardised 8-week group programme
* Combines mindfulness meditation with gentle yoga

**Programme Structure:**

| Week | Focus | Practices |
|------|-------|-----------|
| 1-2 | Awareness | Body scan meditation, mindful eating |
| 3-4 | Attention | Sitting meditation, mindful movement |
| 5-6 | Emotional regulation | Walking meditation, yoga |
| 7 | Integration | Choice of practice |
| 8 | Maintenance | Personal practice planning |
| Day-long | Intensive practice | Silent retreat (~6 hours) |

**Core Practices:**

1. **Body Scan Meditation** (45 minutes)
   * Systematic attention to body regions
   * Non-judgmental awareness of sensations
   * Foundation practice for pain management

2. **Sitting Meditation** (45 minutes)
   * Breath awareness
   * Expanding to body, sounds, thoughts
   * Returning attention without judgment

3. **Mindful Movement** (Hatha Yoga)
   * Gentle stretching with awareness
   * Adapted for physical limitations
   * Exploring edge of comfort

4. **Walking Meditation**
   * Slow, deliberate movement
   * Attention to sensations of walking

**Home Practice Requirement:**
* 45 minutes daily, 6 days/week
* Compliance affects outcomes significantly

**Evidence for Chronic Pain:**

*JAMA RCT (Cherkin et al., 2016) - Chronic Low Back Pain:*
* **N = 342** adults with chronic low back pain
* Three arms: MBSR vs CBT vs usual care
* **Results:** MBSR and CBT both superior to usual care
* MBSR comparable to CBT for pain and function
* Benefits maintained at 26 and 52 weeks

*Systematic Reviews:*
* Strong evidence for 8-week and 6-month follow-ups
* Moderate evidence for 1-2 year follow-ups
* Effects on physical function comparable to CBT
* 77% of surveyed patients prefer MBSR over opioid analgesics

**Mechanisms:**
* Reduces pain catastrophizing
* Enhances self-efficacy
* Decreases emotional reactivity to pain
* Promotes acceptance without resignation
* Changes in brain regions associated with pain processing

**Limitations:**
* Evidence quality generally low
* Outcomes vary by chronic pain condition
* Measuring "mindfulness" is challenging
* Requires significant time commitment
* Home practice compliance affects outcomes

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** MBSR is a feasible non-pharmacological option comparable to CBT. Particularly suited for patients interested in mind-body approaches. The 45-minute daily practice requirement may limit adherence.

#### 6.4.3.5. MINDFULNESS-BASED COGNITIVE THERAPY (MBCT)

**Overview:**
* Combines mindfulness practices from MBSR with cognitive therapy elements
* Originally developed for depression relapse prevention
* Adapted for chronic pain populations
* 8-week group programme format

**Key Differences from MBSR:**
* Includes cognitive therapy elements (recognising negative thought patterns)
* More structured approach to thoughts and emotions
* Specific focus on depressive relapse prevention
* May be preferable when depression is prominent comorbidity

**Evidence:**
* Less chronic pain-specific research than MBSR or CBT
* Promising results for pain with comorbid depression
* Comparable to MBSR in available studies

#### 6.4.3.6. GRADED EXPOSURE THERAPY

**Theoretical Basis:**
* Targets fear-avoidance model of chronic pain disability
* Based on exposure principles from anxiety treatment
* Systematic confrontation with feared activities
* Extinguishes conditioned fear responses

**The Fear-Avoidance Model:**
```
Pain → Catastrophizing → Pain-related fear → Avoidance → Disuse/disability → Depression → More pain
```

**Treatment Process:**

1. **Education**
   * Explaining fear-avoidance cycle
   * Distinguishing hurt from harm
   * Setting expectations for exposure

2. **Fear Hierarchy Construction**
   * Identifying avoided activities
   * Rating fear/avoidance (0-100)
   * Ordering from least to most threatening

3. **Graded Exposure Sessions**
   * Beginning with lower-hierarchy items
   * Performing feared activity until fear reduces
   * Progressing through hierarchy
   * Behavioural experiments testing catastrophic predictions

4. **Generalisation**
   * Applying skills to novel situations
   * Relapse prevention

**Evidence:**
* Strong evidence for reducing kinesiophobia
* Improves disability and function
* Particularly effective for chronic low back pain
* May be combined with CBT elements

#### 6.4.3.7. PAIN MANAGEMENT PROGRAMMES (PMPs)

**Definition:**
Interdisciplinary rehabilitation programmes delivered according to cognitive behavioural principles, typically in group settings, aiming to promote behaviour change and reduce pain's impact on functional ability. [Source: BJA Education 2017]

**Programme Structure:**

| Component | Content |
|-----------|---------|
| **Duration** | 3-8 weeks (intensive) or longer (less intensive) |
| **Format** | Group-based (6-12 participants) |
| **Hours** | 50-150+ hours total contact |
| **Team** | Physician, physiotherapist, psychologist, occupational therapist, nurse |

**Core Components:**

1. **Pain Neuroscience Education**
   * Understanding chronic pain mechanisms
   * Central sensitisation
   * Neuroplasticity and recovery

2. **Physical Reconditioning**
   * Graded exercise
   * Stretching and flexibility
   * Aerobic conditioning
   * Functional activities

3. **Psychological Interventions**
   * CBT principles
   * Cognitive restructuring
   * Relaxation training
   * Goal setting

4. **Activity Management**
   * Pacing strategies
   * Activity-rest balance
   * Baseline establishment
   * Gradual increase

5. **Medication Review**
   * Rationalisation of analgesics
   * Reduction of unnecessary medications
   * Managing expectations

6. **Vocational Rehabilitation**
   * Return-to-work planning
   * Workplace modifications
   * Liaison with employers

**Evidence (Cochrane Review - Multidisciplinary Rehabilitation):**
* **More effective than usual care** (moderate quality evidence)
* **More effective than physical treatments alone** (low quality evidence)
* Improves pain, disability, and psychological outcomes
* Effects maintained at follow-up

**Patient Selection:**
* Failed single-modality treatment
* Significant functional impairment
* Motivation for active participation
* Psychosocial factors contributing to disability
* Absent or addressed red flags

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** PMPs shift focus from pain control to living well despite pain. High-level evidence supports their use as part of integrated pain management. The group format provides peer support and normalisation of the chronic pain experience.

#### 6.4.3.8. PATIENT SELECTION FOR PSYCHOLOGICAL THERAPIES

**Indicators for Referral:**

| Indicator | Rationale |
|-----------|-----------|
| High psychological distress (anxiety, depression) | Psychological factors perpetuate pain and disability |
| Pain catastrophizing (PCS ≥30) | Strongest predictor of poor treatment outcome |
| Fear-avoidance behaviour | Drives deconditioning and disability spiral |
| Low self-efficacy | Predicts poor functional outcomes |
| Poor quality of life despite adequate medical management | Psychological factors likely dominant |
| Functional impairment disproportionate to pathology | Central sensitisation with psychological amplification |
| Multiple failed interventions | Need alternative approach |
| Interest in self-management | Motivation enhances outcomes |

**Relative Contraindications:**

| Factor | Consideration |
|--------|---------------|
| Active psychosis | Requires stabilisation first |
| Severe cognitive impairment | May not benefit from cognitively demanding therapy |
| Active unmanaged substance abuse | Address addiction first |
| Acute suicidality | Requires crisis intervention |
| Complete unwillingness to engage | May need motivational interviewing first |

**Matching Patients to Therapies:**

| Patient Characteristics | Suggested Approach |
|------------------------|-------------------|
| Catastrophizing, negative cognitions | CBT |
| Older adults, longer pain duration | ACT may be more effective |
| Younger adults, acute onset | CBT may be more effective |
| Interest in mind-body approaches | MBSR |
| High kinesiophobia, avoidance | Graded exposure |
| Multiple psychosocial issues, complex presentation | Multidisciplinary PMP |
| Comorbid depression | MBCT or CBT with depression focus |

#### 6.4.3.9. STEPPED CARE MODEL FOR PSYCHOLOGICAL INTERVENTIONS

**Step 1: Self-Management Support**
* Pain neuroscience education
* Written materials and resources
* Supported self-management programs
* Online resources and apps

**Step 2: Brief Psychological Intervention**
* Single-session education
* Brief CBT (4-6 sessions)
* Bibliotherapy with guidance
* Group psychoeducation

**Step 3: Specialist Psychological Therapy**
* Full CBT programme (8-12 sessions)
* ACT programme (6-8 sessions)
* MBSR/MBCT (8 weeks)
* Individual therapy for complex cases

**Step 4: Intensive Multidisciplinary Programme**
* Pain Management Programme
* Inpatient or day-patient rehabilitation
* Comprehensive interdisciplinary care
* Extended duration programmes

**Principles:**
* Start with least intensive intervention appropriate to risk level
* Step up if insufficient response
* Step down when stable
* Match intensity to need

#### 6.4.3.10. DELIVERY FORMATS

**Individual vs Group Therapy:**

| Format | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|--------|------------|---------------|
| **Individual** | Tailored content, flexible scheduling, privacy | Higher cost, no peer support |
| **Group** | Cost-effective, peer support, normalisation | Fixed schedule, less individualised |

**Remote/Digital Delivery:**

*Cochrane Review (2023) - Remotely-Delivered Psychological Therapies:*
* Online CBT may improve pain and disability
* Face-to-face ACT superior to internet-delivered ACT for physical outcomes
* Quality of life effects unclear
* Useful for increasing access

**Emerging Modalities:**
* Smartphone apps (guided and unguided)
* Virtual reality applications
* Telehealth individual therapy
* Hybrid models (online + in-person)

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Face-to-face delivery generally produces larger effects than remote delivery, particularly for ACT. However, remote delivery increases accessibility and may be appropriate for motivated patients with limited access to specialist services.

#### 6.4.3.11. INTEGRATION WITH MEDICAL MANAGEMENT

**Timing:**
* Early psychological intervention may prevent chronification
* Concurrent with rather than sequential to medical treatment
* Consider before invasive interventions in high-risk patients

**Communication:**
* Clear referral pathways between pain medicine and psychology
* Shared treatment goals
* Avoid "nothing more can be done medically" messaging
* Frame as part of comprehensive care, not psychiatric referral

**Medication Considerations:**
* Psychological therapy may enable medication reduction
* Address medication beliefs and expectations
* Coordinate tapering with therapy progress
* Consider medication effects on cognitive function

⚠️ **Safety Warning:** Do not delay psychological intervention until all medical options exhausted. Early intervention targeting psychological risk factors may prevent chronic pain development and disability.

**Bottom Line:** Psychological therapies are essential components of chronic pain management. CBT has the strongest evidence base with small but consistent benefits for disability and distress. ACT shows promise, particularly for pain acceptance and functioning, but evidence remains limited. MBSR is comparable to CBT for certain conditions. Pain Management Programmes offer comprehensive interdisciplinary intervention for complex patients. Selection should be guided by patient characteristics, risk stratification, and available resources. A stepped care model ensures appropriate matching of intervention intensity to patient need.

[Source: Cochrane Library 2020; PMC6174685; J Pain 2023; BJA Education 2017; JAMA 2016]

### 6.4.4. SOCIAL INTERVENTIONS

* **Spouse-assisted coping skills training:** Larger decreases in fear and catastrophizing than standard multidisciplinary intervention
* **Workplace liaison:** Modified duties, graduated return-to-work programs
* **Support groups:** Peer support and normalisation
* **Social prescribing:** Community engagement, physical activity groups

## 6.5. SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS

### 6.5.1. SEX AND GENDER FACTORS

Women report greater pain prevalence, severity, and frequency:

**Biological mechanisms:**
* Hormonal influences (symptom variation across menstrual cycle)
* Enhanced temporal summation
* Reduced diffuse noxious inhibitory control (DNIC)
* Sex differences in endogenous opioid receptor binding

**Psychosocial mechanisms:**
* Gender role expectations influence pain reporting
* Catastrophizing mediates sex differences in pain outcomes
* Women more willing to report pain and seek healthcare

### 6.5.2. TRAUMA AND CHRONIC PAIN

* **Early-life trauma:** Increases chronic widespread pain risk 2-3 fold
* **Childhood abuse:** 97% increased risk for painful somatic syndromes
* **PTSD:** Acts as mediator between trauma and chronic pain; effects persist >30 years
* **Traumatic brain injury:** >50% report chronic pain complaints

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Screen for trauma history in patients with pain disproportionate to objective findings or poor treatment response. Consider trauma-informed care approaches.

**Bottom Line:** The biopsychosocial model is the foundation of modern chronic pain assessment and management. Yellow flags (psychological risk factors) are powerful predictors of chronic pain development - more so than pain intensity. Comprehensive assessment using validated tools identifies modifiable risk factors. Treatment should be stratified by risk level, with high-risk patients requiring multidisciplinary intervention addressing all biopsychosocial domains. Catastrophizing is the single most important modifiable risk factor for treatment failure.
