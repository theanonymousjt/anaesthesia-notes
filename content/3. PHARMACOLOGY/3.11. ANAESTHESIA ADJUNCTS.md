# 1. HISTAMINE AND ASPIRATION PROPHYLAXIS









## 1.1. HISTAMINE PHYSIOLOGY










#### 1.1.1.1. SYNTHESIS AND METABOLISM

* **Found in:** CNS, gastric mucosa, and other peripheral tissues [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Synthesis:** Produced by decarboxylation of the amino acid histidine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Role:** Plays a major role in secretion of hydrochloric acid by parietal cells in the stomach [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

![Histamine synthesis pathway](ADJUNCTS_ADJUNCTS_Untitled.png_150fcf.png_6cd97b.png)

* **Receptor subtypes:** Multiple histamine receptors (H1-4) with different signaling mechanisms [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * H1 receptors: Activate phospholipase C [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * H2 receptors: Activate cAMP [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * H3 receptors: Mediate negative feedback [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * H4 receptors: Active in allergy and inflammation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Metabolism:** Histamine is metabolized by histamine-N-methyltransferase to inactive metabolites [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

#### 1.1.1.2. SYSTEMIC EFFECTS

* **Cardiovascular effects:**
  * Reduces arterial blood pressure, but increases heart rate and myocardial contractility [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * H1 effects: Increases capillary permeability and ventricular irritability [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * H2 effects: Increases heart rate and contractility [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Both H1 and H2 cause peripheral arteriolar dilation and some coronary vasodilation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Respiratory effects:**
  * H1 effects: Constricts bronchiolar smooth muscle [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * H2 effects: Produces mild bronchodilation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Variable effects on pulmonary vasculature - H1 causes pulmonary vasodilation, H2 causes pulmonary vasoconstriction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Gastrointestinal effects:**
  * H2 effects: Increases gastric acid secretion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * H1 effects: Causes contraction of intestinal smooth muscle [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Dermal effects:**
  * H1 effects: Classic wheal and flare response due to increased capillary permeability and vasodilation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Immunological effects:**
  * H1 effects: Attracts leukocytes and induces synthesis of prostaglandins [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * H2 effects: Activates suppressor T lymphocytes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Histamine is a crucial mediator of allergic responses, gastric acid secretion, and vascular permeability that acts through four receptor subtypes (H1-H4) with complex and sometimes opposing physiological effects.

## 1.2. H1 RECEPTOR ANTAGONISTS










#### 1.2.1.1. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* **Competitive antagonism** at H1 receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* Many H1 antagonists also have considerable **antimuscarinic (atropine-like) activity**, causing dry mouth and other anticholinergic effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* Some also have **antiserotonergic activity**, contributing to their antiemetic properties [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

![H1 antagonist structural comparison](ADJUNCTS_ADJUNCTS_Untitled_1.png_ef2d3b.png_a144df.png)

#### 1.2.1.2. CLASSIFICATION

* **First-generation** (older) antihistamines:
  * Cross blood-brain barrier readily, causing significant sedation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Examples: diphenhydramine, promethazine, cyclizine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Second-generation** (newer) antihistamines:
  * Limited penetration across blood-brain barrier, producing little or no sedation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Examples: loratadine, cetirizine, fexofenadine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

#### 1.2.1.3. CLINICAL USES

* **Multiple applications** due to histamine antagonism and additional antimuscarinic/antiserotonergic effects [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Suppression of allergic reactions and symptoms of upper respiratory tract infections (urticaria, rhinitis, conjunctivitis) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Management of vertigo, nausea, and vomiting (motion sickness, Ménière disease) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Sedation (particularly first-generation agents) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Suppression of cough [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Management of dyskinesias (parkinsonism, drug-induced extrapyramidal side effects) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* ⚠️ H1 blockers **prevent the bronchoconstrictive response to histamine** but are **ineffective in treating bronchial asthma**, which is primarily due to other mediators [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* ⚠️ H1 blockers **will not completely prevent the hypotensive effect of histamine** unless an H2 blocker is administered concomitantly [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

#### 1.2.1.4. SPECIFIC AGENT: CYCLIZINE

* **Structure:** Piperazine derivative [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Mechanism:** H1 antagonism, mainly vestibular and cerebellar in action with some antimuscarinic activity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Presentation:** The parenteral preparation is prepared with lactic acid at pH 3.2, making intramuscular and intravenous injection particularly painful [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Dose:** 25-50mg every eight hours in adults, 0.1mg/kg in children [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Side effects:**
  * Tachycardia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Dry mouth [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Drowsiness [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Blurred vision [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Increased lower esophageal sphincter tone [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
* **Clinical uses:** Used for motion sickness, radiotherapy, PONV, opioid-induced emesis, and Ménière's disease [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
* **Pharmacokinetics:** Well absorbed orally with high bioavailability (approximately 75%) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 1.2.1.5. SPECIFIC AGENT: DIPHENHYDRAMINE

* **Dosing:** 25 to 50 mg (0.5–1.5 mg/kg) PO, IM, or IV every 3-6 hours [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **CNS effects:** May cause significant sedation, but usually minimal effects in the absence of other sedative medications [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* ⚠️ **Interactions:** Potentiates other CNS depressants - benzodiazepines, opioids, barbiturates [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** H1 receptor antagonists are versatile medications used for allergic symptoms, motion sickness, and as adjuncts in anesthesia practice. First-generation agents cause significant sedation and anticholinergic effects, while second-generation agents are more selective with fewer CNS effects.

## 1.3. H2 RECEPTOR ANTAGONISTS










#### 1.3.1.1. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* **Competitive inhibition** of histamine binding to H2 receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* Reduces gastric acid output and raises gastric pH [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* These drugs affect the pH of only those gastric secretions that occur after their administration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

![H2 antagonist structural comparison](ADJUNCTS_ADJUNCTS_Untitled_2.png_662627.png_2dfd67.png)

#### 1.3.1.2. PHYSIOLOGY OF GASTRIC SECRETION 

* Gastrin and acetylcholine (ACh) stimulate parietal cells (via gastrin and muscarinic receptors) to secrete H+ into the gastric lumen [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* ACh is released from parasympathetic postganglionic fibers while gastrin is released from G-cells in the antral mucosa [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* The main stimulus for parietal cell acid secretion is via histamine receptor activation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Gastrin and ACh also stimulate the adjacent paracrine cells to produce and release histamine, which acts on the parietal cell, increasing cAMP and therefore acid secretion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 1.3.1.3. CLINICAL USES

* All H2 receptor antagonists are equally effective in treatment of:
  * Peptic ulcer disease (PUD) and gastric ulcers [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Hypersecretory states (Zollinger-Ellison syndrome) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Perioperative aspiration prophylaxis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

#### 1.3.1.4. PHARMACOKINETICS

* **Administration timing:**
  * Typically administered at bedtime for chronic conditions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * For perioperative prophylaxis, given 2 hours before surgery [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Dosage adjustment:** Requires dose reduction in patients with significant renal impairment [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

#### 1.3.1.5. SIDE EFFECTS

* **Cardiovascular:** Rapid IV administration of cimetidine or ranitidine rarely associated with hypotension, bradycardia, arrhythmias, and cardiac arrest [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Long-term cimetidine use:**
  * Hepatotoxicity (elevated serum transaminases) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Interstitial nephritis (elevated serum creatinine) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Granulocytopenia and thrombocytopenia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * ⚠️ Binds to androgen receptors, causing gynecomastia and impotence [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Delirium, especially in elderly patients [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Newer agents:** Ranitidine, nizatidine, and famotidine do not affect androgen receptors and penetrate the blood-brain barrier poorly, producing fewer side effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Respiratory system:** Low-grade aspiration of gastric content that has been stripped of its acidic, antibacterial environment may result in increased nosocomial pulmonary infections in critically ill ventilated patients [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 1.3.1.6. DRUG INTERACTIONS

* **Cimetidine:** Binds to cytochrome P450, slowing the metabolism of many anesthetic drugs [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Metabolic interactions:** Cimetidine inhibits hepatic cytochrome P450 and will slow the metabolism of the following drugs: lidocaine, propranolol, diazepam, phenytoin, tricyclic antidepressants, warfarin, and aminophylline [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Ranitidine:** Weak inhibitor of P450, but no significant drug interactions have been demonstrated [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Famotidine and nizatidine:** Do not affect cytochrome P450 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

#### 1.3.1.7. SPECIFIC AGENTS

* **Cimetidine:**
  * Selective H2 receptor competitive antagonist [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Only H2 receptor antagonist with an imidazole structure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Acts on parietal cells in stomach to reduce acid secretion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Can trigger bradycardia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Has anti-androgen effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * CYP450 inhibitor [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Pharmacokinetics: Well absorbed from the small bowel (oral bioavailability approximately 60%), poorly plasma protein-bound (20%), partially metabolized (up to 60% if administered orally) in the liver by cytochrome P450 and approximately 50% excreted unchanged in the urine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  
* **Ranitidine:**
  * Stronger than cimetidine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * No inhibition of hepatic enzymes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * ⚠️ Associated with stomach and esophageal cancer, now largely obsolete [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * More potent than cimetidine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Also used in combination with antibiotics to eradicate H. pylori and widely used in labor with apparently no deleterious effects on the fetus or progress of labor [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Pharmacokinetics: Well absorbed from the gut, poorly protein-bound (15%) and partially metabolized in the liver. Undergoes greater first-pass metabolism than cimetidine (oral bioavailability approximately 50%), while 50% of an administered dose is excreted unchanged in the urine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Other agents:**
  * Nizatidine and famotidine are newer H2 antagonists with increased potency [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Like ranitidine, they do not inhibit hepatic cytochrome P450 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

**Bottom Line:** H2 receptor antagonists are effective for reducing gastric acidity and volume in perioperative aspiration prophylaxis. Newer agents (ranitidine, famotidine) have fewer side effects and drug interactions than cimetidine, which significantly inhibits cytochrome P450 enzymes.

## 1.4. ANTACIDS










#### 1.4.1.1. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* Neutralize acidity of gastric fluid by providing a base that reacts with hydrogen ions to form water [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* Immediate effect on raising gastric pH [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* ⚠️ However, they generally increase intragastric volume [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

#### 1.4.1.2. CLASSIFICATION

* **Particulate antacids:**
  * Contain aluminum or magnesium hydroxide [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * ⚠️ Aspiration of particulate antacids produces abnormalities in lung function comparable to those that occur following acid aspiration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Side effects:
	* Aluminum compounds tend to work more slowly and cause constipation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Can interact with other medications such as tetracyclines [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Magnesium-containing antacids can induce diarrhea [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Neither is absorbed from the gut significantly and due to their relatively low water solubility, they are long-acting provided they remain in the stomach [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Aluminum ions form complexes with some drugs (e.g., tetracycline) and reduce their absorption [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Nonparticulate antacids:**
  * Sodium citrate or sodium bicarbonate [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * 💡 Much less damaging to lung alveoli if aspirated [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Preferred for perioperative aspiration prophylaxis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * These antacids are water-soluble and their onset of action is faster than the aluminumand magnesium-containing antacids [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * They are absorbed into the systemic circulation and may cause a metabolic alkalosis if taken in excess [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Sodium bicarbonate releases carbon dioxide as it reacts with gastric acid, resulting in belching [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 1.4.1.3. CLINICAL USES

* **Perioperative aspiration prophylaxis:**
  * 💡 Particularly valuable in emergency surgery when rapid neutralization is needed [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Commonly used in obstetric anesthesia before cesarean delivery [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Treatment of PUD and GERD** by raising pH of gastric contents [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* ⚠️ **Limitations:**
  * Lose effectiveness 30-60 minutes after ingestion [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Need to carefully balance pH benefit against increased gastric volume [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

#### 1.4.1.4. SPECIFIC PREPARATIONS

* **Sodium citrate (Bicitra, Polycitra):**
  * 0.3 M solution of sodium citrate (Bicitra contains sodium citrate and citric acid; Polycitra contains sodium citrate, potassium citrate, and citric acid) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * **Dosing:** 15 to 30 mL orally, 15 to 30 minutes prior to induction [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * 💡 Frequently used in emergency obstetric cases due to rapid onset [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Often described as having an unpleasant taste [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Thirty milliliters of 0.3 M sodium citrate is often used with ranitidine to reduce gastric acidity before cesarean section. It should be given less than 10 minutes before the start of surgery due to its limited duration of action [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .

**Bottom Line:** Nonparticulate antacids like sodium citrate provide rapid neutralization of gastric acid for perioperative aspiration prophylaxis. While effective in raising gastric pH, their short duration of action (30-60 minutes) and the potential for increased gastric volume must be considered when timing administration.

## 1.5. METOCLOPRAMIDE










#### 1.5.1.1. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* **Peripheral action:** Acts as a cholinomimetic by facilitating acetylcholine transmission at selective muscarinic receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Prokinetic effects in upper GI tract are abolished by anticholinergic agents [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Does not depend on vagal innervation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Does not stimulate secretions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Central action:** Dopamine receptor antagonist in the chemoreceptor trigger zone (CRTZ) of the CNS [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Structural similarities:** Has structural similarities to procainamide although it has no local anesthetic properties [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Prokinetic mechanism:** Mediated by antagonism of peripheral dopaminergic (D2) receptors and selective stimulation of gastric muscarinic receptors (which can be blocked by atropine) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 1.5.1.2. CLINICAL EFFECTS

* **Gastrointestinal effects:**
  * Stimulatory effects on intestinal smooth muscle (via acetylcholine) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Increases lower esophageal sphincter tone [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* Speeds gastric emptying [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* Lowers gastric fluid volume [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * 💡 Particularly valuable for aspiration prophylaxis due to these multiple beneficial effects [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Its peripheral actions result in increased lower esophageal sphincter tone and relaxation of the pylorus [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .
  * Has no effect on gastric secretion [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .
* **Central effects:**
  * Blocks dopamine receptors in CRTZ [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * ⚠️ During perioperative period, drug's ability to reduce PONV is negligible compared to dedicated antiemetics [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

#### 1.5.1.3. PHARMACOKINETICS

* **Dosing:** 10-15 mg (0.25 mg/kg) PO, IM, IV (administer over 5 minutes) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Larger doses (1-2 mg/kg) have been used to prevent emesis during chemotherapy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Onset of action:**
  * Much more rapid following IV administration (3-5 minutes) than oral administration (30-60 minutes) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Elimination:** Requires dose reduction in patients with kidney dysfunction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* Well absorbed from the gut although first-pass metabolism varies significantly, producing a wide range in oral bioavailability (30–90%) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Conjugated in the liver and excreted along with unchanged drug in the urine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 1.5.1.4. SIDE EFFECTS

* **Gastrointestinal:** Rapid IV administration can cause abdominal cramping [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Contraindications:**
  * ⚠️ Contraindicated in patients with complete intestinal obstruction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * ⚠️ Can cause hypertensive crisis in patients with pheochromocytoma by releasing catecholamines [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Neurological:**
  * Sedation, nervousness [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * ⚠️ Extrapyramidal signs from dopamine antagonism (i.e., akathisia) are uncommon and reversible [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Should be avoided in patients with Parkinsonism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Extrapyramidal effects are more common in young females (1 in 5000) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Rarely may precipitate the neuroleptic malignant syndrome [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Sedation is seen more commonly during long-term administration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Agitation occasionally seen following intramuscular premedication with 10–20 mg [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Endocrine:** Metoclopramide-induced increases in aldosterone and prolactin secretion are probably inconsequential during short-term therapy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Cardiovascular:** Hypotension, tachyand bradycardias have been reported following rapid intravenous administration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Metabolic:** May precipitate hyperprolactinemia and galactorrhoea, and should be avoided in porphyria. Inhibits plasma cholinesterase activity in vitro and may therefore prolong the effects of drugs metabolized by this enzyme [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 1.5.1.5. DRUG INTERACTIONS

* **Antimuscarinic drugs:** Atropine and glycopyrrolate block the GI effects of metoclopramide [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Other antipsychotics:** Concurrent use of phenothiazines or butyrophenones (droperidol) increases the likelihood of extrapyramidal side effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

#### 1.5.1.6. CLINICAL EFFICACY 

* Approximately half of the clinical studies have demonstrated placebo to be as effective as metoclopramide as an antiemetic [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* However, metoclopramide appears to be most effective when 20 mg is given at the end of anesthesia rather than at induction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

**Bottom Line:** Metoclopramide enhances gastric emptying and increases lower esophageal sphincter tone, making it valuable for aspiration prophylaxis. Its antiemetic properties are less pronounced in the perioperative setting, and care must be taken to avoid extrapyramidal side effects, especially when combined with other dopamine antagonists.

## 1.6. PROTON PUMP INHIBITORS (PPIS)










#### 1.6.1.1. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* Bind to hydrogen-potassium ATPase (proton pumps) of parietal cells in gastric mucosa and inhibit acid secretion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* Irreversibly inhibit the final common pathway of acid secretion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* More potent and longer-lasting acid suppression than H2 receptor antagonists [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* A proton pump (K+/H+ ATPase) in the membrane of the parietal cell mediates the final common pathway of gastric acid secretion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 1.6.1.2. CLINICAL USES

* Peptic ulcer disease (PUD) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* Zollinger-Ellison syndrome (ZES) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* Perioperative aspiration prophylaxis in high-risk patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* 💡 Often used for patients on long-term NSAID therapy to prevent gastric ulceration [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

#### 1.6.1.3. SPECIFIC AGENTS AND DOSING

* **Omeprazole:** 20mg PO, also available IV [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Irreversibly blocks proton pump (K+/H+/ATPase) on parietal cell luminal membrane [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Can cause hyponatremia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * ⚠️ Interacts with clopidogrel by inhibiting CYP2C19, which activates clopidogrel, leading to reduced plasma concentrations of active clopidogrel [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Has a chiral center and is administered as a racemic mixture of the Rand S-enantiomers or as the enantiopure preparation esomeprazole, which contains only the S-enantiomer [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Is degraded in gastric acid and so is prepared as a capsule with enteric-coated granules so that absorption occurs in the small intestine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Is a prodrug, becoming active within the parietal cell [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Undergoes complete hepatic metabolism by cytochrome CYP2C19 (and to a lesser extent CYP3A4) to inactive metabolites, which are excreted in the urine (80%) and bile (20%) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * CYP2C19 is subject to genetic polymorphism with ultrarapid metabolisers (CYP2C19*17, which may result in ineffective treatment) and poor metabolisers (CYP2C19*3, with markedly reduced or absent enzyme activity in up to 20% of Asians) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Is the most potent inhibitor of CYP2C19 of all the PPIs and may interfere with the metabolism of the prodrug to the active form of clopidogrel, thereby reducing its antiplatelet effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Lansoprazole:** 15mg PO [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Rabeprazole:** 20mg PO [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Pantoprazole:** 40mg PO [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]. May be considered as an alternative to omeprazole in patients taking clopidogrel [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Esomeprazole:** May be considered as an alternative to omeprazole in patients taking clopidogrel [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 1.6.1.4. SIDE EFFECTS

* Generally well tolerated, with few side effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Common side effects** are usually GI-related:
  * Nausea, abdominal pain, constipation, diarrhea [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Rare but serious side effects:**
  * Myalgia, anaphylaxis, angioedema, severe dermatological reactions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Gastric enterochromaffin-like cell hyperplasia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * ⚠️ Increased risk of pneumonia secondary to bacterial colonization in the higher-pH environment [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Long-term use associated with increased fracture risk, vitamin B12 deficiency, and possibly dementia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Metabolic effects:** Inhibition of hepatic cytochrome P450. This is limited, and although close monitoring is recommended with concurrent use of warfarin and phenytoin, their effects are rarely potentiated. The effects of diazepam may be increased via a similar mechanism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 1.6.1.5. EFFECTS ON GI PHYSIOLOGY 

* The acidity and volume of gastric secretions is reduced [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* No change is seen in lower esophageal sphincter tone or gastric emptying [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

**Bottom Line:** Proton pump inhibitors provide potent and long-lasting suppression of gastric acid secretion, making them effective for peptic ulcer disease, GERD, and aspiration prophylaxis. While generally well-tolerated, they carry risks with long-term use, including increased pneumonia risk, and can interact with other medications like clopidogrel.

## 1.7. MUCOSAL PROTECTORS AND PROSTAGLANDIN ANALOGUES 










#### 1.7.1.1. SUCRALFATE 

* **Mechanism:** Exerts a generalized cytoprotective effect by forming a barrier over the gut lumen [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Actions:** Protects ulcerated regions specifically. Does not alter gastric pH, motility, or lower esophageal sphincter tone, although it has been reported to have bacteriostatic effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Pharmacokinetics:** Acts locally with virtually none absorbed from the gut. Consequently, it has no effect on the central nervous or cardiorespiratory systems [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Side effects:**
  * Minor gastric disturbances [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Enhanced aluminum absorption in patients with renal dysfunction or on dialysis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * May reduce the absorption of certain drugs (ciprofloxacin, warfarin, phenytoin, and H2 antagonists) by direct binding [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 1.7.1.2. MISOPROSTIL (PROSTAGLANDIN ANALOGUE) 

* **Structure:** Synthetic analogue of prostaglandin E1 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Uses:** Prevention and treatment of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory induced ulcers [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Mechanism of action:** Inhibits gastric acid secretion and increases mucous secretion, thereby protecting the gastric mucosa [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Side effects:**
  * **Endocrine:** Increases uterine tone and may precipitate miscarriage. Menorrhagia and vaginal bleeding have been reported [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * **GI:** Severe diarrhea and other intestinal upset [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * **Cardiovascular:** At normal doses, it is unlikely to produce hypotension, but its use is cautioned where hypotension could precipitate severe complications (i.e., in cerebrovascular or cardiovascular disease) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Pharmacokinetics:** Rapidly absorbed from the gut. Metabolism is by fatty-acid-oxidizing systems throughout the body, and no alteration in dose is required in renal or hepatic impairment [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

**Bottom Line:** Mucosal protectors like sucralfate form a physical barrier over ulcerated areas without altering gastric pH, while prostaglandin analogues like misoprostil inhibit acid secretion and enhance protective mucous secretion. These agents provide alternative approaches to gastric protection, particularly for NSAID-induced ulcers.

# 2. SEROTONIN AND POSTOPERATIVE NAUSEA AND VOMITING [PART A/B/C]









## 2.1. SEROTONIN PHYSIOLOGY










#### 2.1.1.1. SYNTHESIS AND METABOLISM

* **Location:** Found in large quantities in platelets and the gastrointestinal tract (enterochromaffin cells and myenteric plexus) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Synthesis:** Formed by hydroxylation and decarboxylation of the amino acid tryptophan [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Metabolism:** Monoamine oxidase inactivates serotonin into 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

#### 2.1.1.2. RECEPTOR TYPES

* At least 7 receptor types exist, most with multiple subtypes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * **5HT1:** Neuronal inhibition [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * **5HT2A:** Mediates smooth muscle contraction and platelet aggregation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * **5HT3:** Mediates vomiting and is found in GIT and brain (area postrema; nucleus tractus solitarius) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * **5HT4:** Mediates GIT secretion and peristalsis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * **5HT6, 5HT7:** Found in the limbic system and associated with depression [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* All serotonin receptors except 5-HT3 are coupled to G proteins and affect either adenylyl cyclase or phospholipase C [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* 5-HT3 receptor effects are mediated via an ion channel [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

#### 2.1.1.3. SYSTEMIC EFFECTS

* **Cardiovascular effects:**
  * Serotonin is a powerful vasoconstrictor except in heart and skeletal muscle [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * In healthy endothelium, coronary vessels vasodilate; in damaged endothelium, coronary vessels vasoconstrict [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Pulmonary and renal vasculatures are very sensitive to the arterial vasoconstrictive effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Modest and transient increases in cardiac contractility and heart rate may occur immediately following serotonin release; reflex bradycardia often follows [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * ⚠️ Excessive serotonin can produce serotonin syndrome, characterized by hypertension, hyperthermia, and agitation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Respiratory effects:**
  * Contracted smooth muscles increases airway resistance [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Bronchoconstriction is a prominent feature of carcinoid syndrome [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Gastrointestinal effects:**
  * Direct smooth muscle contraction (via 5-HT2 receptors) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Serotonin-induced release of acetylcholine in the myenteric plexus (via 5-HT3 receptors) greatly augments peristalsis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Secretions are unaffected [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Hematological effects:**
  * Causes platelet aggregation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Serotonin is a multifunctional neurotransmitter and local hormone that acts on multiple receptor subtypes throughout the body. The 5-HT3 receptor is particularly relevant to anesthesia practice as it mediates nausea and vomiting, making it a target for antiemetic medications.

## 2.2. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF PONV










#### 2.2.1.1. NEURAL PATHWAYS OF PONV

* The emetic response involves coordination between several neural structures [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * **Chemoreceptor trigger zone (CTZ)** located in the area postrema of the medulla [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Outside the blood-brain barrier, activated by substances in circulation such as anesthetics and opioids [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * **Nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS)** coordinates the vomiting reflex [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * **Vomiting center** in the medulla integrates signals from multiple sources [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

![Neurological pathway involved in PONV](ADJUNCTS_ADJUNCTS_Untitled_6.png_6b8cd7.png_5da923.png)

* **Afferent pathways** that can trigger nausea and vomiting [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * **Vestibular system** – motion sickness, positional change [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * **Vagal and sympathetic afferents** from the GI tract [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * **CTZ** – detects emetogenic substances in blood [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * **Cerebral cortex** – psychogenic (anticipatory) nausea/vomiting [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

#### 2.2.1.2. NEURAL INPUTS SUMMARY 

* The vomiting center (VC) coordinates vomiting without a discrete anatomical site but as a collection of effector neurons situated in the medulla [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* VC projects to the vagus and phrenic nerves and also to the spinal motor neurons supplying the abdominal muscles, which when acting together bring about the vomiting reflex [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* VC has important input from the CTZ, which lies in the area postrema on the floor of the fourth ventricle but is functionally outside the blood-brain barrier [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* CTZ is rich in dopamine (D2) receptors and serotonin (5-HT) receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Acetylcholine (ACh) is important in neural transmission from the vestibular apparatus [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 2.2.1.3. KEY NEUROTRANSMITTERS

* Multiple neurotransmitters are involved in the emetic response [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * **Serotonin (5-HT3)** – primary mediator, especially for chemotherapy-induced and early PONV [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * **Dopamine (D2)** – important in the CTZ [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * **Histamine (H1)** – particularly in vestibular-mediated nausea [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * **Acetylcholine (muscarinic)** – in vestibular-mediated and central pathways [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * **Substance P (NK1)** – mediator in both central and peripheral pathways [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

#### 2.2.1.4. PHASES OF PONV

* **Acute PONV** (0-24 hours):
  * Early phase (0-2 hours) – primarily serotonin-mediated [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Late phase (2-24 hours) – multiple neurotransmitters involved [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Delayed PONV** or Postdischarge Nausea and Vomiting (PDNV) – beyond 24 hours [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

#### 2.2.1.5. ANESTHETIC FACTORS CONTRIBUTING TO PONV

* **Volatile anesthetics** – dose-dependent effect, particularly in early post-op period [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Nitrous oxide** – may have independent emetogenic effect [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Opioids** – dose-dependent effect through action on the CTZ [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Neostigmine** – may contribute through cholinergic stimulation of GI tract [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Anesthesia duration** – longer cases associated with increased risk [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

#### 2.2.1.6. NON-PHARMACOLOGICAL PREVENTION 

* Treatment should aim at reducing the afferent supply to the VC [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
* Minimizing the administration of opioids by using non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
* Avoiding unnecessary anticholinesterase administration [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
* When propofol is used to maintain anesthesia for minor surgery, where the use of opioids is limited, it may reduce the incidence of PONV [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .

**Bottom Line:** PONV involves complex neural pathways including the chemoreceptor trigger zone, nucleus tractus solitarius, and vomiting center, with multiple neurotransmitters (particularly serotonin, dopamine, and substance P) mediating the response. Understanding this pathophysiology is essential for implementing effective prophylaxis and treatment strategies.

  * 1 factor: 20% PONV risk [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * 2 factors: 40% PONV risk [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * 3 factors: 60% PONV risk [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * 4 factors: 80% PONV risk [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

#### 2.3.1.3. RISK FOR POSTDISCHARGE NAUSEA AND VOMITING (PDNV)

* **Society for Ambulatory Anesthesia (SAMBA) guidelines** suggest risk factors for PDNV include [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Female sex [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * History of PONV [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Age 50 years or younger [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Use of opioids in the postanesthesia care unit (PACU) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Nausea in the PACU [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

#### 2.3.1.4. CLINICAL APPLICATION ``

* **Low risk (0-1 factors):** Consider no prophylaxis or single-agent if there are consequences of vomiting [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].
* **Moderate risk (2 factors):** Consider combination therapy with agents from different classes [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].
* **High risk (3+ factors):** Multimodal approach recommended with 2-3 antiemetics from different classes and consideration of total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** The Apfel score is a simple, validated tool for predicting PONV risk using four factors: female gender, non-smoking status, history of PONV/motion sickness, and postoperative opioid use. Risk stratification enables targeted, cost-effective prophylaxis, with multimodal therapy recommended for patients with moderate to high risk.

## 2.4. 5HT3 RECEPTOR ANTAGONISTS










#### 2.4.1.1. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* Ondansetron, granisetron, tropisetron, and dolasetron selectively block serotonin 5-HT3 receptors, with little or no effect on dopamine receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* 5-HT3 receptors are located in two key areas for PONV [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * **Peripherally:** Abdominal vagal afferents [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * **Centrally:** Chemoreceptor trigger zone (area postrema) and nucleus tractus solitarius [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* The chemoreceptor trigger zone is activated by substances such as anesthetics and opioids and signals the nucleus tractus solitarius, resulting in PONV [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* The activation of 5-HT3 receptors peripherally and centrally appears to induce vomiting [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Chemoand radiotherapy may cause the release of 5-HT from enterochromaffin cells. Peripheral 5-HT3 receptors in the gut are then activated and stimulate vagal afferent neurons that connect to the VC, again via 5-HT3 receptors. Thus 5-HT3 antagonists may act both peripherally and centrally [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 2.4.1.2. SPECIFIC AGENT: ONDANSETRON

* **Structure:** Synthetic carbazole, originally licensed for chemotherapy-associated nausea and vomiting [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Presentation:** Available as tablets (4–8 mg), a strawberry-flavored lyophilisate (4–8 mg) to dissolve on the tongue, a suppository (16 mg), and as a clear solution containing 2 mg/ml for slow intravenous injection [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Pharmacokinetics:**
  * **Absorption:** 60% oral bioavailability [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * **Distribution:** 75% protein bound [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * **Metabolism:** Hepatic, producing inactive metabolites [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Requires reduced dosing in hepatic impairment [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * **Excretion:** Renal [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Well absorbed from the gut with an oral bioavailability of about 60%. It is 75% protein-bound and undergoes significant hepatic metabolism by hydroxylation and subsequent glucuronide conjugation to inactive metabolites. Its half-life is 3 hours [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Dosing:** 4-8 mg or 0.1 mg/kg in children, typically administered at the end of surgery [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Pediatric use:** Licensed for children above 2 years of age [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
* **Timing:** Should be used immediately prior to emergence from anesthesia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .

#### 2.4.1.3. CLINICAL USES

* **Perioperative:**
  * Generally administered at the end of surgery for PONV prophylaxis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Effective for both prevention and treatment of PONV [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Part of multimodal antiemetic regimens for high-risk patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Postdischarge nausea and vomiting (PDNV):** Effective for preventing symptoms after discharge [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Other:**
  * Chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Radiotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Limitations:** Ineffective for vomiting induced by motion sickness or dopamine agonists [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .

#### 2.4.1.4. SIDE EFFECTS

* Essentially devoid of serious side effects, even in amounts several times the recommended dose [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* Do not cause sedation, extrapyramidal signs, or respiratory depression [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Most common side effects:**
  * Headache [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Dizziness [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Constipation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * ⚠️ **QT prolongation:** All 5-HT3 antagonists slightly prolong QT interval on ECG [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* FDA has issued a warning regarding QT prolongation with an IV ondansetron bolus larger than 16 mg [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* The manufacturer has removed the 32 mg single-dose formulation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* Requires dose adjustment in those with impaired liver function for ondansetron [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* Other mild effects include flushing and bradycardia following rapid intravenous administration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

**Bottom Line:** 5-HT3 receptor antagonists like ondansetron are first-line agents for PONV prophylaxis and treatment due to their efficacy and favorable side effect profile. They act by blocking serotonin receptors both peripherally and centrally. While generally very safe, potential QT prolongation should be considered, especially with high doses or in patients with cardiac risk factors.

## 2.5. BUTYROPHENONES AND OTHER DOPAMINE ANTAGONISTS










#### 2.5.1.1. BUTYROPHENONES

* **Droperidol:**
  * **Mechanism:** Blocks dopamine receptors in the chemoreceptor trigger zone [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * **Dosing:** 0.625-1.25 mg IV for PONV prophylaxis (much lower than the 5-15 mg used historically for neurolept anesthesia) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * **Uses:** Has been shown to be effective in the prevention and treatment of PONV at doses from 0.25–5 mg, although the incidence of side effects increases with dose. Also used in neurolept analgesia and in the control of mania [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * ⚠️ **FDA black box warning:** QT prolongation and development of torsades des points arrhythmia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* 💡 However, there is limited evidence that use of droperidol at the low doses used for PONV causes clinically significant arrhythmias [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * **Contraindications:** Relative contraindication in those with Parkinsonism and extrapyramidal disorders [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * **Side effects:**
	* **CNS:** Sedation is more pronounced compared to the phenothiazines. The true incidence of extrapyramidal effects is unknown but increases with higher doses. May develop more than 12 hours after administration and up to 25% of patients may experience anxiety up to 48 hours after administration. In sufficient dose it induces neurolepsis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
	* **Metabolic:** May cause hyperprolactinemia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
	* **Cardiovascular:** Hypotension resulting from peripheral α-adrenoceptor blockade may occur. Was withdrawn for a while over concerns of QT prolongation and VT, but has now regained its UK license for low-dose (0.625–1.25 mg) treatment of PONV [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * **Pharmacokinetics:** Usually given intravenously although it is absorbed readily after intramuscular injection. Highly plasma protein-bound (approximately 90%) and extensively metabolized in the liver to products that are excreted in the urine, only 1% as unchanged drug [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Domperidone:**
  * **Distinguishing feature:** Does not cross blood-brain barrier [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Primarily used for GI motility disorders rather than PONV [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Can cause gynecomastia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * This D2 antagonist is less likely to cause extrapyramidal effects as it does not cross the blood-brain barrier [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Its use in children is limited to nausea and vomiting following chemotherapy or radiotherapy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * The intravenous preparation was withdrawn following serious arrhythmias during administration of large doses. It is only available as tablets or suppositories [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 2.5.1.2. PHENOTHIAZINES 

* Main group of anti-psychotic drugs (neuroleptics) with limited role in the treatment of vomiting [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Divided into three groups on the basis of structure, which confers typical pharmacological characteristics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  1. **Propylamine:** Chlorpromazine
  2. **Piperidine:** Thioridazine
  3. **Piperazine:** Prochlorperazine, perphenazine

* **Prochlorperazine (Compazine):**
  * **Structure:** Phenothiazine neuroleptic (antipsychotic) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * **Mechanism:** D2 antagonist, primarily acting on the chemoreceptor trigger zone [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * **Uses:** Effective in the prevention and treatment of PONV and vertigo, as well as in schizophrenia and other psychoses [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * **Dosing:** 12.5 mg IM (adults) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * **Side effects:**
	* Increased QTc interval [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Venous thromboembolism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Neuroleptic malignant syndrome [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Lower seizure threshold [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Parkinsonism and other extrapyramidal effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Oculogyric crisis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Dry mouth [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* **CNS:** Extrapyramidal effects are seen more commonly in this class of phenothiazine. Acute dystonias and akathisia seem to be the most commonly encountered effects. Children and young adults are the most affected groups. When used peri-operatively it produces only mild sedation and may prolong the recovery time but the effects are not marked [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
	* **Group specific:** In common with other phenothiazines, prochlorperazine may cause cholestatic jaundice, haematological abnormalities, skin sensitization, hyperprolactinaemia and rarely the neuroleptic malignant syndrome [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * **Pharmacokinetics:** Hepatic metabolism and renal excretion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Absorption by the oral route is erratic and the oral bioavailability is very low due to an extensive hepatic first-pass metabolism. It may be given by suppository, intravenous or intramuscular injection [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Affects multiple receptors (histaminergic, dopaminergic, muscarinic) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Chlorpromazine:**
  * **Structure:** A propylamine antipsychotic [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Similar properties to prochlorperazine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Proprietary name 'Largactil' hints at the widespread effects of this drug [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * **Uses:** Used in schizophrenia for its sedative properties and to correct altered thought. Its effects on central neural pathways are complicated but are thought to involve isolating the reticular activating system from its afferent connections. This results in sedation, disregard of external stimuli and a reduction in motor activity (neurolepsy). It is sometimes used to control vomiting or pain in terminal care where other agents have been unsuccessful. It has also been shown to be effective in preventing PONV. It is occasionally used to treat hiccup [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * **Mechanism of action:** Antagonizes the following receptor types: dopaminergic (D2), muscarinic, noradrenergic (α1 and α2), histaminergic (H1) and serotonergic (5-HT). It also has membrane stabilizing properties and prevents noradrenaline uptake into sympathetic nerves (uptake 1) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * **Side effects:**
	* **CNS:** Extrapyramidal effects due to central dopamine antagonism. The neuroleptic malignant syndrome occurs rarely. Variable effects on hypothalamic function, reducing the secretion of growth hormone while increasing the release of prolactin (dopamine functions as prolactin release inhibitory factor). Temperature regulation is altered and may result in hypothermia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
	* **Cardiovascular:** Antagonizes α-adrenoceptors resulting in peripheral vasodilation, hypotension and increased heat loss [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
	* **Anticholinergic:** Moderate anticholinergic effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
	* **GI:** Appetite is increased and patients tend to gain weight (exacerbated by inactivity). While it has been shown to be an adequate antiemetic, its other effects have limited this role [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
	* **Miscellaneous:** Contact sensitization - direct contact should be avoided unless actually taking chlorpromazine. Cholestatic jaundice, agranulocytosis, leucopenia, leucocytosis and haemolytic anaemia are all recognized [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * **Pharmacokinetics:** Absorption from the gut is good but, due to a large hepatic first-pass metabolism (limiting its oral bioavailability to about 30%), it is often given parenterally. The large number of hepatic metabolites is excreted in the urine or bile, while a variable but small fraction is excreted unchanged in the urine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Perphenazine:**
  * Similar indications and kinetics to prochlorperazine and has been shown to be effective in the prevention and treatment of PONV [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Associated with a higher incidence of extrapyramidal effects and increased post-operative sedation than prochlorperazine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Promethazine:**
  * Combines anticholinergic and antihistamine properties [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Useful for motion sickness and as an antiemetic [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

#### 2.5.1.4. BENZAMIDES

* **Metoclopramide:**
  * **Mechanism of action:**
	* D2 receptor antagonism at chemoreceptor trigger zone [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* 5HT3 receptor antagonism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Prokinetic effect on stomach (improves gastric emptying) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * **Side effects:**
	* ⚠️ Crosses the blood-brain barrier [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Extrapyramidal side effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Oculogyric crisis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Dystonia (more common in young women and elderly) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Hyperprolactinemia (dopamine is a natural prolactin inhibitor) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Can trigger porphyric crisis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * More detailed information on metoclopramide appears in Section 1.5 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Dopamine antagonists are effective antiemetics but carry risk of significant side effects, particularly extrapyramidal symptoms and QT prolongation. Low-dose droperidol is effective for PONV despite its black box warning, while phenothiazines and metoclopramide offer combined antiemetic mechanisms but require careful patient selection due to their side effect profiles.

## 2.6. ANTICHOLINERGICS 










#### 2.6.1.1. GENERAL ANTICHOLINERGIC PRINCIPLES 

* "Anticholinergic" agents are effective antagonists at muscarinic receptors, with little activity at nicotinic receptors and may be thought of as essentially selective agents at normal doses [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* The naturally occurring tertiary amines, atropine and hyoscine, are esters formed by the combination of tropic acid and an organic base (tropine or scopine) and are able to cross the blood-brain barrier [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Their central effects include sedation, amnesia, antiemesis, and the central anticholinergic syndrome [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Glycopyrrolate is a synthetic quaternary amine (therefore charged) with no central effects as it is unable to cross the blood-brain barrier [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 2.6.1.2. HYOSCINE (SCOPOLAMINE) 

* **Structure:** Hyoscine is a racemic mixture, but only l-hyoscine is active [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Uses:**
  * Has traditionally been given with an intramuscular opioid as premedication, and in this setting has been shown to reduce PONV [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Has also been used as a sedative and amnesic agent [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * 💡 Particularly effective for motion sickness and vestibular-mediated nausea [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .
* **Mechanism of action:**
  * Antimuscarinic effects in the vomiting center and vestibular system [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Formulation:** Commonly used as transdermal patch [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Side effects:**
  * Sedation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Central anticholinergic syndrome - often presents as postoperative agitation. Characterized by excitement, ataxia, hallucinations, behavioral abnormalities, and drowsiness [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Very dry mouth [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Pharmacokinetics:**
  * Absorption is variable and oral bioavailability lies between 10–50% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Transdermal administration is effective in reducing PONV and motion sickness despite very low plasma levels [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Extensively metabolized by liver esterases and only a small fraction is excreted unchanged in the urine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Its duration of action is shorter than that of atropine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 2.6.1.3. ATROPINE 

* **Structure:** Atropine is a racemic mixture, but only l-atropine is active [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Uses:**
  * Used to treat bradycardia and as an anti-sialogogue [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Also used to antagonize the muscarinic side effects of anticholinesterases [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Not used to treat PONV because of its cardiovascular effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Side effects:**
  * **CNS:** Less likely to cause a central cholinergic crisis than hyoscine and is less sedative [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * **Cardiovascular:** May cause an initial bradycardia following a small intravenous dose. This may be due to its effects centrally on the vagal nucleus or reflect a partial agonist effect at cardiac muscarinic receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * **Respiratory:** Bronchodilation is more marked than with hyoscine, leading to an increase in dead space. Bronchial secretions are reduced [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * **GI:** Less effective anti-sialogogue than hyoscine. The tone of the lower esophageal sphincter is decreased and there is a small decrease in gastric acid secretion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * **Miscellaneous:** Sweating is inhibited and this may provoke a pyrexia in pediatric patients. When administered topically, it may increase intra-ocular pressure, which may be critical for patients with glaucoma [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Pharmacokinetics:**
  * Intestinal absorption is rapid but unpredictable [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * It is 50% plasma protein-bound and extensively metabolized by liver esterases [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * It is excreted in the urine, only a tiny fraction unchanged [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 2.6.1.4. GLYCOPYRROLATE 

* **Uses:** Indicated for anti-sialogogue premedication, the treatment of bradycardias and to protect against the unwanted effects of anticholinesterases [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Properties:** Its charged quaternary structure gives it a different set of characteristics compared to the tertiary amines [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Pharmacokinetics:**
  * Intestinal absorption is negligible and the oral bioavailability is consequently less than 5% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Does not cross the blood-brain barrier and so it is devoid of central effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * It is minimally metabolized and 80% is excreted in the urine unchanged [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

While effective, central anticholinergic side effects like sedation, dry mouth, and occasionally the central anticholinergic syndrome limit its use. Transdermal delivery provides a convenient administration route with lower plasma levels and potentially fewer side effects.

## 2.7. CORTICOSTEROIDS FOR PONV










#### 2.7.1.1. PHARMACOLOGY

* **Structure:** Synthetic glucocorticoid [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Mechanism of action for antiemetic effect:**
  * Acts on nuclear glucocorticoid receptors, but exact pathway is unclear [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Possibly via stimulation of appetite, which may reduce release of serotonin from the GI tract [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Anti-inflammatory effects may reduce tissue inflammation and associated serotonin release [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Pharmacokinetics:**
  * Predominantly renal excretion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Long duration of action (biological half-life 36-72 hours) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

#### 2.7.1.2. GENERAL CORTICOSTEROID PROPERTIES 

* The adrenal cortex releases two classes of steroidal hormones into the circulation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Glucocorticoids (from the zona fasciculata and zona reticularis)
  * Mineralocorticoids (from the zona glomerulosa)
* The main endogenous glucocorticoid in humans is hydrocortisone [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Synthetic glucocorticoids include prednisolone, methylprednisolone, betamethasone, dexamethasone, and triamcinolone [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Glucocorticoids have metabolic, anti-inflammatory, and immunosuppressive effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 2.7.1.3. ANTI-INFLAMMATORY EFFECTS OF CORTICOSTEROIDS 

* Glucocorticoids reduce the production of tissue transudate and cell edema in acute inflammation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Circulating polymorphs and macrophages are prevented from reaching inflamed tissue [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* The production of inflammatory mediators (prostaglandins, leukotrienes, and platelet-activating factor) is suppressed by the stimulation of lipocortin, which inhibits phospholipase A2 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Normally phospholipase A2 would facilitate the breakdown of membrane phospholipids to arachidonic acid, the precursor of inflammatory mediators, in particular prostaglandins [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* These effects may reduce the patient's resistance to infection (latent tuberculosis may become reactivated), and the normal clinical features usually present may be absent until the infection is advanced [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 2.7.1.4. IMMUNOSUPPRESSIVE EFFECTS OF CORTICOSTEROIDS 

* Glucocorticoids depress macrophage function and reduce the number of circulating T-lymphocytes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* The transport of lymphocytes and their production of antibodies are also reduced [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Interleukin 1 and 2 production is inhibited, which reduces lymphocyte proliferation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 2.7.1.5. CLINICAL USES

* **PONV prophylaxis:**
  * As effective as ondansetron in reducing incidence of PONV [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * 💡 Should be given at induction rather than at the end of surgery for optimal effect [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Works synergistically with other antiemetics in multimodal regimens [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Other uses:**
  * Anti-inflammatory effect (particularly used in head and neck surgery to reduce postoperative swelling) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Immunosuppression (commonly used in inflammatory and immune diseases) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Analgesic effect (mainly via anti-inflammatory action) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Adjunct in malignant spinal cord compression and raised ICP associated with malignancy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

#### 2.7.1.6. DOSING

* **Standard dosing:** 4-8 mg IV for adults [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Pediatric dosing:** 0.1 mg/kg [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* 💡 Single dose of dexamethasone for PONV prophylaxis generally has minimal side effects [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* Low-dose regimes (3.3 mg) appear to be as effective as higher doses [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] .

#### 2.7.1.7. SIDE EFFECTS

* **Acute/short-term:**
  * ⚠️ Deranged blood glucose in diabetic patients [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Transient perineal burning sensation during IV administration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Chronic/long-term** (not typically relevant for single-dose PONV prophylaxis):
  * Metabolic effects (hyperglycemia, central adiposity) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Protein breakdown, muscle weakness, wasting, thin skin, osteoporosis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Adrenal suppression, immunosuppression [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

#### 2.7.1.8. OTHER CORTICOSTEROIDS

* **Equivalent doses** of common steroids:
  * Prednisolone 25 mg [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Hydrocortisone 100 mg [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Methylprednisolone 20 mg [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Dexamethasone 4 mg [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Triamcinolone 20 mg [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* While other corticosteroids may have antiemetic properties, dexamethasone is most widely studied and used for PONV [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

#### 2.7.1.10. PERIOPERATIVE STEROID SUPPLEMENTATION [PART A/B/C] 

* **Adrenal insufficiency categories:**
  * Primary (adrenal gland disease/failure)
  * Secondary (deficient ACTH or CRH production)
  * Tertiary (the result of glucocorticoid therapy, sometimes classified within Secondary) [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]] 
* **Considerations for peri-operative steroid supplementation:**
  * Doses as low as 5 mg prednisolone and standard doses of inhaled glucocorticoids taken for more than 1 month can cause an inadequate response to the short synacthen test [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]] .
  * Side effects of peri-operative steroid supplementation are minimal compared to an inadequate glucocorticoid stress response [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]] .
  * Patients with adrenal insufficiency require perioperative steroid supplementation in addition to their usual steroid medication [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]] .
* **Supplementation for primary and secondary adrenal insufficiency:**
  * Surgery under regional or general anesthesia: Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV at induction, followed immediately by an infusion of 200 mg over 24 hrs [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]] .
  * Post-operative: Hydrocortisone 200 mg IV over 24 hrs for patients with PONV or NPO. On resumption of oral route – double hydrocortisone doses for 24-48 hrs or for up to a week following major surgery [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]] .
* **Supplementation for tertiary adrenal insufficiency (those on ≥5 mg prednisolone or equivalent):**
  * Major surgery: Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV at induction, followed immediately by an infusion of 200 mg over 24 hrs. Alternatively, dexamethasone 6-8 mg IV will suffice for 24 hrs [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]] .
  * Post-operative: Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV over 24 hrs for patients with PONV or NPO. On resumption of oral route – return to normal pre-operative dose or double if recovery is complicated for up to a week [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]] .
* ⚠️ Dexamethasone is not recommended in Primary AI due to its lack of mineralocorticoid effect [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]] .

**Bottom Line:** Dexamethasone is a highly effective antiemetic when given at induction, with a single dose carrying minimal risk of side effects. It works synergistically with other antiemetics and offers additional benefits of anti-inflammatory and mild analgesic effects, making it a valuable component of multimodal PONV prophylaxis.

## 2.8. NEUROKININ-1 RECEPTOR ANTAGONISTS










#### 2.8.1.1. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* **Substance P:** A neuropeptide that interacts with NK1 receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* NK1 antagonists inhibit substance P at both central and peripheral receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* Central action in the vomiting center on neurokinin-1 receptors inhibits the emetogenic effects of substance P [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* NK1 receptor antagonists block the actions of substance P in the brainstem nuclei of the dorsal vagal complex which is central to the regulation of vomiting [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 2.8.1.2. SPECIFIC AGENT: APREPITANT

* **Pharmacology:**
  * Selectively blocks NK1 receptors with high affinity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Unlike 5-HT3 antagonists, particularly effective for delayed nausea and vomiting [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Oral bioavailability is about 60% and is 97% plasma protein-bound [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Metabolized by CYP3A4 to a number of weakly active metabolites which are excreted in the urine and feces [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Clinical use:**
  * Additive effect when combined with ondansetron to reduce PONV [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Particularly valuable in high-risk patients and for prevention of delayed PONV [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Used for chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting (CINV) and PONV [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Appears to potentiate the effects of other antiemetics (ondansetron and dexamethasone) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Appears most useful in the control of delayed CINV [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Side effects:**
  * Fatigue [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Hiccups [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Loss of appetite [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Alopecia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* Fosaprepitant is an intravenous prodrug form of aprepitant [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

**Bottom Line:** Neurokinin-1 receptor antagonists like aprepitant provide enhanced PONV protection by blocking substance P effects, complementing the action of 5-HT3 antagonists. They are particularly effective for delayed PONV and are a valuable addition to multimodal antiemetic regimens for high-risk patients.

## 2.9. OTHER ANTIEMETICS










#### 2.9.1.1. PROPOFOL

* May act via chemoreceptor trigger zone [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* Total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) with propofol associated with lower PONV incidence compared to volatile anesthesia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* Subhypnotic doses (10-20 mg) may have antiemetic effects in the PACU [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

#### 2.9.1.2. CANNABINOIDS

* Nabilone is a synthetic cannabinoid with antiemetic properties [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* Limited use in anesthetic practice, more commonly used in chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* Side effects include sedation, dysphoria, and potential for abuse [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* Acts at the VC and has been used as an antiemetic following chemotherapy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 2.9.1.3. BENZODIAZEPINES 

* Lorazepam is used as an antiemetic during chemotherapy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Has amnesic and sedative properties [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Its mode of action as an antiemetic is uncertain but it may modify central connections to the VC and prevent the anticipatory nausea that is seen with repeated doses of chemotherapy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 2.9.1.4. NON-PHARMACOLOGICAL APPROACHES

* **Acupuncture, acupressure, and transcutaneous electrical stimulation** of the P6 acupuncture point (located on the wrist) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* Meta-analyses suggest modest efficacy comparable to some pharmacological approaches [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* No significant side effects [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* Several studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of acupuncture in the prevention of PONV [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
* The acupuncture point lies between the tendons of flexor carpi radialis and palmaris longus about 4 cm from the distal wrist skin crease [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
* It should be performed on the awake patient and is free from side effects [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .

**Bottom Line:** Beyond the main antiemetic classes, options like propofol-based TIVA, and non-pharmacological techniques can all contribute to PONV management. These approaches are especially valuable in a multimodal strategy for high-risk patients or when first-line agents are contraindicated.

#### 2.10.1.1. SAMBA ALGORITHM OVERVIEW

#### 2.10.1.2. KEY PRINCIPLES OF THE SAMBA ALGORITHM `` ``

* **Risk assessment** using validated tools (e.g., Apfel score) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Risk reduction** through modifiable risk factors [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Use of propofol for induction and maintenance [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Avoidance of nitrous oxide [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Avoidance of volatile anesthetics [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Minimization of perioperative opioids [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Adequate hydration [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Prophylaxis based on risk level:**
  * Low risk (0-1 factors): No prophylaxis or single agent if consequences of vomiting are severe [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Moderate risk (2 factors): Combination therapy (typically 5-HT3 antagonist + dexamethasone) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * High risk (3+ factors): Triple therapy plus TIVA [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

#### 2.10.1.3. MULTIMODAL APPROACH STRATEGY ``

* **First-line agents** (for prophylaxis and treatment) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * 5-HT3 receptor antagonists (ondansetron 4 mg IV) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Dexamethasone (4-8 mg IV at induction) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * NK1 receptor antagonists (aprepitant) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Low-dose droperidol (0.625-1.25 mg IV) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Second-line agents** (typically for rescue) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Antihistamines (dimenhydrinate, meclizine) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Anticholinergics (scopolamine patch) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Phenothiazines (promethazine) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* 💡 **Key principle:** Use antiemetics that target different mechanisms for additive or synergistic effects [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

#### 2.10.1.4. RESCUE TREATMENT ``

* If prophylaxis fails, treatment should be with an antiemetic from a different pharmacological class than those used for prophylaxis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* For patients who received no prophylaxis, low-dose 5-HT3 antagonist is recommended as first-line rescue [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* Consider alternative causes for nausea/vomiting (surgical complications, bowel obstruction, medications) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

#### 2.10.1.5. SPECIAL POPULATIONS ``

* **Pediatric patients:** Similar approach to adults, with appropriate dose adjustments [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Pregnancy:** Limited options - metoclopramide and ondansetron commonly used [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Ambulatory/day surgery:** Consider longer-acting agents or combination therapy for PDNV prevention [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** The SAMBA algorithm provides a comprehensive, evidence-based approach to PONV management, emphasizing risk assessment, risk reduction, multimodal prophylaxis based on risk level, and rescue therapy using agents from different classes. Implementing this algorithm can significantly reduce PONV incidence and improve patient outcomes and satisfaction.

# 3. OXYTOCIC DRUGS 









## 3.1. OXYTOCIN 










#### 3.1.1.1. PHYSIOLOGICAL BACKGROUND 

* The posterior pituitary gland secretes two peptides each of nine amino acids (seven similar, two different): oxytocin and vasopressin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Oxytocin has central and peripheral actions. The peripheral actions are well understood and are critical to breastfeeding (via the letdown reflex) and uterine contraction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 3.1.1.2. PRESENTATION AND DOSING 

* Presented as a clear liquid in 1 ml vials containing 5 or 10 mg and in combination with ergometrine as syntometrine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Used to induce or augment labor and to increase uterine tone following delivery to facilitate delivery of the placenta [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Controlled intravenous infusions are used due to its short plasma half-life (3–5 minutes) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 3.1.1.3. MECHANISM OF ACTION 

* Oxytocin binds to oxytocin G-coupled receptors in the uterus and promotes calcium release thereby activating uterine contraction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Due to its similarity to vasopressin it has weak anti-diuretic-like effects which are only significant during prolonged infusions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 3.1.1.4. SIDE EFFECTS
