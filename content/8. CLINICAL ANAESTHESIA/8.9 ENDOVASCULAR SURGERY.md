# 1.1. OVERVIEW OF ATHEROSCLEROTIC CARDIOVASCULAR DISEASE

**Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) is the leading cause of morbidity and mortality globally, affecting anesthetic management significantly.**

* **Global Impact:** More than 80 million adults in the United States carry ASCVD diagnosis
* **Current Statistics:** ASCVD accounts for >17.3 million deaths worldwide per year
* **Projected Increase:** Expected to reach >23.6 million deaths by 2030 due to aging population
* **US Mortality:** Nearly 1 in 3 deaths related to ASCVD despite medical advances
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Advances in medical management have decreased total ASCVD deaths but prevalence continues to rise

# 1.2. EPIDEMIOLOGY AND TRENDS IN VASCULAR SURGERY

* **Increasing Volume:** Estimated 1-2 million vascular procedures will be performed annually by 2030 in the US
* **Patient Demographics:** Increasing age and screening programs expanding surgical candidates  
* **Risk Paradox:** Improved medical management delays intervention until severe systemic disease present
* **Endovascular Revolution:** Many previously high-risk patients now considered surgical candidates
* **Surgical Mortality Trends:** 
  * 1960s: >25% for major aortic reconstruction
  * Current: As low as 3% due to improved perioperative optimization

💡 **High-Yield Fact:** The anesthesiologist may have greater influence in reducing morbidity and mortality in vascular surgery than in any other area of anesthesia.

# 1.3. ANESTHESIOLOGIST'S ROLE IN VASCULAR SURGERY

* **Complex Patient Population:** High-risk patients with multiple comorbidities
* **Procedural Complexity:** High-risk surgical procedures requiring sophisticated management
* **Perioperative Optimization:** Critical role in reducing morbidity and mortality
* **Technical Challenges:** Managing hemodynamic lability, aortic cross-clamping effects, organ protection
* **Team Communication:** Essential coordination with surgical team for optimal outcomes

**Bottom Line:** Vascular surgery represents a high-risk patient population undergoing complex procedures where anesthesiologists have significant influence on outcomes through meticulous perioperative management and optimization.

# 2.1. ATHEROSCLEROSIS PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

**Atherosclerosis is a generalized inflammatory disorder of the arterial tree and the most common pathophysiologic mechanism underlying cardiovascular disease.**

## UNDERLYING MECHANISMS

* **Endothelial Damage:** Initial injury from various causes
* **Hemodynamic Shear Stress:** Mechanical forces contributing to vessel injury  
* **Chronic Inflammation:** From infections and other inflammatory mediators
* **Hypercoagulability:** With resultant thrombosis
* **Lipid Effects:** Destructive effect of low-density lipoproteins (LDLs)

## TWO-STAGE DEVELOPMENT PROCESS

1. **Injury Stage:** 
   * Intact vascular endothelium serves as barrier between blood and thrombogenic subendothelial tissues
   * Injured epithelial cells express leukocyte adhesion molecules
   * Increased adherence of macrophages and leukocytes

2. **Response to Injury Stage:**
   * Increased endothelial permeability permits LDL and leukocyte entry
   * Modified LDL particles induce further leukocyte adhesion
   * Formation of "fatty streak" - earliest recognizable lesion

## PLAQUE FORMATION AND PROGRESSION

* **Cellular Components:**
  * Lipid-rich macrophages and T lymphocytes accumulate in vessel intima
  * Monocyte-derived macrophages act as scavenging and antigen-presenting cells
  * Transform into foam cells in enriched cytokine environment

* **Inflammatory Mediators:**
  * Monocyte chemotactic protein-1
  * Macrophage and granulocyte-macrophage colony stimulating factors  
  * Intercellular adhesion molecule-1
  * Tumor necrosis factor α
  * Interleukins 1, 3, 6, 8, and 18

* **Plaque Structure:**
  * Core: Foam cells and extracellular lipid
  * Surrounding: Smooth-muscle cells and collagen-rich matrix
  * Results in luminal narrowing and oxygen supply-demand imbalance

![Rate of cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, or major adverse cardiac event by presence, severity, and extent of coronary artery disease (CAD)](https://78449c25-3737-4cc1-bf9c-0335e5c6e938.ngrok-free.app/api/placeholder/600/400)

## VULNERABLE PLAQUE VS. DISEASE BURDEN CONCEPT

* **Traditional "Vulnerable Plaque" Model:** 
  * Inflammatory mediators and proteolytic enzymes weaken fibrous cap
  * Makes plaque prone to ulceration, rupture, and acute thrombosis
  * Treatment focused on presumed high-risk lesions

* ⚠️ **Current Evidence:** No conclusive evidence that high-risk plaque characteristics are independent risk factors for clinical events
* 💡 **Modern Approach:** Focus on overall atherosclerotic disease burden rather than individual plaque features may be more important

## PLATELET ROLE IN THROMBOSIS

* **Platelet Activation:** Play pivotal role when plaque rupture occurs
* **Genetic Factors:** Platelet polymorphisms found to be independent risk factor following vascular surgery
* **Clinical Significance:** Important for antiplatelet therapy decisions

# 2.2. CONCURRENT VASCULAR DISEASE IN SURGICAL PATIENTS

**ASCVD in one vascular bed often predicts significant disease in other areas of the body.**

![Typical overlap of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease by vascular bed](https://78449c25-3737-4cc1-bf9c-0335e5c6e938.ngrok-free.app/api/placeholder/600/400)

## RISK FACTORS CLASSIFICATION

### NON-MODIFIABLE RISK FACTORS

* Strong family history
* Non-white ethnicity  
* Male sex
* Increasing age

### MODIFIABLE RISK FACTORS

* **Smoking:** Major modifiable risk factor
* **Atherogenic Dyslipidemia:** 
  * Hypertriglyceridemia
  * Elevated LDL cholesterol
  * Decreased high-density lipoprotein (HDL)
* **Abdominal obesity**
* **Hypertension**
* **Insulin resistance**  
* **Renal insufficiency**
* **Proinflammatory states**

## DISEASE CORRELATION BETWEEN VASCULAR BEDS

### CEREBROVASCULAR DISEASE AS CAD PREDICTOR

* **Carotid Intima-Media Thickness (CIMT):** 
  * Each 0.1-mm increase in CIMT → 10-15% increased future MI risk
  * Each 0.01 mm/yr decrease in CIMT progression → nearly 20% reduction in nonfatal MI risk
* **Carotid Plaque Presence:** Independent predictor of MI and cardiovascular death regardless of CIMT

### CORONARY ARTERY DISEASE PREVALENCE IN VASCULAR SURGERY PATIENTS

* **Hertzer Study:** 1,000 consecutive patients for elective vascular surgery
  * Overall CAD prevalence: ~50%
  * Severe, correctable CAD: 25% of entire series
  * Higher rates with known CAD risk factors (34% vs 14%)

**Table: CAD Severity in Vascular Surgery Patients**  

| CAD Severity | Routine Surveillance (%) | Suspected Disease (%) |
|--------------|--------------------------|----------------------|
| Normal coronaries | 14 | 4 |
| Mild to moderate CAD | 49 | 18 |
| Advanced but compensated CAD | 22 | 34 |
| Severe, correctable CAD | 14 | 34 |
| Severe, inoperable CAD | 1 | 10 |

## CLINICAL PREDICTION OF SEVERE CAD

💡 **Predictive Factors for Absence of Severe CAD (96% PPV):**
1. No diabetes mellitus
2. No prior angina  
3. No previous MI
4. No history of congestive heart failure (CHF)

💡 **Predictive Factors for Absence of Critical CAD (94% PPV):**
1. No prior angina
2. No previous MI
3. No history of CHF

## DISEASE PREVALENCE CORRELATIONS

* **PAD with CAD/CVD:** 40-60% prevalence
* **CAD with concurrent PAD:** 10-30% prevalence  
* **Concurrent carotid and peripheral disease:** 25-50% prevalence
* **Autopsy findings:** Patients with fatal MI twice as likely to have significant cerebral and peripheral arterial disease

# 2.3. MEDICAL OPTIMIZATION STRATEGIES [PART B/C]

**Cardiovascular complications are a major source of morbidity and mortality following vascular surgery, with nearly 10% showing significant myocardial injury perioperatively.**

## PERIOPERATIVE CARDIAC EVENTS

* **Myocardial Injury:** ~10% of vascular surgery patients
* **Major Adverse Cardiac Events (MACE):** ~2% of patients
* **In-hospital Mortality:** 15-25% for perioperative MI patients  
* ⚠️ **Critical Point:** Nonfatal perioperative MI is independent predictor for cardiovascular death within 6 months

## LIFESTYLE MODIFICATIONS

* **Exercise and Weight Loss:** Regular exercise, weight reduction
* **Dietary Modification:** Heart-healthy nutrition plans
* **Smoking Cessation:** Critical for disease progression prevention
* **Risk Factor Management:** Aggressive treatment of hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes

💡 **Modern Medical Therapy Impact:** Many landmark surgical intervention trials were performed when medical therapy was limited compared to current multimodal treatment approaches.

## Β-BLOCKADE THERAPY

### RATIONALE AND MECHANISM

* **Pathophysiology:** Significant proportion of postoperative ischemic events secondary to exaggerated sympathetic response
* **Heart Rate Effects:** Substantial elevations increase myocardial oxygen demand while decreasing supply
* **Proposed Benefit:** Modulating heart rate response may decrease incidence and severity of ischemia

![ST segment changes with heart rate during an ischemic episode](https://78449c25-3737-4cc1-bf9c-0335e5c6e938.ngrok-free.app/api/placeholder/600/400)

### CLINICAL EVIDENCE EVOLUTION

* **Early Studies:** Several RCTs demonstrated significant MACE reduction benefit
* **POISE-1 Trial:** >8,000 patients - decreased perioperative MI but increased risk of death or stroke
* **Current Evidence:** 17 large RCTs comparing perioperative β-blockade vs placebo

### CURRENT ACC/AHA RECOMMENDATIONS (2014)

* **Class I (Level B):** Continue β-blockers in patients chronically on therapy
* **Class IIb (Level C):** May be reasonable to begin β-blockers in patients with:
  * Intermediateto high-risk myocardial ischemia on preoperative testing
  * Three or more cardiac risk factors
* **Class IIb (Level B):** Reasonable to start therapy in advance to establish safety/tolerability
* ⚠️ **Class III (Level B):** Should NOT start on day of surgery

### VASCULAR SURGERY-SPECIFIC EVIDENCE

* **SVS-VQI Analysis:** Preoperative β-blockade (excluding high-risk open AAA repair):
  * Did not decrease mortality or MACE rate
  * Increased rates of other adverse events
* **POBBLE Trial:** >100 patients undergoing infrarenal vascular surgery - no significant MACE difference
* **MaVS Trial:** ~500 patients undergoing aortic/peripheral revascularization - no significant MACE difference

### AGENT-SPECIFIC CONSIDERATIONS

* **Atenolol vs Metoprolol:** Epidemiologic analysis of 40,000 patients showed decreased mortality with atenolol
* **Stroke Risk:** Preoperative and intraoperative metoprolol associated with increased stroke risk vs other agents

![Temporal trends in β-blockade initiation within 30 days of surgery](https://78449c25-3737-4cc1-bf9c-0335e5c6e938.ngrok-free.app/api/placeholder/600/400)

## Α2-ADRENERGIC AGONISTS

* **Rationale:** Blunt surgical stress response and reduce cardiovascular morbidity
* **POISE-2 Trial:** >10,000 patients with/at risk for ASCVD
  * Did not reduce perioperative MI or composite death/nonfatal MI
  * More clinically significant hypotension
  * Increased incidence of nonfatal cardiac arrest
* ⚠️ **Current Recommendation:** Not recommended for MACE prevention (Class III, LOE B)
* ⚠️ **Withdrawal Risk:** Abrupt discontinuation can cause rebound sympathetic surge, hypertension, tachycardia, diaphoresis, pulmonary edema

## RENIN-ANGIOTENSIN SYSTEM (RAS) BLOCKERS

### BENEFICIAL EFFECTS

* **Anti-inflammatory:** Effects on inflammation, endothelial function, plaque stability
* **Ventricular Remodeling:** Beneficial effects following acute MI
* **Ischemia-Reperfusion:** Protective effects on ischemia-reperfusion injury
* **Renal Protection:** Benefits for perioperative acute kidney injury

### CLINICAL EVIDENCE

* **Cardiac Surgery:** Large retrospective study found increased perioperative morbidity and mortality when continued
* **Vascular Surgery:** Similar association with 30-day mortality following major vascular surgery
* **Vasoplegia Risk:** Well-recognized association with refractory vasoplegia

### CURRENT RECOMMENDATIONS

* **Class IIa (LOE B):** Reasonable to continue perioperatively
* **Class IIa (LOE C):** Reasonable to restart as soon as clinically feasible if stopped
* **Clinical Practice:** Many providers hold preoperatively due to vasoplegia risk

## STATIN THERAPY

### MECHANISMS BEYOND LIPID CONTROL

* **Anti-inflammatory Effects:** Inhibit inflammatory response
* **Ischemia-Reperfusion:** Reduce ischemia-reperfusion injury  
* **Antithrombotic:** Reduce thrombosis, enhance fibrinolysis
* **Platelet Effects:** Decrease platelet reactivity
* **Endothelial Function:** Restore endothelial function

### CLINICAL EVIDENCE

* **Cardiac/Vascular Surgery:** Associated with reduced cardiac morbidity and mortality
* **Discontinuation Effects:** 
  * Interferes with endothelial function
  * Increases inflammatory markers and oxidative stress
  * Associated with increased in-hospital death and myonecrosis

### VASCULAR SURGERY-SPECIFIC DATA

* **Mixed Results:** Meta-analyses show conflicting results
* **One Analysis:** No difference in 30-day mortality or MACE
* **Another Analysis:** Lower risk of all-cause mortality, MI, stroke, and composite outcomes

### CURRENT RECOMMENDATIONS  

* **Class I (LOE B):** Continue statins during perioperative period
* **Class IIa (LOE B):** Reasonable to initiate in statin-naïve patients undergoing vascular surgery

## ANTIPLATELET AGENTS

### RATIONALE AND EVIDENCE

* **DAPT Benefits:** Dual antiplatelet therapy reduces ischemic events
* **Aspirin Withdrawal Syndrome:** Associated with acute perioperative cardiovascular events
* **Stroke Prevention:** Patients with previous ischemic stroke should continue therapy
* **Carotid Surgery:** Lower perioperative stroke rates when antiplatelet agents continued
* **Coronary Stent Patients:** DAPT essential following stent implantation

### CONFLICTING EVIDENCE

* **VQI Review:** No difference in in-hospital MI or death with/without antiplatelet agents
* **Increased Bleeding:** Greater blood loss and transfusion rates for all procedures
* **POISE-2 Trial:** No difference in primary outcome (death or nonfatal MI at 30 days)

### MECHANISM CONSIDERATIONS

* **Type I vs Type II MI:** 
  * Type I: Morphologically complex plaque rupture and thrombus
  * Type II: Demand ischemia from supply-demand mismatch
  * Perioperative setting may be predominantly Type II MI

### CURRENT RECOMMENDATIONS

* **Class I (LOE C):** Management determined by consensus of treating clinicians
* **Class IIb (LOE B):** May be reasonable to continue aspirin when MACE risk > bleeding risk
* **Class III (LOE B/C):** Recommend against initiation/continuation in elective noncardiac, noncarotid surgery unless ischemic risk > bleeding risk

## GLUCOSE MANAGEMENT

### ORAL HYPOGLYCEMIC AGENTS

* **Sulfonylureas:** Hold due to hypoglycemia risk with fasting
* ⚠️ **Metformin:** Hold due to lactic acidosis risk with hypovolemia, renal dysfunction, contrast agents
* ⚠️ **SGLT-2 Inhibitors:** Discontinue ≥3 days preoperatively due to euglycemic ketoacidosis risk

### INSULIN THERAPY EVOLUTION

* **Initial Evidence:** Tight glucose control (80-110 mg/dL) decreased mortality and multiorgan failure
* **Subsequent Studies:** Failed to replicate results, increased unrecognized hypoglycemia and death risk
* **Intraoperative Considerations:** Both hyperglycemia and tight control associated with adverse outcomes
* **Current Approach:** Maintain glucose 140-180 mg/dL rather than attempt normoglycemia
* **Stroke Risk:** Hyperglycemia may exacerbate neurologic injury - especially important for carotid and thoracic aortic procedures

## TRANSFUSION MANAGEMENT

### TRADITIONAL VS MODERN APPROACH

* **Traditional Practice:** Transfuse high-risk patients to hematocrit 30%
* **Anemia Association:** Hematocrit <28% may increase postoperative myocardial ischemia and MACE
* **FOCUS Trial:** No outcomes difference between restrictive vs liberal transfusion even in high-risk population

### VASCULAR SURGERY-SPECIFIC CONSIDERATIONS

* **Mixed Results:** Both transfusion and perioperative anemia independently associated with increased 30-day morbidity/mortality
* **Decision Factors:** Base on evidence of compromised end-organ perfusion, rate/cause of blood loss, likelihood of hemorrhage control

## CORONARY REVASCULARIZATION

### RATIONALE

* **High CAD Prevalence:** Given high concurrent CAD likelihood in vascular surgery patients
* **Long-term Benefits:** Myocardial revascularization may benefit patients with triple vessel CAD, left main disease, poor LV function
* **Mortality Impact:** Presence of uncorrected CAD appears to double 5-year mortality following vascular surgery

### CLINICAL TRIALS

* **Monaco Study:** >200 patients randomized to routine vs selective coronary angiography
  * More revascularization in routine surveillance group
  * No difference in in-hospital MACE
  * Improved long-term survival and freedom from cardiovascular events

![Cumulative survival and freedom from major adverse cardiac event](https://78449c25-3737-4cc1-bf9c-0335e5c6e938.ngrok-free.app/api/placeholder/600/400)

* **CARP Trial:** >500 patients with prophylactic cardiac revascularization
  * Aggressive medical therapy (>80% β-blockers, >70% aspirin, >50% statins)
  * No long-term benefit with preoperative revascularization
  * CABG patients: fewer MIs, shorter hospital stays vs PCI
  * Limited to single/two-vessel disease with normal LV ejection fraction

### CURRENT RECOMMENDATIONS

* **General Principle:** Preoperative coronary revascularization not recommended unless independently indicated
* **Exception:** Patients with unprotected left main disease may benefit from prophylactic revascularization

### TIMING CONSIDERATIONS

* **Post-MI Surgery:** Risk decreases significantly with time
  * Recommend waiting ≥60 days following MI before elective noncardiac surgery without intervention
* **Post-CABG:** No specific timing recommendations - based on clinical urgency, optimization, fitness
* **Post-PCI:** Specific recommendations for stent type and dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT)

#### STENT-SPECIFIC TIMING

* **Bare Metal Stent (BMS):** Delay elective surgery 30 days (Class I, LOE B-NR)
* **Drug-Eluting Stent (DES):** Delay elective surgery ideally 6 months (Class I, LOE B-NR)
* **Early Surgery After DES:** May consider after 3 months if delay risk > stent thrombosis risk (Class IIb, LOE C-EO)
* ⚠️ **Contraindications:** 
  * Do not perform elective surgery within 30 days of BMS (Class III, LOE B-NR)
  * Do not perform elective surgery within 3 months of DES if DAPT must be discontinued (Class III, LOE B-NR)

# 2.4. PREOPERATIVE ANESTHESIA EVALUATION [PART B/C]

**The purpose is to delineate extent of comorbid pathology and medically optimize the patient, focusing on ASCVD assessment.**

## HISTORY AND PHYSICAL EXAMINATION

### COMPREHENSIVE HISTORY

* **Diagnosed ASCVD:** Define extent of known disease
* **Undiagnosed Disease Screening:**
  * Angina or anginal equivalents
  * Transient ischemic attack (TIA) symptoms
  * Mesenteric ischemia symptoms  
  * Peripheral ischemia symptoms
* **Comorbidity Control:** Evaluate diabetes, hypertension management
* **Pulmonary Assessment:** Thorough smoking history and underlying lung disease

### PHYSICAL EXAMINATION FOCUS

* **End-organ Involvement:**
  * Diminished pulses
  * S4 gallop  
  * Residual stroke deficits
* **Cardiovascular Decompensation:**
  * New or worsened murmur
  * Jugular venous distention
  * Third heart sound
  * Rales, shortness of breath
  * Peripheral edema

## LABORATORY STUDIES

* **Complete Blood Count:** Risk of major blood loss, concurrent medical diseases
* **Coagulation Studies:** If on anticoagulants or regional anesthesia planned
* **Metabolic Panel:** 
  * Increased likelihood of renal insufficiency
  * Baseline for postoperative renal dysfunction risk
  * Electrolyte abnormalities

## CARDIAC BIOMARKERS AND ADVANCED IMAGING

* **Biomarkers:** Troponin I, NT-pro-BNP, cystatin C, C-reactive protein
* **Advanced Imaging:** Coronary artery calcium scores, CIMT
* **Utility:** Long studied for long-term cardiovascular outcomes; newer area for perioperative risk prediction

## PREOPERATIVE CARDIAC TESTING DECISION FRAMEWORK

### BASELINE STUDIES

* **12-lead ECG:** Reasonable as baseline given increased MACE risk
* **Echocardiography:** Reasonable for:
  * Previously documented LV dysfunction
  * Worsening clinical status
  * Not assessed within previous year
  * Dyspnea of unknown origin
* **Not Recommended:** Routine echocardiography in asymptomatic patients

### RISK STRATIFICATION IMPORTANCE

* **Prediction:** Postoperative troponin elevation/MI predicts 26%/55% lower 5-year survival respectively
* **Balance:** Over-utilization can cause false positives, surgery delays, patient harm from invasive workup

![Univariate Kaplan–Meier survival curves, stratified according to postoperative myocardial ischemia](https://78449c25-3737-4cc1-bf9c-0335e5c6e938.ngrok-free.app/api/placeholder/600/400)

### ACC/AHA 2014 GUIDELINES ALGORITHM

![Proposed algorithm for cardiovascular evaluation and management](https://78449c25-3737-4cc1-bf9c-0335e5c6e938.ngrok-free.app/api/placeholder/600/400)

#### STEP-BY-STEP PROCESS

1. **Urgency Assessment:**
   * **Emergency:** Proceed without delay (e.g., ruptured AAA)
   * **Urgent:** Abbreviated cardiac workup if impacts management (e.g., critical limb ischemia)

2. **Acute Conditions Assessment:**
   * New, crescendo, unstable angina
   * MI within last 60 days
   * Symptomatic heart failure
   * New/worsening valvular heart disease  
   * Unstable arrhythmia/conduction disease

3. **Risk Estimation Tools:**
   * **ACS NSQIP Risk Calculator**
   * **Revised Cardiac Risk Index (RCRI)**
   * **VSG-New England Model:** More accurate than RCRI for vascular surgery
	 * Underestimated MACE risk by 1.7to 7.4-fold with RCRI
	 * Independent predictors: age, smoking, insulin-dependent diabetes, CAD, CHF, abnormal stress test, long-term β-blocker, COPD, elevated creatinine
   * **Vascular Quality Initiative (VQI) Cardiac Risk Index:** Procedure-specific cardiac risk calculator (see detailed scoring below)

4. **Functional Status Assessment:**
   * **Moderate to Excellent (≥4 METs):** Proceed without further testing
   * **Poor or Unknown:** Collaborative decision for further testing

5. **Additional Testing Criteria:**
   * Reasonable if results will change management decisions
   * Consider coronary revascularization, medical optimization, or palliative care alternatives
   * Most vascular surgery patients fall in elevated risk category with poor/unknown functional status

### VASCULAR QUALITY INITIATIVE (VQI) CARDIAC RISK INDEX

**Purpose:** Estimate risk of in-hospital postoperative myocardial infarction for specific vascular procedures

**Available Calculators:**
1. Elective Carotid Endarterectomy (CEA)
2. Elective Open Infrarenal AAA Repair / EVAR
3. Infra/Suprainguinal Bypass

**Online Tool:** https://svs-vqi.shinyapps.io/CRICalculators/

#### RISK FACTORS AND SCORING (Varies by Procedure)

**Common Risk Factors Across Procedures:**
* **Age:** Scored by categories (e.g., age brackets with increasing points)
* **BMI:** Body mass index categories
* **Race:** White vs non-white
* **Serum Creatinine:** <88.42 μmol/L vs ≥88.42 μmol/L
* **Previous Vascular Surgery:** Yes vs no
* **COPD History:** None / no treatment / on medications/O2
* **CHF History:** Yes vs no
* **Previous CABG/PCI:** Yes vs no
* **Stress Test in Last 2 Years:** Not done / normal / abnormal

**Procedure-Specific Factors:**

**For Open AAA Repair:**
* **Proximal Clamp Level:**
  * Infrarenal
  * Above 1 renal artery
  * Above 2 renal arteries
  * Supracoeliac
* **Distal Anastomosis Level:**
  * Aorta
  * Common iliac artery (CIA)
  * Common femoral artery (CFA)
  * External iliac artery (EIA)

**For Bypass Procedures:**
* Distal anastomosis location
* Bypass configuration

#### INTERPRETATION

* **Calculator Output:** Predicted probability of in-hospital MI
* **Risk Stratification:** Helps guide:
  * Preoperative cardiac evaluation intensity
  * Intraoperative monitoring strategies
  * Postoperative surveillance protocols
  * Cardiac biomarker screening frequency

#### CLINICAL APPLICATION

* **Preoperative Planning:**
  * Identify very high-risk patients requiring optimization
  * Guide discussion of risks and benefits
  * Inform anesthetic planning
* **Postoperative Management:**
  * Higher-risk patients: more intensive troponin surveillance
  * Consider ICU vs floor admission
  * Duration of postoperative monitoring

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** VQI CRI is more discriminatory than general risk indices because it's derived from large vascular surgery-specific databases and accounts for procedure-specific factors like clamp level in AAA repair.

**Example Use Case:**
* 72-year-old male
* BMI 28
* Serum Cr 100 μmol/L
* Previous PCI 2 years ago
* COPD on inhalers
* Planned open AAA repair with infrarenal clamp
* Calculator provides specific MI risk percentage to guide perioperative planning

**Bottom Line:** Vascular surgery patients require comprehensive preoperative assessment focusing on ASCVD, with systematic risk stratification using procedure-specific tools like VQI CRI and evidence-based medical optimization. The anesthesiologist plays a crucial role in coordinating multidisciplinary care to optimize perioperative outcomes in this high-risk population.

# 3.1. CEREBROVASCULAR DISEASE AND CAROTID ENDARTERECTOMY

**Cerebral ischemia results from imbalance between cerebral oxygen supply and demand, leading to temporary or permanent neurological deficits.**

## EPIDEMIOLOGY AND IMPACT

* **Annual US Strokes:** ~800,000 patients per year
* **Mortality Ranking:** 5th leading cause of death (1 in 19 deaths)
* **Quality of Life Impact:** Called "most burdensome chronic condition" due to psychological consequences and disability
* **Mortality Trends (2009-2019):**
  * Age-adjusted death rate decreased >5% (37.0 per 100,000)
  * Actual number of stroke deaths increased 16.4%
* **Risk Factor Control:** Hypertension management had greatest influence on mortality decline

## PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND CLINICAL PRESENTATION

### CAROTID STENOSIS PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

* **Endothelial Injury:** Initial damage from hemodynamic stress and atherosclerotic risk factors
* **Plaque Formation:**
  * Fatty deposits accumulate in vessel wall
  * Inflammatory cells (macrophages, T lymphocytes) infiltrate
  * Debris and calcification develop over time
* **Plaque Stability:**
  * **Stable Plaques:** Thick fibrous cap, less inflammatory infiltrate
  * **Unstable (Vulnerable) Plaques:** Thin fibrous cap, large lipid core, increased inflammation
  * Plaque rupture leads to acute thrombosis and embolization
* **Predominant Etiology:** Degenerative/atherosclerotic (~95%)
* **Less Common Causes:**
  * Fibromuscular dysplasia (especially young females)
  * Arteritis (e.g., Takayasu)
  * Post-radiation changes
  * Carotid dissection

### STROKE MECHANISMS AND EPIDEMIOLOGY

* **Stroke Types:** Most strokes ischemic rather than hemorrhagic
* **Carotid Disease Role:** Accounts for ~20% of all ischemic strokes
* **Mechanism:** Typically embolic rather than occlusive
  * Plaque rupture → distal embolization
  * Critical stenosis → flow-limiting ischemia (less common)

### ASSOCIATED VASCULAR DISEASE

* **Significant coronary artery disease:** Very high prevalence in carotid disease patients
* **Vascular dementia:** Progressive cognitive decline from chronic hypoperfusion
* **Risk Factors:**
  * Hypertension
  * Hyperlipidemia
  * Peripheral vascular disease
  * Alcohol use
  * Smoking (strongest modifiable risk factor)

### CLINICAL MANIFESTATIONS OF CAROTID DISEASE

* **Transient attacks of monocular blindness (amaurosis fugax)**
* **Paresthesia**
* **Weakness or clumsiness**
* **Facial drooping**  
* **Speech problems**
* **Transient Ischemic Attack (TIA):** Focalized neurologic deficit <24 hours duration with no permanent infarction

💡 **Critical Timing:** Up to 15% of strokes heralded by TIA, with most important risk factor for future stroke being recent TIA symptoms.

## EVIDENCE FOR CAROTID ENDARTERECTOMY

### INDICATIONS FOR ELECTIVE CEA

**Optimal timing: >2 days but <2 weeks from last non-disabling neurologic event**

**Symptomatic Carotid Disease:**
* **≥70% Stenosis with Symptoms:**
  * **NASCET Study:** Absolute risk reduction (ARR) = 16%
  * Strong indication for CEA
  * Symptoms include: TIA, amaurosis fugax, non-disabling stroke
* **50-69% Stenosis with Symptoms:**
  * **NASCET Study:** ARR = 4.6%
  * Moderate benefit
  * Symptoms: Hemispheric numbness/paresis, unilateral monocular blindness
* **<50% Stenosis:** No benefit to CEA over medical management

**Asymptomatic Carotid Disease:**
* **≥60% Stenosis:**
  * **ACAS/ACST Studies:** ARR = 5.4%
  * Modest benefit if surgical risk <3%
  * Longer life expectancy needed to realize benefit
  * Patient preference regarding intervention vs surveillance important
* **<60% Stenosis:** Medical management preferred

**Medical Management (All Patients):**
* Antiplatelet therapy (aspirin or clopidogrel)
* Statin therapy (high-intensity)
* Blood pressure control
* Smoking cessation
* Diabetes management

### SYMPTOMATIC CAROTID DISEASE TRIALS

* **NASCET Trial:** North American Symptomatic Carotid Endarterectomy Trial
* **ECST:** European Carotid Surgery Trial
* **VACS:** Veterans Affairs Cooperative Studies Program
* **All demonstrated benefit for CEA over medical management for severe (>70-80%) ICA stenosis**

### POOLED ANALYSIS RESULTS

* **>70% stenosis:** Significant 5-year benefit to surgery (ARR ~16%)
* **50-70% stenosis:** Marginal benefit (ARR ~4.6%)
* **30-49% stenosis:** No benefit
* **<30% stenosis:** Increased risk of ipsilateral ischemic stroke
* **<50% stenosis:** No benefit to treatment

### ASYMPTOMATIC CAROTID DISEASE

* **ACAS:** Asymptomatic Carotid Atherosclerosis Study
* **ACST:** Asymptomatic Carotid Surgery Trial
* **Meta-analysis:** Small absolute risk reduction ~1% per year for ≥60% stenosis (ARR 5.4% at 5 years)
* **Operative Criteria:** CEA justifiable only if operative morbidity/mortality < natural risk of untreated ischemic events (<3%)

⚠️ **Important Limitation:** These trials performed when medical therapy consisted primarily of aspirin - modern multimodal therapy (statins, intensive BP control, antiplatelets) may reduce relative benefit of surgical intervention.

## PREOPERATIVE EVALUATION AND PREPARATION

### TIMING CONSIDERATIONS

* **Stroke Risk After TIA:** >10% return with acute stroke within 90 days, nearly half within 2 days
* **NASCET/ECST Pooled Analysis:** For ≥70% stenosis, attributable risk reduction decreased from:
  * 30% when surgery within 2 weeks
  * 18% at 2-4 weeks  
  * 11% at 4-12 weeks
* **Current Recommendation:** Definitive intervention within 2 weeks of index event (COR 2a, LOE C-LD)

### CARDIAC EVALUATION

* **Reasonable to Obtain:** Due to known CAD-carotid disease association
* **Do Not Delay:** Definitive intervention should not be delayed for extensive cardiac evaluation
* **Treat as CAD Present:** Prudent approach for all CEA patients with strict hemodynamic control
* **Cardiac Disease Prevalence:** 50% of TIA/stroke patients have abnormal stress test despite no angina symptoms; 60% have severe CAD by angiography

## NEUROPHYSIOLOGIC MONITORING AND CEREBRAL PROTECTION

### RATIONALE FOR MONITORING

* **Embolic Origin:** Most perioperative strokes are embolic
* **Cross-Clamp Hypoperfusion:** Temporary carotid occlusion can cause cerebral ischemia
* **Collateral Dependence:** Blood supply depends on intact circle of Willis during cross-clamping

### CIRCLE OF WILLIS ANATOMY AND VARIATIONS

![Intact and hypoplastic circle of Willis](https://78449c25-3737-4cc1-bf9c-0335e5c6e938.ngrok-free.app/api/placeholder/600/400)

* **Anatomic Anomalies:** Majority of specimens show anomalies
* **Hypoplasia:** Most frequent anomaly (24%)
* **Complete Absence:** Additional 6% have incomplete circle
* **Clinical Impact:** Even anatomically intact circle may not provide adequate flow if:
  * Contralateral carotid/vertebral occlusive disease present
  * Patient becomes relatively hypotensive

### CAROTID SHUNTING PRACTICE VARIATIONS

* **Never Shunt:** Rely on expedient surgery and hemodynamic control
* **Selective Shunting:** Based on neurophysiologic monitoring changes
* **Always Shunt:** Routine shunting approach
* **Shunt Complications:**
  * Atheromatous or air emboli
  * Arterial dissection
  * Nerve injury, hematoma, infection
  * Long-term restenosis
* 💡 **Clinical Reality:** Shunting unnecessary in ~85% of patients
* **Limited Efficacy:** Shunt only beneficial if neurologic dysfunction due to inadequate blood flow, but 65-95% of neurologic deficits during CEA are thromboembolic

### ELECTROENCEPHALOGRAPHY (EEG) MONITORING

**Most commonly employed neurophysiologic monitor for carotid surgery.**

#### CEREBRAL BLOOD FLOW THRESHOLDS

* **Normal CBF:** ~50 mL/min/100g brain tissue
* **Well Tolerated:** Decreases to ~22 mL/min/100g with no EEG changes
* **EEG Deterioration:** Usually begins below 15-18 mL/min/100g
* **Cellular Failure:** Occurs below 10-12 mL/min/100g

![Characteristic EEG changes with reduction in cerebral blood flow](https://78449c25-3737-4cc1-bf9c-0335e5c6e938.ngrok-free.app/api/placeholder/600/400)

#### EEG CHANGES AND CLINICAL CORRELATION

* **Most Common Manifestations:** Ipsilateral slowing and/or attenuation
* **Timing:** Changes noted within seconds of cross-clamping
* **Reversibility:** Typically reversible with hemodynamic augmentation/shunt placement

#### EEG LIMITATIONS

* **Poor Sensitivity:** <60% sensitivity for cerebral ischemia, 1% false-positive rate
* **Pre-existing Deficits:** May be falsely negative in patients with fluctuating neurologic deficits
* **Cortical Only:** Monitors cortical but not deep brain structures
* **Confounding Factors:** Hypothermia, anesthetic depth changes
* **Global Monitor:** Unable to detect smaller thromboembolic strokes (most likely etiology)

### SOMATOSENSORY-EVOKED POTENTIALS (SSEP)

**Electrical stimuli to peripheral nerves evaluating amplitude/latency over cerebral cortex.**

#### ADVANTAGES OVER EEG

* **Deep Brain Assessment:** Evaluates deep brain structures unlike EEG
* **Nerve-Specific Information:**
  * Median nerve: Middle cerebral artery watershed
  * Tibial nerve: Anterior cerebral artery parenchyma
* **Baseline Ischemia:** Particularly useful in patients with baseline cerebral ischemia

#### LIMITATIONS

* **Anesthetic Sensitivity:** Sensitive to virtually all anesthetic agents
* **High False-Positive Rate:** Due to anesthetic interference
* **Light Anesthesia Required:** Must maintain light plane if ascribing changes to hypoperfusion
* **False Negatives:** Focal internal capsule ischemia can cause false negatives
* **Variable Performance:** Studies show ~80% sensitivity, 57% specificity

### MOTOR-EVOKED POTENTIALS (MEP)

**Transcranial electrodes stimulate cerebral cortex, recording motor response in contralateral extremity.**

#### ADVANTAGES

* **Higher Sensitivity:** When combined with SSEP, more sensitive for ischemia detection  
* **No False Negatives:** One study showed 0% false negatives vs 1.5% with SSEP alone

#### LIMITATIONS

* **Anesthetic Sensitivity:** Exquisitely sensitive to anesthetic interference
* **Neuromuscular Blockade:** Must be avoided
* **False Positives:** More prone to unnecessary intervention due to high sensitivity

### TRANSCRANIAL DOPPLER (TCD)

**Measures maximum velocity of blood flow through middle cerebral artery as surrogate for CBF.**

#### UNIQUE ADVANTAGES

* **Microemboli Detection:** Can detect phenomena missed by global cerebral function measures
* **Surgical Feedback:** Detection of embolic showers allows operative team to alter surgical plan
* **Flow Confirmation:** Confirms adequate CBF after cross-clamping or through shunt
* **Postoperative Surveillance:** Useful for postoperative monitoring when most strokes occur
* **Easy Application:** Quick, interpretable without expertise, allows serial evaluation

#### SPECIFIC APPLICATIONS

* **Acute Thrombosis:** Can identify postoperative thrombosis
* **Hyperperfusion Syndrome:** Can identify patients at risk
* **Real-time Assessment:** Allows immediate surgical technique modification

#### LIMITATIONS

* **Operator Dependence:** Requires skilled technician
* **Technical Limitations:** 10-20% of patients lack adequate temporal windows

### CAROTID STUMP PRESSURE

**Direct measurement of pressure in carotid stump distal to clamp.**

#### ADVANTAGES

* **Direct Assessment:** Direct gauge of collateral cerebral perfusion
* **Simple and Cost-Effective:** Quick to obtain, no sophisticated equipment needed
* **No Expert Interpretation:** Straightforward numerical value

#### CLINICAL APPLICATION

* **Threshold Values:** Generally >40-50 mmHg considered adequate
* **Individual Variation:** Some patients adequate below threshold, others insufficient despite higher pressures
* **Regional Anesthesia Data:** Retrospective series of >400 patients showed 40 mmHg cutoff as reliable as EEG
* **Generalizability:** Unclear applicability to general anesthesia patients

### NEAR-INFRARED SPECTROSCOPY (NIRS)

**Uses relative absorption of specific light wavelengths to estimate frontal lobe cerebral perfusion (rSO2).**

#### ADVANTAGES

* **Portability:** Easily portable and inexpensive
* **Simple Interpretation:** No special training required
* **Non-invasive:** No patient risk

#### LIMITATIONS

* **Limited Coverage:** Global assessment only of frontal grey matter
* **Previous Infarction:** Potentially inaccurate over areas of previous stroke
* **Posterior Ischemia:** May miss ischemia in other brain areas

#### CLINICAL EVIDENCE

* **Conflicting Data:** Studies show widely variable sensitivity/specificity
* **One Study:** 19% drop in rSO2 = 100% sensitivity, 98% specificity, 82% PPV, 100% NPV
* **Another Study:** Same threshold = 60% sensitivity, 25% specificity
* **Generalizability:** Regional anesthesia studies may not apply to general anesthesia

## ANESTHETIC CONSIDERATIONS FOR CAROTID ENDARTERECTOMY

### PREOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT

* ⚠️ **Avoid Premedication:** Sedatives avoided to facilitate rapid emergence and neurologic assessment
* **If Necessary:** Smallest effective midazolam dose titrated to effect
* **Standard Monitoring:** Apply upon OR arrival
* **Invasive BP Monitoring:** Recommended due to potential hemodynamic lability
* **Hemodynamic Goals:** Maintain within 20% of baseline due to altered cerebral autoregulation
* **Central Monitoring:** Rarely necessary unless specific patient risk factors
* **IV Access:** At least one mediumto large-bore IV (low blood loss/fluid shift risk)

### REGIONAL VS GENERAL ANESTHESIA

#### REGIONAL ANESTHESIA BENEFITS

* **Continuous Neurologic Monitoring:** Ultimate monitor for cerebral ischemia
* **Immediate Detection:** Abrupt mental status changes alert team sooner than indirect monitors
* **Hemodynamic Stability:** Avoids labile induction/emergence periods
* **Cardiovascular Benefits:** Avoids negative inotropic anesthetic agents

#### REGIONAL ANESTHESIA REQUIREMENTS AND RISKS

* **Patient Cooperation:** Must lie still for entire procedure duration
* **Communication Ability:** Critical for neurologic assessment
* **Contraindications:**
  * Inability to communicate
  * Orthopnea  
  * Painful arthritis
  * Claustrophobia

**Superficial Cervical Plexus Block:**
* **Efficacy:** As effective as deep/combined block
* **Safety:** Avoids complications of deep cervical plexus block

**Deep Cervical Plexus Block Complications:**
* Accidental subarachnoid injection → brainstem anesthesia
* Intravascular injection → seizure
* Phrenic, vagus, recurrent laryngeal nerve blockade → respiratory complications

#### GENERAL ANESTHESIA BENEFITS

* **Patient Comfort:** Improved comfort, especially for anxious patients
* **Team Communication:** More frank intraoperative communication
* **Avoids Emergent Conversion:** Prevents urgent conversion scenarios
* **Table Positioning:** Head often positioned away from anesthesia provider

#### CLINICAL EVIDENCE: GALA TRIAL (2008)

* **Study Design:** International, multicenter RCT of >3,500 patients
* **Primary Endpoint:** Stroke, MI, or death between randomization and 30 days
* **Primary Results:** No difference in clinical outcomes between general and regional anesthesia
* **Regional Anesthesia Advantages Identified:**
  * Reduced unnecessary shunt placement (patient awake → direct neurologic monitoring)
  * Lower shunt-related complications (emboli, bleeding)
  * Decreased postoperative bleeding risk
  * Immediate detection of neurologic changes
* **General Anesthesia Considerations:**
  * Requires surrogate monitoring (EEG, SSEP, TCD, stump pressure)
  * Higher likelihood of shunt placement (lack of direct monitoring)
  * Controlled airway and complete patient immobility
* **Overall Conclusion:** Both techniques have similar clinical outcomes - choose based on patient factors, surgeon preference, and institutional experience

* **Meta-analysis:** 14 RCTs involving >4,500 operations confirmed no difference in:
  * Death, stroke, or MI rates
  * Major morbidity
  * Postoperative complications
  * Hospital length of stay
  * Patient satisfaction

![Odds ratio for outcome of stroke or death, or death for carotid endarterectomy](https://78449c25-3737-4cc1-bf9c-0335e5c6e938.ngrok-free.app/api/placeholder/600/400)

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** GALA 2008 demonstrated that while RA offers advantages in reducing unnecessary shunting, both techniques are safe when properly executed. Choice should be individualized based on patient cooperation, comorbidities, and team expertise.

### GENERAL ANESTHESIA TECHNIQUE

#### AIRWAY MANAGEMENT

* **ETT vs LMA:** 
  * ETT offers more secure airway, better gas exchange control, less neck anatomy distortion
  * LMA may cause less hemodynamic lability (avoids direct laryngoscopy)
* **Anesthetic Depth:** Maintain "light" level ensuring amnesia but minimally interfering with neuromonitoring

#### INDUCTION AND MAINTENANCE

* **Induction Agents:**
  * Propofol or etomidate (both decrease CMRO2 and cerebral oxygen requirements)
  * Etomidate may preserve cardiovascular stability
  * Short-acting opioid (fentanyl, remifentanil) to blunt intubation response
* **Airway Preparation:** Atomized lidocaine to trachea to minimize ETT stimulation and prevent emergence coughing
* **Opioid Use:** Small amounts short-acting opioids; avoid large/long-acting doses to prevent neurologic assessment confounding
* **Local Anesthetic Supplement:** Cervical plexus block and surgeon-administered local can reduce/eliminate opioid needs
* **Muscle Relaxation:** Not mandated but allows lighter anesthesia and reduces EEG interference

#### VOLATILE VS INTRAVENOUS AGENTS

**No Evidence for Superiority of Either Technique**

**Propofol-Based Advantages:**
* **Hemodynamic Stability:** Less labile emergence
* **Recovery:** More rapid awakening for neurologic assessment
* **PONV:** Lower postoperative nausea/vomiting rates
* **Safety:** Prevents retching on fresh neck incision, decreases hematoma risk

**Volatile Agent Considerations:**
* **Cost:** Approximately 9 times less expensive than IV techniques
* **Agent Selection:** Desflurane or sevoflurane preferable to isoflurane (rapid offset, cognitive recovery benefits)

#### VENTILATION MANAGEMENT

* **Normocapnia:** Should be maintained during CEA
* ⚠️ **Avoid Hyperventilation:** May cause cerebral vasoconstriction and decreased CBF during critical cross-clamp periods
* ⚠️ **Avoid Hypercapnia:** May cause "steal" phenomenon - normal vessels dilate while ischemic vessels already maximally dilated

#### TEMPERATURE MANAGEMENT

* **Hypothermia Benefits:** Can depress cerebral activity and decrease cellular oxygen requirements below minimum for viability
* **Theoretical Protection:** Most effective method of cerebral protection with mild temperature decrease
* **Cardiovascular Risk:** Even mild hypothermia can induce shivering, significantly increasing myocardial oxygen consumption and work
* **Current Evidence:** No definitive evidence that hypothermia brain protection justifies myocardial risks

### EMERGENCE AND EXTUBATION

* **Timing:** Nearly always extubated at end of surgery
* **Deep Extubation:** May be considered in appropriate patients to mitigate hemodynamic lability and prevent coughing/straining
* **Blood Pressure Control:** Tight control must continue through extubation and postoperatively
* **Neurologic Assessment:** Confirm neurologic integrity before leaving OR

## POSTOPERATIVE CONSIDERATIONS

### STROKE PREVENTION PROGRESS

* **Historical Incidence:** ~6% postoperative stroke following CEA
* **Modern Incidence:** ~1% due to three key interventions:
  1. Improved perioperative medication management
  2. Perioperative neurophysiologic monitoring  
  3. Perioperative hemodynamic control

### CEREBRAL HYPERPERFUSION SYNDROME (CHS)

* **Incidence:** Up to 3% of CEA cases
* **Mechanism:** Impaired cerebral autoregulation following high-grade stenosis relief
* **Clinical Presentation:**
  * Ipsilateral cerebral edema
  * Headache
  * Seizures  
  * Focal neurologic deficit
  * Intracerebral hemorrhage
* **Management:** 
  * Pharmacologic hypertension control
  * Limitation of cerebral perfusion rises
  * Early recognition and treatment paramount
  * Complete recovery possible

### HEMODYNAMIC MANAGEMENT

#### BLOOD PRESSURE TARGETS THROUGHOUT PROCEDURE

**Pre-Cross-Clamp Period:**
* **Target:** Maintain BP within 20% of baseline
* **Rationale:** Cerebral autoregulation may be impaired from chronic stenosis
* **Agents:** Short-acting vasodilators or vasopressors titrated to effect

**During Cross-Clamp Period:**
* **Target:** 20% above baseline (MAP 80-100 mmHg typical)
* **Rationale:** Maximize collateral perfusion via circle of Willis
* **Agents:**
  * Vasopressors: Phenylephrine, norepinephrine for induced hypertension
  * Beta-blockers: Esmolol or metoprolol for heart rate control
* 💡 **Critical Point:** Hypertension during clamp helps perfuse at-risk territory via collaterals

**Post-Cross-Clamp (After Reperfusion):**
* **Target:** Return to baseline (systolic BP <160 mmHg)
* **Rationale:** Prevent cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome
* **Agents:**
  * Labetalol (alpha and beta blockade)
  * Hydralazine
  * Nicardipine
  * Continue beta-blockade for sympatholysis

**Emergence and Immediate Postoperative:**
* **Target:** Systolic BP <160 mmHg
* **Critical Period:** Tight BP control essential
* **Goal:** Smooth, controlled emergence without hypertensive surges
* **Antiemetics:** Liberal use to prevent retching/straining on fresh anastomosis

#### PATTERNS AND COMPLICATIONS

* **Common Pattern:** Hypertension more common than hypotension
* **Hypertension Risks:**
  * Acute myocardial ischemia and failure
  * Cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome (up to 3% incidence)
  * Neck hematoma formation
  * Anastomotic bleeding
* **Evidence:** Post-CEA hypertension significantly associated with stroke, death, and cardiac complications
* **Hypotension/Bradycardia:** Do not appear to correlate with adverse outcomes

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The transition between cross-clamp periods requires anticipation and preparation - have both vasopressors and vasodilators immediately available.

### CARDIAC COMPLICATIONS

* **MI Incidence:** <1% but remains leading cause of death following CEA
* **Management:** Appropriate perioperative medication management critical
* **ECG Monitoring:** Some centers obtain postoperative ECG in recovery

### FLUID MANAGEMENT

* **Approach:** Limit total perioperative fluid
* **Rationale:** 
  * Small intraoperative losses
  * Short surgical time with limited exposure
  * Frequent diastolic dysfunction
* **Risk:** Fluid overload common cause of demand ischemia, congestion, respiratory/cardiovascular morbidity

### SECONDARY HYPERTENSION CAUSES

* Full bladder
* Hypoxemia  
* Hypercarbia
* Pain (balance with neurologic monitoring needs)

### RESPIRATORY COMPLICATIONS

**Multiple Potential Causes:**
* Underlying pulmonary pathophysiology
* Recurrent laryngeal nerve injury
* Hypoglossal nerve injury
* Neck hematoma
* Altered carotid body chemoreceptor response

#### CAROTID BODY FUNCTION

* **Surgical Impact:** May damage nerve supply to carotid body
* **Unilateral Loss:** Unlikely to be clinically significant
* **Bilateral Loss:** May prevent appropriate ventilatory response to hypercapnia/hypoxemia
* **High Risk:** Patients with previous contralateral CEA
* **Management:** 
  * Supply supplemental oxygen postoperatively
  * Limit/avoid respiratory depressant drugs

### WOUND COMPLICATIONS

* **Hematoma Incidence:** Up to 2% of CEA patients
* **Small Hematomas:** Conservative management with reversal of residual heparin or compression
* ⚠️ **Rapidly Expanding Hematoma:** Clinical emergency due to tracheal compression risk
* **Emergency Management:**
  * Immediate evaluation required
  * Impaired lymphatic drainage can cause sudden severe pharyngolaryngeal edema
  * Have difficult airway equipment available (videolaryngoscopy, small ETT, surgical airway)
  * Best managed in operating room for both airway management and surgical re-exploration

**Bottom Line:** Carotid endarterectomy requires comprehensive perioperative management with focus on neurologic protection, hemodynamic control, and early recognition of complications. Both regional and general anesthesia are acceptable techniques with equivalent outcomes when properly executed.

# 3.2. AORTIC RECONSTRUCTION [PART B/C]

**Surgical manipulation of the aorta produces significant and potentially catastrophic hemodynamic effects, making aortic reconstruction the most technically challenging procedure for vascular anesthesiologists.**

## TYPES OF AORTIC SURGERY

1. **Aneurysmal Disease Reconstruction**
2. **Aortic Dissection Reconstruction**

### ANEURYSM DEFINITION AND CLASSIFICATION

* **Definition:** >50% dilation of normal expected arterial diameter
* **Abdominal Threshold:** >3.0 cm diameter for most patients
* **Frequency:** Abdominal location ~9x more frequent than thoracic
* **Combined Involvement:** ~15% involve both thoracic and abdominal aorta

### ABDOMINAL AORTIC ANEURYSM (AAA) CLASSIFICATION

* **Infrarenal:** 85% of AAAs
* **Juxtarenal:** Minority involvement
* **Suprarenal:** Minority involvement
* 💡 **Critical Point:** Location dictates aortic cross-clamp level with major anesthetic implications

## PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF AORTIC DISEASE

### ANEURYSM FORMATION VS ATHEROSCLEROTIC DISEASE

**Aortic aneurysm formation is a distinct degenerative process from atherosclerotic disease, though risk factors overlap.**

#### ANEURYSM PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

* **Connective Tissue Degradation:** Primarily medial and adventitial layers
  * Degradation of structural proteins in intima and media
  * Progressive loss of elastin and collagen integrity
* **Inflammatory Response:** Immune system activation
* **Biomechanical Stress:** Wall stress effects
  * Hemodynamic forces and hypertension promote further dilation
  * LaPlace's law: increased diameter → increased wall tension → progressive expansion

#### ETIOLOGICAL CLASSIFICATION

* **Degenerative/Atherosclerotic:** 80-85% of AAAs
  * Most common cause
  * Associated with generalized atherosclerotic disease
* **Hereditary Connective Tissue Disorders:** 15-20%
  * Marfan syndrome
  * Ehlers-Danlos syndrome
  * Family history significantly increases risk
* **Less Common Causes:**
  * Mycotic (infectious) aneurysms
  * Vasculitis (e.g., Takayasu arteritis)
  * Traumatic
  * Anastomotic (post-surgical)

#### EPIDEMIOLOGY AND ASSOCIATIONS

* **Prevalence:** 10% of individuals >65 years old
* **Extension Patterns:**
  * 25% extend to common iliac arteries
  * 10% have concurrent cerebral aneurysms
* **Major Risk Factors:**
  * COPD (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease)
  * Hypertension
  * Hyperlipidemia
  * Smoking (most modifiable risk factor)
* **Concurrent Disease:** Significant association with coronary artery disease and peripheral vascular disease

#### SIZE-RELATED RISK STRATIFICATION

**Size is single most important predictor of rupture and mortality.**

* **Prospective Study:** 300 consecutive patients managed nonoperatively
* **Growth Rate:** Median increase 0.3 cm per year (>0.5 cm per 6 months considered rapid growth)
* **6-Year Cumulative Rupture Incidence:**
  * <4.0 cm: 1%
  * 4.0-4.9 cm: 2%
  * > 5.0 cm: 20%

![Cumulative incidence of abdominal aortic aneurysm rupture, according to aneurysm diameter](https://78449c25-3737-4cc1-bf9c-0335e5c6e938.ngrok-free.app/api/placeholder/600/400)

* **Annual Rupture Risk by Size:**
  * 5.0 cm: ~5% per year
  * 6.0 cm: ~10% per year
  * 7.0 cm: ~20% per year
  * 8.0 cm: ~50% per year
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Rupture risk increases exponentially with diameter, not linearly

#### CURRENT SURGICAL RECOMMENDATIONS

**Indications for Elective Surgery:**

* **Size-Based (Western Guidelines):**
  * **Men:** AAA ≥5.5 cm
  * **Women:** AAA ≥5.0-5.4 cm
* **Asian Population Thresholds:** Consider surgery at lower threshold (>5.0 cm for both males and females)
* **Symptomatic AAA:** Regardless of size
  * Abdominal or back pain
  * Distal embolization
  * Claudication symptoms
* **Rapid Growth:** >0.5 cm per 6 months
* **High-Risk Morphology:**
  * Saccular shape (higher rupture risk than fusiform)
  * Non-degenerative etiology (hereditary, inflammatory)
* **Risk-Benefit Consideration:** Annual rupture risk must exceed morbidity and mortality risk of the repair procedure

⚠️ **Rupture Mortality:** Nearly always lethal with 85-90% mortality rates

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** First presentation is rupture in 50% of AAA patients - emphasizing importance of screening programs

#### COMPLICATIONS OF UNTREATED AAA

* **RUPTURE:** Most catastrophic complication
  * First presentation in 50% of patients
  * 85-90% mortality rate
  * Typically into retroperitoneum (contained) or peritoneum (free rupture - rapidly fatal)
* **Pseudoaneurysm:** Contained rupture with hematoma formation
* **Fistula Formation:**
  * Aorto-caval fistula → high-output cardiac failure
  * Aorto-enteric fistula → GI bleeding
  * Left renal vein thrombosis (from compression)
* **Thromboembolic Complications:**
  * Distal embolization from mural thrombus
  * Acute limb ischemia
  * "Blue toe syndrome"
* **Branch Vessel Thrombotic Occlusion:**
  * Mesenteric ischemia
  * Renal artery occlusion
* **Compression of Adjacent Structures:**
  * Vertebral body scalloping (anterior vertebral erosion)
  * Ureteral obstruction
  * Duodenal compression
  * Inferior vena cava compression

### ACUTE AORTIC DISSECTION

* **Definition:** Life-threatening medical catastrophe with high morbidity/mortality
* **Hallmark Lesion:** Intimal tear creating false lumen propagated by pulsatile blood flow
* **Classification:** 
  * **Temporal:** Acute (<14 days symptoms) vs Chronic (>2 weeks)
  * **Anatomic:** Location-based classification systems

#### LOCATION AND MORTALITY PATTERNS

* **Ascending Aorta:** ~50% of dissections
  * Surgical emergency
  * Death from: acute aortic regurgitation, pericardial tamponade, myocardial ischemia from coronary ostial compromise
* **Descending Aorta:** Next most common (just distal to left subclavian)
  * Death from: end-organ compromise due to visceral vessel malperfusion
* **Abdominal Aortic:** Rare (1.3% incidence)

### BLOOD VOLUME REDISTRIBUTION

![Blood volume redistribution following aortic cross-clamp placement](https://78449c25-3737-4cc1-bf9c-0335e5c6e938.ngrok-free.app/api/placeholder/600/400)

#### MECHANISM

* **Passive Venous Recoil:** Distal to cross-clamp shifts blood volume proximally
* **Infraceliac Clamping:** Blood can shift into compliant splanchnic vasculature
* **Supraceliac Clamping:** Splanchnic circulation unable to absorb volume shift
  * Decreased arterial flow → decreased venous capacitance from elastic recoil
  * Net result: Increased venous return, central filling pressures, cardiac output

#### CARDIAC RESPONSE

* **Increased Preload and Afterload:** Increases myocardial work
* **Compensatory Vasodilation:** Coronary vessels dilate to maximize oxygen delivery
* **Limited Reserve Patients:** Those with decreased EF or significant CAD may develop:
  * Myocardial ischemia
  * Heart failure
  * Regional wall motion abnormalities

### CROSS-CLAMP MANAGEMENT STRATEGY

**Anticipation of cross-clamp placement should prompt hemodynamic manipulation.**

#### PRAGMATIC APPROACH

1. **Heart Rate Control:** Esmolol to target 60-65 bpm (decreases myocardial oxygen imbalance risk)
2. **Direct Vasodilation:** Control arterial pressure via:
   * IV vasodilating agents (nitroprusside, nitroglycerin, nicardipine)
   * Thoracic epidural local anesthetic
   * Deepen anesthetic depth
3. **Goal:** Offset increased afterload and myocardial work with systemic vasodilation

⚠️ **Critical Balance:** Attempts to normalize SVR above clamp can further compromise flow distal to clamp. Must maintain adequate perfusion pressure below clamp to prevent visceral/spinal cord ischemia.

#### DETAILED PERIOPERATIVE PHARMACOLOGIC MANAGEMENT

**Just Before Cross-Clamp Application:**
* **Vasodilators:** GTN (nitroglycerin) infusion initiated
* **Beta-Blockade:** Ensure adequate beta-blockade (esmolol bolus/infusion)
* **Anesthetic Deepening:**
  * Propofol bolus or increased infusion rate
  * Increased volatile anesthetic (sevoflurane) concentration
* **Goal:** Preemptively blunt hypertensive response to cross-clamping

**During Cross-Clamp Period:**
* **Volume Loading:** Fluid administration to target CVP ~5 mmHg
  * Prepares for washout effect at clamp release
  * Compensates for third-space losses
  * Avoid excessive warming of lower limbs (to minimize reperfusion injury and metabolite washout)
* **Maintain Hemodynamics:** Continue vasodilators and beta-blockade as needed
* **Monitor:** Blood loss from aneurysm sac manipulation between clamps

**Unclamping Preparation and Management:**
* **Stop GTN:** Discontinue vasodilators just before clamp release
* **Reduce Volatile Agent:** Decrease sevoflurane concentration
* **Prepare Vasopressors:** Have norepinephrine/phenylephrine ready and primed
* **Optimize Oxygen Delivery:**
  * Ensure adequate preload (complete volume loading)
  * FiO2 100%
  * Optimize cardiac output (consider inotropes if needed)
* **Gradual Release:** Surgeon opens clamp slowly to control mediator washout
* **Immediate Support:** Start vasopressors immediately upon BP drop
* **Sequential Reperfusion:** If bilateral iliac clamps, release one at a time

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The transition from cross-clamp to unclamp represents one of the most hemodynamically challenging moments in anesthesia - preparation and rapid response are critical.

### AORTIC UNCLAMPING EVENTS

#### TWO DISTINCT UNCLAMPING EVENTS

1. **Proximal Anastomosis:** Clamp moved from native aorta to graft (usually hemodynamically insignificant)
2. **Distal Clamp Release:** Results in profound physiologic changes

![Hemodynamic response to aortic cross-clamp release](https://78449c25-3737-4cc1-bf9c-0335e5c6e938.ngrok-free.app/api/placeholder/600/400)

#### UNCLAMPING PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

**Complex cascade culminating in profound vasodilation and hypotension:**
* **Inflammatory Mediator Release**
* **Decreased Cardiac Output**
* **Hypoxemia-Mediated Vasodilation**  
* **Reactive Hyperemia**
* **Result:** Up to 80% decrease in SVR, profound LV pressure decrease, relative central hypovolemia

#### MANAGEMENT STRATEGIES

**No evidence for superiority of one method over another:**

* **Volume Loading:** During cross-clamp period
* **Pharmacologic Support:**
  * Vasoconstrictors: phenylephrine, norepinephrine
  * Inotropes: epinephrine, calcium chloride
* **Anesthetic Manipulation:** 
  * Decrease anesthetic depth
  * Discontinue epidural infusions
* **Preferred Method:** Gradual cross-clamp release for controlled mediator release
* **Sequential Reperfusion:** If bilateral iliac clamps used, reperfuse sequentially

⚠️ **Communication Critical:** Clear coordination with surgeon vital. If bleeding at anastomosis requires reclamping after vasoactive boluses, profound proximal hypertension can ensue.

## ORGAN-SPECIFIC CONSIDERATIONS

### RENAL PROTECTION AND HEMODYNAMICS

#### RENAL FAILURE IMPACT

* **Mortality:** 4-5x higher in patients developing acute renal failure
* **Best Predictor:** Preoperative renal dysfunction most powerful predictor of postoperative dysfunction
* **Prevention:** No proven protective strategy other than:
  * Minimizing ischemia length
  * Avoiding profound/prolonged hypotension

#### LEVEL-DEPENDENT RENAL EFFECTS

* **Infrarenal Cross-clamping:** ~5% acute renal failure incidence
* **Suprarenal Cross-clamping:** ~13% acute renal failure incidence
* **Suprarenal Occlusion Effects:**
  * Up to 80% decrease in renal blood flow
  * Redistributed flow favoring cortical/juxtamedullary over hypoxia-prone medulla
* **Infrarenal Occlusion:** Nearly 50% decrease in renal blood flow, 70% increase in renal vascular resistance
* **Mechanism:** Predominantly neurohumoral activation rather than hemodynamic/cardiac output changes

#### RECOVERY PATTERN

* **Persistence:** Alterations persist ≥30 minutes after cross-clamp release and cardiovascular baseline return
* **Management:** Best initial approach to decreased urine output may be observation for recovery

#### PHARMACOLOGIC "RENOPROTECTION"

**Multiple strategies advocated but limited clinical evidence:**

* **Mannitol:**
  * Increases diuresis and free radical scavenging
  * No clinical trials demonstrating reduced postoperative renal failure in AAA repair
* **Diuretics:**
  * No correlation between intraoperative urine output and acute renal insufficiency development
  * Converting oliguric to polyuric renal failure while causing intravascular depletion
  * Reserve for demonstrably volume-overloaded patients
* **Dopamine:** Does not appear to improve postoperative renal dysfunction
* **Fenoldopam:** 
  * Selective dopamine agonist showing promise
  * Conflicting results in cardiovascular surgery
* **Ischemic Preconditioning:** 
  * Ali et al. study found beneficial effect on renal and myocardial injury
  * 23% decrease in postoperative renal insufficiency incidence

### SPINAL CORD ISCHEMIA AND PROTECTION

#### SPINAL CORD VASCULAR SUPPLY

![Vascular supply to the spinal cord](https://78449c25-3737-4cc1-bf9c-0335e5c6e938.ngrok-free.app/api/placeholder/600/400)

* **Anterior Spinal Artery:** Single vessel supplying 75% of spinal cord blood flow (anterolateral cord, motor tracts)
* **Posterior Spinal Arteries:** Paired vessels supplying 25% of flow (posterior columns, sensory tracts)
* **Radicular Arteries:** Series arising from aorta with variable collateralization
* **Artery of Adamkiewicz:** Most important radicular artery
  * 75% originate between T8-T12
  * Additional 10% at L1-L2
  * Supplies thoracolumbar cord

#### CLINICAL RISK AND PREVENTION

* **Incidence:** Up to 10% for distal aortic repair
* **Risk Factors:**
  * Previous aortic surgery (vascular exclusion of thoracic radicular collaterals)
  * Open surgical repair
  * Aortic cross-clamp location and duration
  * Length of aortic replacement
  * Intraoperative hypotension/hypoperfusion

#### DEFINITIVE PREVENTION MEASURES

* **Short Cross-clamp Time:** Most important factor
* **Normal Cardiac Function Maintenance**
* **Higher Perfusion Pressures**
* **Segmental Sequential Repair:** Minimize ischemia duration to any vascular bed

#### ADVANCED PROTECTION TECHNIQUES

**Used primarily for thoracoabdominal repairs:**
* **Distal Aortic Perfusion + CSF Drainage:** Markedly reduced neurologic deficit incidence
* **CSF Drainage Rationale:** 
  * Augment spinal cord perfusion pressure by increasing forward pressure (MAP) or relieving obstructing pressure (CSF pressure)
  * Aortic cross-clamping → reflexive CSF pressure increase → effectively lower spinal cord perfusion pressure
* **CSF Management:** Continuous monitoring, slow removal to maintain CSF pressure <12 mmHg
* **AAA Application:** Less commonly used for isolated AAAs as artery of Adamkiewicz perfusion less likely at risk

## ANESTHETIC MANAGEMENT OF OPEN AORTIC RECONSTRUCTION

### OPEN AAA REPAIR CHARACTERISTICS

**Open aortic aneurysm repair represents a high-risk, high-reward procedure with distinct advantages and challenges.**

#### PROCEDURAL FEATURES

* **Blood Loss:** Moderate to massive potential
  * Swift losses possible when aneurysm sac opened between clamps
  * Require large-bore IV access and blood products immediately available
* **Duration:** Typically prolonged (4-8 hours depending on complexity)
* **Emergency Considerations:**
  * Elective procedures: 2-5% mortality
  * Ruptured AAA (rAAA): ~50% mortality despite intervention
* **Heparinization:**
  * Systemic heparin prior to cross-clamping
  * +/- Protamine reversal at case completion (surgeon preference varies)

#### MAJOR INTRAOPERATIVE COMPLICATIONS

* **Cardiovascular:**
  * Acute myocardial infarction (most common cause of perioperative death)
  * Hemodynamic instability from cross-clamping/unclamping physiology
* **Renal:** Acute kidney injury (especially with suprarenal clamping)
* **Gastrointestinal:** Bowel ischemia (higher risk in ruptured AAA)
* **Vascular:**
  * Lower limb ischemia (from embolic load, cross-clamp time, vasopressors, hypovolemia, BP swings)
  * Spinal cord ischemia (rare in infrarenal repairs)
* **Abdominal Compartment Syndrome:** Postoperative complication requiring decompression

#### ANATOMIC CONSIDERATIONS FOR COMPLEXITY

* **Incision Type:** Depends on aneurysm level and extent
  * Midline laparotomy (transabdominal)
  * Lateral retroperitoneal approach
* **Kidney/Bowel Involvement:** Determines cross-clamp level and complexity
* **Segmental Artery Reimplantation:** May be required for juxtarenal/suprarenal aneurysms
  * Lumbar arteries
  * Inferior mesenteric artery
  * Renal arteries

#### OUTCOMES: HIGH-RISK, HIGH-REWARD PROFILE

**Advantages:**
* **Suitable for All Anatomies:** No anatomical constraints (vs EVAR limitations)
* **Durable Results:** Lower reintervention rate
* **Definitive Repair:** Lower late rupture risk
* **No Surveillance Requirements:** Unlike EVAR endoleak monitoring

**Disadvantages:**
* **Higher Perioperative Risk:** Especially respiratory and cardiac complications
* **Extended Recovery:** Longer hospital stay, ICU requirement
* **Major Laparotomy:** Associated with ileus, respiratory splinting, pain

**Mortality Trends:**
* Historical (1960s): >25% mortality for major aortic reconstruction
* Current elective: 2-5% with modern perioperative optimization
* Ruptured AAA: Remains ~50% despite advances

### SURGICAL APPROACH CONSIDERATIONS

#### TRANSABDOMINAL APPROACH

* **Incision:** Thoracoabdominal midline
* **Access:** Via peritoneum
* **Advantages:** Generous exposure, favored for complex reconstruction
* **Applications:** Complex aortic replacement

#### RETROPERITONEAL APPROACH  

* **Incision:** Lateral border of left rectus, 12th rib to below umbilicus
* **Access:** Aorta from diaphragmatic crux to bifurcation
* **Benefits:**
  * Comparable surgical exposure with decreased fluid shifting
  * Fewer postoperative pulmonary complications
  * Faster return of bowel function
  * Shorter ICU stays
  * Decreased hospital costs
* **Preferred For:** Truncal obesity, COPD, intra-abdominal adhesions, juxtarenal aneurysms

### NEURAXIAL ANESTHESIA CONSIDERATIONS

* **Epidural Catheter:** Should be considered barring contraindications
* **Intraoperative Benefits:**
  * Manage hemodynamic lability of aortic cross-clamping
  * Decrease postoperative sympathetic stimulation
  * Aid postoperative pain control
  * Potentially aid mechanical ventilation weaning
* **Heparin Timing:** Delay administration per current guidelines if neuraxial instrumentation performed
* ⚠️ **Traumatic Placement:** Frank risk/benefit consideration for proceeding vs delaying due to epidural hematoma risk

### GENERAL ANESTHESIA MANAGEMENT

#### INDUCTION CONSIDERATIONS

* **Hemodynamic Risk:** Induction/intubation can cause dramatic lability and sympathetic stimulation → aneurysm rupture risk
* **Preparation:** 
  * Ensure adequate blood products in OR
  * Large-bore peripheral IV access
  * Consider preinduction arterial line for appropriate agent titration
* **Goal:** Smooth induction with stable hemodynamics, avoid tachycardia/hypertension
* **No Single "Best" Technique:** Depends on appropriate titration regardless of agents chosen

#### MONITORING REQUIREMENTS

* **Central Venous Access:** Generally employed for volume trend monitoring and reliable vasoactive medication delivery
* **Cardiac Function Assessment:** Either pulmonary artery catheter or TEE

##### TEE ADVANTAGES

* Most sensitive for new regional wall motion abnormalities
* Direct visualization of ventricular filling
* Real-time assessment capabilities

##### PULMONARY ARTERY CATHETER ADVANTAGES

* Useful intraoperatively and postoperatively for resuscitation guidance
* Provides quantitative hemodynamic data
* **Controversy:** Conflicting evidence regarding benefit in high-risk surgical patients

#### ANESTHETIC TECHNIQUE

* **Drug Selection:** Virtually all anesthetic techniques described
* **Priority:** Ability to maintain hemodynamic equilibrium and attention to detail more important than drug choice
* **Volatile Agents:** May improve preconditioning mechanisms for myocardial ischemia (demonstrated in animal models and cardiac surgery)
* **Hemodynamic Goal:** Maintain within 20% of baseline range
* **Vasoactive Agents:** Array of short-acting vasodilators, vasopressors, inotropes should be immediately available

### PERIOPERATIVE VOLUME AND HEMODYNAMIC MANAGEMENT

#### PRE-CROSS-CLAMP PERIOD

* **Volume Status:** Keep slightly hypovolemic (assessed by CVP, PCWP, or echo) to limit hypertensive extremes
* **Cross-Clamp Placement:** Vasodilating infusions may be required
* **Alternative Approaches:**
  * Deepen anesthesia
  * Epidural local anesthetic injection
  * Both require careful attention to avoid unintended hypotension
* **Spinal Cord Perfusion Concerns:** May allow permissive hypertension above clamp level for higher distal perfusion pressure (may compromise myocardial well-being)

#### CROSS-CLAMP PERIOD

* **Blood Loss Management:** Swift loss from vessels arising between clamps after sac incision
* **Monitoring Limitations:** Significant blood loss may occur without hypotension/tachycardia due to circulation exclusion
* **Autotransfusion:** Employ device to reduce autologous blood needs
* **Volume Loading:** Prudent during cross-clamp period to prepare for washout and reactive hyperemia upon clamp release

#### PRE-UNCLAMPING PREPARATION

* **Vasodilator Discontinuation:** Stop before clamp removal
* **Gradual Release:** Surgeon opens clamp gradually
* **Support Measures:** Ongoing fluid resuscitation, vasopressor and inotropic support frequently necessary
* **Emergency Reclamping:** Severe refractory hypotension temporarily managed with clamp reapplication

#### MULTIPLE CLAMP CYCLES

* **Frequent Applications:** May occur to limit ischemic time and "test" anastomoses
* **Anesthesiologist Requirements:** 
  * Flexibility and adaptability essential
  * Variety of short-acting vasoactive agents available
* **Each Removal:** "Washout" of vasoactive agents with hemodynamic instability potential
* **Monitoring Challenges:** Central and pulmonary filling pressures may be elevated despite systemic hypotension due to:
  * Lactic acid washout
  * Inflammatory mediators
  * Pulmonary vasoconstriction
  * Cardiac stunning

### EMERGENCE AND POSTOPERATIVE CONSIDERATIONS

#### EXTUBATION DECISION FRAMEWORK

**Extubate if Uneventful Elective Case:**

Requires ALL of the following criteria (ABC):
* **A - Awake:** Patient responsive and following commands
* **B - Breathing:** Adequate spontaneous ventilation, good respiratory mechanics
* **C - Comfortable:** Adequate analgesia in place (epidural functioning or alternative plan)

PLUS:
* **Hemodynamically Stable:** MAP within 20% baseline without excessive vasopressor support
* **Normothermic:** Core temperature >36°C
* **Metabolically Stable:** Lactate clearing, minimal base deficit, adequate urine output

**Keep Intubated and Plan ICU Ventilation if:**
* **Preexisting Cardiopulmonary Disease:**
  * Severe COPD with baseline hypercapnia
  * Significant cardiac dysfunction (EF <30%)
  * Pulmonary hypertension
* **Intraoperative Complications:**
  * Prolonged surgery (>6-8 hours)
  * Massive blood transfusion (>10 units PRBCs or >1.5 blood volumes)
  * Significant hemodynamic instability requiring multiple vasopressors
* **Ongoing Instability:**
  * Not hemodynamically stable
  * Not normothermic (core temp <35.5°C)
  * Ongoing bleeding or coagulopathy
* **Metabolic Derangements:**
  * Severe acidosis (pH <7.25)
  * Elevated lactate not trending down
  * Acute kidney injury with oliguria

#### POSTOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT PRIORITIES

* **β-Blocker Continuation:** Resume as tolerated perioperatively (critical for cardiac protection)
* **Epidural Management:**
  * Redosing allows excellent pain control
  * May facilitate mechanical ventilation weaning (especially COPD patients)
  * For intubated patients: permits light IV sedation while allowing neurologic monitoring
* **ICU Monitoring:**
  * Continuous hemodynamic monitoring
  * Frequent neurologic assessment (especially with suprarenal clamping)
  * Urine output monitoring (goal >0.5 mL/kg/hr)
  * Serial lactate and base deficit trending
* **Early Mobilization:** When stable, to reduce respiratory complications

**Bottom Line:** Open aortic reconstruction requires sophisticated hemodynamic management with thorough understanding of cross-clamp pathophysiology, organ-specific protection strategies, and complex intraoperative decision-making. Success depends on meticulous preparation, continuous monitoring, and flexible response to rapidly changing conditions.

# 3.3. PERIPHERAL ARTERY DISEASE MANAGEMENT

**Peripheral artery disease affects up to 20% of the population and serves as a risk factor for adverse cardiovascular events with annual rates of 5-7%.**

## EPIDEMIOLOGY AND RISK FACTORS

### POPULATION IMPACT

* **Prevalence:** Up to 20% of population affected
* **Symptomatic Disease:** Majority of patients asymptomatic
* **Cardiovascular Risk:** Annual adverse event rate 5-7%

### RISK FACTOR PROFILE

* **Non-white race**
* **Male gender**  
* **Increasing age**
* **Smoking**
* **Diabetes**
* **Hypertension**
* **Dyslipidemia**
* **Chronic renal insufficiency**
* **Hyperviscous/hypercoagulable states**
* **Hyperhomocysteinemia**
* **Elevated inflammatory markers**

## CLINICAL PRESENTATIONS AND INDICATIONS FOR SURGERY

### THREE CLINICAL INDICATIONS FOR ELECTIVE PAD SURGERY

1. **Intermittent Claudication (IC)**
2. **Ischemic Rest Pain or Ulceration**
3. **Gangrene**

### INTERMITTENT CLAUDICATION (IC)

* **Definition:** Reproducible discomfort in defined muscle group induced by activity and relieved with rest
* **Prevalence:** 10-35% of PAD patients present with IC symptoms
* **Clinical Manifestations:** Depend on occlusion location, severity, and collateral blood flow presence

#### LOCATION-SPECIFIC PATTERNS

* **Aortoiliac Occlusive Disease (AIOD) - "Inflow Disease":**
  * Buttock or hip claudication
  * Usually originates in distal aorta or proximal common iliac arteries
* **"Outflow Disease" - Femoropopliteal or Infrapopliteal:**
  * Thigh or leg claudication

#### NATURAL HISTORY AND TREATMENT APPROACH

* **Progression:** Typically indolent with slow, progressive functional decline
* **Conservative Management:** Aggressive risk factor management prevents disease progression in vast majority
* **Surgical Decision Making:** Must be individualized considering:
  * Response to therapy
  * Loss of function
  * Effect on quality of life
  * Patient activity level (very active person may be debilitated by mild symptoms; severe symptoms may be well tolerated by sedentary individual)

### CHRONIC LIMB ISCHEMIA (CLI)

* **Definition:** Existing arterial blood flow unable to meet basal metabolic demands
* **Clinical Manifestations:** Rest pain, ulceration, gangrene
* **Pathophysiology:** Aggressive PAD manifestation with severe, multisegmental occlusion
* **Risk Factors:** Age, diabetes, smoking, hyperlipidemia
* **Prevalence:** PAD progresses to CLI in 1-2% of population

#### CLINICAL OUTCOMES

* **One-Year Prognosis:**
  * 25% progression to amputation
  * 25% death from cardiovascular causes
* **Concurrent Disease:** ~50% have concomitant CAD
* **Mortality:** Higher from MI or stroke compared to less aggressive PAD forms
* ⚠️ **Urgent Intervention Required:** Due to progressive ischemia and variable limb loss risk

## SURGICAL MANAGEMENT OPTIONS

### DIRECT VS EXTRA-ANATOMIC RECONSTRUCTION

#### AORTOILIAC OCCLUSIVE DISEASE REPAIR OPTIONS

* **Direct Reconstruction:** 
  * Aortoiliac or aortofemoral bypass
  * Long-term patency: >80%
  * Higher perioperative morbidity/mortality
* **Extra-Anatomic Reconstruction:**
  * Axillary-femoral +/- femoral-femoral bypass
  * 5-year patency: 55-80%
  * Less morbid but less durable

#### EXTRA-ANATOMIC BYPASS INDICATIONS

* **High-Risk Direct Reconstruction Candidates:**
  * Previous graft or stent complications
  * Infection
  * Previous intra-abdominal surgery with adhesions

#### INFRAINGUINAL DISEASE

* **Repair Depends On:** Lesion level (femoral, popliteal, or infrapopliteal vessels)
* **Individualized Approach:** Based on anatomy and patient factors

## ANESTHETIC MANAGEMENT OF LOWER EXTREMITY REVASCULARIZATION

### AORTOILIAC DISEASE MANAGEMENT

#### AORTOFEMORAL BYPASS

* **Similar to OAR:** Anesthetic considerations parallel open aortic reconstruction
* **Neuraxial Anesthesia:** Consider epidural catheter for postoperative pain control barring contraindications
* **General Anesthesia:** ETT typically employed due to case length and hemodynamic lability potential
* **Monitoring Requirements:**
  * Large-bore peripheral IV access
  * Arterial line for resuscitation guidance and hemodynamic assessment
* **Cross-Clamping Considerations:** 
  * Generally better tolerated than aneurysmal disease
  * Patient accustomed to high aortic resistance
  * More stable hemodynamics due to:
	* Clamp location (typically distal aortic)
	* Extensive collateralization from chronic atherosclerotic obstruction
  * Contrast to aneurysmal disease with limited pre-existing collateral flow
* **Central Venous Access:** Reasonable to ensure reliable vasoactive medication delivery
* **Additional Cardiac Monitoring:** Consider PA catheter or TEE based on patient/surgical factors

#### EXTRA-ANATOMIC BYPASS

* **No Aortic Manipulation:** Avoids aortic cross-clamping/unclamping
* **Procedure:** Axillary to ipsilateral femoral +/- subsequent femoral-femoral bypass
* **Hemodynamic Stability:** Less lability than aortic cross-clamping
* **Monitoring Requirements:**
  * Large-bore peripheral IV and arterial line still prudent
  * Less critical need for extensive central/cardiac monitoring unless independently indicated
* **Arterial Line Placement:** Should be contralateral to surgical bypass (ipsilateral line nonfunctional during cross-clamp)
* **Subcutaneous Tunneling:** More sympathetically stimulating than cross-clamp placement - attention to hemodynamic swings and patient movement prevention

### INFRAINGUINAL REVASCULARIZATION

#### ANESTHETIC TECHNIQUE OPTIONS

* **General Anesthesia**
* **Neuraxial Anesthesia** 
* **Regional Anesthesia**
* **Choice Individualized:** Based on patient, surgeon, and anesthesiologist factors

#### REGIONAL ANESTHESIA BENEFITS

* **Hemodynamic:** Avoids hyperdynamic responses to intubation/extubation
* **Physiologic:** Blunted perioperative catecholamine response
* **Vascular:** Improved vascular blood flow, higher graft patency rates
* **Pulmonary:** Lower pulmonary complications

#### CLINICAL EVIDENCE

**NSQIP Database Analysis:** ~15,000 patients receiving neuraxial vs general anesthesia
* **Neuraxial Benefits:**
  * Decreased likelihood of reintervention
  * Decreased rates of graft failure
  * Decreased postoperative respiratory and cardiovascular complications

**CLI-Specific Analysis:** No differences in perioperative morbidity between general vs neuraxial anesthesia

**Cochrane Review:** Insufficient evidence to rule out clinically important differences for most outcomes (mortality, MI, amputation rates)

💡 **Conclusion:** Anesthetic technique should be governed by clinical expertise, patient and surgeon preferences rather than outcome data.

### MORBIDITY AND MORTALITY PATTERNS

* **Primary Cause:** Cardiac origin
* **NSQIP Database:** 
  * Overall cardiac mortality: 2.7%
  * 30-day combined mortality/major morbidity: ~20%
* **CLI vs IC:** Higher risk with CLI likely due to:
  * Greater disease burden
  * More technically challenging repairs
  * Multilevel, extensive disease

**Bottom Line:** Peripheral artery disease management requires understanding of disease patterns, surgical options, and individualized anesthetic approaches. While endovascular techniques are increasingly used, open surgical reconstruction remains important for complex disease, with anesthetic management focused on optimizing cardiovascular stability and outcomes.

# 4.1. CAROTID ARTERY STENTING

**The advent of endovascular techniques has revolutionized vascular surgery, offering minimally invasive approaches with generally improved perioperative outcomes but trade-offs in long-term durability.**

## HISTORICAL DEVELOPMENT AND CURRENT STATUS

* **First Description:** Late 1970s and early 1980s
* **Initial Limitations:** Early complications from distal plaque embolization limited enthusiasm
* **Breakthrough:** 1990s development of embolic protection devices renewed interest
* **Current Application:** Especially for patients deemed high risk for open surgery

## CLINICAL EVIDENCE: RANDOMIZED CONTROLLED TRIALS

### FIVE LANDMARK RCTS COMPARING CEA TO CAROTID STENTING

1. **EVA-3S Trial**
2. **SAPPHIRE Trial** 
3. **SPACE Trial**
4. **ICSS Trial**
5. **CREST Trial**

### CREST TRIAL (2010) - PIVOTAL COMPARATIVE STUDY

**Key Findings Comparing Carotid Stenting vs CEA:**
* **Perioperative Stroke Risk:**
  * Stenting: Higher perioperative CVA risk
  * CEA: Lower perioperative CVA risk
* **Perioperative MI Risk:**
  * CEA: Higher perioperative myocardial infarction risk
  * Stenting: Lower perioperative MI risk
* **Long-term Outcomes (4 years):**
  * No difference in composite risk of stroke, MI, or death
  * Equivalent stroke prevention long-term
* **Age-Dependent Outcomes:**
  * **Younger Patients (<70 years):** Lower complication rates with stenting
  * **Older Patients (>70 years):** Lower complication rates with CEA
  * Age appears to be important factor in treatment selection

### 2020 META-ANALYSIS RESULTS

* **Symptomatic Patients:** Carotid stenting associated with higher risk of periprocedural stroke or death than endarterectomy
* **Stroke Type:** Mostly minor, nondisabling strokes in elderly population
* **Long-term Outcomes:** Beyond perioperative period, carotid stenting as effective as endarterectomy in preventing recurrent stroke
* **Age Factor:** Most differences limited to elderly patients

💡 **Unique Finding:** Unlike other vascular beds where endovascular repair shows short-term perioperative benefits, carotid stenting shows opposite pattern with higher perioperative morbidity but equivalent long-term outcomes.

💡 **Clinical Decision-Making:** CREST data suggest treatment choice should consider patient age, with younger patients potentially benefiting more from stenting and older patients from CEA, though both are effective long-term.

### CURRENT CONSENSUS

* **No Clear Superiority:** Contemporary literature fails to reach consensus on superiority of open vs endovascular technique
* **Individual Considerations:** Medical, surgical, and operator factors must be weighed
* **Gold Standard:** CEA remains gold standard, but continuous technological advances changing trends
* **Ongoing Studies:** Modern era studies currently evaluating outcomes with improved technology, protection devices, and operator skill

## NOVEL HYBRID APPROACHES

### TRANSCAROTID ARTERY REVASCULARIZATION (TCAR)

* **Technique:** Hybrid approach involving small common carotid cutdown and stent deployment
* **Innovation:** Creates temporary reversal of cerebral blood flow via novel cerebral protection device
* **Clinical Results:**
  * **ROADSTER Trials:** Lowest perioperative stroke rates (1.4%) for endovascular techniques
  * **Equivalency:** Stroke rates equivalent to open CEA
  * **Superiority:** Lower stroke/death risk than traditional transfemoral carotid stenting

### PREFERRED INDICATIONS FOR STENTING OVER OPEN CEA

* **Reoperation cases**
* **Very high lesions**
* **Post-radiation neck anatomy**

## ANESTHETIC MANAGEMENT OF CAROTID ARTERY STENTING

### MAJOR ANESTHETIC ADVANTAGE

* **Continuous Neurologic Monitoring:** Ability to continuously monitor neurologic integrity during procedure
* **Minimal Sedation Required:** Little to no sedation beyond local anesthetic at access site
* **Patient Cooperation:** Vital that patient remains alert and cooperative for serial neurologic examinations

### PREOPERATIVE PREPARATION

* **Critical Intervention:** Candid preoperative discussion to set appropriate patient expectations
* **Education:** Explain need for patient cooperation and minimal sedation approach

### INTRAOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT

* **Sedation Strategy:**
  * Lowest necessary doses of short-acting sedatives
  * Titrated to effect carefully
  * ⚠️ **Avoid Patient Disinhibition**
* **Monitoring:** Standard intraoperative monitoring sufficient
* **Vascular Access:** Single mediumto large-bore peripheral IV typically adequate
* **Invasive Monitoring:** Arterial access may be considered but rarely warranted (can monitor from surgical access if needed)

### PHYSIOLOGIC CONSIDERATIONS

* **Carotid Baroreceptor Reflex:** Angioplasty and stent deployment may trigger reflex with:
  * Bradycardia
  * Hypotension
  * Similar to external carotid manipulation during open surgery
* **Management:** 
  * Cessation of manipulation should extinguish response
  * Prophylaxis with anticholinergic agent may be considered
* **Postoperative:** Altered carotid baroreceptor function possible - treat hemodynamic instability as for CEA

**Bottom Line:** Carotid artery stenting offers minimally invasive alternative to CEA with continuous neurologic monitoring advantages, but requires patient cooperation and careful sedation management. Outcomes are equivalent to CEA in appropriate patient populations with modern techniques.

# 4.2. ENDOVASCULAR AORTIC REPAIR (EVAR) [PART B/C]

**Endovascular repair has revolutionized AAA management, rapidly becoming the predominant treatment modality despite trade-offs between short-term benefits and long-term surveillance requirements.**

## HISTORICAL CONTEXT AND ADOPTION

* **Pre-Endovascular Era:** Limited options - open aortic repair (OAR) vs medical management
* **OAR Limitations:** Significant morbidity/mortality with near 100% rupture mortality for out-of-hospital cases
* **Initial Application:** Reserved for high-risk patients with straightforward anatomy deemed unfit for open repair
* **Technology Advancement:** Customized fenestrated, branched modular stents, and chimney techniques expanded candidacy

### RAPID ADOPTION STATISTICS

* **2000:** EVAR accounted for 5.2% of AAA repairs
* **2004:** EVAR surpassed OAR as more commonly employed technique
* **2010:** 74% of repairs performed via EVAR
* **Current Application:** Also treatment of choice for complicated acute type B dissections

## MAJOR CLINICAL TRIALS COMPARING EVAR VS OAR

### FOUR MAJOR MULTICENTER RCTS

1. **DREAM Trial (Dutch)**
2. **EVAR Trial**
3. **OVER Trial**
4. **ACE Trial (French)**

### POOLED ANALYSIS RESULTS

* **Short-term Mortality:** EVAR shows significantly lower perioperative mortality than OAR
* **Long-term Outcomes:** Benefit lost in intermediateto long-term follow-up
* **Major Morbidity:** Similar between groups including myocardial, neurologic, renal complications
* **Pulmonary Complications:** Significantly higher in OAR vs EVAR group
* **Reintervention Rate:** Significantly higher in EVAR group, necessitating lifetime surveillance

💡 **ACE Trial Exception:** Found no significant perioperative or long-term mortality differences, but patients were considered lowto intermediate-risk for surgery.

### RECENT TRIAL EVIDENCE AND SPECIFIC POPULATION OUTCOMES

#### IMPROVE TRIAL (2017) - RUPTURED AAA

**Key Finding:** Endovascular-first strategy protocol improved 30-day mortality for ruptured AAA
* Emergency ruptured AAA mortality remains high (~30-40% in-hospital mortality)
* Protocol-driven approach with EVAR-first strategy when anatomically suitable
* Improved early outcomes with systematic approach

#### POPULATION-SPECIFIC EVAR OUTCOMES

**Recent Meta-Analyses and Large Database Studies:**

* **Non-Surgical Candidates:**
  * EVAR provides better survival vs conservative management
  * Even high-risk patients benefit from intervention if reasonable life expectancy

* **Obese Patients:**
  * No increase in complication rates with EVAR
  * May have advantages over open repair (avoid difficult laparotomy)

* **Asymptomatic AAA:**
  * Lower early complication rate vs open repair
  * Less reintervention in short-term compared to open approach
  * Long-term surveillance requirement remains

* **Suspected/Confirmed Ruptured AAA:**
  * Higher early reintervention rate vs open repair
  * Better 3-year mortality compared to open
  * Equivalent 7-year mortality
  * EVAR preferred when anatomy suitable and resources available

#### CONTEMPORARY EVAR VS OPEN REPAIR COMPARATIVE OUTCOMES

**EVAR Advantages:**
* Shorter operative time
* Reduced blood loss
* Shorter postoperative hospital stay
* Less postoperative pain
* Lower 30-day mortality (especially ruptured AAA)
* Lower 1-year mortality
* Lower major adverse cardiac events (MACE)

**EVAR Disadvantages:**
* Requires lifelong surveillance imaging
* Higher risk of rupture if complicated anatomy
* Higher risk of endoleak requiring reintervention
* Higher all-cause long-term mortality if complicated anatomy
* Not suitable for all anatomies

**Open Repair Advantages:**
* Suitable for all anatomies
* No surveillance requirements
* Durable long-term results
* Lower reintervention rates

**Open Repair Disadvantages:**
* Higher surgical/perioperative risk
* Laparotomy-related complications (ileus, respiratory issues, wound complications)
* **Obesity-Specific Risks:** Higher AKI and surgical site infection rates
* Longer recovery and hospital stay

💡 **Clinical Decision-Making:** Choice between EVAR and open repair should consider patient age, comorbidities, anatomy, life expectancy, and patient preference regarding surveillance burden.

### RISK STRATIFICATION FOR EVAR

#### EVAR-2 TRIAL

* **Population:** Patients deemed unfit for OAR
* **Ineligibility Factors:** Major cardiac, pulmonary, or renal comorbidities
* **Results:** EVAR reduces aneurysm-related but not overall mortality vs medical therapy
* **Interpretation:** Factors making patients high-risk for OAR likely contributed to high all-cause mortality
* **Conclusion:** Patients with poor life expectancy due to coexisting disease do not benefit from EVAR

## EVOLUTION OF COMPLEX ANEURYSM MANAGEMENT

### PATIENT AND ANATOMIC SELECTION FOR EVAR

#### DECISION FRAMEWORK: EVAR VS OPEN REPAIR

**Ideal Candidates for EVAR:**
* **Age Considerations:**
  * Especially suitable for >80 years old
  * Lower threshold in elderly due to reduced physiologic reserve
* **Anatomic Criteria for Standard EVAR:**
  * Adequate proximal landing zone (neck) >1.5 cm
  * Neck angulation <60 degrees
  * Iliac artery diameter >7 mm (for sheath access)
  * Infrarenal aneurysm without complex anatomy

**Ideal Candidates for Open Repair:**
* **Age:** Especially <65 years (longer life expectancy benefits from durability)
* **Life Expectancy:** >8 years (justifies perioperative risk for long-term benefit)
* **Fitness:** Adequate cardiopulmonary reserve for major surgery
* **Anatomic Factors:**
  * Unfavorable anatomy for EVAR (short neck, severe angulation, small iliacs)
  * Young patients benefit from definitive repair without surveillance burden

**Ruptured AAA Considerations:**
* **EVAR Preferred for:**
  * Contained stable rupture after resuscitation
  * Suitable anatomy
  * Rapid deployment capability
* **Open Repair Indicated for:**
  * Unstable rupture (anterior wall)
  * Unfavorable anatomy
  * Hemodynamic instability precluding endovascular approach

**Conservative Management:**
* Not fit for any operative intervention
* Life expectancy <1 year
* Severe comorbidities with prohibitive surgical risk

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Risk of rupture when aneurysm >4 cm approaches 5% annually - intervention typically warranted when annual rupture risk exceeds procedural mortality risk.

### TRADITIONAL LIMITATIONS

* **Original Design:** Custom Dacron tube endograft limited to infrarenal aortic segments
* **Device Evolution:** Bifurcated and modular grafts allowed extension beyond aortic bifurcation
* **Anatomic Constraints:** Juxtaor suprarenal aneurysms excluded due to visceral vessel involvement
* **Inadequate Seal Zone:** Up to 15% of infrarenal aneurysms have inadequate proximal seal length

### ADVANCED ENDOVASCULAR TECHNIQUES

#### FENESTRATED/BRANCHED EVAR (F/B-EVAR)

* **Concept:** Custom grafts allowing continued visceral organ blood flow while permitting proximal extension
* **Design Process:** Based on 3D reconstructions (CT angiography) with custom fenestration positioning
* **Manufacturing Time:** 2-12 weeks for custom fabrication
* **Vessel Incorporation:** Fenestrations/branches created for:
  * Celiac axis
  * Superior mesenteric artery (SMA)
  * Renal arteries (bilateral)
  * May include additional visceral vessels as needed
* **Anatomic Suitability:** Suitable for 50-80% of patients' anatomy
* **Limitations:**
  * Custom manufacturing: costly and time-consuming
  * **Not Option for:**
    * Emergency procedures (due to manufacturing time)
    * Excessively large aneurysms
    * Rapidly expanding aneurysms
  * Limited availability

#### PHYSICIAN MODIFIED ENDOGRAFTS (PMEG)

* **Concept:** Surgeon modifies off-the-shelf endograft in operating theater immediately before implantation
* **Application:** Complex anatomy requiring fenestrations but emergency situation precludes waiting for custom graft
* **Advantages:**
  * Available for emergency situations
  * Allows treatment of complex anatomy without delay
* **Disadvantages:**
  * ⚠️ **No Quality Control:** Unlike factory-made fenestrated grafts
  * Decreased operating theater efficiency
  * Higher blood loss compared to planned F/B-EVAR
  * Higher early reintervention rates
  * Questionable long-term graft integrity
  * Potential contamination concerns
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl:** PMEG represents a "bridge" solution when anatomy requires fenestrations but patient cannot wait for custom graft fabrication

#### CHIMNEY EVAR (CH-EVAR)

* **Technique:** Distinct endograft positioned parallel to main aortic stent (between aortic wall and main stent)
* **Variations:**
  * **"Snorkel":** Blood flow from above main stent level
  * **"Periscope":** Blood flow from below main stent level  
  * **"Sandwich":** Visceral snorkels between two aortic graft segments
* **Advantages:**
  * Available "off the shelf"
  * Option for urgent situations
  * Less expensive than custom fenestrated stents

### CLINICAL OUTCOMES FOR COMPLEX REPAIRS

#### F/B-EVAR VS OAR

* **Systematic Review:** Trend toward decreased perioperative mortality with f/b-EVAR but higher long-term mortality trend
* **Statistical Significance:** Neither difference reached significance
* **Confounding Factors:**
  * Inherent bias in published literature
  * Non-comparable patient cohorts (morbidity, anatomy complexity, urgency)
  * Early learning curve considerations
  * Initial reservation for high-risk patients unsuitable for open repair

#### CH-EVAR OUTCOMES

* **Perioperative Mortality:** Comparable to OAR (4-5%) but higher than standard EVAR
* **Risk Factors:** Both patient medical complexity and surgical complexity contribute
* **Type I Endoleak Risk:** Increased due to coaxial stent deployment
* **Stroke Risk:** Increased compared to OAR or f/b-EVAR due to upper extremity vascular access requirements in atherosclerotic arch anatomy
* **Technical Success:** Promising shortand intermediate-term success and patency rates
* **Applications:** Valuable for complex urgent repairs when custom stent unavailable

## ANESTHETIC MANAGEMENT OF EVAR

### ANESTHETIC TECHNIQUE OPTIONS

* **Versatility:** Can be performed under almost any anesthesia type:
  * Local anesthesia
  * Regional anesthesia  
  * Neuraxial anesthesia
  * General anesthesia

### TECHNIQUE SELECTION FACTORS

#### CONTRAINDICATIONS TO REGIONAL/NEURAXIAL

* **Antiplatelet Agents:** Concurrent use
* **Therapeutic Anticoagulation:** May preclude neuraxial/regional techniques

#### PATIENT FACTORS FAVORING GENERAL ANESTHESIA

* **Positioning Intolerance:** Inability to lie flat for extended periods
* **Communication Issues:** Inability to effectively communicate
* **High Anxiety:** Patients unable to cooperate

#### SURGICAL FACTORS

* **Duration:** Anticipated length of surgery
* **Complexity:** Difficulty level of repair
* **Conversion Risk:** Potential need for open conversion

### CLINICAL EVIDENCE FOR ANESTHETIC CHOICE

#### NSQIP DATABASE REVIEW (>5,500 PATIENTS)

**Local/Regional vs General Anesthesia Benefits:**
* Decreased pulmonary morbidity
* 10-20% decrease in hospital length of stay

#### VASCULAR QUALITY INITIATIVE ANALYSIS

**Ruptured AAA EVAR Benefits of Local vs General:**
* Lower perioperative mortality rates
* Decreased transfusion rates
* Reduced pulmonary complications  
* Shorter ICU length of stay

#### META-ANALYSIS RESULTS

**Local vs General Anesthesia:**
* Decreased operative time
* Shorter hospital stay
* Reduced postoperative complications
* **Patient Population Bias:** Patients receiving local anesthesia tended to be older with increased cardiovascular/respiratory disease burden

⚠️ **Evidence Limitation:** No randomized controlled trials demonstrate superiority of one technique over another.

### INTRAOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT

#### PROCEDURAL CHARACTERISTICS

**Simple/Standard EVAR:**
* **Approach:** Femoral or iliac artery access (percutaneous or cut-down)
  * +/- Axillary artery access for complex cases
  * +/- Surgical cut-down if unfavorable anatomy
* **Blood Loss:** Typically minimal (<500 mL) for uncomplicated cases
* **Contrast Use:** Moderate (risk of contrast-induced nephropathy)
* **Duration:** 1-3 hours for routine infrarenal EVAR
* **Heparinization:** Systemic heparin 70-100 units/kg (target ACT 200-250 seconds)
  * +/- Protamine reversal (surgeon preference)
* **Hemodynamic Control:**
  * MAP reduction to 50-60 mmHg during stent positioning (reduces migration risk)
  * Breath-hold required for juxta-renal stent deployment (or apnea if intubated)

**Complex EVAR (Fenestrated/Branched/Chimney):**
* **Longer Duration:** 3-8 hours
* **Higher Blood Loss:** Up to moderate (especially PMEG cases)
* **Access Requirements:** May require multiple access sites (bilateral femoral, axillary/brachial)
* **Higher Contrast Load:** Increased CIN risk

#### MONITORING AND ACCESS REQUIREMENTS

* **Hemodynamic Changes:** Should be relatively insignificant during routine EVAR
* **IV Access:** Two large-bore peripheral IVs adequate
* **Blood Products:** Ensure adequate availability
* **Resuscitative Equipment:** Cell saver and rapid infusion devices readily available

#### INTRAOPERATIVE COMPLICATIONS

* **Vascular Access Complications:**
  * Vessel dissection or laceration
  * Puncture site hemorrhage (femoral, axillary)
  * Retroperitoneal hemorrhage (occult bleeding requiring high index of suspicion)
  * Pseudoaneurysm formation
* **Stent-Related:**
  * Wrong or failed deployment
  * Stent migration during deployment
  * Immediate endoleak detection
* **Aortic Complications:**
  * Aortic rupture (rare but catastrophic)
  * Aortic dissection
* **Systemic:**
  * Hypothermia (prolonged exposure in hybrid OR)
  * Contrast-induced nephropathy (see renal protection strategies below)
* **Later Complications:**
  * Abdominal compartment syndrome
  * Paraplegia (rare, more common with extensive aortic coverage)
  * Distal embolization

#### CONVERSION CONSIDERATIONS

* **Conversion Rate:** <1% perioperative conversion from EVAR to OAR
* **Conversion Indications:**
  * Difficult arterial access
  * Vessel dissection
  * Poor anatomic parameters
  * Stent malposition or migration
  * Aneurysm rupture
  * Severe endoleak not amenable to endovascular management
* **Preparation:** Must always consider possible urgent conversion to general anesthesia
* **Emergency Conversion Mortality:** Significantly higher than planned open repair

#### PROCEDURAL CONSIDERATIONS

* **Stent Deployment:** Most self-deploy without ballooning
* **Aortic Ballooning:** When needed, produces transient hemodynamic changes:
  * Short periods of hypertension
  * Increased afterload  
  * Analogous to external cross-clamping
  * Typically brief and not requiring intervention

#### BLOOD LOSS CONSIDERATIONS

* **Routine Cases:** Minimal in experienced hands
* **Complex Repairs:** f/b-EVAR or Ch-EVAR can involve significant and insidious blood loss
* **Rupture Management:** Emergency proximal control via endoscopic balloon occlusion, may require open conversion

### CENTRAL VENOUS ACCESS INDICATIONS

**Not Typically Warranted for Routine EVAR Due To:**
* Rare hemodynamic changes
* Rare need for vasoactive infusions  
* Rare postoperative ICU stays

**Exceptions Consider Central Access:**
* **TEVAR Cases:** May need rapid PA catheter placement if conversion to open TAAA requiring cardiopulmonary bypass
* **Complex Abdominal Repairs:** Snorkel/chimney cases due to:
  * Multiple arterial sheaths required
  * Longer, more complicated procedures
  * Greater blood loss potential
  * Need for aggressive resuscitation/vasoactive infusions

### ANTICOAGULATION AND DEVICE DEPLOYMENT

* **Heparin Administration:** Systemic heparin before device insertion
* **ACT Goal:** ≥200 seconds
* **Additional Medications:**
  * GTN or papaverine for vessel spasm prevention
  * Short-acting agents to facilitate postoperative neurologic assessment
* **Deployment Requirements:**
  * Patient breath-holding (or ventilation hold for anesthetized patients)
  * Temporary MAP reduction request to minimize distal stent migration
* **Completion Assessment:** Angiogram performed to evaluate technical success and complications
* **Reversal:** Anticoagulation reversed after deployment
* **Emergence:** Typically extubated in OR

### CONTRAST-INDUCED NEPHROPATHY (CIN) PREVENTION

**CIN Risk Factors:**
* **Patient-Related:**
  * Age >70 years
  * Preexisting chronic kidney disease (CKD stage ≥3)
  * Diabetes mellitus
  * Congestive heart failure
  * Perioperative dehydration
* **Medication-Related:**
  * ACE inhibitors or ARBs
  * Diuretics
  * Aminoglycosides
* **Procedural:**
  * Repeat contrast administration within 24 hours
  * Large contrast volumes

**Renal Protection Strategies:**
* **Hydration:**
  * Maintain euvolemia or slight hypervolemia
  * Consider isotonic saline or sodium bicarbonate
* **Hemodynamic Goals:**
  * Maintain MAP >65-70 mmHg
  * Ensure adequate renal perfusion pressure
* **Pharmacologic (Evidence Mixed):**
  * Consider N-acetylcysteine (NAC) - limited benefit
  * Mannitol or furosemide - controversial, not routinely recommended
* **Contrast Minimization:**
  * Use minimum necessary contrast volume
  * Consider CO₂ angiography in high-risk patients (see section below)
* **Monitoring:**
  * Monitor urine output intraoperatively and postoperatively
  * Serial creatinine and eGFR postoperatively
* **Avoidance:**
  * Avoid nephrotoxic agents (NSAIDs, aminoglycosides)
  * Hold metformin perioperatively

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Adequate hydration and maintaining renal perfusion pressure are the most evidence-based renal protective strategies - pharmacologic agents show limited benefit.

### CO₂ CONTRAST ANGIOGRAPHY CONSIDERATIONS

**Indications:**
* Chronic kidney disease or high CIN risk
* Contrast allergy
* Slow-flow lesions (lower viscosity than liquid contrast)

**Advantages:**
* Rapidly eliminated via respiratory system
* No renal toxicity
* Can be used in patients with contrast allergy

**Technique Considerations:**
* May need 10-20 mL liquid contrast to identify dependent abnormalities (CO₂ floats)
* Allow 2-3 minute intervals between boluses (can cause pain)
* Use low-pressure injection to avoid vessel dissection

**Risks and Contraindications:**
* ⚠️ **DO NOT use above diaphragm:** Risk of cerebral air embolism (stroke/death)
* Position patient in slight Trendelenburg for abdominal imaging
* **Venography Risks:**
  * Pulmonary embolism
  * CVA/MI (paradoxical embolism)
  * Right ventricular obstruction
  * Position patient in left lateral position for venography
* **Side Effects:**
  * Pain at injection site
  * Nausea
  * Paresthesias
* **Anesthetic Interactions:**
  * ⚠️ Avoid nitrous oxide - mixes with CO₂ and reduces solubility
  * May require regional anesthesia or general anesthesia for pain control

### RESUSCITATIVE ENDOVASCULAR BALLOON OCCLUSION OF THE AORTA (REBOA)

**REBOA represents a temporary endovascular solution for hemodynamic control in ruptured AAA.**

#### CONCEPT AND APPLICATION

* **Indication:** Ruptured AAA with hemodynamic instability
* **Mechanism:**
  * Balloon occlusion of aorta proximal to rupture
  * Reduces bleeding distal to balloon
  * Maintains cardiac and cerebral perfusion
* **Deployment:** Via femoral artery, fluoroscopic or landmark-guided placement
* **Duration:** Temporary measure (~30 minutes maximum occlusion time)

#### ADVANTAGES

* Rapid deployment
* Avoids emergency laparotomy in hybrid OR
* Buys time for resuscitation and EVAR preparation
* Can be placed under local anesthesia
* Allows controlled EVAR in more stable setting

#### RISKS AND COMPLICATIONS

* **Ischemic Injury:**
  * Reduces hepatic, mesenteric, and renal blood flow
  * End-organ ischemia (kidneys, liver, bowel)
  * Spinal cord ischemia (if prolonged)
* **Aortic Injury:**
  * Aortic rupture from balloon overdistension
  * Aortic dissection or perforation
* **Embolization:**
  * Cholesterol embolization
  * Air embolism
  * Distal limb ischemia
* **Access Complications:**
  * Sheath insertion site injury
  * Pseudoaneurysm formation
  * AV fistula formation
  * Limb ischemia from large sheath
* **Reperfusion Injury:** Upon balloon deflation

#### ANESTHETIC CONSIDERATIONS

* **Hemodynamic Management:**
  * Permissive hypotension until balloon inflated (sBP 90-120 mmHg for ruptured AAA)
  * Avoid excessive fluid resuscitation before proximal control
  * Maintain adequate coronary perfusion (diastolic BP >60 mmHg)
* **Monitoring:**
  * Invasive arterial pressure monitoring essential
  * Central venous access for resuscitation
  * Consider cerebral oximetry
  * Urine output monitoring
* **Balloon Inflation Physiology:**
  * Similar to aortic cross-clamping
  * Increased afterload above balloon
  * Decreased perfusion below balloon
* **Balloon Deflation:**
  * Similar to aortic unclamping
  * Prepare for hypotension, metabolic acidosis, hyperkalemia
  * Have vasopressors ready
* **Transition to Definitive Repair:** Must coordinate with surgical team for REBOA deflation timing

## COMPLICATIONS OF EVAR

### REINTERVENTION REQUIREMENTS

* **Higher Rate Than OAR:** 29.6% vs 18.1%
* **Surveillance Need:** Mandates lifetime surveillance
* **Complication Types:**
  * Endoleaks
  * Stent graft occlusion
  * Migration
  * Kinking
  * Infection
* **Intervention Nature:** Majority catheter-based with limited morbidity/mortality
* **Cumulative Exposure:** Each intervention exposes to radiation, contrast, anesthesia risks

### ENDOLEAKS: CLASSIFICATION AND MANAGEMENT

**Most common indication for reintervention following EVAR.**

#### INCIDENCE AND IMPACT

* **Frequency:** Complicate 10-25% of EVAR cases
* **Reintervention Rate:** Upward of 12%
* **Consequence:** Persistent blood flow into aneurysm sac → increased pressure → expansion → potential rupture

#### FIVE TYPES OF ENDOLEAKS

![Types of endoleaks](https://78449c25-3737-4cc1-bf9c-0335e5c6e938.ngrok-free.app/api/placeholder/600/400)

**Type I Endoleaks:**
* **Source:** Direct communication with systemic circulation
* **Management:** Mandate reintervention
* **Treatment:** Repeat balloon molding or proximal/distal stent extension

**Type II Endoleaks:**
* **Source:** Retrograde filling by branch vessels
* **Management:** Controversial
* **Natural History:** Majority remain stable or decrease due to low flow and spontaneous thrombosis
* **Treatment When Needed:** Endovascular embolization of feeding vessel

**Type III Endoleaks:**
* **Source:** Direct communication with systemic circulation
* **Management:** Mandate reintervention  
* **Treatment:** Stent relining

**Type IV Endoleaks:**
* **Source:** Graft porosity
* **Management:** Typically self-limited once coagulation returns to baseline

**Type V Endoleak ("Endotension"):**
* **Source:** Enlarging aneurysm sac without demonstrable endoleak
* **Etiology:** Not completely elucidated
* **Management:** Open conversion is mainstay (though conservative management or endovascular reintervention may have role)

### LATE CONVERSION TO OPEN REPAIR

* **Primary Indication:** Endoleak (>60% of late reinterventions)
* **Incidence:** Reported 2-4% (may be increasing with more endovascular repairs and complex cases)
* **Technical Challenge:** Late conversion is technically challenging
* **Mortality Risk:** Relatively high, particularly if performed emergently (up to 30% for nonelective repair)

### OTHER EVAR COMPLICATIONS

#### STENT MIGRATION

* **Mechanism:** Ongoing downward pulsatile pressure
* **Consequence:** May re-expose aneurysm to systemic pressurization
* **Risk Factors:**
  * Hostile aneurysm anatomy (angulation, short proximal landing zone)
  * Inadequate graft fixation and short overlaps

#### AORTIC STENT GRAFT INFECTION

* **Incidence:** Up to 1% of cases
* **Sources:** Direct primary contamination or secondary source
* **Clinical Presentation:** Nonspecific (fever, back pain, leukocytosis)
* **Treatment:** Broad-spectrum antibiotics, may require explantation and open bypass

#### STENT GRAFT KINKING/INFOLDING

* **Incidence:** <5% of cases
* **Consequence:** Flow-restricting stenosis, graft thrombosis, occlusion
* **Treatment:** 
  * Acute occlusion: catheter-directed thrombolysis
  * Mechanical thrombectomy if pharmacologic contraindicated

### RENAL COMPLICATIONS

* **Multifactorial Etiology:** Contrast exposure, pre-existing dysfunction, procedure-related factors
* **Best Predictor:** Preoperative renal insufficiency
* **Prevention Strategies:**
  * Serial creatinine/GFR monitoring ensuring recovery from diagnostic angiography
  * Limit contrast use (gadolinium and CO2 alternatives)
  * Better x-ray equipment and operator experience for lower contrast dosing
  * Preoperative fluid loading (1 mL/kg/hr × 12 hours optimal, though most patients outpatient)
  * Initial anesthesiologist fluid loading prudent
  * Sodium bicarbonate and N-acetyl cysteine infusions may provide small benefit

**Bottom Line:** EVAR offers significant short-term perioperative advantages over open repair but requires lifetime surveillance and has higher reintervention rates. Anesthetic management is generally less complex than open procedures, with flexibility in technique choice, but must always prepare for potential conversion to open repair.

# 4.3. ENDOVASCULAR MANAGEMENT OF PERIPHERAL ARTERY DISEASE

**The management of PAD has shifted heavily toward endovascular approaches, with technological advances making endovascular treatment reasonable first-line therapy even for complex disease.**

## TREATMENT ALGORITHM EVOLUTION

### TRADITIONAL TASC II RECOMMENDATIONS

* **Focal, Discrete Lesions:** Endovascular revascularization recommended
* **Severe Disease:** Open surgical revascularization recommended

### CURRENT PRACTICE TRENDS

* **Endovascular Preference:** National trends heavily favor endovascular repair even for diffuse, complex disease
* **Statistical Trends:**
  * Endovascular interventions increased >3-fold
  * Open peripheral bypass surgery decreased >40%
* **Technology Drivers:** 
  * Improved stent technology
  * Advanced imaging techniques
  * Drug-device combinations

### MODERN GUIDELINES

* **Society for Vascular Surgery:** Endorses endovascular treatment as reasonable first-line approach in most circumstances, even for complex disease
* **Hybrid Approaches:** Development of hybrid operating rooms allows real-time decision making and multiple procedures under one anesthetic
* **Decision Factors:** Disease severity and location, patient risk factors, proceduralist skill

## CLINICAL EVIDENCE FOR ENDOVASCULAR VS OPEN MANAGEMENT

### LIMITED RANDOMIZED TRIAL DATA

* **Evidence Base:** Majority based on observational data
* **Aortoiliac Disease:** No large RCTs comparing open vs endovascular for complex AIOD
* **Systematic Review:** >5,300 patients undergoing open vs endovascular
  * **Open Repair:** Greater perioperative morbidity and mortality but better long-term durability
  * **Endovascular:** Lower short-term morbidity, similar amputation-free survival

### FEMOROPOPLITEAL DISEASE EVIDENCE

#### BASIL TRIAL

* **Design:** Multicenter RCT comparing endovascular vs open repair
* **Short-term Results:** Endovascular therapy resulted in:
  * Less short-term morbidity
  * Similar amputation-free survival
* **Long-term Results:** For patients surviving ≥2 years:
  * Open revascularization associated with increased overall survival
  * Trend toward improved amputation-free survival

#### ONGOING RESEARCH

* **BEST-CLI Trial:** Prospective, multicenter RCT comparing treatment efficacy, functional outcomes, cost-effectiveness, and quality of life for CLI patients

### INFRAPOPLITEAL DISEASE EVOLUTION

* **Traditional Approach:** Reserved for CLI patients due to limited success and high complication rates
* **Historical Problems:** Unacceptable restenosis, dissection, and thrombosis rates
* **Modern Technology:** Drug-eluting stents (DES) changing treatment paradigm
* **Current Evidence:** Several recent RCTs and meta-analyses support DES use for symptomatic infrapopliteal disease anatomically suitable for intervention

## ENDOVASCULAR TECHNIQUES AND DEVICE TECHNOLOGY

### PROCEDURAL WORKFLOW FOR LIMB ANGIOPLASTY

**Standard Sequence:**
1. **Intravascular Ultrasound (IVUS):** Determine need for vessel preparation
2. **Vessel Preparation:** Optimize vessel for advanced therapy (if needed)
3. **Advanced Therapy:** Drug delivery or stent placement
4. **Completion Angiography:** Assess technical success

### VESSEL PREPARATION METHODS

**Purpose:** Enhance drug uptake, improve results from advanced therapies, treat calcified lesions

#### BALLOON ANGIOPLASTY (STANDARD)

* **Traditional Approach:** Plain balloon dilation
* **Mechanism:** Controlled vessel wall stretch and plaque fracture
* **Limitations:**
  * Elastic recoil
  * Dissection risk
  * Limited efficacy in heavily calcified lesions

#### MODIFIED BALLOON TECHNOLOGIES

**Cutting Balloon:**
* Microsurgical blades (atherotomes) on balloon surface
* Controlled longitudinal incisions in plaque
* Reduces barotrauma to vessel wall
* Better results in focal, calcified lesions

**Scoring Balloon:**
* Nitinol scoring elements create controlled fractures
* Less aggressive than cutting balloon
* Reduced vessel trauma

**Serrated Balloon:**
* Multiple semi-compliant elements on balloon
* Distributes dilating forces
* Improved vessel expansion with less trauma

**Intravascular Lithotripsy:**
* Ultrasonic pulses fracture calcium within plaque
* Minimal vessel wall injury
* Particularly effective for heavily calcified lesions
* Novel approach allowing treatment of previously untreatable calcified disease

#### ATHERECTOMY DEVICES

**Rotational Atherectomy (Rotablation):**
* High-speed diamond-coated burr
* Ablates calcified plaque
* Creates smoother luminal surface

**Directional Atherectomy:**
* Cutting device removes plaque
* Can be directed toward specific plaque

**Orbital Atherectomy:**
* Diamond-coated crown orbits at high speed
* Differential cutting (hard vs soft tissue)

**Laser Atherectomy:**
* Excimer laser vaporizes plaque
* Useful for in-stent restenosis
* Treats chronic total occlusions (CTOs)

### ADVANCED THERAPY OPTIONS

#### DRUG-COATED BALLOONS (DCB)

* **Coating:** Paclitaxel or other antiproliferative drugs
* **Mechanism:** Local drug delivery during balloon inflation
* **Advantages:**
  * Prevents restenosis
  * No permanent implant
  * Suitable for small vessels
* **Applications:** Femoropopliteal and infrapopliteal disease

#### STENT TECHNOLOGIES

**Bare Metal Stents (BMS):**
* Mechanical scaffolding only
* Higher restenosis rates than DES
* Lower cost

**Drug-Eluting Stents (DES):**
* **Coating:** Paclitaxel or other agents
* **Mechanism:** Inhibits smooth muscle regeneration and neointimal hyperplasia
* **Benefits:** Significantly reduced restenosis rates
* **Applications:** Infrapopliteal disease, femoropopliteal disease, complex lesions

### PHARMACOLOGIC ADJUNCTS DURING PROCEDURE

* **Heparin:** 70 units/kg (~5,000 units total)
  * Target ACT 200-250 seconds
  * Prevents thrombosis during manipulation
* **GTN (Nitroglycerin):** Prevents and treats vessel spasm
* **Papaverine:** Alternative for vessel spasm
* **Contrast Agent:** Iodinated contrast or CO₂
  * Contrast-induced nephropathy risk (see CIN prevention strategies)

## CLINICAL PRESENTATIONS AND TREATMENT OBJECTIVES

### CHRONIC LIMB-THREATENING ISCHEMIA (CLTI)

**Definition (formerly "Critical Limb Ischemia"):**
* Ankle-Brachial Index (ABI) <0.4
* Absolute ankle pressure <50 mmHg
* Absolute toe pressure <30 mmHg
* Transcutaneous PO₂ <30 mmHg
* Minimally pulsatile pulse volume recording

**Presentation Patterns:**
* 1/3 present with claudication
* 2/3 asymptomatic until severe disease
* 1-3% progress to CLTI
* **Associated Conditions:** Diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, metabolic syndrome

**Treatment Objectives:**
1. Relieve rest pain
2. Allow ischemic ulcers to heal
3. Maintain ambulatory status and functional extremity
4. Prevent major amputation

**Long-term Outcomes to Monitor:**
* Acute limb ischemia episodes
* Major amputation for vascular causes
* Unplanned revascularization for recurrent ischemia

### ACUTE LIMB ISCHEMIA

* **Medical Emergency:** Requires urgent intervention
* **Endovascular Options:** Catheter-directed thrombolysis, mechanical thrombectomy
* **Hybrid Approaches:** Often combined with open surgery

## ANESTHETIC MANAGEMENT OF ENDOVASCULAR PAD PROCEDURES

### STANDARD APPROACH

* **Primary Technique:** Local anesthesia or monitored anesthesia care
* **Sedation Strategy:** Small doses of short-acting agents allowing patient cooperation
* **Patient Requirements:**
  * Ability to tolerate lying flat for several hours
  * Cooperative throughout procedure
* **General Anesthesia Indications:**
  * Particularly anxious patients
  * Inability to cooperate
  * Extended procedure duration anticipated

### MONITORING AND ACCESS REQUIREMENTS

* **Hemodynamic Changes:** Minimal anticipated intraoperatively
* **Blood Loss Risk:** Minimal
* **Invasive Monitoring:** Rarely indicated
* **Vascular Access:** Single mediumto large-bore IV sufficient

### PROCEDURAL CHARACTERISTICS

* **Minimally Invasive Nature:** Generally well-tolerated procedures
* **Duration:** Variable based on complexity (1-4 hours typically)
* **Patient Communication:** Important for comfort and cooperation
* **Conversion Risk:** Minimal risk of urgent conversion to open surgery

### COMPLICATIONS OF LIMB ANGIOPLASTY

#### CARDIOVASCULAR

* **Acute Myocardial Infarction:** Most common cause of death following peripheral revascularization
* **Arrhythmias:** From volume shifts, contrast, guidewire manipulation
* **Hemodynamic Instability:** Rare but possible with large volume contrast or complications

#### VASCULAR ACCESS COMPLICATIONS

* **Access Site Hematoma:** Most common vascular complication
* **Pseudoaneurysm:** Requires ultrasound-guided compression or thrombin injection
* **Arteriovenous Fistula:** May require surgical repair
* **Retroperitoneal Hematoma:** Occult bleeding requiring high index of suspicion
* **Limb Ischemia:** From access site thrombosis or dissection

#### PROCEDURE-SPECIFIC COMPLICATIONS

* **Distal Embolization:**
  * Plaque/thrombus dislodged during manipulation
  * "Trash foot" or blue toe syndrome
  * May require additional intervention
* **Vessel Dissection:** From guidewire or device manipulation
* **Vessel Perforation:** Requires immediate recognition and management
* **Limb Edema:** Especially after chronic total occlusion (CTO) treatment
  * Tissue hyperperfusion phenomenon
  * Reperfusion injury
  * Usually self-limited but may be severe

#### SYSTEMIC COMPLICATIONS

* **Wound Infection:** Groin/access site infections
* **Contrast-Induced Nephropathy:** Significant risk in this population (DM, CKD common)
* **Respiratory Complications:**
  * Elderly population
  * High prevalence of COPD
  * Smoking history universal
  * Aspiration risk if oversedated

#### CHRONIC TOTAL OCCLUSION (CTO) SPECIFIC

* **Prolonged Procedure Time:** 2-6 hours typical
* **Higher Contrast Load:** Increased CIN risk
* **Reperfusion Syndrome:**
  * Severe limb edema
  * Compartment syndrome risk
  * Metabolite washout
* **Higher Embolization Risk:** Manipulation of chronic organized thrombus

### POSTOPERATIVE OPTIMIZATION STRATEGIES

**Minimize Complications by:**
* **Good Analgesia:** Adequate pain control reduces sympathetic surge
* **Normothermia:** Active warming throughout procedure
* **Supplemental Oxygen:** 1-4 days postoperatively for high-risk patients
* **Hemodynamic Stability:**
  * Avoid tachycardia
  * Treat hypertension
  * Maintain normovolemia and adequate blood pressure
* **Anticoagulation Measures:**
  * Recognize hypercoagulable state postoperatively
  * Calf compression devices
  * LMWH prophylaxis unless contraindicated
* **Oxygen Carrying Capacity:** Optimize hemoglobin
* **Stress Reduction:** Minimize physiologic stress

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Despite minimal intraoperative stress, these patients remain at high risk for MI - maintain vigilance for 4-6 weeks postoperatively.

**Bottom Line:** Endovascular management of PAD has become first-line therapy for most anatomically suitable lesions, offering reduced morbidity and good short-term outcomes. While anesthetic management is typically straightforward, recognition of high-risk patient population and potential complications remains critical for optimal outcomes.

# 4.4. DIALYSIS ACCESS CREATION AND MANAGEMENT

**Vascular access creation for hemodialysis represents a unique subset of vascular surgery requiring specialized anesthetic considerations for end-stage renal failure patients.**

## TYPES OF VASCULAR ACCESS

### ARTERIOVENOUS FISTULA (AVF) - PREFERRED ACCESS

**AVF represents the gold standard for hemodialysis access due to superior long-term outcomes.**

#### SURGICAL STAGES

**Stage 1: AVF Creation**
* **Procedure:** Surgical anastomosis between artery and vein
* **Common Configurations:**
  * **Radiocephalic (Brescia-Cimino):** Radial artery to cephalic vein at wrist (~70% success)
  * **Brachiocephalic:** Brachial artery to cephalic vein at antecubital fossa (~90% success)
  * **Brachiobasilic:** Brachial artery to basilic vein with transposition
* **Anastomosis Types:**
  * Side-to-side: Artery side to vein side
  * End-to-side: Artery side to vein end (most common)
* **Duration:** 1-2 hours typically
* **Anesthesia:** Regional anesthesia preferred (see below)

**Stage 2: Superficialization (if needed)**
* **Indication:** Basilic vein AVF requiring elevation to accessible position
* **Timing:** After initial maturation period
* **Procedure:** Surgical transposition of vein to subcutaneous position

#### AVF MATURATION CRITERIA ("RULE OF 6s")

**Successful Maturation Requires:**
* **Flow:** >600 mL/min
* **Diameter:** >6 mm
* **Depth:** <6 mm below skin surface
* **Length:** >6 cm of suitable vein for cannulation
* **Timeframe:** Typically occurs by 6 weeks post-creation

**Maturation Failure:** 20-50% of AVFs fail to mature adequately

#### AVF COMPLICATIONS

**Early Complications:**
* **Inadequate Maturation:** Most common (20-50%)
  * Low flow
  * Small diameter
  * Early thrombosis (mechanical failure)
* **Early Thrombosis:**
  * Mechanical/technical factors
  * Hypercoagulable state
  * Hypotension

**Late Complications:**
* **Stenosis:** Intimal hyperplasia at anastomosis or outflow vein
* **Thrombosis:** From stenosis or repeated cannulation trauma
* **Steal Syndrome:**
  * Mild: Only symptomatic during hemodialysis
  * Severe: Continuous ischemic symptoms (rare)
  * More common with brachial artery-based fistulas
* **Pseudoaneurysm:** From repeated needle puncture sites
* **High-Output Cardiac Failure:** Rare, from excessive shunt flow

**Central Complications (Any Access Type):**
* **Central Venous Stenosis:** From previous central lines
* **Venous Hypertension:**
  * Upper limb edema
  * Prominent varicosities
  * Prolonged bleeding post-dialysis
  * Skin changes

### ARTERIOVENOUS GRAFT (AVG)

* **Indication:** Inadequate native veins for AVF creation
* **Material:** Synthetic (PTFE) or biological graft
* **Advantages:**
  * Earlier use (2-4 weeks vs 6+ weeks for AVF)
  * More predictable maturation
* **Disadvantages:**
  * Higher infection risk
  * Higher thrombosis rates
  * Lower long-term patency vs AVF
  * More frequent interventions required

### CENTRAL VENOUS CATHETER (CVC)

* **Types:** Tunneled (Permacath) vs non-tunneled
* **Indication:** Temporary access or when AVF/AVG not feasible
* **Disadvantages:**
  * Highest infection risk
  * Central vein stenosis/thrombosis
  * Lower flow rates
  * Not suitable for long-term use

## ANESTHETIC MANAGEMENT FOR AVF CREATION

### PATIENT ASSESSMENT AND OPTIMIZATION

#### END-STAGE RENAL FAILURE (ESRF) CONSIDERATIONS

**Metabolic Derangements:**
* **Uremia:** Multiple organ system effects
  * Platelet dysfunction (qualitative defect)
  * Encephalopathy
  * Peripheral neuropathy
  * Pericarditis risk
* **Electrolyte Abnormalities:**
  * Hyperkalemia (especially if missed dialysis)
  * Hypocalcemia / Hyperphosphatemia
  * Metabolic acidosis
* **Volume Status:** Often fluid overloaded
* **Anemia:** Erythropoietin deficiency (despite treatment)

**Cardiovascular:**
* **Hypertension:** Nearly universal
* **Left Ventricular Hypertrophy:** From chronic volume/pressure overload
* **Coronary Artery Disease:** Accelerated atherosclerosis
* **Congestive Heart Failure:** Common
* **Arrhythmias:** From electrolyte shifts

**Indications for Urgent Dialysis (AEIOU):**
* **A**cidosis (refractory metabolic acidosis)
* **E**lectrolytes (life-threatening hyperkalemia)
* **I**ngestions (certain toxins/poisons)
* **O**verload (refractory pulmonary edema)
* **U**remia (pericarditis, encephalopathy, bleeding)

#### MEDICATION CONSIDERATIONS

* **Regular Medications:**
  * Sodium bicarbonate (for acidosis)
  * Diuretics (though often ineffective)
  * Erythropoietin (ESA therapy)
  * Phosphate binders
* **Timing of Dialysis:** Should have dialysis day before surgery, NOT on day of surgery
  * Optimizes volume status
  * Corrects electrolytes
  * Avoids residual heparin effect

### ANESTHETIC TECHNIQUE SELECTION

#### REGIONAL ANESTHESIA - STRONGLY PREFERRED

**Evidence Base (Chen et al 2020 Systematic Review):**

**Regional Anesthesia (vs Local) Showed:**
* **↑ Primary Patency Rates:** Better long-term fistula function
* **↑ Brachial Artery Diameter:** Improved arterial inflow
* **Shorter Operative Duration:** Facilitated surgical conditions
* **Less Need for Postoperative Analgesia:** Superior pain control
* **Sympatholysis Effect:** Vasodilation improves early flow
* **Vasodilation:** Enhanced arterial and venous dilation

**Regional Anesthesia Benefits:**
* **Superior Analgesia:** Excellent intraoperative and postoperative pain control
* **Sympathetic Blockade:** Vasodilation enhances early fistula flow and maturation
* **Awake Patient:** Allows continuous monitoring and cooperation
* **Avoids GA Risks:** Particularly important in high-risk renal patients

#### REGIONAL ANESTHESIA OPTIONS

**Upper Extremity Blocks for AVF:**

**Supraclavicular Block:**
* **Coverage:** Entire upper extremity
* **Success Rate:** High for distal AVF
* **Risks:** Pneumothorax (use ultrasound guidance)

**Infraclavicular Block:**
* **Coverage:** Forearm and hand
* **Advantages:** Lower pneumothorax risk than supraclavicular
* **Applications:** Distal AVF procedures

**Interscalene Block:**
* **Coverage:** Shoulder and proximal arm
* **Application:** Proximal AVF (brachiobasilic)
* **Risks:** Phrenic nerve blockade common

**Axillary Block:**
* **Coverage:** Forearm and hand (spares intercostobrachial nerve)
* **Success Rate:** ~70% for distal procedures
* **Advantages:** Safest approach (no risk of pneumothorax)
* **Disadvantages:**
  * May miss intercostobrachial nerve (medial arm sensation)
  * Lower success rate than supraclavicular

**Costoclavicular Block:**
* **Coverage:** Entire upper limb
* **Advantages:** Compact brachial plexus anatomy at this level
* **Applications:** Any upper limb AVF

#### LOCAL ANESTHESIA

* **Application:** Surgeon-administered field block
* **Advantages:** Avoids block complications, simple
* **Disadvantages:**
  * No sympatholysis benefit
  * Inferior analgesia
  * Large volume local anesthetic required
  * No postoperative analgesia

#### GENERAL ANESTHESIA

**Indications:**
* Patient refusal of regional
* Contraindications to regional (coagulopathy, infection)
* Inability to cooperate
* Prolonged or complex procedure

**Disadvantages:**
* Loss of sympatholysis benefit
* Potentially inferior outcomes (vs regional)
* Higher risk in renal failure patients

### PERIOPERATIVE DRUG CONSIDERATIONS IN ESRF

**Induction Agents:**
* **Propofol:** Higher induction dose required (30-50% increase)
  * Increased volume of distribution
  * Decreased protein binding
* **Thiopentone:** Reduce dose by 30-50%
  * Decreased protein binding → increased free fraction
* **Etomidate:** Use with caution (adrenal suppression concerns)
* **Ketamine:** Cardio-stable option, good for unstable patients

**Muscle Relaxants:**
* **Succinylcholine:** ⚠️ AVOID or use with extreme caution
  * Risk of hyperkalemia (K+ rise 0.5-1.0 mEq/L)
  * Only if K+ <5.0 mEq/L and urgent indication
* **Rocuronium:** Preferred non-depolarizing agent (hepatic metabolism)
* **Vecuronium:** Use with caution (active metabolites renally excreted)
* **Cisatracurium:** Safe option (Hoffman elimination)

**Opioids:**
* **Morphine:** ⚠️ Reduce dose significantly
  * Active metabolites (M3G, M6G) accumulate
  * Prolonged effect and respiratory depression risk
* **Fentanyl:** Preferred short-acting opioid
* **Remifentanil:** Excellent choice (esterase metabolism, no renal clearance)

**Reversal Agents:**
* **Sugammadex:** ⚠️ NOT recommended in ESRF
  * Renally excreted
  * Complex not cleared → potential for recurarization
  * If eGFR <30: avoid or use with extreme caution
* **Neostigmine:** Can be used (though anticholinesterase effects prolonged)

**Local Anesthetics:**
* **Standard Dosing:** Generally safe for single-shot blocks
* **LAST Risk:** Reduced protein binding may increase free fraction
* **Lipid Emulsion:** Have available for LAST treatment

### INTRAOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT

**Vascular Access:**
* **IV Placement:** ⚠️ NEVER in arm designated for AVF creation
  * Preserve all veins in that extremity
  * No blood draws, no BP cuffs, no IVs
  * Place signage to prevent inadvertent use
* **Opposite Arm:** Preferred for IV access and monitoring
* **Central Access:** Avoid if possible (risk of central stenosis)

**Monitoring:**
* **Blood Pressure:** Non-invasive on contralateral arm
* **Avoid:** BP cuff on AVF arm (may damage developing veins)
* **Standard Monitoring:** ECG, SpO2, capnography (if GA)

**Hemodynamic Goals:**
* **Maintain Adequate Blood Pressure:** Ensure fistula perfusion
* **Avoid Hypotension:** May cause early thrombosis
* **Fluid Management:**
  * Careful fluid administration (volume overload risk)
  * Consider ultrafiltration status from recent dialysis

**Anticoagulation:**
* **Platelet Dysfunction:** Baseline qualitative defect from uremia
* **Post-Dialysis:** Residual heparin effect if dialyzed same day
* **Regional Anesthesia Timing:** Safe if >4 hours post-dialysis
* **Bleeding Risk:** Monitor for excessive bleeding

### POSTOPERATIVE CONSIDERATIONS

**Immediate Assessment:**
* **Fistula Thrill:** Palpable thrill indicates flow
* **Bruit:** Auscultate for continuous bruit
* **Distal Perfusion:** Assess hand perfusion (steal syndrome)
* **Pain Control:** Adequate analgesia without oversedation

**Postoperative Instructions:**
* **Arm Protection:**
  * No BP measurements on AVF arm
  * No venipuncture in AVF arm
  * No tight clothing/jewelry
  * Avoid sleeping on AVF arm
* **Exercise:** Hand exercises to promote maturation
* **Follow-up:** Vascular surgery clinic for maturation assessment

**Complications to Monitor:**
* **Early Thrombosis:** Loss of thrill/bruit
* **Excessive Bleeding:** Hematoma formation
* **Steal Syndrome:** Hand pain, coldness, weakness
* **Infection:** Less common with AVF vs graft

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Regional anesthesia for AVF creation provides not just analgesia but also sympatholysis-mediated vasodilation that may improve early maturation and long-term patency.

**Bottom Line:** AVF creation in ESRF patients requires specialized knowledge of renal failure physiology, careful drug selection accounting for altered pharmacokinetics, and preferential use of regional anesthesia to optimize both patient safety and fistula outcomes.

# 4.5. RADIATION SAFETY IN ENDOVASCULAR SURGERY
<!-- EPA-8:K3:remote_location_hazards EPA-8:K4:safety_guidelines EPA-8:EQ2:ir_safety -->

**Ionizing radiation exposure is an occupational hazard for anesthesiologists working in hybrid operating rooms and interventional suites, requiring understanding of radiation physics, biological effects, and protective strategies.**

## RADIATION PHYSICS AND DOSIMETRY

### RADIATION DOSE UNITS AND CONCEPTS

**Absorbed Dose:**
* **Unit:** Gray (Gy)
* **Definition:** Energy absorbed per unit mass (1 Joule / 1 kg = 1 Gy)
* **Measurement:** Direct tissue energy deposition

**Equivalent Dose:**
* **Unit:** Sievert (Sv) or millisievert (mSv)
* **Definition:** Absorbed dose × radiation weighting factor
* **Purpose:** Accounts for different biological effectiveness of radiation types
* **Organ-Specific:** Different organs have different sensitivities

**Effective Dose (WHO/IAEA Standard):**
* **Definition:** Whole-body sum of equivalent doses to different organs
* **Purpose:** Standardized risk assessment across different exposure patterns
* **Clinical Use:** Comparing radiation exposure from different procedures

### SCATTER RADIATION IN THE OPERATING THEATER

**Source:**
* **Primary Beam:** Directed at patient (controlled by radiologist/surgeon)
* **Scatter Radiation:** Radiation that bounces off patient and objects
  * Primary hazard to operating room personnel
  * Decreases with distance from source (inverse square law)

**Under-Couch Beam Systems:**
* X-ray tube positioned under patient table
* Scatter radiation greatest near patient and table
* Staff positioning critical for dose reduction

## RADIATION EXPOSURE LIMITS AND TYPICAL DOSES

### OCCUPATIONAL EXPOSURE LIMITS

**Annual Limits for Healthcare Workers:**
* **Whole Body:** 20 mSv/year (average over 5 years, not to exceed 50 mSv in any single year)
* **Eye/Lens:** 100 mSv per 5 years (maximum 50 mSv in any single year)
* **Skin:** 500 mSv/year (averaged over 1 cm² area)
* **Dosimetry Required:** If annual exposure likely to exceed 6 mSv

**Public (Non-Occupational) Limits:**
* **General Public:** 1 mSv/year = ~250 commercial flight hours
* **Pregnant Healthcare Workers (Fetal):** 1 mSv for entire pregnancy

### TYPICAL RADIATION EXPOSURES

**Procedural Exposures:**
* **TAAA (Thoracoabdominal Aortic Aneurysm):** ~0.17 mSv/case
* **Lower Limb Angioplasty:** ~3.5 mSv/patient
* **L-Spine Surgery:** ~1.5 mSv/patient (with fluoroscopy)
* **Hip Surgery:** ~0.7 mSv/patient (with fluoroscopy)

**Comparative Exposures:**
* **Chest X-Ray:** ~0.02 mSv/shot
* **CT Abdomen/Pelvis:** ~10 mSv/scan
* **One Hour in Sun:** ~0.00004 mSv
* **Acute Cancer-Causing Dose:** ~100 mSv (single exposure)

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** A single CTAP delivers more radiation than hundreds of TAAA repairs for the anesthesiologist - the scatter exposure to staff is minimal with proper technique.

## BIOLOGICAL EFFECTS OF IONIZING RADIATION

### MECHANISMS OF INJURY

* **Reactive Oxygen/Nitrogen Species:** Primary mechanism of cellular damage
* **DNA Damage:**
  * Direct strand breaks
  * Indirect damage via free radicals
  * Impaired DNA repair mechanisms
* **Cellular Effects:**
  * Cell death (apoptosis)
  * Mutations
  * Malignant transformation

### DETERMINISTIC (TISSUE REACTION) EFFECTS

**Characteristics:**
* Threshold dose required
* Severity increases with dose
* Occur within weeks to months
* Predictable response

**Specific Effects:**
* **Cataracts:** No safe threshold identified
  * Cumulative lifetime exposure
  * Develops years after exposure
  * Eye protection critical
* **Skin Effects:**
  * Erythema (redness)
  * Hair loss
  * Melanoma / Basal cell carcinoma
  * Threshold: varies by individual

### STOCHASTIC (PROBABILISTIC) EFFECTS

**Characteristics:**
* No threshold - any dose carries risk
* Probability (not severity) increases with dose
* Occur years to decades after exposure
* Unpredictable in individuals but quantifiable in populations

**Cancer Risks:**
* **Thyroid Cancer:** Especially with inadequate thyroid shielding
* **Brain Tumors:** Left-sided predominance in interventionalists (tube side)
* **Breast Cancer:** Especially in females
* **Leukemia:** Especially AML and myelodysplastic syndrome
* **Non-Hodgkin Lymphoma:** Increased risk

**Cardiovascular Effects:**
* **Accelerated Atherosclerosis:** CVA, myocardial infarction
* **Mechanism:** Endothelial damage and inflammation

**Population Data:**
* **Interventional Physicians:** ~20% increased cancer risk vs non-exposed physicians
* **Lifetime Risk:** Cumulative with repeated exposures

## PROTECTIVE EQUIPMENT AND SHIELDING

### LEAD APRONS

**Standard Specifications:**
* **Thickness:** 0.5 mm lead equivalent (blocks 99% when new)
* **Weight:** 2.5-6.5 kg
* **Coverage:** Front protection (most common), or 360° wrap-around

**Effectiveness and Limitations:**
* **Systematic Review (Gurumurthy et al):**
  * 60-70% of lead aprons are defective (cracks, holes)
  * Actually reduces exposure by only ~33% (vs theoretical 99%)
  * Annual inspection essential
* **Care and Maintenance:**
  * NEVER fold aprons (creates permanent cracks)
  * NEVER sit on aprons
  * Hang properly when not in use
  * Clean before use
  * Annual X-ray inspection for integrity

**Reducing Musculoskeletal Pain from Lead Aprons:**
* **Two-Piece Design:** Distributes weight more evenly (vest + skirt)
* **Proper Sizing:** Critical for comfort and effectiveness
* **Lumbar Belt Strapping:** Use provided straps to support lower back
* **Weight Management:** Personal weight loss reduces strain
* **DON'T Sit Down:** While wearing lead (increases spinal compression)
* **Lighter Alternatives:**
  * **0.25 mm Lead:** ~90% protection, significantly lighter
  * **Non-Lead Composite:** Antimony, bismuth, barium sulfate - comparable protection, 20-25% lighter

### THYROID SHIELDS

* **Essential Protection:** Thyroid highly radiosensitive
* **Design:** Wrap-around collar
* **Effectiveness:** Blocks >90% of scatter radiation to thyroid
* **Usage:** Should be worn whenever in fluoroscopy suite

### LEAD GLASSES / EYE PROTECTION

* **Indication:** Eye lens exposure limits are low (20 mSv/year averaged)
* **Design:** Side shields important (lateral scatter)
* **Effectiveness:** Reduces eye exposure by 90-95%
* **Consideration:** Cumbersome, may fog, prescription options available

### MOBILE LEAD SHIELDS

* **Floor-Mounted:** Rolling barrier between radiation source and staff
* **Ceiling-Mounted:** Suspended acrylic/lead shields
* **Effectiveness:** Standing 2 steps behind lead shield reduces exposure by 80%
* **Limitation:** Restricts movement and access

## RADIATION REDUCTION STRATEGIES - ALARA PRINCIPLE

**ALARA: As Low As Reasonably Achievable**

### TIME - MINIMIZE EXPOSURE DURATION

* **Preplan Procedures:** Imaging strategy determined before starting
* **Shorter Fluoroscopy Runs:** Use intermittent vs continuous fluoroscopy
* **Limit Exposure:** Turn off fluoroscopy when not actively needed
* **Communication:** Clear verbal warnings before X-ray activation ("shooting")
* **Procedure Skill:** Experienced operators = less fluoroscopy time

### DISTANCE - MAXIMIZE DISTANCE FROM SOURCE

**Inverse Square Law:**
* Radiation intensity ∝ 1/distance²
* **Example:** Doubling distance = ¼ radiation exposure

**Practical Applications:**
* **Patient Distance:** Primary radiation source
  * Patient should be >1 meter from anesthesia workstation/console
  * Unshielded personnel >3 meters from patient during fluoroscopy
* **Step Back:** Standing 5 cm away from table edge reduces exposure by 33%
* **Behind Shield:** Standing 2 steps behind mobile lead shield reduces exposure by 80%
* **Airway Management:** Position at head of bed (furthest from primary scatter)

### SHIELDING - BARRIERS BETWEEN SOURCE AND PERSONNEL

**Provided Equipment:**
* **Lead Apron:** Primary protection (wear always)
* **Thyroid Shield:** Essential (wear always)
* **Lead Glasses:** Strongly recommended
* **Mobile Shields:** Position between source and staff

**Facility Design:**
* **Hybrid OR Layout:** Anesthesia workstation positioned optimally
* **Control Area:** Radiation-shielded zones for unnecessary personnel

## GOOD RADIATION SAFETY PRACTICES

### BEFORE THE PROCEDURE

* **Dosimeter Placement:** Wear personal dosimeter at collar level (outside lead apron)
  * Measures unshielded exposure (represents eye/thyroid dose)
  * Alternative: wear under apron to measure body exposure
* **Area Dosimetry:** If personal dosimeters unavailable
* **Equipment Check:** Verify lead apron integrity, proper fit
* **Alternative Imaging:** Use ultrasound where possible instead of fluoroscopy

### DURING THE PROCEDURE

* **Verbal Warning:** "Shooting" or "X-ray" before activation
* **Maximum Distance:** Stand as far from patient as practical
* **Direct Tube Away:** Ensure X-ray tube directed away from anesthesia provider
* **Image Receptor Side:** Stand on detector/image receptor side when possible (opposite tube)
* **Collimation:** Request tight collimation (smaller field = less scatter)
* **Patient Immobilization:** By shielded relative, not staff
* **Hand Protection:** NEVER image own hands - use instruments
* **Shield Use:** Position mobile shields optimally
* **Reduce Receptor Distance:** Minimize distance from patient to image detector

### DOSE MONITORING AND LIMITS

* **Maximum Dose Reached:** Leave room if occupational limit approached
* **Pregnancy:** Immediate notification and dose reassessment
  * Fetal limit: 1 mSv entire pregnancy
  * May require temporary reassignment
* **Documentation:** Log procedures and estimated exposures

### IF CT FLUOROSCOPY USED

* **Higher Doses:** CT fluoroscopy delivers significantly higher doses than conventional
* **Mandatory Apron:** Lead apron absolutely required
* **Clear Room:** Only essential personnel present

## SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS

### PREGNANCY

* **Notification:** Inform radiation safety officer and supervisors
* **Fetal Limit:** 1 mSv entire pregnancy (same as general public annual limit)
* **Monitoring:** More frequent dosimetry
* **Work Modification:** May require temporary reassignment from high-exposure procedures
* **Lead Apron:** Additional abdominal shielding

### CUMULATIVE CAREER EXPOSURE

* **Track Lifetime Dose:** Some institutions provide cumulative tracking
* **Career Planning:** Consider total exposure over career
* **Dose Records:** Keep personal records of dosimetry results
* **Alternative Roles:** Consider rotation through lower-exposure duties

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The three principles of radiation protection are TIME, DISTANCE, and SHIELDING - but distance is often the most practical and effective for anesthesiologists. Simply stepping back an additional meter can reduce exposure by 75%.

**Bottom Line:** Radiation safety in endovascular surgery requires understanding of radiation physics, consistent use of protective equipment, and adherence to ALARA principles. While individual procedure doses are low, cumulative career exposure necessitates vigilant protective practices to minimize long-term cancer and cataract risks.

# 5.1. PERIOPERATIVE COMPLICATIONS

**Vascular surgery patients are at elevated risk for multiple perioperative complications due to advanced age, extensive comorbidities, and complex procedures requiring sophisticated management strategies.**

## CARDIOVASCULAR COMPLICATIONS

### MAJOR ADVERSE CARDIAC EVENTS (MACE)

* **Incidence:** ~10% demonstrate significant myocardial injury; ~2% suffer MACE
* **Mortality Impact:** 15-25% in-hospital mortality for perioperative MI patients
* **Prognostic Significance:** Nonfatal perioperative MI is independent predictor for cardiovascular death within 6 months
* **Prevention Strategy:** Comprehensive preoperative optimization and perioperative monitoring

### HEMODYNAMIC INSTABILITY

* **Aortic Surgery:** Most dramatic with supraceliac cross-clamping (up to 50% MAP increase)
* **Management Priorities:**
  * Anticipation and preparation
  * Short-acting vasoactive agents
  * Clear surgical team communication
  * Gradual clamp release protocols

### MYOCARDIAL ISCHEMIA AND INFARCTION

* **Risk Factors:** Pre-existing CAD, hemodynamic lability, prolonged procedures
* **Detection:** Continuous ECG monitoring, serial cardiac biomarkers
* **Management:** Immediate cardiology consultation, optimization of medical therapy

## NEUROLOGICAL COMPLICATIONS

### STROKE IN CAROTID SURGERY

* **Historical Incidence:** ~6% postoperative stroke following CEA
* **Modern Incidence:** ~1% due to improved management
* **Key Prevention Strategies:**
  1. Improved perioperative medication management
  2. Perioperative neurophysiologic monitoring
  3. Perioperative hemodynamic control

### CEREBRAL HYPERPERFUSION SYNDROME (CHS)

* **Incidence:** Up to 3% of CEA cases
* **Pathophysiology:** Impaired cerebral autoregulation following high-grade stenosis relief
* **Clinical Manifestations:**
  * Ipsilateral cerebral edema
  * Headache, seizures
  * Focal neurologic deficits
  * Intracerebral hemorrhage
* **Management:** 
  * Pharmacologic blood pressure control
  * Early recognition critical
  * Complete recovery possible with prompt treatment

### SPINAL CORD ISCHEMIA

* **Incidence:** Up to 10% for distal aortic repair
* **Risk Factors:**
  * Previous aortic surgery
  * Cross-clamp level and duration
  * Intraoperative hypotension
* **Prevention:**
  * Short cross-clamp times
  * Maintain adequate perfusion pressures
  * CSF drainage for complex repairs

## RESPIRATORY COMPLICATIONS

### POST-CEA RESPIRATORY ISSUES

* **Multiple Etiologies:**
  * Underlying pulmonary disease
  * Recurrent laryngeal nerve injury
  * Hypoglossal nerve injury
  * Neck hematoma
  * Altered carotid body function
* **Management:**
  * Supplemental oxygen
  * Avoid respiratory depressants
  * Serial neurological assessments

### POST-AORTIC SURGERY RESPIRATORY FAILURE

* **Risk Factors:** Pre-existing COPD, prolonged surgery, fluid overload
* **Prevention:** Epidural analgesia, early mobilization, pulmonary hygiene
* **Treatment:** Mechanical ventilation, bronchodilators, diuresis as appropriate

## RENAL COMPLICATIONS

### ACUTE KIDNEY INJURY

* **Incidence:** 
  * Infrarenal cross-clamping: ~5%
  * Suprarenal cross-clamping: ~13%
* **Risk Factors:** Pre-existing renal dysfunction, contrast exposure, prolonged hypotension
* **Prevention:** 
  * Minimize ischemia time
  * Maintain adequate perfusion
  * Limit contrast exposure
  * Preoperative hydration

### CONTRAST-INDUCED NEPHROPATHY

* **High-Risk Patients:** Pre-existing renal insufficiency, diabetes, advanced age
* **Prevention Strategies:**
  * Pre-procedure hydration
  * Minimize contrast volume
  * Consider alternative agents (gadolinium, CO2)
  * N-acetyl cysteine, sodium bicarbonate protocols

## WOUND AND ACCESS SITE COMPLICATIONS

### NECK HEMATOMA POST-CEA

* **Incidence:** Up to 2% of patients
* **Classification:**
  * Small hematomas: Conservative management
  * ⚠️ **Rapidly expanding:** Clinical emergency requiring immediate intervention
* **Emergency Management:**
  * Immediate evaluation
  * Difficult airway equipment available
  * Best managed in operating room

### ACCESS SITE COMPLICATIONS FOR ENDOVASCULAR PROCEDURES

* **Hematoma Formation:** Most common complication
* **Vascular Injury:** Dissection, pseudoaneurysm formation
* **Infection:** Risk increases with duration of instrumentation
* **Management:** 
  * Compression for minor bleeding
  * Surgical repair for major complications
  * Antibiotic therapy for infections

# 5.2. LONG-TERM MANAGEMENT AND SURVEILLANCE [PART B/C]

**Vascular surgery patients require comprehensive long-term management with particular attention to endovascular repair surveillance and medical optimization.**

## ENDOVASCULAR SURVEILLANCE REQUIREMENTS

### EVAR-SPECIFIC SURVEILLANCE

* **Lifetime Requirement:** Mandatory surveillance due to unique complication profile
* **Imaging Modalities:** CT angiography, ultrasound, plain radiographs
* **Surveillance Schedule:**
  * 1 month post-procedure
  * 6 months
  * Annually thereafter
  * More frequent if complications detected

### ENDOLEAK MONITORING AND MANAGEMENT

* **Detection Methods:** CT angiography most sensitive
* **Management Algorithm:**
  * Type I and III: Require intervention
  * Type II: Observation vs intervention based on sac behavior
  * Type IV: Usually self-limited
  * Type V: Often requires open conversion

### REINTERVENTION PLANNING

* **Higher Rate Than Open Surgery:** 29.6% vs 18.1%
* **Types of Interventions:**
  * Endovascular procedures (majority)
  * Open conversion (when endovascular options exhausted)
* **Patient Education:** Important regarding need for lifelong surveillance

## MEDICAL MANAGEMENT OPTIMIZATION

### CARDIOVASCULAR RISK FACTOR MODIFICATION

* **Antiplatelet Therapy:** Long-term management essential
* **Statin Therapy:** Continue indefinitely
* **Blood Pressure Control:** Target appropriate levels
* **Diabetes Management:** Optimal glycemic control
* **Smoking Cessation:** Critical for long-term outcomes

### SECONDARY PREVENTION STRATEGIES

* **Cardiac Monitoring:** Regular cardiology follow-up
* **Medication Adherence:** Patient education and monitoring
* **Lifestyle Modifications:** Diet, exercise, weight management
* **Regular Assessment:** Annual comprehensive evaluations

## QUALITY IMPROVEMENT AND SYSTEMS-BASED PRACTICE

### OUTCOME MONITORING

* **Database Participation:** Vascular Quality Initiative, NSQIP
* **Outcome Metrics:** Mortality, morbidity, reintervention rates
* **Continuous Quality Improvement:** Regular review and protocol updates

### MULTIDISCIPLINARY CARE COORDINATION

* **Team Approach:** Surgeons, anesthesiologists, cardiologists, nephrologists
* **Communication Systems:** Clear handoff protocols
* **Patient Safety:** Standardized protocols and checklists

### EVIDENCE-BASED PRACTICE INTEGRATION

* **Literature Review:** Regular assessment of new evidence
* **Protocol Updates:** Based on latest guidelines and research
* **Education:** Continuous professional development

**Bottom Line:** Vascular surgery complications require systematic approaches to prevention, early recognition, and management. Long-term success depends on comprehensive surveillance programs, optimal medical management, and multidisciplinary care coordination within a systems-based practice framework.
