## 1. MRI ANAESTHESIA AND SAFETY

**The MRI environment presents unique safety challenges for anaesthesia providers due to the powerful static magnetic field, radiofrequency fields, and restricted patient access.**

### 1.1. MRI PHYSICS AND HAZARDS

#### 1.1.1. MAGNETIC FIELD CHARACTERISTICS

**The static magnetic field (B0) of an MRI scanner is always on, even between scans, requiring constant vigilance** [AAGBI 2019]:

* **Field strength**: Clinical MRI scanners typically operate at 1.5T or 3T (30,000-60,000 times Earth's magnetic field)
* **Fringe field**: The magnetic field extends beyond the scanner bore; the 5 Gauss (0.5 mT) line has historically been the "pacemaker line" for safety
* **Recent update**: IEC standard revised fringe field limit to 9 Gauss (0.9 mT) [ACR 2024]
* **Key principle**: The magnetic field attracts ferromagnetic objects and accelerates them toward the center of the bore

#### 1.1.2. SIX PRIMARY MRI HAZARDS

**Ferromagnetic objects interacting with MRI fields create six distinct dangers** [OpenAnesthesia]:

| Hazard | Mechanism | Clinical Consequence |
|--------|-----------|---------------------|
| **Projectile effect** | Ferromagnetic objects become missiles | Injury or death to patient/staff |
| **Torque/Twisting** | Rotation of metallic implants | Tissue damage, implant displacement |
| **Thermal injury** | RF heating of metallic objects | Burns to patient |
| **Imaging artifacts** | Metal distorts magnetic field | Non-diagnostic images |
| **Device malfunction** | Magnetic field affects electronics | Pacemaker failure, pump malfunction |
| **Acoustic injury** | Gradient coils produce >90dB noise | Hearing damage |

**💡 Clinical Pearl**: Even small ferromagnetic objects become dangerous projectiles capable of injuring or killing anyone in their path. Deaths have been reported from oxygen cylinders, floor polishers, and other equipment entering Zone IV.

### 1.2. ACR SAFETY ZONE CLASSIFICATION

**The American College of Radiology defines four safety zones to manage magnetic field exposure and protect personnel and patients** [ACR 2024]:

#### 1.2.1. ZONE DEFINITIONS

| Zone | Description | Access | Anaesthetic Relevance |
|------|-------------|--------|----------------------|
| **Zone I** | Public areas freely accessible | Unrestricted | Patient waiting areas; no special equipment restrictions |
| **Zone II** | Interface between public and controlled zones | Supervised | Patient screening occurs here; complete safety questionnaire |
| **Zone III** | Restricted area with hazardous fields | Screened personnel and patients only | Pre-anaesthetic assessment; airway equipment preparation |
| **Zone IV** | The MRI scanner room itself | Constant direct supervision required | All anaesthetic care delivered here; strict equipment protocols |

#### 1.2.2. ZONE-SPECIFIC REQUIREMENTS

**Zone I**:
* All areas freely accessible to the general public without supervision
* Magnetic fringe fields <9 Gauss (0.9 mT)
* Standard equipment can be used

**Zone II**:
* Interface between public zone I and controlled zones III/IV
* Patient and staff screening must occur here
* MR safety questionnaire completed and documented
* Identification of all implants and devices

**Zone III**:
* **Area where fringe, gradient, or RF magnetic fields are sufficiently strong to present physical hazards** to unscreened patients and personnel [ACR 2024]
* Free access by unscreened persons or ferromagnetic objects can result in serious injury or death
* All access strictly restricted to screened individuals
* Resuscitation equipment staged here

**Zone IV**:
* **Always located within Zone III; houses the MRI scanner** [ACR 2024]
* Highest field strength and greatest risk
* All ferromagnetic objects must be excluded
* Patient under constant direct supervision of trained personnel
* ⚠️ **The MR Projectile Area surrounds the scanner where ferromagnetic objects become projectiles**

### 1.3. MRI DEVICE SAFETY CLASSIFICATION

#### 1.3.1. TERMINOLOGY (ASTM/FDA STANDARD)

**All equipment must be classified according to MR safety status** [AAGBI 2019]:

| Classification | Definition | Zone IV Entry |
|----------------|------------|---------------|
| **MR Safe** | Poses no known additional MR-related hazards in any MR environment | Always permitted |
| **MR Conditional** | Poses no known hazards in a specified MR environment under specific conditions of use | Permitted with documented conditions met |
| **MR Unsafe** | Poses hazards in all MR environments | Never permitted |

**MR Conditional conditions may include**:
* Static field strength limits (e.g., ≤3T)
* Spatial gradient limits
* Rate of change of magnetic field (dB/dt)
* Specific absorption rate (SAR) limits
* Equipment configuration requirements

⚠️ **Safety Warning**: The terms "MRI compatible" and "MRI safe" are outdated and should not be used. Only use the standardized ASTM/FDA terminology [ACR 2024].

#### 1.3.2. ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATIONS TO MRI

**Items prohibiting MRI entry** [OpenAnesthesia, AAGBI 2019]:

* **Cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs)** - non-MR conditional pacemakers, ICDs
* **Neuromodulation implants** - deep brain stimulators, spinal cord stimulators (unless MR conditional)
* **Medication infusion pumps** - standard pumps with ferromagnetic components
* **Cochlear implants** (older models)
* **Intraocular metallic foreign bodies** - risk of movement and blindness
* **Swan-Ganz catheters** with thermistor wires
* **Ferromagnetic intracranial aneurysm clips** - risk of clip displacement and hemorrhage
* **Retained foreign bodies** - bullets, shrapnel, welding debris in critical locations

#### 1.3.3. RELATIVE CONTRAINDICATIONS (REQUIRE MANUFACTURER REVIEW)

**Items requiring manufacturer-specific protocols before proceeding**:

* Coronary and peripheral stents (most modern stents are MR conditional after 6 weeks)
* Programmable CSF shunts (require reprogramming post-scan)
* Metallic tracheostomy tubes
* Wire-reinforced epidural catheters
* Vagal nerve stimulators
* Intrauterine devices (IUDs)
* Surgical clips and wires
* Joint prostheses
* Recent tattoos (<6 weeks) - risk of heating
* Body piercings (must be removed if ferromagnetic)

### 1.4. MRI-COMPATIBLE MONITORING EQUIPMENT

**Monitoring in the MR unit must conform to the same standards as in the operating theatre** [BJA Education]:

#### 1.4.1. ELECTROCARDIOGRAPHY

**ECG monitoring in MRI presents significant challenges due to electrical interference**:

* **Problem**: Current induction in leads causes spikes, T-wave amplitude changes, and ST-segment distortion
* **Clinical impact**: Arrhythmias and ischaemic changes difficult to detect
* **Solutions**:
  * Use high-impedance, braided, short leads
  * MR-safe electrodes placed in a narrow triangle on chest
  * Position electrodes close together for high amplitude signal
  * ⚠️ **Wired leads should not be used as conductive metal can heat and burn** [OpenAnesthesia]
* **Baseline ECG**: Obtain 12-lead ECG before MRI if ST changes or arrhythmias suspected

#### 1.4.2. PULSE OXIMETRY

**MR-safe pulse oximeters are preferable** [BJA Education]:

* Susceptible to magnetic field interference
* May display inaccurate readings or complete signal loss
* Fibreoptic pulse oximeters reduce burn risk from electrical conductors
* Probe placement should avoid creating conductive loops

#### 1.4.3. CAPNOGRAPHY

**Essential but technically challenging in MRI**:

* Long sampling lines increase time delay up to 20 seconds
* Trend monitoring acceptable despite accuracy limitations
* Side-stream sampling required; mainstream sensors often MR unsafe
* **💡 Clinical Pearl**: Despite delays, capnography remains the most reliable respiratory monitor in MRI where visual patient assessment is limited

#### 1.4.4. BLOOD PRESSURE MONITORING

**Non-invasive arterial pressure monitoring requirements**:

* Change ferrous connections to nylon
* Oscillometric systems appropriate
* Cuff inflation may disturb lightly sedated patients (consider longer intervals if stable)

**Invasive monitoring**:
* Minimize pressure line length to reduce damping
* Use MR-safe transducers and tubing
* Position transducers appropriately for Zone IV

#### 1.4.5. TEMPERATURE MONITORING

* **Thermistor and thermocouple probes can show falsely elevated readings** due to direct heating from RF fields
* Fibreoptic temperature sensors are MR safe
* Monitor for both hypothermia (children, prolonged scans) and hyperthermia (pediatric patients with long imaging duration)

### 1.5. MRI-COMPATIBLE ANAESTHETIC EQUIPMENT

#### 1.5.1. ANAESTHETIC MACHINES AND VENTILATORS

**MR-conditional anaesthetic machines and ventilators are available** [AAGBI 2019]:

* Can be sited adjacent to magnet bore (determined by individual magnet field)
* Minimizes breathing system length for optimal ventilation
* **Only MR-Safe vaporizers and gas cylinders should be used within the scanning room**
* Example: GE Aespire MRI machine includes integrated Gauss Alarm warning when moved too close to magnet
* ⚠️ **Deaths have occurred from expiratory limb occlusion in long circle systems causing tension pneumothorax** [AAGBI 2019]

#### 1.5.2. INFUSION PUMPS

**Standard infusion pumps are strongly ferromagnetic, become projectiles, and malfunction near magnetic fields**:

* **MR-Safe pumps available** (e.g., IRadimed MRidium) operate up to 10,000 Gauss line on 3T scanners
* Cannot administer complex sedation regimens or target-controlled infusion (TCI)
* **Alternative "workaround" techniques are dangerous**:
  * Running long lines from conventional pumps through sealed MRI door/waveguide
  * ⚠️ **Deaths reported from uncontrolled propofol infusions without volumetric pumps** [AAGBI 2019]
* MR-safe syringe drivers provide safer alternative for sedation infusions

#### 1.5.3. AIRWAY EQUIPMENT

**Airway equipment in Zone III/IV must be MR-safe or MR-conditional** [AAGBI 2019]:

* MR-safe laryngoscopes (fibreoptic or disposable plastic)
* MR-conditional video laryngoscopes (verify field strength limits)
* Standard laryngeal mask airways (ensure no reinforcement wire)
* MR-safe endotracheal tubes (avoid wire-reinforced tubes unless verified)
* Suction with MR-safe components

**💡 Clinical Pearl**: To avoid confusion during emergencies, airway equipment immediately available in Zone III should be MR-conditional for all scanners in the location [AAGBI 2019].

### 1.6. RADIOFREQUENCY (RF) BURNS

**Three mechanisms cause RF-induced thermal injury** [OpenAnesthesia]:

#### 1.6.1. BURN MECHANISMS

| Mechanism | Description | Prevention |
|-----------|-------------|------------|
| **Inductive heating** | Changing magnetic fields generate electrical currents in conductors | Minimize conductive loops |
| **Resonant loops** | Coiled wires near patients create voltage and heat | Pad between skin and wires |
| **Antenna effect** | Elongated conductors (ECG leads) heat dangerously | Use shortest possible leads |

#### 1.6.2. PATIENT POSITIONING TO PREVENT BURNS

**Critical positioning requirements**:

* **No skin-to-skin contact** (ankles touching, hand-to-hip) - can cause bilateral burns
* Pad between patient and any conductive material
* Remove all conductive loops from patient
* Ensure monitoring cables exit scanner bore without looping
* Verify no contact between patient skin and scanner bore

⚠️ **Safety Warning**: Even seemingly minor skin-to-skin contact can create conductive paths resulting in serious burns during RF pulses.

### 1.7. CARDIAC ARREST IN MRI - EMERGENCY PROTOCOL

**Advanced resuscitation (defibrillation) is NOT possible in Zone IV** [ASA 2015, OpenAnesthesia]:

#### 1.7.1. ASA 2015 THREE-STEP EMERGENCY PROTOCOL

| Step | Action | Details |
|------|--------|---------|
| **1** | **Immediate patient removal from Zone IV** | Begin CPR concurrently during removal |
| **2** | **Call for help** | Summon emergency response team |
| **3** | **Resuscitate in designated area** | Area should be as close to Zone IV as possible |

#### 1.7.2. RESUSCITATION AREA REQUIREMENTS

**The designated resuscitation area should contain**:

* Advanced cardiac monitors
* Supplemental oxygen delivery systems
* Complete intubation equipment (MR-safe in Zone III)
* Functional defibrillator
* Full code cart with resuscitation drugs
* All equipment positioned outside Zone IV

**💡 Clinical Pearl**: The resuscitation area should be established BEFORE starting any MRI anaesthetic, with equipment checked and readily accessible. Staff should know the exact location and evacuation route.

#### 1.7.3. RATIONALE FOR PATIENT REMOVAL

**Why patients must be removed from Zone IV for resuscitation**:

* **Defibrillators cannot function in Zone IV** due to ferromagnetic interference and magnetic field effects on internal components
* **Prevents introduction of hazardous ferromagnetic materials** by resuscitation team who may bring standard equipment
* **Enables full resuscitation capabilities** including defibrillation, invasive procedures, and advanced airway management
* ⚠️ **Time is critical**: Practice evacuation drills to minimize delay to defibrillation

#### 1.7.4. MRI-CONDITIONAL DEFIBRILLATORS

**Recent developments in MRI emergency response**:

* MR-conditional external defibrillators are in development for resuscitation within the scanner bore
* Currently, standard defibrillators remain contraindicated in Zone IV
* **Current best practice**: Rapid evacuation to Zone III resuscitation area remains the standard of care

### 1.8. EMERGENCY QUENCH PROCEDURE

#### 1.8.1. DEFINITION AND MECHANISM

**Quench**: Emergency shutdown procedure that rapidly expels liquid helium from the superconducting magnet coils, dissipating the magnetic field.

* Liquid helium maintains superconductivity of magnet coils at near absolute zero temperatures
* During quench, helium rapidly boils off and is vented (ideally through external ducting)
* Magnetic field dissipates over seconds to minutes

#### 1.8.2. HAZARDS OF QUENCHING

| Hazard | Mechanism | Consequence |
|--------|-----------|-------------|
| **Asphyxiation** | Helium displaces atmospheric oxygen | Hypoxia, death if room not evacuated |
| **Cold injury** | Rapid helium expulsion causes extreme cooling | Frostbite to personnel/patients |
| **Liquid oxygen formation** | Air condenses on cold surfaces | Fire hazard - any liquid on cold surfaces must be presumed to be liquid oxygen |
| **Equipment damage** | Rapid demagnetization | Expensive scanner repair required |

#### 1.8.3. QUENCH ACTIVATION CRITERIA

**Only activate quench for life-threatening emergencies** [AAGBI 2019]:

* Fire in the scanner room
* Personnel pinned to scanner by ferromagnetic object that cannot be removed
* Patient trapped in bore who cannot be safely removed

⚠️ **Quench is expensive and potentially dangerous** - MRI scanners cannot be easily shut down, and quenching should never be performed for non-life-threatening situations.

#### 1.8.4. POST-QUENCH RESPONSE

**Immediate actions if quench occurs**:

1. **Evacuate all staff and patients immediately** from Zone IV
2. **Make oxygen available** for affected individuals
3. **Do not re-enter room** until atmospheric monitoring confirms safe oxygen levels
4. **Treat any liquid on cold surfaces as liquid oxygen** (fire hazard)
5. **Report incident** according to institutional protocols

### 1.9. AAGBI/ASSOCIATION OF ANAESTHETISTS GUIDELINES (2019)

#### 1.9.1. STAFFING REQUIREMENTS

**All hospitals providing MRI anaesthesia should have a lead anaesthetist** [AAGBI 2019]:

* Responsible for MRI anaesthesia delivery and governance
* Whenever possible, experienced consultants should provide anaesthesia
* **Inexperienced staff unfamiliar with MR environment should NOT manage patients**, particularly out-of-hours
* Anesthetist should be supported throughout by a suitably skilled anesthetic assistant familiar with MR requirements

#### 1.9.2. PATIENT SCREENING AND SAFETY

**Mandatory screening requirements**:

* **All patients and staff must be screened** for implants and devices that may contraindicate safe scanning
* Screening should be collaborative between referring team, MR-responsible person, and anaesthetist
* **MRI should only be undertaken if diagnostic benefit outweighs risk** - requires multidisciplinary team discussion for complex patients

#### 1.9.3. SAFETY CHECKLISTS

**MR safety checklists should be used in conjunction with WHO checklist** [AAGBI 2019]:

* MR safety checklist and WHO theatre checklist have different functions
* Both should be completed with relevant teams
* Checklists available for:
  * General anaesthesia
  * Intraoperative MRI
  * Transfer of ICU patients

#### 1.9.4. HEARING PROTECTION

**Anyone remaining in scanning room during scanning should be provided with ear protection** [AAGBI 2019]:

* Gradient coils produce noise exceeding 90dB during scanning
* Both patients and staff require protection
* Options include MR-safe headphones, earplugs, or noise-cancelling systems
* Special consideration for sedated patients who cannot report discomfort

### 1.10. ANAESTHETIC TECHNIQUE CONSIDERATIONS

#### 1.10.1. GOALS OF ANAESTHESIA FOR MRI

**The aims of anaesthesia in MRI are** [BJA Education]:

1. **Provide immobility** to obtain the best possible images
2. **Maintain patient safety** in a challenging environment
3. **Ensure patient comfort** throughout the procedure

#### 1.10.2. PATIENT SELECTION CONSIDERATIONS

**Factors determining anaesthetic technique**:

| Factor | GA Favored | Sedation May Be Appropriate |
|--------|------------|----------------------------|
| Age | Young children | Cooperative adults |
| Scan duration | >30-45 minutes | Short scans |
| Body region | Body/spine (breath holds) | Brain (no movement artifact) |
| Patient factors | Claustrophobia, cognitive impairment | Motivated, anxious-only patients |
| Medical status | Unstable, airway concerns | Stable, no airway issues |

#### 1.10.3. SPECIAL CHALLENGES IN MRI ANAESTHESIA

**Environmental challenges**:

* **Limited patient visibility** when positioned in scanner bore
* **Restricted access** - cannot reach patient during scan
* **Communication difficulties** due to high scanner noise
* **Remote monitoring** via window, cameras, and audio systems
* **Equipment limitations** - standard equipment cannot be used

**Clinical responses**:

* **Thorough pre-scan preparation** - ensure all equipment, lines, and monitoring secure
* **Plan for interruption** - know how quickly patient can be removed for emergency
* **Video monitoring** - maintain visual contact throughout scan
* **Intercom systems** - two-way communication with patient (if sedated) and scanner operator
* **💡 Clinical Pearl**: Invest extra time in setup and positioning before starting the scan - problems are much harder to address once scanning begins

#### 1.10.4. TEMPERATURE MANAGEMENT

**Temperature regulation concerns** [BJA Education]:

* **Young children and neonates** experience significant hypothermia under anaesthesia in cool MRI environment
* **Longer imaging duration** correlates with hyperthermia in pediatric patients (RF heating effects)
* Active warming devices must be MR-safe (forced air warmers with appropriate hoses)
* Temperature monitoring recommended for all pediatric patients and prolonged adult scans

### 1.11. PRE-MRI ANAESTHETIC CHECKLIST

**Recommended checklist elements prior to MRI anaesthesia**:

**Patient Factors**:
- [ ] MR safety questionnaire completed and reviewed
- [ ] All implants and devices documented with MR safety status confirmed
- [ ] Surgical history reviewed for potential retained materials
- [ ] Occupational history (welding, metalwork) assessed
- [ ] Standard pre-anaesthetic assessment completed
- [ ] Informed consent including MRI-specific risks

**Equipment Verification**:
- [ ] All monitoring equipment MR-safe/conditional and functional
- [ ] Anaesthetic machine checked (MR-conditional)
- [ ] Airway equipment MR-safe and appropriate sizes available
- [ ] IV access established with MR-safe extension tubing
- [ ] Emergency drugs drawn up and available in Zone III
- [ ] Resuscitation area prepared with defibrillator accessible

**Environment**:
- [ ] All ferromagnetic objects removed from patient
- [ ] All ferromagnetic objects removed from accompanying staff
- [ ] Patient positioning prevents skin-to-skin contact
- [ ] Monitoring cables routed to prevent loops
- [ ] Hearing protection applied
- [ ] Communication system tested

**Emergency Preparedness**:
- [ ] Evacuation route confirmed
- [ ] Resuscitation area location known to all team members
- [ ] Quench button location known (but not for routine use)
- [ ] Emergency contact numbers available

**Bottom Line:** MRI anaesthesia requires comprehensive understanding of magnetic field hazards, strict adherence to zone protocols, exclusive use of MR-safe/conditional equipment, and robust emergency preparedness including patient evacuation plans for cardiac arrest. The AAGBI 2019 guidelines and ACR safety zone classification provide the framework for safe practice.

[Sources: BJA Education 2017, AAGBI Guidelines 2019, ACR Manual on MR Safety 2024, ASA Practice Advisory 2015]
