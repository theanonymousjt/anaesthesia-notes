# 2. COMPONENTS OF RESUSCITATION


## 2.1. ABCDE FRAMEWORK
**The ABCDE framework provides a structured approach to assessment and management of critically ill patients.**

* **Components**: Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Drugs, and Electrical therapy
* **Traditional divisions**:
	* *Basic Life Support (BLS)*: Basic airway management, rescue breathing, manual chest compressions
	* *Advanced Cardiac Life Support (ACLS)*: Advanced interventions beyond BLS
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: The foundation of proficient ACLS is immediate and exquisite BLS
* **BLS evolution**:
	* Includes public-access automatic external defibrillators (AEDs)
	* Focuses on uninterrupted, effective chest compressions (improves outcomes more than advanced therapies)

**Bottom Line:** The ABCDE framework provides an essential systematic approach to resuscitation, with high-quality BLS forming the foundation of successful ACLS interventions.

# 3. AIRWAY MANAGEMENT


## 3.1. BASIC AIRWAY TECHNIQUES
**Non-instrumental techniques to establish and maintain a patent airway.**

* **Head tilt–chin lift**: Primary recommended method
	* Extend head by pressure on brow
	* Pull mandible forward to lift tongue from posterior pharynx
* **Jaw thrust maneuver**: Effective alternative
	* Apply pressure behind rami of mandible
	* Preferred in suspected cervical spine injury

**Bottom Line:** Basic airway maneuvers are the first critical step in airway management during resuscitation, creating a patent airway through simple manual techniques without equipment.

## 3.2. AIRWAY ADJUNCTS
**Devices that assist in maintaining airway patency or facilitating ventilation during resuscitation.**

* **Oropharyngeal/nasopharyngeal airways**:
	* Relieve upper airway obstruction by displacing tongue from posterior pharynx
	* ⚠️ Risk of inducing vomiting or laryngospasm in semiconscious patients
* **Supraglottic airways** (e.g., laryngeal mask, King airway):
	* Similar outcomes to tracheal intubation in resuscitation scenarios
	* Easier insertion with less training required
	* Allow ventilation without interrupting compressions
* **Tracheal intubation**:
	* Provides best airway control
	* Prevents aspiration
	* Allows most effective ventilation
	* ⚠️ Should not interfere with chest compressions unless absolutely necessary
* **Emergency Front of Neck Access (eFONA)**:
	* Indicated when other methods fail (Cannot Intubate, Cannot Oxygenate - CICO)
	* Includes tracheotomy or cricothyroidotomy
	* 🔑 **HY**: Anesthesiologists must understand surgical airway management fundamentals

**Bottom Line:** Airway adjuncts range from simple devices that displace the tongue to advanced techniques like tracheal intubation, with device selection based on provider skill, patient status, and resuscitation circumstances.

## 3.3. FOREIGN-BODY AIRWAY OBSTRUCTION (FBAO)
**Acute obstruction of the airway by aspirated material requiring immediate intervention.**

* **Epidemiology**: ~5,000 US deaths (2020); common in elderly and young children
	* Most occur during eating (meat)
	* Risk factors: Poorly chewed food, poor dentition/dentures, elevated blood alcohol
* **Assessment**:
	* *Complete obstruction*: No air movement despite effort, inability to speak/cough
	* *Partial obstruction*: Rasping/wheezing respirations with coughing
	* If good air movement/forceful cough → no intervention needed
	* If cough weakens or cyanosis develops → treat as complete obstruction
* **Management techniques**:
	* **Back blows**: Forceful blows between scapulae over thoracic spine
	* **Abdominal thrusts (Heimlich maneuver)**:
		* *Awake victim*: Fist in epigastrium (between xiphoid/umbilicus) from behind
		* *Unconscious victim*: Heel of hand in epigastrium while kneeling astride
		* ⚠️ Avoid xiphoid pressure; thrust midline
	* **Sternal thrusts**: Alternative for obese or pregnant victims
	* 🏥 **Clinical Practice**: Repeat attempts may work as hypoxia causes muscle relaxation
	* ⚠️ **Complications**: Liver/spleen laceration, gastric rupture, fractured ribs, regurgitation
	* **eFONA**: May be indicated if other maneuvers fail

**Bottom Line:** FBAO requires rapid recognition and appropriate intervention with back blows and abdominal/chest thrusts, progressing to invasive techniques if necessary, with special considerations for different patient populations.

# 4. VENTILATION



## 4.1. PHYSIOLOGY OF VENTILATION DURING CPR
**Physiological principles governing gas exchange during cardiopulmonary resuscitation.**

* **Without endotracheal tube (ETT)**:
	* Gas distribution depends on lung vs. stomach impedance
	* *Esophageal opening pressure*: ~20 cm H₂O
	* Keep inspiratory pressures low to avoid gastric insufflation
* **Consequences of gastric insufflation**:
	* Impedes ventilation
	* Increases risk of regurgitation and gastric rupture
	* Primarily caused by partial airway obstruction increasing airway pressure
* **Recommended ventilation parameters**:
	* Tidal volume: 0.5-0.6 L (enough for noticeable chest rise)
	* Rescue breath duration: 1 second each
	* Given during pause in chest compressions (if no advanced airway)

**Bottom Line:** During CPR, ventilation requires careful balance between adequate tidal volume and avoiding gastric insufflation, with parameters adjusted to maximize oxygenation while minimizing interruptions to compressions.

## 4.2. TECHNIQUES OF RESCUE BREATHING
**Methods to provide artificial ventilation in the absence of spontaneous breathing.**

* **Mouth-to-mouth**:
	* Maintain open airway (head tilt-jaw lift)
	* Pinch nose, seal mouth, exhale (watch for chest rise)
	* Remove mouth for exhalation (listen for escaping air)
* **Mouth-to-nose**:
	* Surround nose with lips, hold victim's lips closed
	* May need mouth open for exhalation
* **Ventilation schedule**:
	* Adults & 1-rescuer child CPR: 2 breaths after 30 compressions (30:2)
	* Two-rescuer child CPR: 2 breaths after 15 compressions (15:2)

**Bottom Line:** Rescue breathing techniques deliver oxygen and remove CO2 through direct ventilation methods, with specific protocols for different patient populations and numbers of rescuers.

## 4.3. VENTILATION ADJUNCTS
**Devices that assist in providing artificial ventilation during CPR.**

* **Face mask**:
	* Held with thumbs/index fingers; other fingers apply jaw thrust
	* One-way valve masks direct exhaled gas away
* **Self-inflating resuscitation bag and mask (BVM)**:
	* Advantages: Non-contact, allows supplemental oxygen
	* 🏥 **Clinical Practice**: Difficult for single rescuer to use effectively
	* 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: Two rescuers recommended (one holds mask/airway, one squeezes bag)
	* Can be used with supraglottic airways and ETTs

**Bottom Line:** Ventilation adjuncts enhance the effectiveness and safety of rescue breathing during CPR, with BVM providing the best combination of oxygen delivery and infection control when used with proper technique.

## 4.4. ADVANCED AIRWAY CONSIDERATIONS
**Specific considerations regarding ventilation through advanced airways during CPR.**

* **With supraglottic airway or ETT**:
	* No need to synchronize ventilation with compressions
	* ⚠️ Blood flow drops rapidly when compressions stop; recovers slowly
	* Advanced airway placement should ideally occur *without* stopping compressions
	* After placement: Continuous compressions, ventilation rate ≤ 1 breath every 6 seconds (≤ 10 breaths/min)
* **Intrathoracic pressure effects**:
	* ⚠️ Positive-pressure ventilation increases intrathoracic pressure
	* Reduces venous return and preload
	* Worsens cardiac output during compressions
* 🔑 **HY**: Studies have not clearly shown advanced airway management improves outcomes over effective bag-mask ventilation during CPR

**Bottom Line:** Advanced airways allow continuous compressions without interruption for ventilation, but require careful rate control to avoid excessive intrathoracic pressure that can impair hemodynamics during CPR.

# 5. CIRCULATION



## 5.1. PHYSIOLOGY OF CIRCULATION DURING CLOSED-CHEST COMPRESSION
**Mechanisms generating blood flow during closed-chest cardiopulmonary resuscitation.**

### 5.1.2. CARDIAC PUMP MECHANISM
**Direct compression of heart between sternum and spine to generate cardiac output.**

* **Mechanism**:
	* Chest pressure compresses heart between sternum/spine → ↑ ventricular pressure
	* Closes AV valves → ejects blood into lungs/aorta
	* Relaxation → negative intrathoracic pressure → facilitates venous return
	* AV valves open → ventricular filling
	* Aortic pressure → aortic valve closure & coronary perfusion (during relaxation)
* **Clinical implications**: Requires adequate compression depth and full chest recoil

**Bottom Line:** The cardiac pump mechanism relies on direct heart compression between sternum and spine, emphasizing the importance of proper compression depth and complete recoil for cardiac filling.

### 5.1.3. THORACIC PUMP MECHANISM
**Blood flow generation through cyclic changes in intrathoracic pressure affecting all thoracic vessels.**

* **Mechanism**:
	* Increased intrathoracic pressure compresses all structures equally → forces blood out
	* Venous valves (subclavian/jugular) prevent backward flow
	* Thick arterial walls resist collapse
	* Heart acts as passive conduit (AV valves may stay open)
	* Carotid-jugular pressure difference favors cerebral flow
	* Poor flow below diaphragm (no IVC valves)
* **Clinical correlate**: Excessive positive-pressure ventilation can worsen cardiac output
* *Diagram takeaway*: Chest compression increases intrathoracic pressure, squeezing blood out of the chest cavity, primarily towards the head due to venous valves

**Bottom Line:** The thoracic pump mechanism relies on global pressure changes within the chest cavity to generate forward blood flow, with venous valves ensuring preferential flow to the cerebral circulation.

### 5.1.4. COMBINED MECHANISMS
**Integration of both cardiac and thoracic pump effects during actual CPR.**

* **Contribution**: Both direct cardiac compression and intrathoracic pressure changes likely contribute
* **Influencing factors**: Chest compliance, heart size, compression force, arrest duration, age
	* Younger patients: Cardiac pump may predominate
	* Older patients: Thoracic pump may predominate
* **Variability**: Predominant mechanism can vary between victims and during resuscitation

**Bottom Line:** Both cardiac and thoracic pump mechanisms contribute to blood flow during CPR, with the dominant mechanism varying based on patient characteristics and resuscitation technique.

## 5.2. DISTRIBUTION OF BLOOD FLOW DURING CPR
**Patterns of regional perfusion during cardiac arrest and CPR.**

* **Cardiac output**: 10-33% of normal during CPR
* **Blood flow distribution**:
	* Favors above diaphragm:
		* Cerebral perfusion: 50-90% of normal
		* Myocardial perfusion: 20-50% of normal
	* Severely reduced below diaphragm: ~5% of normal
* **Flow changes over time**:
	* Total flow decreases with prolonged CPR
	* Relative distribution remains similar
	* **EPINEPHRINE** helps sustain cardiac output and improves flow to brain/heart (not abdominal organs)

**Bottom Line:** CPR generates limited cardiac output with preferential distribution to brain and heart, while abdominal organ perfusion is severely compromised, explaining the importance of limiting arrest duration.

## 5.3. GAS TRANSPORT DURING CPR
**Oxygen and carbon dioxide transport characteristics during resuscitation.**

* **CO₂ excretion**:
	* Decreased proportionally to reduced cardiac output (due to shunting)
	* CO₂ accumulates in non-perfused areas
	* Temporary ↑ CO₂ excretion upon ROSC (Return of Spontaneous Circulation) = washout
* **Blood gas abnormalities**:
	* Arterial: Respiratory alkalosis
	* Venous: Respiratory acidosis
	* Markedly elevated arteriovenous CO₂ difference (due to low cardiac output)
* **End-tidal CO₂ (ETCO₂)**:
	* Low during CPR (ventilation-perfusion mismatch)
	* Correlates well with cardiac output
	* Increases as flow improves
	* Important monitoring parameter

**Bottom Line:** Gas transport during CPR is characterized by reduced CO2 elimination, mixed acid-base disturbances, and end-tidal CO2 values that correlate with perfusion quality, making capnography a valuable monitoring tool during resuscitation.

## 5.4. TECHNIQUE OF CLOSED-CHEST COMPRESSION
**Proper method for performing external chest compressions during CPR.**

* **Indications**:
	* Unresponsive individual with abnormal/absent breathing
	* Activate emergency response, start compressions immediately
	* Start compressions if pulse not definitely felt within 10 seconds
	* Witnessed collapse + unresponsiveness (no seizure) = presumed cardiac arrest
* **Positioning**:
	* Victim: Supine on firm surface, head level with heart
	* Rescuer: Beside victim, hips at/above victim's chest level
* **Hand placement**:
	* Heel of one hand on lower half of sternum
	* Other hand on top
	* ⚠️ Avoid xiphoid process (risk of liver laceration)
	* Apply pressure through heel only, fingers off chest wall
* **Compression technique**:
	* Force direction: Straight down
	* Arms: Straight, elbows locked, use upper body weight
	* ⚠️ Allow complete chest recoil (no leaning!)
	* Hands maintain contact, but all pressure removed during recoil
* **Compression parameters**:
	* Depth: 5-6 cm (2-2.4 in) in adults/teens
	* Rate: 100-120 compressions/minute
	* Duration: Equal compression and relaxation times
	* Minimize interruptions
* **Compression-ventilation ratio**:
	* Standard (no advanced airway): 30:2
	* With advanced airway: Continuous compressions, 1 breath every 6 seconds
	* ⚠️ Avoid excessive ventilation

**Bottom Line:** High-quality chest compressions are the cornerstone of effective CPR, requiring proper positioning, technique, and consistent adherence to rate, depth, and recoil parameters to maximize blood flow to vital organs.

## 5.5. ALTERNATIVE METHODS OF CIRCULATORY SUPPORT
**Mechanical and invasive techniques to supplement or replace manual chest compressions.**

### 5.5.2. MECHANICAL CHEST COMPRESSION DEVICES
**Automated devices that provide consistent external chest compressions.**

* **Load-distributing band (LDB) device**: Pneumatic/electronic circumferential band
	* Similar survival to manual CPR
* **LUCAS device**: Gas/electric piston with suction cup for active decompression
	* `Rate: 100 compressions/min`, `Depth: 5 cm`
* **Clinical applications**: Useful when manual CPR difficult (moving vehicle, cath lab, prolonged CPR)
	* ⚠️ Can increase hands-off time during deployment/removal; requires trained team

**Bottom Line:** Mechanical chest compression devices provide consistent, untiring compressions in challenging circumstances, though deployment time must be minimized to avoid increasing hands-off intervals.

### 5.5.3. IMPEDANCE THRESHOLD DEVICE (ITD)
**Valve device that enhances venous return during chest recoil phase of CPR.**

* **Mechanism**: Valve impedes air entry during chest recoil → ↓ intrathoracic pressure → ↑ venous return
* **Usage**: With ETT and active compression-decompression CPR, or conventional CPR + tight mask/SGA
* **Evidence**: Improved short-term outcomes in some studies; no difference in long-term outcomes in large trial. May be useful adjunct for trained professionals

**Bottom Line:** The impedance threshold device works by enhancing negative intrathoracic pressure during chest recoil to improve venous return, showing some promise as an adjunct but without definitive evidence for improved long-term outcomes.

### 5.5.4. INVASIVE TECHNIQUES
**Direct cardiac or extracorporeal methods of supporting circulation.**

* **Open-chest cardiac massage**: Useful if skilled personnel already present (e.g., post-cardiac surgery)
* **Extracorporeal CPR (ECPR)**: Venoarterial ECMO via femoral vessels
	* Provides perfusion/gas exchange while treating reversible causes
	* *Best candidates*: Younger patients, reversible causes (MI, PE, VF, hypothermia), short arrest-to-CPR interval
	* 🏥 **Clinical Practice**: Consider assessment after ~10 min unsuccessful CPR. Shorter time to ECPR improves survival
	* Maintain high-quality noninvasive CPR during ECPR initiation

**Bottom Line:** Invasive circulatory support techniques provide higher blood flow than conventional CPR and can extend the resuscitation window for select patients with potentially reversible causes of arrest, particularly in hospital settings with appropriate resources.

## 5.6. ASSESSING ADEQUACY OF CIRCULATION DURING CPR
**Methods to evaluate the effectiveness of CPR in generating adequate circulation.**

### 5.6.2. HEMODYNAMIC MONITORING
**Assessment of circulatory parameters to guide CPR effectiveness.**

* **Pulse palpation limitations**:
	* Reflects systolic pressure (brain perfusion) but not mean pressure (cardiac output)
	* Femoral pulse may be venous
* **Critical values for successful resuscitation**:
	* Myocardial blood flow: >15-20 mL/min/100g
	* Aortic diastolic pressure: >40 mmHg
	* Coronary perfusion pressure (CPP): >15-25 mmHg (Aortic diastolic - Right atrial diastolic)
	* 🔑 **HY**: Coronary perfusion during CPR occurs primarily during the relaxation (diastole) phase

**Bottom Line:** Hemodynamic monitoring during CPR focuses on achieving threshold values for coronary and cerebral perfusion, with coronary perfusion pressure serving as a key predictor of successful resuscitation.

### 5.6.3. END-TIDAL CO₂ MONITORING
**Use of capnography to assess CPR-generated blood flow and predict outcomes.**

* **Characteristics during CPR (with ETT)**:
	* Flow-dependent (correlates with cardiac output)
	* Very low (<10 mmHg) in low-flow states
	* Rises with improved blood flow (>20 mmHg suggests effective CPR)
	* Sudden increase to >40 mmHg often indicates ROSC
* **Clinical value**:
	* Correlates with cardiac output, CPP, and survival
	* ETCO₂ <10 mmHg after 20 mins CPR predicts unsuccessful resuscitation
	* 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: Use continuous waveform capnography to guide compression quality. Aim to maximize ETCO₂
	* ⚠️ **SODIUM BICARBONATE** temporarily increases ETCO₂ (returns to baseline in 3-5 min)

📋 **MNEMONIC: Critical Values in CPR (MACE)**

* **M**: Myocardial blood flow >15-20 mL/min/100g
* **A**: Aortic diastolic pressure >40 mmHg
* **C**: Coronary perfusion pressure >15-25 mmHg
* **E**: End-tidal CO₂ >10 mmHg (ideally >20 mmHg)

**Bottom Line:** End-tidal CO2 monitoring provides a non-invasive, continuous assessment of CPR-generated blood flow, with values correlating with resuscitation effectiveness and providing valuable prognostic information for guiding further interventions.

# 6. PHARMACOLOGIC THERAPY IN CPR



## 6.1. OVERVIEW
**Role and priorities of drug therapy during cardiopulmonary resuscitation.**

* **Drug therapy is secondary** to high-quality compressions and early defibrillation
* Establish IV/IO access and administer drugs *after* critical mechanical interventions
* *Clinical context*: Strong animal data, but limited evidence for improved long-term human survival with vasopressors

**Bottom Line:** While medications have a role in resuscitation, their importance is secondary to high-quality chest compressions and early defibrillation, with prioritization of mechanical interventions before pharmacologic ones.

## 6.2. ROUTES OF ADMINISTRATION
**Access pathways for medication delivery during cardiopulmonary arrest.**

### 6.2.2. IV ADMINISTRATION
**Intravenous route for medication delivery during resuscitation.**

* **Preferred route**
* Central venous access gives fastest/highest levels but usually requires stopping CPR
* Peripheral IV (antecubital, external jugular preferred) is effective
	* Poor flow below diaphragm delays drug delivery from lower extremity sites
	* Upper extremity drugs may take 1-2 minutes to reach central circulation
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: Follow drug bolus with 20-30 mL IV fluid flush to speed delivery

**Bottom Line:** IV administration is the preferred route for resuscitation drugs, with peripheral upper extremity or external jugular access providing the best balance between effectiveness and minimal interruption to CPR.

### 6.2.3. INTRAOSSEOUS (IO) ADMINISTRATION
**Bone marrow access for emergency medication and fluid delivery.**

* **Good alternative** to IV
* Similar drug delivery kinetics to IV
* *Preferred site*: Proximal humerus (rapid access without stopping CPR, fast transit time)
	* Flow rate ~100 mL/min under pressure (faster than tibial IO)
* ⚠️ Avoid dislodgement during patient movement

**Bottom Line:** Intraosseous access provides a rapid, reliable alternative to IV with similar drug delivery effectiveness, with proximal humerus offering advantages in adult resuscitation due to higher flow rates and rapid central circulation access.

### 6.2.4. ENDOTRACHEAL (ET) ADMINISTRATION
**Medication delivery through an endotracheal tube during resuscitation.**

* **Alternative** if IV/IO access unobtainable
* Suitable for: **EPINEPHRINE**, **VASOPRESSIN**, **LIDOCAINE**, **ATROPINE** (Mnemonic: NAVEL - Naloxone, Atropine, Vasopressin, Epinephrine, Lidocaine)
* ⚠️ Do NOT give **SODIUM BICARBONATE** via ET route
* No data for ET **AMIODARONE**
* *Dosing*: `Dose: 2-2.5 × IV dose`
* Flush with 5-10 mL normal saline
* Inconsistent absorption and time to effect

**Bottom Line:** Endotracheal drug administration is a backup option when vascular access is unavailable, limited to lipid-soluble drugs (NAVEL) at higher doses, with less reliable absorption compared to vascular routes.

## 6.3. VASOPRESSORS AND CATECHOLAMINES
**Drugs that increase vascular tone to improve perfusion during CPR.**

### 6.3.2. MECHANISM OF ACTION
**Pharmacologic basis for vasopressor effectiveness in cardiac arrest.**

* **Efficacy based on α-adrenergic properties**: Peripheral vasoconstriction → ↑ aortic diastolic pressure → ↑ CPP → ↑ myocardial blood flow
* **Effective drugs**: Strong α-agonists (**EPINEPHRINE**, **PHENYLEPHRINE**, **NOREPINEPHRINE**) or non-adrenergic vasopressors (**VASOPRESSIN**)
* **Ineffective drugs**: β-agonists without α activity (**ISOPROTERENOL**, **DOBUTAMINE**) are no better than placebo
* **Blockade effects**: α-blockade prevents resuscitation; β-blockade has no effect on ROSC

**Bottom Line:** Vasopressors are effective in resuscitation due to their α-adrenergic effects that increase peripheral vascular resistance, improving coronary and cerebral perfusion pressures critical for successful resuscitation.

### 6.3.3. EPINEPHRINE
**Primary vasopressor used in cardiac arrest resuscitation.**

* **Function**: Generates critical CPP for myocardial perfusion
* **Target**: Aortic diastolic pressure >40 mmHg or CPP >20 mmHg
* **Dosing**: `Dose: 1 mg IV/IO` every 3-5 minutes (approx. every other 2-min CPR cycle)
* **Higher doses**: Consider `Dose: 3-7 mg` if standard dose ineffective or in β-blocker/calcium channel blocker overdose
* 🔑 **HY**: No conclusive evidence that higher doses improve long-term survival vs standard doses. May improve ROSC but not necessarily good neurologic outcome

**Bottom Line:** Epinephrine remains the first-line vasopressor in cardiac arrest, administered at 1 mg every 3-5 minutes to achieve critical perfusion pressures, though higher doses may be considered in specific scenarios despite limited evidence for improved outcomes.

### 6.3.4. VASOPRESSIN
**Non-adrenergic vasopressor alternative in cardiac arrest.**

* **Mechanism**: Potent non-adrenergic vasoconstrictor (V1 receptors)
* **Dose**: `Dose: 40 U IV/IO` (one-time dose)
* **Half-life**: 10-20 minutes (longer than epinephrine)
* **Clinical evidence**: As effective as, but not superior to, epinephrine
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: May be useful in prolonged resuscitation, especially with acidemia (maintains potency unlike catecholamines)
* ⚠️ Causes more post-ROSC myocardial depression and splanchnic vasoconstriction than epinephrine (treatable)
* 🏥 **Clinical Practice**: No longer in standard ACLS algorithm but consider in known acidemia or prolonged CPR

**Bottom Line:** Vasopressin is an alternative vasopressor that maintains effectiveness in acidosis and has a longer duration than epinephrine, making it potentially useful in prolonged resuscitation situations despite no longer being in standard algorithms.

## 6.4. ANTIARRHYTHMIC DRUGS
**Medications that stabilize cardiac membranes and control dysrhythmias during resuscitation.**

### 6.4.2. GENERAL CONSIDERATIONS
**Principles guiding antiarrhythmic use in cardiac arrest.**

* **Use**: Aids defibrillation in refractory VF or prevents recurrence post-conversion
* ⚠️ No antiarrhythmic is superior to defibrillation or more effective than placebo
* ⚠️ Do NOT delay defibrillation for IV access or drug administration

**Bottom Line:** Antiarrhythmic medications play a supportive role in managing shockable rhythms, but should never delay defibrillation, which remains the definitive treatment for ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia.

### 6.4.3. AMIODARONE
**Broad-spectrum antiarrhythmic used in shock-refractory ventricular dysrhythmias.**

* **Classification**: Complex agent (Na+, K+, Ca2+ channel block; α/β block)
* **Clinical evidence**: Improves survival to hospital admission (but not discharge)
* **Dosing in cardiac arrest**:
	* Initial: `Dose: 300 mg` rapid IV/IO push
	* Supplemental: `Dose: 150 mg` IV/IO for recurrent/refractory VF/VT
	* Max total daily dose: 2 g
* **Dosing with intact circulation (e.g., stable VT)**:
	* `Dose: 150 mg IV` over 10 minutes
	* Followed by `Infusion: 1 mg/min` for 6 hours, then `0.5 mg/min`
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: Current IV formulations are less likely to cause hypotension/bradycardia than older versions

**Bottom Line:** Amiodarone is the preferred antiarrhythmic for shock-refractory VF/VT, administered as a 300 mg bolus with possible 150 mg follow-up dose, improving short-term outcomes despite minimal impact on survival to discharge.

### 6.4.4. LIDOCAINE
**Class IB antiarrhythmic alternative for ventricular dysrhythmias.**

* **Mechanism**: Class IB antiarrhythmic; suppresses automaticity, reduces VF threshold in ischemia
* **Dosing**:
	* Initial: `Dose: 1-1.5 mg/kg` IV/IO bolus
	* Additional: `Dose: 0.5-0.75 mg/kg` every 5-10 minutes
	* Maximum total dose: `3 mg/kg`
* **Clinical use**: Alternative to **AMIODARONE** for refractory VF/VT
* 🔑 **HY**: No more effective than **AMIODARONE**, potentially fewer side effects

**Bottom Line:** Lidocaine serves as an alternative to amiodarone for managing shock-refractory ventricular dysrhythmias, with comparable efficacy but potentially fewer hemodynamic side effects, particularly in patients with liver dysfunction.

## 6.5. NON-ROUTINE DRUGS IN CPR
**Medications used in specific resuscitation scenarios rather than routinely.**

### 6.5.2. ATROPINE
**Anticholinergic agent previously used for asystole and PEA.**

* **Mechanism**: Vagolytic → enhances sinus node automaticity and AV conduction
* **Clinical evidence**: No improved outcomes in asystole or PEA (Pulseless Electrical Activity)
* 🏥 **Clinical Practice**: Not recommended for routine use in cardiac arrest
* **Rationale**: Asystole/PEA usually due to severe ischemia, not high vagal tone

**Bottom Line:** Atropine is no longer recommended for routine use in cardiac arrest as evidence shows no benefit in asystole or PEA, where the underlying problem is typically severe ischemia rather than excessive vagal tone.

### 6.5.3. SODIUM BICARBONATE
**Buffer agent to treat metabolic acidosis during prolonged resuscitation or specific scenarios.**

* **Historical use**: Theory that acidosis impairs defibrillation/catecholamine response
* **Clinical evidence**: No proven benefit for routine use
* **Adverse effects**: Metabolic alkalosis, hypernatremia, hyperosmolarity
* **Theoretical concern**: Paradoxical worsening of intracellular/cerebral acidosis (CO₂ diffuses faster than HCO₃⁻)
* **Current recommendations**:
	* Not for routine use
	* *Specific indications*: Known hyperkalemia, severe pre-existing metabolic acidosis (pH < 7.1-7.2), tricyclic antidepressant overdose, phenobarbital overdose. Consider in prolonged CPR after other measures fail
	* *Dose*: `Dose: 1 mEq/kg` IV (ideally guided by blood gas)

**Bottom Line:** Sodium bicarbonate is not indicated for routine use in cardiac arrest but may be appropriate in specific scenarios like severe pre-existing acidosis, hyperkalemia, or certain drug overdoses, where its benefits may outweigh potential adverse effects.

### 6.5.4. CALCIUM
**Electrolyte used in specific resuscitation scenarios involving calcium abnormalities.**

* **Physiologic effects**: Increases contractility and ventricular automaticity
* **Clinical evidence**: No benefit in asystole or PEA
* **Current recommendations**:
	* Not for routine use
	* *Specific indications*: Known hyperkalemia, documented hypocalcemia, calcium channel blocker toxicity
	* *Preferred form*: **CALCIUM CHLORIDE** (higher ionized Ca²⁺ yield)
	* *Dose*: `Dose: 2-4 mg/kg` of 10% **CALCIUM CHLORIDE** solution slowly IV (approx 0.5-1g for adults)
	* 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: **CALCIUM GLUCONATE** has ⅓ the elemental calcium and requires hepatic metabolism

**Bottom Line:** Calcium administration is not indicated for routine cardiac arrest but is appropriate for specific conditions like hyperkalemia, hypocalcemia, and calcium channel blocker toxicity, with calcium chloride preferred over gluconate for its higher bioavailable calcium.

## 6.6. ADULT ACLS DRUG REFERENCE
**Standardized medication doses and indications for adult cardiac arrest management.**

### 6.6.2. STANDARD DRUGS
**Core medications routinely used in cardiac arrest scenarios.**

* **EPINEPHRINE**
	* *Dose*: `1 mg IV/IO`
	* *Interval*: Every 3-5 minutes
	* *Target*: Aortic Diastolic BP >40 mmHg, CPP >20 mmHg, ETCO₂ >20 mmHg
* **AMIODARONE**
	* *Dose*: `300 mg IV/IO` first dose for VF/pulseless VT
	* *Interval*: May repeat `150 mg` once after 3-5 minutes
* **LIDOCAINE** (Alternative to Amiodarone)
	* *Dose*: `1-1.5 mg/kg IV/IO` first dose
	* *Additional doses*: `0.5-0.75 mg/kg` every 5-10 minutes
	* *Maximum dose*: `3 mg/kg`

**Bottom Line:** Standard ACLS medications include epinephrine (administered every 3-5 minutes) and antiarrhythmics like amiodarone or lidocaine for shock-refractory ventricular dysrhythmias, with specific dosing protocols that should be readily available during resuscitation.

### 6.6.3. NON-ROUTINE/SPECIAL CIRCUMSTANCE DRUGS
**Medications used in specific resuscitation scenarios rather than routinely.**

* **VASOPRESSIN**
	* *Dose*: `40 U IV/IO` (one time)
	* *Indications*: Consider in prolonged CPR, known acidemia
* **SODIUM BICARBONATE**
	* *Dose*: `1 mEq/kg IV/IO`
	* *Indications*: Hyperkalemia, severe metabolic acidosis, certain overdoses (TCAs, phenobarbital)
* **CALCIUM CHLORIDE** (10%)
	* *Dose*: `0.5-1.0 g IV/IO` slowly (approx 5-10 mL)
	* *Indications*: Hyperkalemia, hypocalcemia, Ca-channel blocker OD, hypermagnesemia
* **MAGNESIUM SULFATE**
	* *Dose*: `1-2 g IV/IO` over 5-20 min (faster for Torsades de Points)
	* *Indications*: Torsades de Points, known/suspected hypomagnesemia
* **LIPID EMULSION** (20%)
	* *Dose*: `Bolus: 1.5 mL/kg` IV over 1 min
	* *Infusion*: `0.25 mL/kg/min` for at least 10 min after ROSC
	* *Indications*: Local Anesthetic Systemic Toxicity (LAST) (**BUPIVACAINE**, etc.), toxicity from other lipophilic drugs (beta-blockers, Ca-channel blockers, TCAs, antipsychotics)
	* *Related*: Lipid Emulsion Therapy Protocol

🔑 **HY**: Know the core ACLS algorithms:

* Adult Pulseless Arrest (includes VF/pVT, Asystole/PEA)
* Adult Bradycardia Algorithm
* Adult Tachycardia with a Pulse Algorithm

**Bottom Line:** Special circumstance medications include drugs like vasopressin, sodium bicarbonate, calcium, magnesium, and lipid emulsion therapy, each with specific indications and dosing protocols for targeted use in particular arrest scenarios rather than routine administration.

# 7. ELECTRICAL THERAPY IN CARDIAC ARREST



## 7.1. VENTRICULAR FIBRILLATION (VF) FUNDAMENTALS
**Basic principles of ventricular fibrillation pathophysiology and management.**

* **Definition**: Most common initial rhythm in witnessed adult sudden cardiac arrest (SCA)
* **Treatment**: Electrical defibrillation is the only consistently effective therapy
* **Critical factor**: Duration of VF is the most important controllable determinant of success
* **Pathophysiology**:
	* Fibrillating heart has high O₂ consumption → worsens ischemia
	* Prolonged VF → irreversible cell damage
	* ⚠️ Longer VF persists → harder to defibrillate → lower chance of survival
* **Timing**:
	* Defibrillation within 1 minute: CPR may be unnecessary
	* Earlier defibrillation improves ROSC and survival to discharge
	* 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: Time to defibrillation is the single most critical factor in VF arrest

**Bottom Line:** Ventricular fibrillation is the most common initial rhythm in witnessed sudden cardiac arrest, with early defibrillation being the single most important intervention affecting survival, as VF becomes increasingly resistant to treatment with time.

## 7.2. VF WAVEFORM CHARACTERISTICS
**Electrocardiographic features of ventricular fibrillation that influence management.**

* **Fibrillation Amplitude** ("coarseness"):
	* May reflect severity/duration of insult (prognostic)
	* Varies with ECG lead orientation
	* ⚠️ Always check a second lead if "flat line" seen (lead may be perpendicular)
	* Low-amplitude ("fine") VF: Less likely successful defibrillation, more likely post-shock asystole
* **Fibrillation Frequency**:
	* Low frequency associated with poor outcomes
	* Median frequency correlates with myocardial perfusion during CPR and defibrillation success
* **VF Waveform Analysis**: Can predict defibrillation success but not yet used prospectively to guide therapy
* **Effect of Catecholamines**: β-agonists increase VF amplitude but don't improve defibrillation success or reduce energy needs
* 🔑 **HY**: Defibrillation should NOT be delayed for drug administration in VF

**Bottom Line:** VF waveform characteristics like amplitude and frequency reflect myocardial condition and predict defibrillation success, with fine VF indicating poorer outcomes, though this should not delay defibrillation attempts which remain the primary intervention.

## 7.3. DEFIBRILLATOR TECHNOLOGY
**Equipment and technical aspects of electrical defibrillation.**

* **Components**: Power source, capacitor (stores charge), switches (charge/discharge), electrodes
* **Waveforms**:
	* *Monophasic* (older): Current in one direction (damped sinusoid or truncated exponential)
	* *Biphasic* (current standard): Current reverses direction (Biphasic Truncated Exponential - BTE, Rectilinear Biphasic - RLB, Pulsed Biphasic). Biphasic is more effective at lower energies

### 7.3.2. AUTOMATED EXTERNAL DEFIBRILLATORS (AEDS)
**Public-access defibrillators that analyze rhythm and deliver shocks.**

* **Function**: Monitors ECG, recognizes VF/VT, charges, delivers shock
* **Benefits**: Allows rapid defibrillation by laypersons, improves out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) survival
* **Limitations**:
	* Highly specific (rarely shocks non-shockable rhythms) but less sensitive (may miss low-amplitude VF)
	* Can misinterpret pacemaker spikes
	* ⚠️ Rhythm analysis requires pausing compressions (up to 90s), potentially harmful
* **Advanced Features**: Some measure impedance, adjust energy for current-based dosing

**Bottom Line:** AEDs enable early defibrillation by non-medical personnel, with high specificity but imperfect sensitivity for detecting shockable rhythms, improving survival rates through wider access to this critical intervention.

### 7.3.3. PHYSICS OF DEFIBRILLATION
**Electrical principles underlying successful defibrillation.**

* **Mechanism**: Sufficient current through critical myocardial mass → simultaneous depolarization → allows pacemaker reset
* **Key Relationships**: Current is the key determinant of success
	* Energy (J) = Power (W) × Time (s)
	* Power (W) = Voltage (V) × Current (A)
	* Current (A) = Voltage (V) / Resistance (Ω)
* **Implications**:
	* Higher impedance (resistance) → lower current delivery for a given energy setting
	* 🔑 **HY**: Minimize transthoracic impedance for optimal current delivery

**Bottom Line:** Successful defibrillation depends primarily on delivering sufficient current through the myocardium, which is determined by the energy selected and the transthoracic impedance, making impedance reduction an important consideration.

## 7.4. TRANSTHORACIC IMPEDANCE (TTI)
**Resistance to electrical current flow across the chest during defibrillation.**

* **Range**: 15-143 Ω (average 70-80 Ω)
* **Factors affecting TTI**:
	* **Electrode size**: Larger pads/paddles → lower impedance (optimal ~13 cm diameter). Standard pads 8-12 cm
	* **Electrode-skin interface**: Use conductive gel/pads, ensure firm pressure
	* **Phase of respiration**: Slightly higher during inspiration (air is insulator)
	* **Prior shocks**: May decrease impedance slightly
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: With proper technique and modern biphasic defibrillators (using appropriate energy), impedance variations are less clinically significant

**Bottom Line:** Transthoracic impedance affects defibrillation effectiveness by influencing current delivery, though modern biphasic defibrillators with proper pad placement and good skin contact minimize these concerns in clinical practice.

## 7.5. DEFIBRILLATION ENERGY AND SAFETY
**Energy selection and safety considerations for defibrillation.**

* **Adverse Effects**: Repeated high-energy shocks can cause myocardial damage (dysrhythmias, ECG changes, necrosis, elevated CK-MB, AV block)
	* 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: Minimize energy dose and number of shocks needed
* **Energy Requirements**:
	* *Children*: `Dose: 2-4 J/kg` (initial 2 J/kg, subsequent 4 J/kg). Lower doses may be effective
	* *Adults*: Weight variability less critical with biphasic
* **Protocol Evolution**:
	* *Old (Monophasic)*: Stacked shocks (200J → 200-300J → 360J) - caused long pauses
	* *Current (Biphasic)*: More effective at lower energy (85-98% first-shock success)
		* Recommended energy: Device specific, typically `120-200J`. (e.g., 120J RLB, 150-200J BTE)
		* If device-specific dose unknown, use `200J`
		* 🔑 **HY**: Deliver a SINGLE shock, then IMMEDIATELY resume chest compressions for 2 minutes before rhythm/pulse check

**Bottom Line:** Modern defibrillation protocols use a single shock at an appropriate energy level (typically 120-200J for biphasic devices) followed by immediate resumption of CPR, replacing older protocols with stacked shocks that caused harmful interruptions.

## 7.6. TIME-SENSITIVE MODEL OF VENTRICULAR FIBRILLATION
**Conceptual framework describing changing pathophysiology and intervention priorities across VF duration.**

* **Three phases of untreated VF**:
	* **1. Electrical Phase** (0-5 min): Myocardium is ischemic but viable
		* *Intervention*: Immediate defibrillation is key. AEDs most effective
		* 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: Witnessed arrest + immediate AED access → Defibrillate first
	* **2. Circulatory/Hemodynamic Phase** (5-15 min): Myocardial ATP depleted, requires perfusion
		* *Intervention*: High-quality CPR *before* defibrillation is critical to perfuse myocardium
		* ⚠️ Interrupting compressions severely reduces neurologically intact survival chances
	* **3. Metabolic Phase** (>15 min): Severe ischemic injury, poor prognosis
		* *Intervention*: Focus on high-quality CPR, consider advanced therapies (ECPR), address underlying cause
* **ATP Considerations**: VF rapidly depletes myocardial ATP. Effective CPR helps replete/slow depletion by generating CPP

**Bottom Line:** The three-phase model of VF (electrical, circulatory, metabolic) guides intervention priorities based on arrest duration, with immediate defibrillation preferred in early VF but CPR before defibrillation more effective in later phases when the heart requires metabolic support.

## 7.7. BYSTANDER CPR AND BASIC LIFE SUPPORT
**Role and importance of initial resuscitation by non-healthcare providers.**

* **Importance**: Improves survival significantly. Incidence is declining
* **Barriers**: Lack of training, fear, reluctance for mouth-to-mouth
* **Chest Compression-Only CPR**:
	* Recommended for untrained bystanders or those unwilling to do rescue breaths
	* Compressions generate some passive ventilation if airway patent
	* Studies show similar or better outcomes compared to standard BLS by bystanders
	* 🔑 **HY**: Compression-only CPR is significantly better than no CPR
* **Animal Studies**: Minimal interruption CPR (compression-only or 30:2 with short pauses) better than CPR with long pauses for ventilation
* **SHARE Program (Arizona)**: Emphasized continuous compressions → increased bystander CPR and survival

**Bottom Line:** Bystander CPR significantly improves survival, with compression-only CPR recommended for untrained rescuers as it removes barriers to intervention while maintaining effectiveness comparable to traditional CPR with ventilations.

## 7.8. ADVANCED LIFE SUPPORT (ACLS) PRINCIPLES
**Fundamental principles guiding advanced cardiac life support interventions by healthcare providers.**

* **Core Principle**: Minimize interruptions in chest compressions
	* Blood flow stops almost immediately on pausing, recovers slowly on resuming
	* ⚠️ Studies show providers often spend <50% of resuscitation time compressing
* **Minimizing Interruptions**:
	* Pause ONLY when essential (rhythm analysis, shock delivery) and for <10 seconds
	* Place IV/IO, prepare drugs *during* compressions
	* Pulse checks only during rhythm analysis pauses (if organized rhythm present)
	* Initial airway: OPA/NPA + BVM. Delay advanced airway if it requires significant pause
	* Ideal: Place advanced airway while compressions continue
* **Ventilation Considerations**:
	* ⚠️ Excessive ventilation (rate or volume) increases intrathoracic pressure → ↓ venous return, ↓ CO, ↓ CPP → worsens survival
	* Target rate with advanced airway: 1 breath every 6 seconds (10 breaths/min)
	* 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: Avoid hyperventilation

### 7.8.2. RHYTHM ANALYSIS AND DEFIBRILLATION PROTOCOL
**Standardized approach to rhythm assessment and shock delivery during ACLS.**

* **Initial approach**: Start compressions immediately. Apply pads/paddles while compressions continue. Pause briefly for rhythm analysis/shock
* **Shock protocol**:
	* Single biphasic shock highly effective (>90% terminate VF)
	* However, ROSC immediately post-shock is rare (~2.5%)
	* 🔑 **HY**: After shock, immediately resume compressions for 2 minutes. Do NOT check pulse or rhythm immediately post-shock
* **Post-shock**: Successful defibrillation often results in transient asystole or PEA. Immediate compressions provide needed perfusion

**Bottom Line:** Modern ACLS emphasizes minimizing interruptions to chest compressions with a single-shock protocol followed by immediate resumption of CPR, integrating interventions around continuous compressions rather than pausing for procedures unnecessarily.

## 7.9. OUTCOME IMPROVEMENTS WITH MINIMALLY INTERRUPTED CPR
**Evidence supporting continuous chest compression approaches to resuscitation.**

* **Studies** (Wisconsin, Arizona) show significant improvement in neurologically intact survival after implementing protocols emphasizing:
	* High-quality compressions (rate, depth, recoil)
	* Minimal interruptions
	* Appropriate ventilation
	* Early defibrillation when appropriate
* 🔑 **HY**: Focus on CPR quality and minimizing interruptions dramatically improves SCA outcomes

**Bottom Line:** Implementation of minimally interrupted CPR protocols with emphasis on compression quality significantly improves neurologically intact survival from cardiac arrest, supporting system-wide practice changes focused on these principles.

## 7.10. CPR GUIDELINES AND ORGANIZATIONS
**Organizational frameworks guiding resuscitation standards and education.**

* **International**: International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR) - consensus on science
* **USA**: American Heart Association (AHA) - publishes specific guidelines, develops training
* **Training levels**: BLS, ACLS
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: ACLS training associated with better in-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) outcomes

**Bottom Line:** Resuscitation science and practice are guided by international consensus organizations like ILCOR with regional implementation through organizations like the AHA, providing standardized training that measurably improves outcomes.

# 8. ACLS ALGORITHMS


## 8.1. ADULT PULSELESS ARREST ALGORITHM
**Standardized approach to managing adult cardiopulmonary arrest.**

![image.png](ACLS_BCLS_image_1.png_700e83.png)

* **Overview**: Unified algorithm for VF/pVT and Asystole/PEA
* **Start**: Shout for help/Activate emergency response, Start CPR (Compressions-Airway-Breathing). Give O₂, attach monitor/defibrillator
* **Rhythm Check (after 2 min CPR)**:
	* **Shockable Rhythm (VF/Pulseless VT)**:
		1. Shock (Biphasic: 120-200J, Monophasic: 360J)
		2. Immediately resume CPR for 2 min
		3. Establish IV/IO access
		4. Rhythm check after 2 min CPR. If still shockable:
		5. Shock again
		6. Immediately resume CPR for 2 min
		7. Administer **EPINEPHRINE** `1 mg` IV/IO (repeat every 3-5 min). Consider advanced airway, capnography
		8. Rhythm check after 2 min CPR. If still shockable:
		9. Shock again
		10. Immediately resume CPR for 2 min
		11. Administer **AMIODARONE** `300 mg` IV/IO (or **LIDOCAINE** `1-1.5 mg/kg`)
		12. Treat reversible causes (Hs & Ts). Continue cycle: CPR (2 min) → Rhythm/Pulse Check → Shock (if indicated) → CPR → Drugs (Epi q3-5m, Amio/Lido once more if needed)
	* **Non-Shockable Rhythm (Asystole/PEA)**:
		1. Immediately resume CPR for 2 min
		2. Administer **EPINEPHRINE** `1 mg` IV/IO ASAP (repeat every 3-5 min)
		3. Establish IV/IO access. Consider advanced airway, capnography
		4. Rhythm check after 2 min CPR. If still non-shockable:
		5. Resume CPR for 2 min
		6. Treat reversible causes (Hs & Ts)
		7. Continue cycle: CPR (2 min) → Rhythm/Pulse Check → CPR → Drugs (Epi q3-5m). If rhythm becomes shockable, go to shockable side of algorithm. If signs of ROSC, proceed to Post-Cardiac Arrest Care
* **Reversible Causes (Hs & Ts)**: Hypovolemia, Hypoxia, Hydrogen ion (acidosis), Hypo/Hyperkalemia, Hypothermia; Tension pneumothorax, Tamponade (cardiac), Toxins, Thrombosis (pulmonary), Thrombosis (coronary)

**Bottom Line:** The Adult Pulseless Arrest Algorithm provides a systematic approach to cardiac arrest management based on initial rhythm, integrating high-quality CPR, appropriate defibrillation, medication administration, and treatment of reversible causes in a coordinated sequence.

## 8.2. ADULT BRADYCARDIA WITH A PULSE ALGORITHM
**Standardized approach to managing symptomatic bradycardia in adults.**

![image.png](ACLS_BCLS_image_2.png_a8eedb.png)

* **Assess**: Is patient stable or unstable (Hypotension, Altered mental status, Signs of shock, Ischemic chest discomfort, Acute heart failure)?
* **Identify/Treat Underlying Cause**: Maintain patent airway, assist breathing, O₂, cardiac monitor, IV access, 12-lead ECG
* **Unstable Bradycardia**:
	1. **ATROPINE** `Dose: 1 mg` IV bolus. Repeat every 3-5 min (Max total `3 mg`)
	2. If **ATROPINE** ineffective:
		* Transcutaneous Pacing (TCP) OR
		* **DOPAMINE** infusion (`Rate: 5-20 mcg/kg/min`) OR
		* **EPINEPHRINE** infusion (`Rate: 2-10 mcg/min`)
	3. Consider expert consultation, transvenous pacing
* **Stable Bradycardia**: Monitor and observe

**Bottom Line:** The Adult Bradycardia Algorithm differentiates between stable and unstable presentations, with interventions for unstable patients including atropine, pacing, and chronotropic infusions, while emphasizing identification and treatment of underlying causes.

## 8.3. ADULT TACHYCARDIA WITH A PULSE ALGORITHM
**Standardized approach to managing tachyarrhythmias in adults with adequate perfusion.**

![image.png](ACLS_BCLS_image_3.png_40f449.png)

* **Assess**: Stable or Unstable? (Criteria same as bradycardia). Identify/Treat underlying cause
* **Unstable Tachycardia**: Synchronized Cardioversion (Sedate if possible. Narrow regular: 50-100J (AF is 50J; SVT is 100J); Narrow irregular: 120-200J biphasic; Wide regular: 100J; Wide irregular: Defibrillation dose - unsynchronized). Consider antiarrhythmic infusion
* **Stable Tachycardia**:
	* **Assess QRS Duration**: Wide (≥0.12 sec) or Narrow (<0.12 sec)?
	* **Stable Narrow QRS Tachycardia**:
		1. Vagal maneuvers
			- Carotid sinus massage - avoid in elderly as risk of plaque embolism
			- Valsava manuevre
		2. **ADENOSINE** `Dose: 6 mg` rapid IV push. If no conversion, `12 mg` second dose
		3. If Regular rhythm (SVT): Consider Beta-blocker or Calcium channel blocker (Diltiazem 2.5mg every 3min (up to 20mg); Verapamil 1mg every 3min (max 50mg))
		4. If Irregular rhythm (likely A-Fib): Control rate (Beta-blocker, Diltiazem). Consider anticoagulation. Expert consultation
	* **Stable Wide QRS Tachycardia**:
		1. Consider **ADENOSINE** ONLY if regular and monomorphic
		2. Consider Antiarrhythmic Infusion (**PROCAINAMIDE**, **AMIODARONE**, **SOTALOL**)
		3. Expert consultation

**Bottom Line:** The Adult Tachycardia Algorithm guides management based on hemodynamic stability and QRS morphology, with synchronized cardioversion for unstable patients and medication-based approaches for stable patients tailored to the specific arrhythmia characteristics.

# 9. PEDIATRIC CARDIOPULMONARY RESUSCITATION


## 9.1. FUNDAMENTALS OF PEDIATRIC CPR
**Core principles distinguishing pediatric from adult resuscitation.**

* **Etiology**: Usually secondary to respiratory failure or shock (progressive deterioration), not sudden cardiac event
* **Presenting Rhythms**: Most commonly Asystole or PEA
* **Approach**: Start with compressions (C-A-B sequence). Basic principles similar to adults, adjusted for size/physiology

**Bottom Line:** Pediatric cardiac arrest differs from adult presentations in etiology (primarily respiratory rather than cardiac), presenting rhythms (predominantly asystole or PEA), and specific considerations for age-appropriate interventions.

## 9.2. AIRWAY MANAGEMENT IN PEDIATRIC CPR
**Age-specific considerations for airway management in pediatric resuscitation.**

* **Critical Importance**: Effective ventilation is paramount due to respiratory cause of arrest
* **Initial Options**: Mouth-to-mouth/nose (infants), BVM. Intubate when feasible without prolonged interruption
* **Anatomical Considerations**: Higher larynx, larger occiput, larger tongue relative to oropharynx compared to adults

**Bottom Line:** Airway management is particularly critical in pediatric resuscitation due to the predominantly respiratory etiology of arrest, requiring special consideration of pediatric airway anatomy and appropriate sizing of equipment.

## 9.3. CARDIAC COMPRESSION TECHNIQUES
**Age-appropriate techniques for chest compressions in pediatric patients.**

* **Infants (<1 year)**:
	* Two fingers on lower sternum, just below nipple line OR
	* Two-thumb encircling hands technique (preferred for 2 rescuers)
* **Children (1 year to puberty)**:
	* Heel of one hand (or two hands for larger children) on lower half of sternum
* **Compression Guidelines**:
	* Depth: At least ⅓ anteroposterior diameter of chest (Infants: ~4 cm/1.5 in; Children: ~5 cm/2 in)
	* Rate: 100-120/min
	* Ratio:
		* Single rescuer: 30:2
		* Two or more rescuers: 15:2
	* Allow full chest recoil. Minimize interruptions

**Bottom Line:** Pediatric chest compression techniques differ by age group, with special hand positions and depths appropriate to anatomy, and a different compression-to-ventilation ratio for multiple rescuers (15:2) compared to adult CPR.

## 9.4. DEFIBRILLATION IN PEDIATRICS
**Age-appropriate considerations for electrical therapy in pediatric arrest.**

* **Less Frequent Need**: Compared to adults due to primary rhythm often being non-shockable
* **Energy Recommendations**:
	* Initial dose: `Dose: 2 J/kg`
	* Subsequent doses: `Dose: 4 J/kg`. May increase further up to max 10 J/kg or adult dose
* **Pads**: Use pediatric pads if available for <10 kg. Ensure pads do not touch. Anteroposterior placement often used

**Bottom Line:** While less commonly needed than in adults, pediatric defibrillation follows weight-based energy dosing starting at 2 J/kg and increasing to 4 J/kg, with special consideration for pad size and placement in smaller patients.

## 9.5. MEDICATION ADMINISTRATION
**Age-appropriate considerations for drug delivery in pediatric resuscitation.**

* **Routes**: IV preferred
	* ⚠️ **Intraosseous (IO)** access is a rapid, reliable alternative, especially in young children. Preferred sites: proximal tibia, distal femur, proximal humerus
* **Drug Therapy**: Similar drugs to adults, adjusted for weight. Plays significant role due to non-shockable rhythms being common
* **Weight-Based Dosing**: Critical to calculate doses based on accurate weight estimation (length-based tapes, age formulas)

**Bottom Line:** Pediatric medication administration requires weight-based dosing with IO access serving as an excellent alternative when IV access is challenging, particularly important given the higher prevalence of non-shockable rhythms requiring pharmacologic intervention.

## 9.6. PEDIATRIC RESUSCITATION MEDICATIONS
**Weight-based dosing for emergency medications in pediatric resuscitation.**

* **ADENOSINE** (for SVT)
	* *Dose*: `0.1 mg/kg` rapid IV/IO push (max 6 mg)
	* *Repeat dose*: `0.2 mg/kg` (max 12 mg). Follow with rapid saline flush
* **AMIODARONE** (for shock-refractory VF/pVT or perfusing VT)
	* *Dose*: `5 mg/kg` IV/IO bolus during arrest (can repeat up to 2 times)
	* *Max*: 15 mg/kg total dose
	* *Perfusing VT*: Give over 20-60 min
* **ATROPINE** (for bradycardia with poor perfusion, esp. increased vagal tone or cholinergic toxicity)
	* *Dose*: `0.02 mg/kg` IV/IO (min dose 0.1 mg, max single dose 0.5 mg child / 1 mg adolescent). May repeat once
	* *ET Dose*: `0.04-0.06 mg/kg`
* **CALCIUM CHLORIDE** (10%) (for hypocalcemia, hyperkalemia, hypermagnesemia, Ca-channel blocker OD)
	* *Dose*: `20 mg/kg` (0.2 mL/kg) slow IV/IO push
* **EPINEPHRINE** (for pulseless arrest, symptomatic bradycardia, hypotension, anaphylaxis)
	* *Pulseless Arrest Dose*: `0.01 mg/kg` (0.1 mL/kg of 1:10,000) IV/IO every 3-5 min
	* *ET Dose*: `0.1 mg/kg` (0.1 mL/kg of 1:1,000) every 3-5 min
	* *Bradycardia/Hypotension Dose*: `0.01 mg/kg` IV/IO. Infusion: `0.1-1 mcg/kg/min`
* **GLUCOSE** (for hypoglycemia)
	* *Dose*: `0.5-1 g/kg` IV/IO
	* *Concentrations*: D10W (Neonates: 5-10 mL/kg), D25W (Infants/Children: 2-4 mL/kg), D50W (Adolescents: 1-2 mL/kg)
* **LIDOCAINE** (alternative to Amiodarone for shock-refractory VF/pVT)
	* *Bolus*: `1 mg/kg` IV/IO
	* *Infusion*: `20-50 mcg/kg/min`
* **MAGNESIUM SULFATE** (for Torsades de Points, hypomagnesemia, severe asthma)
	* *Dose*: `25-50 mg/kg` IV/IO over 10-20 min (faster for Torsades). Max 2 g
* **NALOXONE** (for opioid toxicity)
	* *Dose*: `0.1 mg/kg` IV/IO/IM/SC for ≤5 yr or ≤20 kg (max 2 mg). `2 mg` for ≥5 yr or >20 kg. Titrate for respiratory depression reversal
* **SODIUM BICARBONATE** (for severe metabolic acidosis, hyperkalemia, TCA overdose)
	* *Dose*: `1 mEq/kg` slow IV/IO push (only if ventilation adequate)

🔑 **HY: For ET administration, use higher doses (Epi/Lido/Atropine: 2-3x IV dose; Naloxone: standard dose okay). Dilute in 3-5 mL saline, follow with PPV breaths.**

**Bottom Line:** Pediatric resuscitation medications follow weight-based dosing principles with special considerations for minimum/maximum doses and administration routes, requiring careful calculation and double-checking to ensure proper dosing in emergency situations.

## 9.7. PEDIATRIC RESUSCITATION ALGORITHMS
**Standardized approaches to common pediatric resuscitation scenarios.**

### 9.7.2. PEDIATRIC PULSELESS ARREST ALGORITHM
**Systematic approach to pediatric cardiopulmonary arrest.**

![image.png](ACLS_BCLS_image_4.png_5cf50a.png)

* **Similar structure to adult**: CPR → Rhythm Check → Shock (if VF/pVT) → CPR → Drugs
* **Emphasis**: High-quality CPR (rate, depth, recoil, minimize interruptions, 15:2 ratio for 2 rescuers). Early **EPINEPHRINE** for non-shockable rhythms. **AMIODARONE** or **LIDOCAINE** for shock-refractory VF/pVT. Treat reversible causes (Hs & Ts)

**Bottom Line:** The Pediatric Pulseless Arrest Algorithm follows a structure similar to the adult algorithm but with pediatric-specific considerations for compression-ventilation ratio, energy doses, and medication dosing, with special emphasis on addressing respiratory causes.

### 9.7.3. PEDIATRIC BRADYCARDIA WITH A PULSE ALGORITHM
**Systematic approach to symptomatic bradycardia in pediatric patients.**

![image.png](ACLS_BCLS_image_5.png_df5493.png)

* **Assess**: Airway, Breathing, Circulation. Is HR < 60/min with poor perfusion despite oxygenation/ventilation?
* **If Yes (HR<60 + poor perfusion)**: Start CPR
* **If No (HR≥60 or HR<60 but adequate perfusion)**: Support ABCs, give O₂, monitor, IV/IO access. Consider causes
* **Persistent Bradycardia causing cardiopulmonary compromise**:
	1. **EPINEPHRINE** `0.01 mg/kg` IV/IO. Repeat q3-5 min
	2. Consider **ATROPINE** `0.02 mg/kg` IV/IO (esp. if increased vagal tone or primary AV block). Max single dose 0.5mg
	3. Consider TCPacing
	4. Treat underlying causes

**Bottom Line:** The Pediatric Bradycardia Algorithm emphasizes respiratory support and identifying/treating underlying causes, with early CPR for severe bradycardia with poor perfusion, and epinephrine as the first-line medication over atropine.

### 9.7.4. PEDIATRIC TACHYCARDIA WITH A PULSE ALGORITHM
**Systematic approach to tachyarrhythmias in pediatric patients.**

![image.png](ACLS_BCLS_image_6.png_51c31f.png)

* **Assess**: Stable or Unstable? Identify/Treat cause
* **Unstable Tachycardia**: Synchronized Cardioversion (`0.5-1 J/kg` initial, increase to `2 J/kg` if needed). Sedate if possible
* **Stable Tachycardia**:
	* **Assess QRS**: Narrow (<0.09 sec) or Wide (≥0.09 sec)?
	* **Stable Narrow QRS (Probable SVT)**:
		1. Vagal maneuvers (if feasible)
		2. **ADENOSINE** `0.1 mg/kg` rapid IV/IO push (max 6 mg). If ineffective, `0.2 mg/kg` (max 12 mg)
		3. If Adenosine ineffective or rhythm recurs: Consider synchronized cardioversion or longer-acting agents (expert consult)
	* **Stable Wide QRS (Probable VT or SVT with aberrancy)**:
		1. Expert consultation strongly advised
		2. Consider **ADENOSINE** if regular and monomorphic
		3. Consider **AMIODARONE** `5 mg/kg` IV over 20-60 min OR **PROCAINAMIDE** `15 mg/kg` IV over 30-60 min
		4. Synchronized Cardioversion

**Bottom Line:** The Pediatric Tachycardia Algorithm addresses both unstable presentations requiring immediate cardioversion and stable presentations managed according to QRS width, with SVT being the most common significant tachyarrhythmia in children and adenosine serving as first-line therapy.

# 10. POSTRESUSCITATION CARE


## 10.1. GENERAL PRINCIPLES
**Fundamental approach to care after return of spontaneous circulation.**

![image.png](ACLS_BCLS_image_7.png_18edd4.png)

* **Goal**: Optimize organ function, prevent secondary injury (esp. brain), treat cause of arrest
* **Major Mortality Factors**: Primary disease progression, anoxic brain injury
* **Improved Survival Factors**: Correcting reversible causes, early reperfusion (e.g., PCI for MI), aggressive supportive care, transport to capable center (PCI, TTM, neuro ICU)
* **Clinical Tools**: Standardized order sets/checklists improve care consistency
* *Diagram takeaway*: Post-ROSC care involves managing airway/ventilation, circulation (hemodynamics), disability (neurologic protection/assessment), exposure (temperature control), and diagnosing/treating the underlying cause

**Bottom Line:** Post-resuscitation care requires a comprehensive, systematic approach addressing multiple organ systems simultaneously, with special focus on neurologic protection, hemodynamic stabilization, and identifying and treating the underlying cause of arrest.

## 10.2. CARDIAC CONSIDERATIONS
**Management of cardiac function and circulatory support after resuscitation.**

* **Global Myocardial Stunning**: Common post-arrest; decreased function may require inotropic/vasopressor support
* **Cause of Death Differences**:
	* *IHCA*: More deaths from withdrawal of support (comorbidities), refractory shock, recurrent arrest
	* *OHCA*: More deaths from neurologic injury leading to withdrawal of support
* **Hemodynamic Goals**: Maintain appropriate MAP (often 65-80 mmHg) to support organ perfusion, especially cerebral

**Bottom Line:** Post-arrest cardiac dysfunction is common due to global myocardial stunning, often requiring hemodynamic support while the heart recovers, with targeted interventions to address the primary cardiac cause in patients with cardiac etiology.

## 10.3. NEUROLOGIC CONSIDERATIONS
**Strategies to protect the brain and assess neurologic recovery after resuscitation.**

* **Cerebral Reperfusion Injury**: Complex process involving no-reflow, hyperemia, then hypoperfusion
* **Brain Injury Management**: Key focus of post-arrest care. Aggressive support improves neurologic outcomes
* **Seizure Management**:
	* Nonconvulsive seizures are common (even with TTM)
	* Continuous/frequent EEG recommended for comatose patients. Treat seizures promptly
* **Intracranial Pressure (ICP)**: Usually not elevated initially, but severe injury can cause edema/↑ICP later

**Bottom Line:** Post-arrest brain injury involves complex pathophysiology including reperfusion phenomena, with aggressive neuroprotective strategies, seizure detection/management, and delayed prognostication critical to improving outcomes.

## 10.4. SUPPORTIVE CARE STRATEGIES
**Comprehensive approach to organ system support after resuscitation.**

* **Duration of Support**: Support comatose patients fully for ≥72 hours post-ROSC (especially if TTM used) before detailed neuroprognostication
* **Ventilation Management**:
	* Optimize oxygenation (Target SpO₂ 94-98%). Avoid hyperoxia
	* Optimize ventilation (Target PaCO₂ 35-45 mmHg). Avoid hypocapnia (causes cerebral vasoconstriction). Use lung-protective ventilation strategies
* **Symptom Management**: Treat pain, agitation, shivering aggressively (may require sedation/paralysis)
* **Hemodynamic Management**:
	* Avoid hypotension (MAP < 65 mmHg often targeted, but may need higher MAP e.g., 80-100 mmHg initially to ensure cerebral perfusion)
	* Avoid significant hypertension. Cerebral autoregulation is impaired post-arrest
* **Glucose Management**:
	* Avoid both hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia
	* Target glucose range often 140-180 mg/dL. Strict control (<110) not shown beneficial and increases hypoglycemia risk
* **Oxygenation**: Avoid hyperoxia (titrate FiO₂ down as tolerated to maintain SpO₂ goal)
* **Pharmacologic Brain Preservation**: No specific drug proven beneficial (**THIOPENTAL**, Ca-channel blockers ineffective)

**Bottom Line:** Post-arrest supportive care requires careful management of ventilation (avoiding both hyperoxia and hypocapnia), hemodynamics (avoiding hypotension and excessive hypertension), glycemic control, and symptom management while maintaining full support pending neurological assessment.

## 10.5. TARGETED TEMPERATURE MANAGEMENT (TTM)
**Therapeutic hypothermia and temperature control strategies after resuscitation.**

* **Evidence**: First intervention proven to improve neurologic outcome after OHCA VF arrest. Benefit likely extends to other rhythms/IHCA
* **Current Recommendations**: Maintain temperature between 32-36°C continuously for at least 24 hours for comatose adult ROSC patients. Prevent fever (>37.7°C) for at least 72 hours
* **Intraoperative Arrest**: Optimal TTM strategy less clear; may increase infection risk
* **Implementation**: Active cooling followed by controlled rewarming (0.25-0.5°C/hour) to prevent rebound hyperthermia

**Bottom Line:** Targeted temperature management (32-36°C for ≥24 hours) is a key neuroprotective strategy for comatose post-arrest patients, with fever prevention for at least 72 hours, representing one of the few post-arrest interventions with proven neurological benefit.

## 10.6. PROGNOSTICATION
**Assessment of neurologic recovery potential after cardiac arrest.**

* **Timing**: Delay detailed neuroprognostication until ≥72 hours after ROSC (longer if sedatives/paralytics used or TTM applied)
* **Neurological Examination**:
	* Poor motor response (no response or extensor posturing) at 72h is concerning but has false-positive rate
	* 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: Do not base withdrawal decisions solely on motor exam early on
* **Reliable Poor Prognostic Signs** (at ≥72 hours, off sedation):
	* Absent pupillary light reflex
	* Absent corneal reflex
	* Absent N20 wave on somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEPs)
	* Status myoclonus persisting >30 min
* **Confirmatory Data**: EEG (burst suppression, status epilepticus), Imaging (CT/MRI showing diffuse anoxic injury), Biomarkers (persistently high Neuron-Specific Enolase - NSE). Multimodal assessment is key

**Bottom Line:** Neurological prognostication should be delayed until at least 72 hours post-arrest and use a multimodal approach incorporating physical examination, electrophysiological studies, imaging, and biomarkers rather than relying on any single finding.

# 11. RESUSCITATION IN THE OPERATING ROOM


## 11.1. UNIQUE FEATURES
**Distinct characteristics of cardiac arrest and resuscitation in the perioperative setting.**

* **Differences from other settings**:
	* Patient monitored
	* Arrest often witnessed
	* Cause often known/anticipated (related to surgery/anesthesia)
	* Resuscitative team (anesthesia, surgery, nursing) immediately present
	* Higher success rate than OHCA/general IHCA
* **Common Arrest Rhythms**: PEA often more common than VF
* **Approach**: Can tailor interventions to likely cause, rather than strictly following generic algorithm. Failure to anticipate/manage = "Failure to Rescue"

**Bottom Line:** OR resuscitation differs from other settings due to continuous monitoring, witnessed arrest, immediate availability of skilled personnel, and often readily identifiable causes, allowing for targeted interventions with higher success rates.

## 11.2. COMMON CAUSES OF OR CARDIAC ARREST
**Specific etiologies of cardiac arrest in the perioperative setting.**

* **Obvious**: Hypoxia (airway/ventilation issue), Hemorrhage
* **Less Obvious / Anesthesia-Related**:
	* Anaphylaxis
	* Malignant Hyperthermia (MH)
	* Hyperkalemia (e.g., **SUCCINYLCHOLINE** in susceptible patient, massive transfusion)
	* Hypocalcemia (massive transfusion)
	* Local Anesthetic Systemic Toxicity (LAST)
	* High spinal/epidural
	* Drug error/overdose
* **Surgical/Medical**:
	* Tension pneumothorax
	* Cardiac tamponade
	* Pulmonary embolism (air, thrombus, cement, fat)
	* Myocardial ischemia/infarction (cardiogenic shock)
	* Electrolyte abnormalities

**Bottom Line:** Perioperative cardiac arrest has unique causes including anesthesia-related factors (drug reactions, regional anesthesia complications, LAST) and surgical factors (hemorrhage, embolism, pneumothorax) that require specific recognition and management approaches.

## 11.3. CRISIS RESOURCE MANAGEMENT (CRM)
**Principles of team coordination and resource utilization during OR emergencies.**

* **Concept**: Applying principles from aviation (teamwork, communication, leadership, resource utilization) to medical crises. Anesthesiology was an early adopter
* **Challenges in OR Arrest**: Requires rapid shift in focus for entire team, overcoming surprise, coordinating roles in a potentially chaotic environment (sterile field, patient positioning)
* **Critical Success Factors**: Planning, Leadership (clear designation), Communication (closed-loop), Situational Awareness, Resource Allocation, Task Management
* **Essential Skills**: Immediate high-quality BLS (may be difficult due to OR environment), rapid defibrillation access/use, clear communication
* **Preparation**:
	* Know location of code cart/defibrillator/difficult airway cart
	* Know institutional process for calling for extra help
	* Regular team simulation/practice (mock codes)
	* Individual skill maintenance

**Bottom Line:** Crisis Resource Management applies principles of teamwork, communication, and resource utilization to optimize emergency response in the OR, requiring leadership, role clarity, and regular practice to overcome the unique challenges of perioperative arrests.

## 11.4. ETHICAL ISSUES: DNR ORDERS IN THE OR
**Ethical considerations regarding Do Not Resuscitate orders in surgical patients.**

* **Ethical Principles**: Beneficence, Nonmaleficence, Autonomy (patient's right to refuse treatment, including CPR)
* **DNR Context**: CPR is unique - requires an order *not* to perform it. Standard of care otherwise
* **DNR in OR**:
	* Historically controversial, often automatically suspended
	* Current standard (ASA, ACS, AORN): DNR orders should NOT be automatically rescinded. Requires mandatory "Reconsideration"
	* Discuss with patient/surrogate preoperatively: goals of care, specific interventions acceptable/unacceptable during perioperative period
* **Clarifying "Resuscitation"**: Many routine OR interventions (vasopressors, fluids, ventilation) could be seen as resuscitative. Discussion should clarify if DNR applies *only* to chest compressions/defibrillation or broader support
* **Approach**:
	* Required discussion for patients with existing DNR orders
	* Document discussion and agreed-upon plan clearly (e.g., "Full code", "Modified code - specify limitations", "DNR remains active")
	* If DNR suspended, specify duration (e.g., intraop only, until PACU discharge). Update orders postoperatively if needed
* 🔑 **HY**: Preoperative discussion about DNR status is essential for patients with existing orders. The standard is reconsideration, not automatic suspension. Document the plan clearly

**Bottom Line:** DNR orders in the perioperative setting require explicit reconsideration through discussion with the patient/surrogate rather than automatic suspension, with clear documentation of the agreed approach and its duration, respecting patient autonomy while acknowledging the unique context of surgery.

## 12.1. WEIGHT ESTIMATION AND WETFLAG ACRONYM
**Methods for rapid estimation of weight and critical parameters in pediatric emergencies.**

* **Weight estimation formulas**:
	* Infant <1 year = (Age/2) + 4
		* a 6m old would be 7kg
	* Child 1 - 5 years = (Age +4) x 2

	* Child 6 - 12 years = (Age x3) + 7
		* A 10 year old would be 37kg
* **WETFLAG acronym components**:
	* **W**eight: Using above formulas
	* **E**nergy (for defibrillation): 4 joules per kilogram
	* **T**ube size: (Age/4) + 4
	* **F**luids: 10ml/kg bolus (for resuscitation)
	* **L**orazepam: 0.1mg/kg
	* **A**drenaline: 0.1 ml/kg of 1:10 000
	* **G**lucose: 2 ml/kg of 10% glucose

## 12.2. PEDIATRIC ALSO ALGORITHM
**Summary of the standard pediatric advanced life support protocol.**

* **Check for response**
* **Assess airway**
* **Look, feel listen for 10 seconds**
* **Five rescue breaths**
* **Check carotid pulse for ten seconds**

**Bottom Line:** The Pediatric Also Algorithm emphasizes airway and ventilation interventions before compressions, reflecting the primarily respiratory etiology of most pediatric arrests, with specific protocols for rhythm assessment and appropriate interventions.

## 12.3. REVERSIBLE CAUSES OF CARDIAC ARREST

* **4H**:
	* Hypoxia
	* Hypovolaemia
	* Hypothermia
	* Hyperkalaemia
* **4T**:
	* Tension pneumothorax
	* Toxins
	* Tamponade
	* Thromboembolism

**Bottom Line:** Reversible causes of pediatric cardiac arrest are categorized as 4Hs and 4Ts, with systematic consideration of these causes essential during resuscitation to identify and address potentially correctable underlying conditions.

## 12.4. INTRAOSSEOUS ACCESS
**Bone marrow access for emergency medication and fluid delivery in children.**

* **Suitable locations for gaining intraosseous access on a child**:
	* Tibial: Anteromedial aspect of the tibia 3cm distal to the tuberosity
	* Femoral: Lateral aspect 3cm proximal to lateral condyle
	* Note that humeral and sternal access are not permitted in children
	* 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: Proximal humerus provides fastest transit time in adults but is not used in paediatrics
* **Contraindications to IO access**:
	* Absolute:
		* Bone trauma
		* Previous IO insertion at that site
		* Overlying infection
	* Relative:
		* Prosthesis of limb (distal to insertion site)
		* Osteogenesis imperfecta
* **Complications of IO access**:
	* Extravasation
	* Subperiosteal infusion
	* Physeal plate injury
	* Fracture
	* Haematoma
	* Compartment syndrome
	* Local infection
	* Fat and bone micro emboli

**Bottom Line:** Intraosseous access provides critical vascular access in pediatric emergencies when IV access is challenging, with specific anatomical sites, contraindications, and potential complications requiring consideration.

## 12.5. PEDIATRIC BLOOD VOLUME
**Age-specific estimation of circulating blood volume in pediatric patients.**

* **Neonate = 85 - 90 ml/kg**
* **Infant = 75 - 80 ml/kg**
* **Child = 70 - 75 ml/kg**

**Bottom Line:** Pediatric blood volume varies by age, with neonates having the highest volume per kilogram and older children approaching adult values, critical knowledge for fluid resuscitation and blood loss assessment.

## 12.6. FOREIGN BODY ASPIRATION MANAGEMENT
**Assessment and management of aspirated foreign bodies in children.**

* **Gold standard investigation for diagnosis of tracheal and bronchial foreign body aspiration**:
	* Bronchoscopy
	* Allows visualisation and potential retrieval
	* Usually a chest or abdominal xray are performed first
	* If a radio-opaque foreign body is seen on chest xray, it may also be located in the:
		* pharynx
		* larynx
		* oesophagus
* **Most common signs on a chest radiograph suggesting inhalation of a foreign body in a child**:
	* Unilateral Obstructive Emphysema
		* Active inspiration is less affected than passive expiration, meaning gas trapping and hyper expansion occurs on the effected side
	* Atelectasis
	* Pneumonia
	* Mediastinal Shift
	* Radio-opaque foreign body
		* It is actually relatively rare to see the foreign body itself
		* The xray can be normal in up to 40% of children with foreign body aspiration
* **Key considerations for anaesthesia in aspiration**:
	* Anaesthesia should not be delayed until fasted in a case of known bronchial foreign body aspiration, as it could migrate to the trachea
	* Children under one should have chest thrusts rather than abdominal thrusts
	* If a gas induction is necessary, avoid nitrous oxide as it can worsen gas trapping and cause a pneumothorax or pulmonary herniation
	* Tracheal deviation is an unreliable sign in foreign body aspiration
	* Ideally avoid paralysis as positive pressure ventilation can worsen gas trapping and barotrauma
	* Right main bronchus is:
		* wider
		* steeper
		* lower

**Bottom Line:** Foreign body aspiration management requires careful radiographic assessment (recognizing that normal x-rays don't exclude aspiration), followed by bronchoscopy for definitive diagnosis and treatment, with specific anesthetic considerations including avoidance of prolonged fasting, nitrous oxide, and paralysis.

# 13. SEPSIS AND SEPTIC SHOCK MANAGEMENT
**Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2021 Guidelines**

## 13.1. DEFINITIONS
**Standardised definitions for sepsis and septic shock based on the Sepsis-3 consensus.**

### 13.1.1. SEPSIS (SEPSIS-3 DEFINITION)
**Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection.**

* **Operationalised definition**: Suspected or documented infection PLUS acute increase in SOFA score ≥2 points
* **Key concept**: Represents organ dysfunction, not just infection with inflammation
* **Mortality**: In-hospital mortality >10%
* **Evolution**: Replaced SIRS-based definition (2016) as SIRS lacked specificity

### 13.1.2. SEPTIC SHOCK
**Subset of sepsis with profound circulatory and cellular/metabolic dysfunction.**

* **Definition** (requires BOTH):
	* Persisting hypotension requiring **vasopressors** to maintain MAP ≥65 mmHg
	* Serum **lactate >2 mmol/L** despite adequate fluid resuscitation
* **Mortality**: In-hospital mortality >40%
* 🔑 **HY**: Both vasopressor requirement AND elevated lactate are required for septic shock diagnosis

**Bottom Line:** Sepsis-3 defines sepsis as infection-related organ dysfunction (SOFA ≥2), while septic shock requires vasopressor-dependent hypotension plus lactate >2 mmol/L, representing a distinct clinical entity with significantly higher mortality.

## 13.2. SCREENING AND EARLY IDENTIFICATION
**Systematic approaches to recognise sepsis for early intervention.**

### 13.2.1. qSOFA (QUICK SOFA) SCORE
**Bedside screening tool for identifying patients at risk of poor outcomes from sepsis.**

* **Components** (1 point each):
	* Respiratory rate ≥22/min
	* Altered mental status (GCS <15)
	* Systolic blood pressure ≤100 mmHg
* **Positive**: Score ≥2 indicates high risk of poor outcome
* **Characteristics**: More specific but LESS sensitive than SIRS
* ⚠️ **SSC 2021**: Recommends AGAINST qSOFA as single screening tool due to low sensitivity
* **Utility**: Useful for identifying deteriorating ward patients; not designed for early sepsis detection

### 13.2.2. SIRS CRITERIA
**Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome criteria - traditional sepsis screening.**

* **Criteria** (2 of 4 required):
	* Temperature >38°C or <36°C
	* Heart rate >90/min
	* Respiratory rate >20/min OR PaCO₂ <32 mmHg
	* WBC >12,000/mm³ or <4,000/mm³ or >10% immature bands
* **Sensitivity**: Higher than qSOFA for sepsis detection
* **Specificity**: Lower - many non-infectious conditions trigger SIRS
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: SIRS with suspected infection = Sepsis (under old definition); now requires organ dysfunction

### 13.2.3. SOFA SCORE
**Sequential Organ Failure Assessment - objective organ dysfunction measurement.**

| System | Score 0 | Score 1 | Score 2 | Score 3 | Score 4 |
|--------|---------|---------|---------|---------|---------|
| Respiration (PaO₂/FiO₂) | ≥400 | <400 | <300 | <200 + vent | <100 + vent |
| Coagulation (Platelets ×10³/μL) | ≥150 | <150 | <100 | <50 | <20 |
| Liver (Bilirubin mg/dL) | <1.2 | 1.2-1.9 | 2.0-5.9 | 6.0-11.9 | >12.0 |
| Cardiovascular | MAP ≥70 | MAP <70 | Dopa ≤5 or Dob | Dopa >5 or NE/E ≤0.1 | Dopa >15 or NE/E >0.1 |
| CNS (GCS) | 15 | 13-14 | 10-12 | 6-9 | <6 |
| Renal (Creatinine or UO) | <1.2 | 1.2-1.9 | 2.0-3.4 | 3.5-4.9 or <500mL/d | >5.0 or <200mL/d |

* **Interpretation**: SOFA ≥2 indicates significant organ dysfunction
* **Baseline**: Compare to baseline if known; acute change ≥2 defines sepsis
* 🔑 **HY**: Each 1-point increase in SOFA score increases mortality by approximately 10%

**Bottom Line:** Multiple screening tools exist for sepsis detection, with qSOFA being specific but insensitive, SIRS being sensitive but non-specific, and SOFA providing objective organ dysfunction quantification; no single tool is recommended as the sole screening method.

## 13.3. HOUR-1 BUNDLE
**Time-critical interventions to be initiated within 1 hour of sepsis recognition.**

* **Philosophy**: View sepsis and septic shock as medical emergencies requiring immediate action
* **Bundle concept**: Interventions with synergistic benefit when performed together

### 13.3.1. BUNDLE COMPONENTS

1. **Measure serum lactate**
	* Remeasure within 2-4 hours if initial lactate >2 mmol/L
	* Target: Decreasing lactate toward normal
	* Lactate clearance >10% associated with improved outcomes

2. **Obtain blood cultures** BEFORE antibiotics
	* ⚠️ Should NOT delay antibiotic administration >45 minutes
	* At minimum: 2 sets from different sites
	* Obtain from intravascular devices if present >48 hours

3. **Administer broad-spectrum antibiotics**
	* Timing stratified by clinical certainty (see 13.5)
	* Cover all likely pathogens
	* Review local antibiograms

4. **Begin rapid crystalloid administration** (30 mL/kg)
	* For hypotension OR lactate ≥4 mmol/L
	* Complete within first 3 hours
	* ⚠️ This is now a WEAK recommendation (downgraded from 2016)

5. **Apply vasopressors** for refractory hypotension
	* If hypotensive during or after fluid resuscitation
	* Target: MAP ≥65 mmHg
	* Start peripherally if central access not available

📋 **MNEMONIC: Hour-1 Bundle (LABS-V)**
* **L**actate measurement
* **A**ntibiotics (broad-spectrum)
* **B**lood cultures
* **S**aline/crystalloid 30 mL/kg
* **V**asopressors for MAP ≥65

**Bottom Line:** The Hour-1 Bundle represents time-critical interventions that should begin immediately upon sepsis recognition, with lactate measurement, blood cultures, antibiotics, crystalloid resuscitation, and vasopressors as indicated forming the core components.

## 13.4. FLUID RESUSCITATION
**Principles and practice of initial and ongoing volume therapy in sepsis.**

### 13.4.1. INITIAL FLUID RESUSCITATION

* **Volume**: At least 30 mL/kg IV crystalloid within first 3 hours
	* ⚠️ WEAK recommendation in 2021 (downgraded from STRONG in 2016)
	* Insufficient prospective trial evidence comparing volumes
	* May require adjustment based on clinical response
* **Timing**: Begin immediately for hypotension or lactate ≥4 mmol/L
* **Rate**: Rapid infusion; complete within 3 hours

### 13.4.2. CRYSTALLOID SELECTION

* **Recommendation**: Suggest **balanced crystalloids** over normal saline (0.9% NaCl)
* **Evidence**: SMART trial showed lower mortality with balanced solutions in sepsis
* **Balanced crystalloid examples**:
	* Lactated Ringer's (Hartmann's solution)
	* Plasma-Lyte
* **Rationale**: Normal saline associated with hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis and possible AKI
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: Lactated Ringer's has minimal lactate (~28 mmol/L) - does not significantly affect serum lactate measurement

### 13.4.3. COLLOIDS

* **Albumin**: May be reasonable when patients require substantial amounts of crystalloid
* **Hydroxyethyl starch (HES)**: Recommend AGAINST in sepsis - associated with increased AKI and mortality

### 13.4.4. ONGOING FLUID MANAGEMENT

* **Dynamic assessment preferred**:
	* Passive leg raise test (PLR) with stroke volume measurement
	* Pulse pressure variation (PPV) if mechanically ventilated
	* Stroke volume variation (SVV)
* **Avoid**:
	* Over-resuscitation → fluid overload, pulmonary oedema, IAH
	* Under-resuscitation → persistent hypoperfusion
* 🏥 **Clinical Practice**: No recommendation for liberal vs restrictive strategy after initial bolus

**Bottom Line:** Initial fluid resuscitation with 30 mL/kg crystalloid remains the recommendation, though now weak, with balanced crystalloids preferred over saline; dynamic measures should guide ongoing fluid therapy to avoid both under- and over-resuscitation.

## 13.5. ANTIMICROBIAL THERAPY
**Evidence-based approach to antibiotic selection and timing in sepsis.**

### 13.5.1. TIMING OF ADMINISTRATION

| Clinical Scenario | Timing Recommendation | Strength |
|-------------------|----------------------|----------|
| Septic shock | Immediately, ideally within 1 hour | STRONG |
| Sepsis (definite) | Immediately, ideally within 1 hour | STRONG |
| Possible sepsis WITH shock | Immediately, within 1 hour | STRONG |
| Possible sepsis WITHOUT shock | Rapid assessment, within 3 hours if infection likely | WEAK |
| Low infection likelihood, no shock | Defer antibiotics, continue monitoring | WEAK |

* 🔑 **HY**: Each hour delay in antibiotics after hypotension onset associated with ~7.6% increase in mortality

### 13.5.2. EMPIRIC ANTIBIOTIC SELECTION

* **Principles**:
	* Cover all likely pathogens based on clinical syndrome and local epidemiology
	* Adequate dose (consider PK changes in sepsis)
	* Review local antibiograms
	* Consider previous cultures, recent antibiotics, healthcare exposure

* **MRSA Coverage**:
	* Recommend for high-risk patients (prior MRSA, healthcare exposure, IV drug use)
	* Recommend AGAINST in low-risk patients

* **Double Gram-Negative Coverage**:
	* Suggest two agents for empiric therapy if high MDR risk
	* Recommend AGAINST double coverage once susceptibilities known
	* De-escalate to single agent when able

* **Antifungal Therapy**:
	* Suggest empiric antifungals only for high-risk patients (febrile neutropenia, abdominal surgery, prolonged broad-spectrum antibiotics)
	* Recommend AGAINST empiric antifungals in low-risk patients

### 13.5.3. ANTIBIOTIC DURATION AND DE-ESCALATION

* **Duration**: Shorter courses preferred over longer (typically 7-10 days for most infections)
* **Daily reassessment** for de-escalation
* **Procalcitonin**:
	* Recommend AGAINST using to guide initiation of antibiotics
	* Can be used to guide discontinuation (with clinical assessment)

### 13.5.4. PHARMACOKINETICS IN SEPSIS

* **Increased volume of distribution**: Higher loading doses may be needed
* **Altered protein binding**: Increased free fraction of highly bound drugs
* **Variable clearance**: May be increased (hyperdynamic) or decreased (organ dysfunction)
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: Consider extended/continuous infusions of time-dependent antibiotics (beta-lactams) in sepsis

**Bottom Line:** Antibiotic timing is critical in sepsis, with immediate administration required for septic shock and definite sepsis; empiric coverage should address likely pathogens with daily reassessment for de-escalation, and shorter courses are now preferred.

## 13.6. VASOPRESSOR THERAPY
**Selection and management of vasoactive medications in septic shock.**

### 13.6.1. FIRST-LINE VASOPRESSOR

* **Norepinephrine** is the first-line vasopressor (STRONG recommendation)
* **Evidence vs Dopamine**:
	* Lower mortality (RR 0.89; 95% CI 0.81-0.98)
	* Decreased arrhythmia risk (RR 0.48; 95% CI 0.40-0.58)
* **Mechanism**: α₁-agonism (vasoconstriction) with some β₁-agonism (inotropy)
* **Dosing**: Typically start at 0.05-0.1 mcg/kg/min, titrate to MAP ≥65 mmHg

### 13.6.2. SECOND-LINE VASOPRESSORS

* **Vasopressin**:
	* Add when norepinephrine dose reaches 0.25-0.5 mcg/kg/min
	* Fixed dose: Up to 0.03 U/min (0.04 U/min maximum)
	* Norepinephrine-sparing effect
	* May be particularly useful in vasodilatory shock refractory to catecholamines
* **Epinephrine**:
	* Add if MAP inadequate on norepinephrine + vasopressin
	* Higher arrhythmogenic potential than norepinephrine
	* ⚠️ May transiently increase lactate (aerobic glycolysis effect)

### 13.6.3. VASOPRESSOR ADMINISTRATION

* **NEW 2021**: Suggest starting vasopressors **peripherally** over delaying for central access
	* Peripheral initiation safe for short-term infusion (<6 hours)
	* Use large-bore, proximal veins (antecubital, external jugular)
	* Transition to central access when feasible
* **Central venous access** preferred for:
	* Prolonged vasopressor use
	* High doses
	* Multiple vasoactive agents

### 13.6.4. HAEMODYNAMIC TARGETS

| Parameter | Target | Evidence |
|-----------|--------|----------|
| MAP | ≥65 mmHg | STRONG recommendation |
| Higher MAP (80-85 mmHg) | NOT recommended | No mortality benefit, increased AF |
| Lactate | Decreasing trend | Guide for resuscitation adequacy |

* 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: Higher MAP targets (80-85 mmHg) may be considered in patients with chronic hypertension or reduced cerebral perfusion

### 13.6.5. AGENTS TO AVOID OR USE CAUTIOUSLY

* **Dopamine**: Only if norepinephrine unavailable; higher arrhythmia risk
* **Phenylephrine**: Pure α-agonist; may reduce cardiac output; consider only if tachyarrhythmia limits norepinephrine use
* **Low-dose dopamine for renal protection**: Recommend AGAINST - no benefit, potential harm

**Bottom Line:** Norepinephrine is the first-line vasopressor for septic shock, with vasopressin added second when norepinephrine dose is 0.25-0.5 mcg/kg/min; peripheral initiation is now acceptable to avoid delays, with a target MAP ≥65 mmHg.

## 13.7. CORTICOSTEROID THERAPY
**Role of steroids in septic shock management.**

### 13.7.1. INDICATIONS

* **Suggest** IV corticosteroids for septic shock with **ongoing vasopressor requirement** (WEAK)
* **Changed from 2016**: Previously recommended against routine use
* **Rationale**: Shortens duration of shock, but unclear mortality benefit

### 13.7.2. DOSING

* **Drug**: Hydrocortisone
* **Dose**: 200 mg/day total
* **Administration options**:
	* 50 mg IV every 6 hours OR
	* Continuous infusion 200 mg/24 hours
* **When to start**: Norepinephrine/epinephrine ≥0.25 mcg/kg/min for ≥4 hours
* **Duration**: Typically until shock resolved; taper vs abrupt discontinuation debated

### 13.7.3. RATIONALE AND EVIDENCE

* **Benefits**:
	* Faster shock resolution
	* Reduced vasopressor duration
	* Possible reduction in ICU and hospital length of stay
* **Risks**:
	* Hyperglycaemia (requires glucose monitoring)
	* Immunosuppression
	* Myopathy with prolonged use
* **Mortality**: Unclear effect - trend toward benefit but not statistically significant

### 13.7.4. RECOMMENDATIONS AGAINST

* ⚠️ High-dose/short-duration steroids (>400 mg/day hydrocortisone equivalent for <3 days)
* Routine use in all sepsis patients without shock

**Bottom Line:** IV hydrocortisone 200 mg/day is now suggested for septic shock with ongoing high-dose vasopressor requirement, representing a change from previous guidelines; it shortens shock duration though mortality benefit remains unclear.

## 13.8. SOURCE CONTROL
**Identification and management of the anatomical source of infection.**

* **Principles**:
	* Rapidly identify specific anatomical diagnosis requiring intervention
	* Implement source control as soon as medically and logistically practical
	* Evidence suggests advantage within 6-12 hours of diagnosis

### 13.8.1. SOURCE CONTROL INTERVENTIONS

* Drainage of abscess (percutaneous or surgical)
* Debridement of infected necrotic tissue
* Removal of infected devices (catheters, prostheses)
* Definitive control of contamination source (e.g., bowel perforation repair)

### 13.8.2. INTRAVASCULAR DEVICE REMOVAL

* Promptly remove potentially infected intravascular access devices
* Consider removal even before culture confirmation if high suspicion
* Replace at new site; do NOT exchange over guidewire

* 🏥 **Clinical Practice**: Balance urgency of source control against patient stability and procedural risks

**Bottom Line:** Source control is a fundamental component of sepsis management, with early intervention (ideally within 6-12 hours) and prompt removal of potentially infected devices improving outcomes.

## 13.9. ADDITIONAL MANAGEMENT CONSIDERATIONS
**Supportive care elements in sepsis and septic shock.**

### 13.9.1. GLUCOSE MANAGEMENT

* Target: Blood glucose <180 mg/dL (10 mmol/L)
* Avoid hypoglycaemia
* Strict glucose control (<110 mg/dL) NOT recommended - increases hypoglycaemia risk without benefit

### 13.9.2. VENOUS THROMBOEMBOLISM PROPHYLAXIS

* Recommend pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis unless contraindicated
* LMWH preferred over unfractionated heparin
* Add mechanical prophylaxis if pharmacologic contraindicated

### 13.9.3. STRESS ULCER PROPHYLAXIS

* Recommend for patients with risk factors for GI bleeding
* PPIs or H2-receptor antagonists

### 13.9.4. NUTRITION

* Initiate early enteral nutrition when feasible
* Avoid mandatory early parenteral nutrition

### 13.9.5. GOALS OF CARE

* Discuss goals of care and prognosis with patient/family within 72 hours
* Incorporate palliative care principles as appropriate

**Bottom Line:** Sepsis management extends beyond haemodynamic resuscitation to include glucose control, VTE prophylaxis, stress ulcer prophylaxis, early nutrition, and goals-of-care discussions as integral components of comprehensive care.

## 13.10. PERFORMANCE IMPROVEMENT
**Quality initiatives to improve sepsis outcomes.**

* **Sepsis performance improvement programmes** are strongly recommended
* **Meta-analysis** (50 studies): Programme implementation associated with:
	* Better bundle adherence
	* Reduced mortality (OR 0.66; 95% CI 0.61-0.72)

### 13.10.1. PROGRAMME COMPONENTS

* Sepsis screening protocols
* Education for healthcare providers
* Measurement of bundle performance
* Patient outcome tracking
* Quality improvement actions for identified opportunities

### 13.10.2. KEY METRICS

* Time to antibiotic administration
* Time to initial fluid bolus
* Lactate measurement compliance
* Blood culture collection before antibiotics
* 3-hour and 6-hour bundle compliance rates
* Mortality rates

**Bottom Line:** Sepsis performance improvement programmes with screening, education, and outcome measurement are strongly associated with reduced mortality, emphasising the importance of systematic approaches to sepsis care.

## 13.11. LONG-TERM OUTCOMES AND RECOVERY
**NEW 2021: Enhanced focus on post-sepsis care and survivorship.**

### 13.11.1. POST-ICU CARE

* Screen for economic and social support needs
* Medication reconciliation at discharge (STRONG)
* Follow-up assessment for physical, cognitive, and emotional problems
* Referral to appropriate rehabilitation services

### 13.11.2. POST-SEPSIS SYNDROME

* **Physical**: Weakness, fatigue, dyspnoea, chronic pain
* **Cognitive**: Memory impairment, concentration difficulties
* **Psychological**: Anxiety, depression, PTSD
* **Healthcare utilisation**: Increased readmission and mortality risk in year following sepsis

* 🔑 **HY**: Sepsis survivors have increased mortality for up to 2 years after discharge

**Bottom Line:** The 2021 guidelines place new emphasis on post-sepsis recovery, acknowledging that sepsis survivors face ongoing physical, cognitive, and emotional challenges requiring systematic follow-up and support.
