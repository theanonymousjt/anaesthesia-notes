# 1. ANESTHESIA WORKSTATION FUNDAMENTALS

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Anesthesia Equipment|Part A]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2: Equipment and Monitoring|Part B]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Systems-Based Practice|Part C]]

## 1.1. BASIC CONFIGURATION AND DEFINITION

**The anesthesia workstation is a comprehensive system for delivering anesthetics to patients, constituting multiple integrated components with extensive safety features.**

* **Definition**: The International Standards Organization (ISO) defines an anesthesia "workstation" as a system for administering anesthetics consisting of:
  * Anesthesia gas delivery system
  * Anesthetic breathing system
  * Required monitoring equipment
  * Alarm systems
  * Protection devices
* **Components and Functions**:
  * **Anesthesia machine**:
	* Receives gases (O₂, N₂O, air) from storage sites (central supply, tanks)
	* Creates gas mixture of known composition and flow rate
	* Delivers to concentration-calibrated vaporizer

  * **Vaporizer**:
	* Converts liquid anesthetic to gas
	* Adds agent in controlled concentration to gas mixture → fresh gas flow

  * **Patient breathing circuit**:
	* Delivers fresh gas flow to patient
	* Facilitates ventilatory exchange (spontaneous, assisted or controlled)

  * **Ventilator**:
	* May be bellows, piston, impeller, or jet type

  * **Waste gas scavenging system**:
	* Collects gas leaving circuit (unless FGF is sufficient to meet patient's O₂ and anesthetic gas requirements)
* **Essential Safety Features**:
  * Gas supply safety features:
	* Copper reinforced hoses to prevent compression and kinking
	* Color-coded equipment for each gas
	* Schrader valves at wall end unique to each gas
	* Non-interchangeable connection systems
	* Pin index system for cylinders
	* Bodok seal and yoke with safety valves
  
  * Monitoring systems:
	* Dual monitoring of FiO₂
	* Oxygen supply failure alarms
	* Pressure monitors and alarms
	* Ventilator disconnection detection

![image.png](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_image.png_79936a.png_fe1709.png)

**Bottom Line:** The anesthesia workstation is an integrated system with safety features that delivers medical gases and anesthetic agents to patients while providing ventilatory support and monitoring. Understanding its components and their functions is essential for safe anesthesia delivery.

## 1.2. WORKSTATION COMPONENTS OVERVIEW

**The anesthesia workstation contains three distinct pressure circuits that interface with each other to deliver controlled gas flow to the patient.**

![image.png](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_image_1.png_c10e6d.png_91a39f.png)

* **Three Pressure Circuits**:

  1. **High-Pressure Circuit (HPC)**:
	 * Includes cylinders and cylinder primary pressure regulators
	 * O₂ at 2200 pounds per square inch (psi) → first-stage pressure regulators → 45 psi

  2. **Intermediate-Pressure Circuit (IPC)**:
	 * From regulated cylinder supply to gas flow control valves
	 * Brings pressure from 45 psi (including pipeline sources at 50-55 psi) → second-stage pressure regulators → 14 psi (or 26 psi within the IPC)
	 * This regulator maintains a constant pressure to the oxygen flow control valve despite fluctuations in oxygen pipeline pressures
	 * Flow from oxygen flow control valve remains constant if oxygen supply pressure exceeds 14 psi

  3. **Low-Pressure Circuit (LPC)**:
	 * Flow control valves to common gas outlet
	 * Includes:
	   * Flow tubes (rotameters)
	   * Flow control systems
	   * Vaporizer manifold
	   * Vaporizers
	   * One-way outlet check valve
	 * Gas sources can be from pipeline (50 psi) or cylinder supply (O₂ - 2200 psi, N₂O - 745 psi)

**Bottom Line:** The anesthesia workstation contains three distinct pressure circuits (high, intermediate, and low) that progressively step down gas pressure from cylinders or pipeline sources through regulators to deliver controlled, safe gas flow to the patient.

# 2. PRESSURE CIRCUITS

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Anesthesia Equipment|Part A]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2: Equipment and Monitoring|Part B]]

## 2.1. HIGH-PRESSURE CIRCUIT (HPC)

**The high-pressure circuit handles the extremely high pressures found in gas cylinders and reduces them to manageable levels for the intermediate-pressure circuit.**

* **Components**:
  * Gas cylinders (O₂ at 2200 psi, N₂O at 745 psi)
  * Pin Index Safety System (PISS) on hanger yoke assembly
  * Cylinder pressure regulators
* **Pressure Regulation**:
  * First-stage pressure regulators reduce cylinder pressure to approximately 45 psi
  * This regulated pressure feeds into the intermediate-pressure circuit
* **Safety Features**:
  * Cylinder-specific connections prevent misconnection
  * Check valves prevent backflow between cylinders
  * Pressure gauges display cylinder pressures

**Bottom Line:** The high-pressure circuit safely manages the transition from extremely high cylinder pressures to the working pressures needed by the anesthesia machine, using specialized regulators and multiple safety features to prevent errors and equipment damage.

## 2.2. INTERMEDIATE-PRESSURE CIRCUIT (IPC)

**The intermediate-pressure circuit receives gas from either cylinders or pipeline sources and distributes it to various machine components at regulated pressures.**

* **Input Sources**:
  * Receives gas from cylinders (regulated to 45 psi)
  * Receives gas from pipelines (50-55 psi)
* **Second-Stage Regulation**:
  * Further reduces pressure to approximately 14 psi for flow control valves
  * Some gases (typically N₂O) may be regulated to 26 psi within the IPC
* **Distribution Paths**:
  * The IPC can direct gas to several destinations:
	* DISS auxiliary O₂ takeoff (for jet ventilation systems)
	* Supply drive gas for pneumatically powered bellows ventilators
	* Through regulators to auxiliary O₂ flowmeters (for nasal cannulas, etc.)
	* To O₂ low-pressure alarm sensors
	* To pressure-sensitive shutoff ('fail-safe') valves
	* To O₂ flush control valves
	* To gas flowmeters (in some machines via a second-stage regulator)
* **Safety Features**:
  * Maintains consistent pressure to ensure reliable performance of downstream components
  * Multiple second-stage regulators isolate different systems for reliability

**Bottom Line:** The intermediate-pressure circuit serves as the distribution hub of the anesthesia machine, ensuring consistent pressure delivery to all machine components while integrating multiple safety systems to prevent hypoxic mixtures if O₂ pressure falls.

## 2.3. LOW-PRESSURE CIRCUIT (LPC)

**The low-pressure circuit is the final stage before gas delivery to the patient, containing flow controls, vaporizers, and safety devices that prepare the final gas mixture.**

* **Components**:
  * Flow control valves (separate IPC from LPC)
  * Flow tubes (rotameters)
  * Vaporizer manifold and vaporizers
  * One-way outlet check valve (in some machines)
  * Common gas outlet
* **Operating Principles**:
  * Flow control valves adjust the flow of O₂ and N₂O
  * O₂ and N₂O flow control valves are linked by a proportioning system to prevent hypoxic mixtures
  * After leaving rotameter tubes, mixed gases enter common manifold → directed to vaporizer
  * Total fresh gas flow (FGF) + vapor flow moves toward common gas outlet
* **Safety Features**:
  * One-way check valve (in some machines) between vaporizers and common gas outlet prevents backflow during positive pressure ventilation
	* ⚠️ The presence or absence of this check valve affects which preoperative leak test (positive vs. negative pressure) is indicated
  * Oxygen flush connection joins the mixed-gas line between one-way check valve and common gas outlet
	* If activated, O₂ flows directly to common gas outlet at 35-75 L/min at 55 psi

**Bottom Line:** The low-pressure circuit contains the components that determine the final composition and flow rate of gases delivered to the patient. It incorporates flow measurement, anesthetic agent addition, and critical safety features to prevent delivery of hypoxic gas mixtures.

# 3. GAS SUPPLY SYSTEMS

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Anesthesia Equipment|Part A]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2: Equipment and Monitoring|Part B]]

## 3.1. PIPELINE SUPPLY SOURCES

**Pipeline systems provide a continuous centralized supply of medical gases to the anesthesia workstation at controlled pressures.**

* **Pipeline Problems**:
  * Pipeline crossovers can occur (wrong gas in pipeline)
  * If pipeline crossover is suspected, two corrective actions needed:
	1. Turn on backup O₂ cylinder	2. Disconnect pipeline supply	   * ⚠️ Essential because the machine will preferentially use the 50 psi pipeline supply source instead of the lower-pressure (45 psi) O₂ cylinder source if wall supply is not disconnected* **Pipeline System Components**:
  * Central supply source (bulk liquid storage or cylinder banks)  * Pressure regulators to maintain 50-55 psi  * Distribution piping throughout facility  * Zone valves for emergency shutoff  * Terminal outlets in each anesthetizing location* **Medical Gas Supply Sources**
  * **Oxygen**: Supplied from vacuum insulated evaporator (VIE) containing liquid oxygen at -150°C and pressure of 10 bar; provides 840 liters of gaseous oxygen per liter of liquid oxygen  * **Medical Air**: Supplied at 400 kPa (4 bar) for respiratory use; 700 kPa (7 bar) when used for surgical equipment* **Pipeline System Construction** 
  * Pipework is made of special high-quality copper alloy, which both prevents degradation of the gases and has bacteriostatic properties 
  * Fittings used are made from brass and are brazed rather than soldered 
  * Pipe size differs according to demand - 42-mm diameter pipes typically used from the manifold, smaller 15-mm tubes after repeated branching 

**Bottom Line:** Pipeline systems provide reliable gas supply at consistent pressures but require vigilance for rare but serious crossover events. When pipeline problems are suspected, providers must quickly shift to cylinder supply and disconnect the potentially contaminated pipeline connection.

## 3.2. WALL OUTLETS











**Wall outlets provide standardized, gas-specific connections between the facility pipeline system and the anesthesia workstation.**

* **Design Features**:
  * Gas-specific and standardized using Diameter Index Safety System (DISS)  * DISS provides threaded, non-interchangeable connections for medical gas lines to minimize risk of misconnection  * Color-coded hoses connect wall outlets to anesthesia machines:
	* Oxygen: White
	* Nitrous oxide: Blue
	* Medical air: Black/white collar
	* Suction: Yellow
	* Scavenging: Colorless
* **Construction**:
  * Copper reinforced hoses to prevent compression and kinking  * Color-coded equipment for each gas* **Safety Features**:
  * Schrader valves at wall end unique to each gas  * Non-interlocking screw thread system at machine end  * Check valve located downstream from inlet to prevent reverse flow of gases from machine to pipeline or atmosphere  
* **Installation Types** 
  * Outlets can be installed as flush-fitting units, surface-fitting units, on booms or pendants or suspended on a hose and gang mounted 
  * Flexible color-coded hoses connect the outlets to the anaesthetic machine 
  * Anaesthetic machine end should be permanently fixed using a nut and liner union in which the thread is gas specific and non-interchangeable (a non-interchangeable screw thread [NIST] is the British Standard)

## 3.3. CYLINDER SUPPLY SOURCES











**Cylinder supply sources provide backup or primary gas when pipeline supply is unavailable, with multiple safety systems to prevent misconnections.**

* **Cylinder Connection System**:
  * Medical gas cylinders attach to the anesthesia machine via the hanger yoke assembly  * Hanger yoke functions:
	* Orients and supports the cylinder	* Provides gas-tight seal using Bodok seal between tank and hanger yoke	* Ensures unidirectional gas flow into machine* **Pin Index Safety System (PISS)**:
  * Each hanger yoke equipped with PISS  * PISS prevents cylinder interchanging by ensuring only the correct gas cylinder can be attached  * Two metal pins on yoke assembly project into corresponding holes in cylinder valve  * Each gas has a specific and unique pin arrangement:
	* Oxygen: positions 2 and 5
	* Air: positions 1 and 5
	* Nitrous oxide: positions 3 and 5
* **Check Valve System**:
  * A check valve is located downstream from each cylinder in double-yoke assemblies  * Functions:
	* Minimizes gas transfer from higher pressure cylinder to lower pressure cylinder	* Allows exchange of empty cylinder while gas flow continues from other cylinder	* Minimizes leakage to atmosphere if one cylinder is absent* **Pressure Gauge**:
  * Cylinder supply pressure gauge located downstream from check valves  * Indicates pressure in cylinder with the higher pressure when two reserve cylinders are open simultaneously* **Color Coding**:
  * Cylinders are color-coded for easy identification:
	* Oxygen: Green
	* Medical air: Yellow
	* CO₂: Black
	* N₂O: Blue
	* Helium: Brown
* **Cylinder Components** 
  * Made of thin-walled, seamless molybdenum steel 
  * The top end (neck) ends in a tapered screw thread containing the valve 
  * Thread is sealed with a material that melts if the cylinder is exposed to intense heat, allowing gas to escape and reducing explosion risk 
  * Plastic disc around the neck indicates when the cylinder was last examined and tested 
  * Cylinders are manufactured in different sizes (A-J); anesthesia machines typically use size E cylinders 
  * Lightweight cylinders can be made from aluminum alloy with a fibreglass covering in an epoxy resin matrix 
* **Cylinder Testing and Maintenance** 
  * Tested every 5-10 years by manufacturers with:
	* Internal endoscopic examination 
	* Flattening, bend and impact tests on at least one cylinder in every 100 
	* Pressure testing at 22,000 kPa (50% above normal working pressure) 
	* Tensile testing on at least one cylinder in every 100

## 3.4. OXYGEN E-CYLINDERS











**Oxygen E-cylinders provide a critical backup supply of oxygen when pipeline systems are unavailable, with volume proportional to cylinder pressure.**

* **Cylinder Pressure and Volume Principles**:
  * Based on Boyle's Law: For a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature, product of pressure × volume is constant (P₁ × V₁ = P₂ × V₂)  * Volume of O₂ available in cylinder is directly proportional to cylinder pressure  * Volume calculation for full E-cylinder:
	* E-cylinder volume is 4.8L
	* Pressure when full is 2000 psig + 14.7 psia (atmospheric pressure) = 2,014.7 psi
	* Formula: (2,014.7 × 4.8)/14.7 = 658L of O₂ at atmospheric pressure* **Estimating Depletion Time**:
  * Approximate remaining time (hours) = Oxygen cylinder pressure (psig)/(200 × oxygen flow rate [L/min])  * Example: E-cylinder at 1,000 psig used at 5 L/min would be depleted in approximately 1 hour  * ⚠️ Use of pneumatically driven mechanical ventilators dramatically increases oxygen utilization rates and decreases remaining time  * 💡 Electrically powered piston-type anesthesia ventilators (Dräger Fabius, Apollo) don't impact oxygen usage rates and may be preferable when oxygen supply is limited* **Integrated Valve and Regulator**:
  * O₂ E-cylinders have an integrated valve and regulator allowing:
	* Controlled delivery of O₂ at flows of 25 L/min or less	* 50 psig delivery through a DISS connection* **Manufacturing and Maintenance**
  * Made from molybdenum steel  * BOC testing includes:
	* Annual inspection inside and outside
	* Hydraulic testing to 50% above working pressure
	* Every 5 years for composite cylinders
	* Every 10 years for steel cylinders
  * Plastic ring around neck indicates next test date  * The British Compressed Gases Association recommends pressure regulators be refurbished or replaced every five years* **Gas Laws and Physical States** 
  * **Boyle's Law**: At a constant temperature, pressure is inversely proportional to volume of a gas (P ∝ 1/V) 
  * **Charles' Law**: At a constant pressure, volume of a gas is directly proportional to its temperature (V ∝ T) 
  * **Gay-Lussac's Law**: At a fixed volume and mass of a gas, the pressure is directly proportional to its temperature (P ∝ T) 
  * **Avogadro's Law**: At a constant temperature and pressure, volume of gas is directly proportional to the amount of gas (V ∝ n) 
  * A full oxygen cylinder at atmospheric pressure can deliver 130 times its capacity of oxygen 
  * A typical size E full oxygen cylinder delivering 4 L/min will last for 2 hours and 50 minutes but will last only 45 minutes when delivering 15 L/min 

![image.png](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_image_4.png_271387.png_f9661f.png)

**Bottom Line:** Oxygen E-cylinders provide a critical backup supply with volume directly proportional to pressure. Cylinder duration can be estimated mathematically, but pneumatic ventilators significantly reduce duration. Regular testing and maintenance help ensure reliability when needed.

## 3.5. NITROUS OXIDE E-CYLINDER











**Nitrous oxide is stored as both liquid and gas in cylinders, with pressure remaining constant until all liquid has vaporized, making pressure gauges unreliable for determining remaining content.**

* **N₂O Chemical Characteristics**:
  * Molecular weight: 44 atomic mass units (AMU)  * Boiling point: -88°C at 760 mmHg (14.7 psia)  * Critical temperature: 36.5°C (highest temperature at which N₂O can exist in liquid form)  * Critical pressure: 1054 psig (highest pressure required to maintain N₂O as liquid)* **Cylinder Storage Characteristics**:
  * E-cylinders factory-filled to 90-95% with liquid N₂O with vapor phase above liquid  * Liquid agent is in equilibrium with vapor or gas phase  * Pressure exerted by gaseous N₂O is its saturated vapor pressure (SVP) at ambient temperature  * At 20°C, SVP of N₂O is 750 psig (approximately 5200 kPa or 39,000 mmHg)* **Volume Determination Challenge**:
  * Volume of N₂O gas in tank cannot be determined by N₂O tank pressure gauge  * Full tank of N₂O contains 1600L of gas at 1 atm  * As long as liquid N₂O is present and ambient temperature remains at 20°C, pressure remains at 750 psig  * ⚠️ Because N₂O is a vapor and not a gas, it does not obey Boyle's law, and volume remaining cannot be calculated from tank pressure* **Methods to Determine Remaining Volume**:
  * Get mass of liquid + gas by weighing the tank and subtracting weight of empty tank  * Once all liquid N₂O has been used (tank contains only vapor), pressure begins to decrease as N₂O is used  * When last drop of liquid has evaporated, nearly one-quarter of a full cylinder remains (~400L)  * From then on, tank pressure gauge reading will fall as N₂O continues to be utilized* **Supply Integration**:
  * N₂O supply from tank enters hanger yoke at 750 psig (at 20°C) → first stage regulator → 40-45 psig  * Also has PISS and check valve (to prevent back leakage)  * N₂O supply from pipeline usually comes from H cylinders (each has 16,000L of gas at atmospheric pressure) at pressures regulated to 50 psig* **Cylinder Information**
  * Stored in french blue cylinders as liquid with vapor phase above  * Filling ratio in UK is 0.75 (weight of liquefied gas compared to weight of water that would fill cylinder at 15°C)  * Information engraved on cylinder:
	* Test pressure
	* Test date
	* Chemical formula
	* Tare weight (weight of empty cylinder)
* **Physical Properties** 
  * At 20°C the nitrous oxide cylinder pressure is 750 psig (approximately 5200 kPa or 39,000 mmHg) 
  * The filling ratio for nitrous oxide in UK is 0.75, reduced to 0.67 in hotter climates to avoid dangerous pressure increases 
  * Filling ratio is the weight of the fluid in the cylinder divided by the weight of water required to fill the cylinder 
  * At constant temperature, nitrous oxide cylinder pressure remains constant as vapor is produced to replace what is used, until all liquid is depleted 
  * Temperature in the cylinder can decrease due to loss of latent heat of vaporization, forming ice on the outside of the cylinder 

**Bottom Line:** Nitrous oxide is stored primarily as a liquid in cylinders with a vapor phase at its saturated vapor pressure, maintaining constant pressure until all liquid is gone. This makes pressure gauges unreliable for volume assessment until the liquid is completely exhausted; weighing is the only reliable method to determine remaining content.

## 3.6. OXYGEN SUPPLY PRESSURE FAILURE SAFETY DEVICES











**Multiple safety devices in the anesthesia workstation minimize the risk of delivering hypoxic gas mixtures if oxygen supply pressure decreases.**

* **Safety Device Types**:

  1. **Pneumatic and Electronic Alarm Devices**:
	 * Older machines may include alarms that activate when O₂ supply pressure decreases below a threshold (30 psig)
	 * Modern machines include oxygen failure alarms that activate if pipeline supply drops below 200 kPa
  2. **O₂ Failure Cutoff ("Fail-safe") Valves**:
	 * Present in gas line supplying each flowmeter except O₂
	 * Controlled by O₂ supply pressure → if decreases, will shut off/proportionately decrease supply of all other gases
	 * ⚠️ Misnomer - does not prevent administration of hypoxic mixture in all circumstances
	 * On older machines, fail-safe valve operates in a threshold manner - either open or closed

	 ![image.png](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_image_5.png_b4e047.png_3ac2ea.png)

  3. **Second-stage O₂ Pressure Regulator**:
	 * Ranges from 12-19 psig
	 * Output from O₂ flowmeter remains constant when O₂ supply pressure exceeds threshold
* **Additional Safety Features**
  * Backup oxygen supplies:
	* Wall oxygen for primary supply	* Cylinder mounted on anesthesia machine for backup  * Dual monitoring of FiO₂:
	* Both monitor and ventilator screens display oxygen levels
**Bottom Line:** Multiple redundant safety systems work together to prevent delivery of hypoxic gas mixtures if oxygen supply pressure decreases. These include alarms, automatic shutoff of other gases, and backup oxygen supply systems with dual monitoring.

## 3.7. FLOWMETER ASSEMBLIES











**Flowmeter assemblies precisely control and measure gas flow to the common gas outlet through tapered flow tubes with indicator floats.**

* **Flowmeter Types**:
  * **Traditional Assemblies**:
	* Flow control needle valve regulates amount of gas flow into a tapered, transparent flow tube known as Thorpe tube
	* Thorpe tube features:
	  * Tapered design (small cross-sectional area at lower/low flow end, larger at upper/high flow end)
	  * Mobile indicator float inside flow tube shows amount of flow passing through flow control valve
	  * Quantity of flow indicated on scale associated with flow tube
  
  * **Electronic Flow Sensors**:
	* Used in contemporary workstations
	* Digital display of gas flows
	
* **Operating Principles of Conventional Flowmeters**:
  * Opening flow control needle valve allows gas to travel through space between float and flow tube (annular space)
  * Indicator float hovers freely in equilibrium position → upward force from gas flow = downward force on float from gravity at given flow rate
  * Float moves to new equilibrium position when flow changes
  * These flowmeters are constant-pressure variable-orifice flowmeters because pressure decrease across float remains constant for all positions in tube
  * Flow tubes are tapered - smallest diameter at bottom, largest at top
  * Annular space between float and inner wall varies with float position (variable orifice)
  * Flow through annular space can be laminar or turbulent depending on flow rate, viscosity (laminar) and density (turbulent)
* **Flow Characteristics and Physics** 
  * Flowmeters on anesthesia machines have an accuracy of about ±2.5% 
  * At low flow rates, the clearance is longer and narrower, acting as a tube, resulting in laminar flow dependent on gas viscosity (Poiseuille's law) 
  * At high flow rates, the clearance is shorter and wider, acting as an orifice, resulting in turbulent flow dependent on gas density 
  * The top of the bobbin has slits (flutes) that cause it to rotate as gas flows past, with a visible dot indicating rotation to show the bobbin isn't stuck 
  * Reading is taken from the top of the bobbin, or from the midpoint when a ball is used

## 3.8. COMPONENTS OF FLOW METER ASSEMBLY











**The flowmeter assembly consists of two primary components: the flow control valve assembly and the flowmeter subassembly, with multiple integrated safety features.**

1. **Flow Control Valve Assembly**:
   * Components:
	 * Flow control knob
	 * Needle valve
	 * Valve seat
	 * Pair of valve stops
   * Function:
	 * Receives pneumatic input from pipeline (50 psig) or second-stage pressure regulator
	 * Needle valve position in valve seat changes size of orifice when adjusted
	 * Valve turned counterclockwise → gas flow increases
	 * Valve turned clockwise → gas flow decreases
	 * Valve stops prevent damage to needle valve and valve seat
   * Safety Features:
	 * O₂ flow control knob is physically distinguishable from other gas knobs
	 * Other knobs are color-coded for gas with name/chemical formula labeled
	 * Knobs are recessed or protected with shield/barrier to minimize accidental changes
	 * If single gas has two flow tubes, they're arranged in series and controlled by a single flow control needle valve
   * Contemporary Workstations:
	 * Flowmeters replaced by electronic control panels with "soft keys"
	 * Operation: (1) select gas/agent with soft key; (2) turn selector knob to adjust flow; (3) press selector to confirm

2. **Flowmeter Subassembly**:
   * Components:
	 * Flow tube
	 * Indicator float with float stops
	 * Indicator scale
   * Flow Tube:
	 * Contemporary flow tubes made of glass
	 * Tapered design
	 * Fine flow tube indicates flow from ~200 mL/min to 1 L/min
	 * Coarse flow tube indicates flow from ~1 mL/min to 10-12 L/min
	 * Two tubes connected in series and supplied from single flow control valve
   * Indicator Floats and Float Stops:
	 * Several float types: plumb-bob floats, rotating skirted floats, and ball floats
	 * Flow read at top of plumb-bob and skirted floats; at center of ball floats
	 * Float stops prevent float from ascending too high or descending too low
	 * Bottom float stop provides central foundation for indicator
   * Scale:
	 * Gradations for equal flow rate increments are closer together at top of scale (annular space increases more rapidly than internal diameter)
	 * Rib guides minimize this compression effect to create nearly linear scale

3. **Flowmeter Problems**:
   * Leaks:
	 * Significant hazard as flowmeters are located downstream from all machine safety devices except O₂ analyzer
	 * Can occur at O-ring junctions between glass flow tube and metal manifold or cracked/broken tube
	 * ⚠️ To reduce dangerous hypoxia from leaks, flowmeter sequence arranges O₂ in downstream position (except if O₂ flow tube is leaking)

   * Inaccuracy from dirt/static electricity (causing float to stick)

4. **Safety Features**
   * Rotameter bobbin has vanes to make it spin and a dot on the side to show it hasn't become stuck   * Anti-static coating prevents sticking in the tube   * Oxygen rotameter is the last gas in the row, preventing hypoxic mixtures in case of upstream leak   * Oxygen rotameter has color-coded and serrated dial that sticks out further than other gases for identification   * Wire stop at top of rotameter tube prevents bobbin disappearing off scale   * Generally very accurate with error of ±2.5%
**Bottom Line:** The flowmeter assembly combines precise flow control with accurate measurement through integrated valve and tube components. Multiple safety features minimize accidental changes, prevent hypoxic mixtures, and ensure reliability, with specialized design elements to make oxygen controls distinctive and difficult to adjust accidentally.

## 3.9. ELECTRONIC GAS FLOW CONTROL











**Modern anesthesia workstations incorporate electronic gas flow control systems that provide precise digital control while maintaining mechanical backup systems.**

* **Power Requirements**:
  * Electronic display requires electrical power for precise visualization of gas flow  * ⚠️ Set gas flows continue uninterrupted if electrical power fails (flow control valves are mechanical)* **Flow Indicators**:
  * Contemporary machines lack individual flow tubes for each gas  * Small conventional pneumatic "fresh gas" or "total flow" indicator provides estimate of total gas quantity flowing from all valves to common gas outlet  * Backup indicator functions even during total power failure* **Control Mechanism**:
  * When gas mixture and flows are set, workstation computer controls opening of each needle valve by adjusting current to solenoid coils  * Traditional needle valve flows: turn counterclockwise to increase flow  * Contemporary machines: turn clockwise to increase and require confirmation of setting  * Backup system still uses mechanical needle valve and rotameter flow tube
![image.png](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_image_12.png_e0dc3b.png_a0355e.png)

* **Automated FGF Optimization**:
  * **Low Flow Wizard (LFW) - Dräger Apollo**:
	* Determines minimum FGF by calculating difference between inspiratory and expiratory minute volume
	* Calculates anesthetic uptake using inspired/expired anesthetic, N₂O, and CO₂ concentrations
	* O₂ uptake calculated using CO₂ uptake (gas analysis system determines CO₂ with greater precision than O₂)
	* ⚠️ During CO₂ insufflation, if ETCO₂ increases, calculated O₂ uptake will increase, and LFW will recommend higher FGF
	* PEEP application or removal can affect LFW calculations
  
  * **GE 'ecoFLOW'**:
	* Determines minimum O₂ flow required to maintain FiO₂
	* Calculation uses patient O₂ uptake, VA dilution effect, and circle breathing system effects

![image.png](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_image_13.png_68ff4f.png_2fe0f7.png)

**Bottom Line:** Electronic gas flow control systems provide precise digital control of gas flows with display screens, while maintaining mechanical backup functionality that continues to operate during power failure. Advanced systems include automated algorithms that optimize fresh gas flow rates to minimize waste.

## 3.10. PROPORTIONING SYSTEMS











**Proportioning systems link oxygen and nitrous oxide controls to prevent creation and delivery of hypoxic gas mixtures through mechanical, pneumatic, or electronic interfaces.**

* **Types of Proportioning Systems**:

  1. **GE-Datex-Ohmeda Link-25 Proportion-Limiting Control System**:
	 * Found in traditional GE machines
	 * Allows independent adjustment of either valve but automatically maintains minimum 25% O₂ concentration with maximum N₂O:O₂ flow ratio of 3:1
	 * Mechanical mechanism:
	   * 14-tooth sprocket attached to N₂O flow control valve
	   * 29-tooth sprocket attached to O₂ flow control valve
	   * Stainless-steel chain physically links sprockets
	   * When N₂O valve turns 2.09 revolutions, O₂ valve revolves once due to gear ratio
	   * Final 3:1 flow ratio occurs because N₂O valve receives ~26 psig from second-stage regulator while O₂ valve receives 14 psig

  2. **Dräger Sensitive Oxygen Ratio Controller System (S-ORC)**:
	 * Used in Dräger workstations (Fabius, Apollo)
	 * Pneumatic N₂O/O₂ interlock system
	 * Maintains fresh gas O₂ concentration of at least 25% ±3% when N₂O is used
	 * Ensures minimum O₂ of 200 mL/min whenever N₂O is used
	 * Mechanism:
	   * Applies opening force to N₂O slave control valve based on back pressure difference between O₂ and N₂O flow
	   * Resistors create back pressure directed to O₂ and N₂O chambers
	   * O₂ resistor value is 3-4× that of N₂O flow resistor - determines fresh gas O₂ concentration
	   * Shaft movement due to pressure adjusts N₂O slave control valve opening
	   * When O₂ flows, back pressure is proportionally higher than N₂O → N₂O valve opens more widely
	   * As N₂O flow increases manually, N₂O back pressure forces shaft toward O₂ chamber → N₂O valve becomes more restrictive, decreasing N₂O flow
	   * When O₂ flow <200 mL/min, slave control valve closes completely

![image.png](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_image_15.png_6be9f9.png_670f91.png)

* **Limitations of Proportioning Systems**:
  * ⚠️ Wrong supply gas in O₂ pipeline can still cause hypoxic mixture; oxygen analyzer is the only workstation monitor besides integrated multigas analyzer that would detect this
  * Defective pneumatics or mechanics could allow hypoxic mixtures
  * Leaks downstream from proportioning system could create hypoxic mixtures
  * Inert gas administration could create hypoxic gas mixture
  * Dilution of inspired O₂ concentration by potent inhaled volatile anesthetics can cause resultant gas/vapor mixture to have inspired O₂ concentration <21% (especially with desflurane)
* **Additional Safety Features**
  * Hypoxic guard (mechanical link between nitrous and oxygen rotameters):
	* Turns oxygen on if nitrous is given
	* Turns nitrous off if oxygen is shut off
* **Anti-Hypoxic Safety Features** 
  * Several approaches to prevent delivery of gas mixtures with oxygen concentrations below 25%:
	* **Mechanical means**: Chain links the oxygen and nitrous oxide flow control valves, with minimum oxygen flow of 175-250 mL/min even when valve appears closed 
	* **Pneumatic means**: Pressure-sensitive diaphragm measuring changes in oxygen and nitrous oxide concentrations, ensuring oxygen flow increases by 25% of any nitrous oxide flow increase 
	* **Paramagnetic analysis**: Continuous oxygen concentration measurement with automatic nitrous oxide shutdown when oxygen falls below 25% 

**Bottom Line:** Proportioning systems use mechanical or pneumatic mechanisms to prevent delivery of hypoxic gas mixtures by linking O₂ and N₂O flow controls. Despite these safeguards, they have limitations, making continuous oxygen analysis crucial for detecting potential hypoxic mixtures from various causes.

## 3.11. OXYGEN FLUSH VALVE











**The oxygen flush valve provides direct high-flow oxygen from the intermediate-pressure circuit to the low-pressure circuit, bypassing flowmeters and vaporizers.**

* **Function and Design**:
  * Direct communication between O₂ intermediate-pressure circuit and low-pressure system  * Flow of O₂ enters low-pressure circuit downstream from vaporizer and from any outlet check valve  * Delivers 100% O₂ at 35-75 L/min  * Delivers oxygen at a pressure of 400 kPa (4 bar)* **Components**:
  * Spring-loaded valve mechanism  * Pushbutton control on anesthesia machine front panel  * Flow restrictor to limit maximum flow rate* **Clinical Uses**:
  * Rapid filling of breathing circuit  * Emergency oxygen delivery  * Clearing anesthetic gases from circuit* **Safety Considerations**:
  * ⚠️ Can dilute anesthetic agent concentration if used during maintenance  * ⚠️ Risk of barotrauma if used during positive pressure ventilation  * Bypasses vaporizers and flowmeters by design
![nse-3312568942644977356-117975.jpg](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_nse-3312568942644977356-117975.jpg_4ae12e.jpg_15c923.jpg)

**Bottom Line:** The oxygen flush valve provides rapid delivery of 100% oxygen directly to the breathing circuit, bypassing flowmeters and vaporizers. While useful for filling the circuit or emergency oxygenation, it must be used cautiously to avoid barotrauma during positive pressure ventilation or dilution of anesthetic agent.

## 3.12. PRESSURE GAUGES AND BOURDON TUBES











**Pressure gauges are essential components that measure the pressure in cylinders or pipelines, providing critical information about gas supply availability.**

* **Basic Design and Function** 
  * Measures pressure in cylinder or pipeline 
  * Located on the front-facing panel of the anesthesia machine 
  * Modern machines may have digital displays of gas supply pressures 
* **Components** 
  * A robust, flexible and coiled tube with oval cross-section 
  * Tube sealed at inner end and connected to a needle pointer that moves over a dial 
  * Other end of the tube exposed to the gas supply 
* **Operating Mechanism (Bourdon Gauge)** 
  * High-pressure gas causes the coiled tube to straighten (uncoil) 
  * This movement causes the needle pointer to move across a calibrated dial, indicating the pressure 
* **Safety Features** 
  * Each pressure gauge is color-coded and calibrated for a particular gas or vapor 
  * Pipeline pressure gauges should not be used to measure cylinder pressure and vice versa, which could cause inaccuracies or damage 
  * If the coiled tube ruptures, gas vents from the back of the pressure gauge casing 
  * The face of the pressure gauge is made of heavy glass as an additional safety feature 

![Image of typical pressure gauges](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_pdf_WORKSTATION.pdf_p62_img1_32df6e.jpeg)

**Bottom Line:** Pressure gauges provide critical information about gas supply availability through a simple mechanical mechanism that converts pressure into movement. Their readings are particularly important for oxygen cylinders where pressure directly reflects remaining content, though less useful for nitrous oxide cylinders where pressure remains constant until all liquid is gone.

## 3.13. PRESSURE REGULATORS (REDUCING VALVES)











**Pressure regulators reduce the high and variable pressures from cylinders to a constant, safe operating pressure for the anesthesia machine components.**

* **Purpose** 
  * Reduces the variable cylinder pressure to a constant safer operating pressure of about 400 kPa (just below pipeline pressure) 
  * Eliminates need to constantly adjust flowmeters as cylinder pressure decreases with use 
  * Protects anesthesia machine components against pressure surges 
  * Allows use of low-pressure piping and connectors within the machine, making consequences of leaks less serious 
* **Positioning**:
  * Located between cylinders and the rest of the anesthesia machine 
  * First-stage regulators in high-pressure circuit, second-stage regulators in intermediate-pressure circuit
* **Components** 
  * An inlet with a filter, leading to a high-pressure chamber with a valve 
  * Valve leading to a low-pressure chamber and outlet 
  * A diaphragm attached to a spring in the low-pressure chamber 
* **Operating Mechanism** 
  * Gas enters the high-pressure chamber and passes into the low-pressure chamber via the valve 
  * The force of high-pressure gas tries to close the valve, while the opposing force of the diaphragm and spring tries to open it 
  * A balance is reached between these opposing forces, maintaining gas flow at a constant pressure of about 400 kPa 
* **Potential Problems** 
  * Ice formation inside the regulator if cylinder contains water vapor, which may condense and freeze due to heat lost during gas expansion 
  * Diaphragm rupture 
* **Safety Features** 
  * Relief valves (usually set at 700 kPa) fitted downstream of regulators allow gas escape if regulators fail 
  * One-way valve within cylinder supply line prevents backflow and loss of gas from pipeline supplies if a cylinder is not connected 

![Image of pressure regulator](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_pdf_WORKSTATION.pdf_p65_img1_5ba3a6.jpeg)

**Bottom Line:** Pressure regulators are essential safety components that convert high and variable cylinder pressures to safe, consistent operating pressures for the anesthesia machine. They function through a balance of mechanical forces, with additional safety features to prevent downstream damage if they fail.

## 3.14. FLOW CONTROL VALVES











**Flow control valves precisely regulate the amount of gas entering the flowmeters, using needle valves with manual adjustment knobs that feature multiple safety elements.**

* **Function** 
  * Control the flow through flowmeters by manual adjustment 
  * Positioned at the base of the associated flowmeter tube 
  * Increasing flow is achieved by turning counterclockwise (opening the valve) 
  * These valves reduce gas pressure from around 44 kPa to just above atmospheric pressure before entry to the flowmeter block 
* **Components** 
  * Body (made of brass) that screws into the base of the flowmeter 
  * Stem that screws into the body and ends in a needle, with screw threads allowing fine adjustment 
  * Flow control knobs that are labeled and color-coded 
  * Touch-coded knob for oxygen flowmeter 
  * Flow control knob guard to protect against accidental adjustment 
* **Safety Features** 
  * The flow control knobs are color-coded for their respective gases 
  * Oxygen control knob is situated to the left (in UK), and in some designs is larger with larger ridges and a longer stem to make it easily recognizable by touch 
  * In the US and Canada, the oxygen control knob is situated to the right 
  * European Standard EN 740 requires means to prevent delivery of gas mixtures with <25% oxygen 
  * Interactive oxygen and nitrous oxide controls prevent hypoxic mixtures 

![Image of flow control valve](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_pdf_WORKSTATION.pdf_p70_img1_1b3693.jpeg)

**Bottom Line:** Flow control valves provide precise adjustment of gas flow into the flowmeters through needle valves that carefully control the orifice size. Multiple safety features focus on preventing hypoxic mixtures and accidental adjustments, with special emphasis on making oxygen controls distinctive by position, size, and tactile characteristics.

## 4.1. PHYSICS OF VAPORIZATION











**The physics of vaporization underpins the function of anesthetic vaporizers, with key concepts including vapor pressure, latent heat, specific heat, and thermal conductivity.**

* **Vapor Pressure**:
  * Volatile anesthetics exist in liquid state at temperatures <20°C
  * In closed chamber, vapor phase is in equilibrium with liquid phase
  * Vapor molecules create the saturated vapor pressure (SVP)
  * As temperature increases → more molecules enter vapor phase → SVP increases

  ![image.png](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_image_16.png_d3246c.png_e405c5.png)

  * SVP is independent of atmospheric pressure; dependent only on temperature and physical characteristics of liquid
	* At 20°C, SVP of isoflurane is 238 mmHg; at 35°C, it is 450 mmHg

  ![image.png](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_image_17.png_756eaf.png_052ef5.png)

  * Boiling point is the temperature at which vapor pressure equals atmospheric pressure
	* Desflurane: 22.8°C; Isoflurane: 48.5°C; Sevoflurane: 58.5°C
	* ⚠️ Desflurane boils at warm temperatures (e.g., pediatric and burn operating rooms), requiring special vaporizer design
* **Latent Heat of Vaporization**:
  * Energy consumed by liquid as it converts into vapor
  * Definition: number of calories required to change 1g of liquid into vapor without temperature change
  * Energy source can be from liquid itself (causing cooling and decreased subsequent vaporization) or external heat source
* **Specific Heat**:
  * Number of calories required to increase temperature of 1g of substance by 1°C
  * Indicates heat required to maintain constant temperature when heat is being lost during vaporization
  * Vaporizer component materials have high specific heat to minimize temperature changes during vaporization
* **Thermal Conductivity**:
  * Measure of rate at which heat flows through a substance
  * Higher conductivity = better heat conduction
  * Vaporizer metals require high thermal conductivity
* **Ideal Vaporizer Features**
  * Unaffected by variable factors such as:
	* Changes in fresh gas flow
	* Amount of liquid in vaporizing chamber
	* Ambient temperature and pressure
	* Temperature changes from latent heat of vaporization
	* Patient ventilation changes causing variations in airway pressure
  * Durable and corrosion resistant  * Economically viable to manufacture and maintain  * Efficient vapor use  * Low resistance
**Bottom Line:** Understanding the physics of vaporization is essential for anesthesia practice as it explains how vaporizers function and why special designs are needed for agents like desflurane. Key concepts include saturated vapor pressure (which increases with temperature), latent heat of vaporization, specific heat, and thermal conductivity.

## 4.2. VARIABLE BYPASS VAPORIZERS











**Variable bypass vaporizers allow precise control of anesthetic vapor concentration through sophisticated design elements that maintain consistent output despite varying conditions.**

* **Classification**,:
  * Variable bypass, flow-over, temperature-compensated, agent-specific, out-of-breathing circuit vaporizers
  * **Variable bypass**: Method for regulating anesthetic agent concentration output
  * **Flow over**: Gas channeled through vaporizing chamber flows over wick system saturated with liquid agent → becomes saturated with vapor
  * **Agent specific**: Each vaporizer designed for a single anesthetic agent
  * **Out of circuit**: Located outside of breathing circuit
  * **Temperature compensated**: Automated temperature compensating device maintains constant vapor concentration output for given dial setting across wide temperature range
* **Basic Operating Principles**:

  ![image.png](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_image_18.png_78b849.png_8e158b.png)

  * Components include concentration control dial, bypass chamber, vaporizing chamber, filler port, and filler cap
	* **Filler port**: Where chamber is filled with liquid agent; maximum fill level predetermined by position (prevents overfilling)
	* **Concentration control dial**: Variable restrictor proportioning gas flow through bypass with agent at SVP leaving vaporizing chamber
  
  * **Operation**:
	* Fresh gas flow enters inlet → most enters bypass chamber; small fraction enters vaporizing chamber → entrains vapor → mixture exits outlet
	* Output = bypass chamber flow + flow through vapor chamber + flow of entrained anesthetic vapor	* Quantity (mL) of liquid agent used proportional to FGF rate and concentration set on dial: 3 × FGF (L/min) × vol% = mL liquid agent/hr  
  * **Dilution Principles**:
	* Example (Sevoflurane):
	  * Sevoflurane SVP 160 mmHg at 20°C at sea level = vapor concentration of 21% (160/760×100)	  * Vaporizer must dilute this 21% to clinically desirable value on dial	  * At vaporizing chamber outflow of 100 mL/min, each 100 mL contains 21 mL sevoflurane vapor + 79 mL carrier gas	  * For 1% dial setting: 21 mL sevoflurane diluted in 2100 mL total → bypass flow = 2000 mL/min	  * Flow ratio = 20:1 or 2000:100	  * At 2% dial setting: 21 mL sevoflurane diluted in 1050 mL → bypass ratio = 950:100 or 9.5:1	
	* Example (Isoflurane):
	  * Isoflurane SVP = 238 mmHg at 20°C = vapor concentration of 31% (238/760)	  * At 1% dial setting: 31 mL diluted in 3100 mL → bypass ratio = 3000:100 = 30:1	  * At 2% dial setting: 31 mL diluted in 1550 mL → bypass ratio = 1450:100 = 14.5:1* **Factors Influencing Vaporizer Output**:
  * Contemporary vaporizers aim to provide constant output for given dial setting regardless of external changes
  
  1. **Fresh Gas Flow Rate**:
	 * If FGF <250 mL/min, output of vaporizing chamber is less than input due to insufficient turbulence to advance vapor molecules upward	 * If FGF >15 L/min, output is less than dial setting due to incomplete mixing and failure to saturate carrier gas  
  2. **Temperature**:
	 * As temperature increases, SVP increases. Temperature compensating valve adjusts:
	   * Bimetallic strip leans right as temperature rises → decreases resistance through bypass chamber → more flow bypasses vaporizing chamber	   * In cold conditions, valve leans left → increases bypass resistance → more flow to vaporizing chamber	 * Wick systems contact metal wall to replace heat consumed during vaporization	 * Vaporizer materials have high specific heat and thermal conductivity to minimize cooling effects
	 ![image.png](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_image_19.png_e05738.png_62c388.png)

	 ![image.png](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_image_20.png_e9ee4b.png_f4086c.png)

  3. **Intermittent Back Pressure**:
	 * Modern vaporizers address this with pressure-compensated inlets (long spiral tube in Dräger Vapor vaporizers)	 * Positive pressure ventilation/oxygen flush may increase vaporizer output in older models
	 * "Pumping effect" issues:
	   * More pronounced at low FGF rates, low concentration settings, low liquid levels
	   * Increased by rapid respiratory rate, high peak pressures, rapid pressure decreases during exhalation
	 * Mechanism:
	   * Retrograde pressure from patient circuit during inspiration
	   * Gas molecules compressed in chambers
	   * Sudden pressure release during expiration → vapor exits vaporizing chamber through both outlet and retrograde through inlet
  
  4. **Fresh Gas Composition**:
	 * When carrier gas changes from 100% air to 30% O₂/70% N₂O → transient decrease in output followed by slow increase to new steady state	 * N₂O is more soluble than O₂ in volatile agent liquid → initially decreases output
	 * Once liquid saturated with N₂O, output increases to new steady state
	 ![image.png](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_image_21.png_f4f3c9.png_798172.png)

* **Safety Features**:
  * Agent-specific, keyed filling devices prevent filling with wrong agent
  * Filler port location prevents overfilling
  * Anti-spillage protection designs
  * Interlock systems prevent simultaneous administration of multiple agents
* **Hazards**:
  * **Misfilling**:
	* If vaporizer for low SVP agent is filled with higher SVP agent, output will exceed dial setting (e.g., isoflurane in sevoflurane vaporizer); vice versa
  
  * **Tipping**:
	* Causes liquid agent to enter bypass chamber → high vapor concentration
	* 1 mL liquid agent produces approximately 200 mL vapor at 20°C and 1 atm
	* Remedy: Purge with high FGF for 20-30 min with vaporizer on
	* "T" transport dial setting isolates sump from bypass chamber
  
  * **Overfilling**:
	* Could cause liquid to enter bypass chamber
	* Modern side-filling designs largely prevent this issue
  
  * **Leaks**:
	* Loose filler cap most common source
	* Can occur at O-ring junctions between vaporizer and manifold
	* Negative pressure leak test more sensitive for internal vaporizer leaks
  
  * **MRI Compatibility**:
	* Not all vaporizers are MRI safe
* **Vaporizer Classification Methods**
  * **By concentration regulation**:
	* Variable bypass (plenum or draw-over)
	* Measured flow (e.g., TEC6 desflurane vaporizer)
  
  * **By vaporization method**:
	* Flow over (plenum)
	* Bubble through
	* Direct injection (TEC6)
  
  * **By temperature management**:
	* Thermocompensation (plenum)
	* Active heating (TEC6)
  
  * **By flow resistance**:
	* Plenum (high resistance) - e.g., TEC vaporizers
	* Draw over (low resistance) - e.g., Goldman and Oxford Miniature vaporizers
  
  * **By agent specificity**:
	* Multiple agent (draw over)
	* Single agent (plenum)
  
  * **By circuit position**:
	* Within circuit (low resistance, draw over)
	* Outside circuit (high resistance, plenum)
* **Plenum Vaporizer Components** 
  * Case with filling level indicator and port for filling device 
  * Percentage control dial on top/face of case that opens counterclockwise 
  * Bypass channel and vaporization chamber with wicks, baffles, cowls or nebulizers to increase surface area for vaporization 
  * Temperature-sensitive valve with bimetallic strip (in older designs inside vaporization chamber, in modern designs outside) 
  * Mounted on back bar using Selectatec Vaporizer Manifold system with male valve ports, each with O-ring for gas-tight fit 
  * Interlocking system prevents more than one vaporizer being used simultaneously 

**Bottom Line:** Variable bypass vaporizers precisely control anesthetic vapor delivery through complex dilution mechanisms and temperature compensation, allowing reliable agent delivery despite varying conditions. Multiple safety features prevent misuse, while specific design elements address challenges like temperature variation and back pressure effects.

## 4.3. DESFLURANE VAPORIZERS











**Desflurane requires specialized vaporizer designs due to its unique properties including high volatility, low boiling point, and high vapor pressure.**

* **Unique Chemical Characteristics of Desflurane**:
  * SVP is 669 mmHg (almost 1 atm pressure) - extremely high volatility  * Boiling point at 22.8°C - can boil at room temperature  * MAC 6-7%, low blood:gas coefficient of 0.45 at 37°C - rapid recovery* **Why Conventional Vaporizers Are Unsuitable**:
  * Vapor production would be uncontrolled and limited only by available heat energy  * Conventional vaporizers lack external heat source  * Though latent heat of vaporization is similar to other agents, MAC is 4-9× higher  * Absolute amount of desflurane vaporized over time is greater than other agents  * Excessive cooling would significantly reduce output  * ⚠️ Desflurane requires an external heat source - otherwise delivery will be unpredictable* **Operating Principles of TEC 6 and D-Vapor**:

  ![Fresh gas circuit is in darker grey; vapor circuit in light gray](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_image_22.png_a32dc3.png_95f9bb.png)

  * **TEC 6 Design**:
	* Two independent gas circuits arranged in parallel
	* FGF enters inlet → passes through R1 (fixed restrictor) → exits vaporizer gas outlet
	* Sump heated to 39°C (above desflurane's BP) → creates reservoir of desflurane vapor at ~1500 mmHg (2 atm)	* Once sump warms to working temperature, shutoff valve fully opens when concentration control valve (R2) is turned "on"	* Pressure regulating valve downstream regulates pressure to ~1.1 atm (74 mmHg) at FGF of 10 L/min	* Vapor flows through R2 and joins FGF through R1 at outlet	
	* **Pressure Control System**:
	  * Constant FGF through fixed restrictor R1 creates back pressure proportional to flow rate
	  * Pressure difference conveyed to vapor circuit via control electronics
	  * Control system adjusts pressure regulating valve to equalize pressure across circuits	  * Working pressure increases linearly with FGF:
		* At FGF 1 L/min: working pressure = 10 millibars (7.4 mmHg gauge)		* At FGF 10 L/min: working pressure = 100 millibars (74 mmHg gauge)
	  ![image.png](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_image_23.png_fb252d.png_8cb755.png)

	* **Operating Examples**:
	  * **Example A**: Effects of Increasing Dial Setting (Constant FGF 1 L/min)
		* Working pressure remains constant at 7.4 mmHg (measured at R1 and R2)		* When dial setting increased → R2 opening increases → vapor flow rate increases proportionally	  
	  * **Example B**: Effects of Increasing FGF (Constant Dial Setting)
		* Initial conditions: FGF 1 L/min, working pressure 7.4 mmHg, R2 dial at 6%, vapor flow 64 mL/min		* When FGF increases 10-fold → working pressure increases to 74 mmHg → R2 receives 10× more pressure → vapor flow increases to 640 mL/min* **Altitude Effects on Vaporizer Output**:
  * Changes in ambient pressure affect older vaporizers' concentration output (potency/partial pressure effect minimal)  
  * **Example with Isoflurane in Older TEC Vaporizer**:
	* At 0.89% at 1 atm (760 mmHg): volume percent = 0.89%, partial pressure = 6.8 mmHg	* At 0.89% at 0.66 atm (10,000 ft elevation): volume percent = 1.75%, partial pressure = 8.77 mmHg (29% increase)	* Concentration in volumes percent must change inversely proportional to barometric pressure  
  * **Contemporary Variable Bypass Vaporizers**:
	* Flow proportioning occurs as gas leaves chamber → constant volume of saturated gas diluted by bypass flow	* Example: Gas leaves isoflurane vaporizer at 100 mL/min
	  * At 1 atm (760 mmHg): gas would be 31.3% by volume (238/760), partial pressure = 238 mmHg	  * At 500 mmHg: gas would be 47.6% by volume (238/500), partial pressure still = 238 mmHg	* These vaporizers are ambient pressure compensated  
  * **TEC 6 Desflurane Vaporizers**:
	* Maintains constant concentration (volume percent), not constant partial pressure	* At high altitudes, partial pressure decreases proportionally to atmospheric pressure	* Example: 10% desflurane at 1 atm = 76 mmHg partial pressure; at 500 mmHg = 50 mmHg	* To maintain equivalent partial pressure at altitude: increase dial setting (e.g., to 15% at 500 mmHg)	* In hyperbaric conditions, decrease dial settings

## 4.7. NON-RETURN PRESSURE RELIEF SAFETY VALVE











**Non-return pressure relief valves protect anesthesia machine components from excessive pressure while preventing back pressure effects from ventilators.**

* **Location and Function** 
  * Situated downstream of the vaporizers either on the back bar itself or near the common gas outlet 
  * Non-return design helps prevent back pressure effects commonly encountered with minute volume divider ventilators 
  * Opens when pressure in the back bar exceeds about 35 kPa 
  * Protects flowmeter and vaporizer components from damage at higher pressures 
* **Back Pressure Effects** 
  * Without non-return valves, minute volume divider ventilators can exert back pressure during cycling 
  * This pressure forces some gas exiting the outlet port back into the vaporizing chamber, where more vapor is added 
  * Can also contaminate the bypass channel 
  * Results in increased inspired concentration of anesthetic agent, potentially reaching toxic levels 
* **Protection System** 
  * Combination of non-return function and pressure relief at a specific threshold 
  * In modern machines, often integrated with other safety systems 

![Non-return pressure relief valve at end of back bar](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_pdf_WORKSTATION.pdf_p95_img1_2a6afe.jpeg)

**Bottom Line:** Non-return pressure relief safety valves serve dual purposes: preventing back pressure from ventilators from affecting vaporizer output and protecting machine components from excessive pressure. These valves are critical for maintaining accurate anesthetic delivery and preventing equipment damage.

# 5. ANESTHESIA VENTILATORS

## 5.1. PNEUMATICALLY DRIVEN BELLOWS VENTILATORS











**Pneumatically driven bellows ventilators use compressed gas to drive a physical bellows, separating the driving circuit from the patient breathing circuit.**

* **Current Ventilator Technologies**:
  * Four technologies generate positive pressure gas flows in anesthesia ventilators:
	1. **Pneumatic-driven bellows** using compressed gas from wall outlet/cylinder:
	   * Ascending (General Electric, Mindray up to A7, Spacelabs, older Dräger)	   * Descending (early generation machines and Mindray Anestar)	2. **Electric piston pumps** (Dräger Fabius, Apollo, Atlan)	3. **Electric turbines** (Dräger Perseus, Zeus)
	4. **Volume exchangers** using compressed medical gas (Maquet/Getinge and Mindray A8/A9)

* **Pneumatically Driven Bellows Ventilator Design**:
  * Traditional ventilator; classified as double-circuit ventilator
  * Patient circuit gas contained inside bellows within rigid plastic housing
  * Driving gas circuit uses compressed gas (O₂ or air or both) connected to rigid housing
  * Driving gas is separate from respired gas (adjusted by provider)
* **Operating Cycle**:
  * **Inspiratory phase**:
	* Driving gas enters bellows chamber → increases pressure within chamber
	* Ventilator relief valve closes to prevent gas escaping to scavenging
	* Bellows compressed to deliver respired gas to patient
  
  * **End of inspiration**:
	* Driving gas circuit inlet valve closes (at chosen tidal volume or pressure)
	* Inspiratory pause occurs
  
  * **Expiratory phase**:
	* Driving gas exits bellows through outlet valve
	* Decreases pressure within housing and pilot controlling ventilator relief valve
	* "Mushroom" valve of ventilator relief valve opens
	* Exhaled gases refill bellows first (weighted ball at valve produces 2-3 cmH₂O back pressure)
	* Once bellows full and exceeds "ball valve" threshold, flow to scavenging occurs
* **Bellows Configuration Differences**:
  * **Ascending (standing) bellows** rise during expiration
  * **Descending (hanging) bellows** fall during expiration
  * ⚠️ Ascending bellows considered safer because they won't fill if disconnection occurs
  * Descending bellows will continue to fill with room air during disconnection due to gravity on weighted bellows
  * CO₂/apnea alarm is essential safety feature with descending bellows
* **Contemporary Designs**:
  * Modern electronic ventilators use electronic timing device and adjustable threshold pressure triggers

![Inspiratory (A) and expiratory (B) phases of gas flow in a traditional circle system with an ascending bellows ventilator. The bellows physically separates the driving gas circuit from the patient gas circuit. The driving gas circuit is located outside the bellows, and the patient gas circuit is inside the bellows.](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_image_27.png_3e4d34.png)

* **Ventilator Classification**
  * **By ventilator mode**:
	* Volume control: Set tidal volume with variable pressure
	* Pressure control: Set pressure with variable volume
	* Pressure control volume guaranteed: Adjusts pressure to achieve set volume
  
  * **By power source**:
	* Electrical
	* Pneumatic
	* Combination
  
  * **By cycling method**:
	* Volume cycling: Switches to expiration when set volume reached
	* Time cycling: Switches when set time reached
	* Pressure cycling: Switches when set pressure reached
	* Flow cycling: Switches when set flow level reached

**Bottom Line:** Pneumatically driven bellows ventilators are traditional anesthesia ventilators that use compressed gas to drive a bellows containing patient gas. Ascending bellows designs are preferred for safety, as they visibly collapse during disconnections. While newer technologies have emerged, these ventilators remain common in clinical practice due to their reliability and visual indication of function.

## 5.2. ELECTRONIC PISTON PUMP VENTILATORS











**Electronic piston pump ventilators use a motor-driven piston rather than compressed gas to deliver tidal volumes, offering precise control and advanced ventilation modes.**

* **Basic Design**:
  * Computer-controlled stepper motor drives piston instead of compressed gas
  * Piston functions like plunger of syringe to deliver tidal volume or airway pressure
  * Does not require compressed gas for operation
  * Sophisticated computerized controls enable advanced ventilation modes (SIMV/PCV/PSAV)
  * Required redesign of traditional circle system to use Fresh Gas Decoupling (FGD)
* **Advantages**
  * More accurate tidal volume delivery
  * Reduced barotrauma risk due to FGD valve isolation during inspiration
  * Maintains positive pressure ventilation despite leaks in low pressure system
	* Unlike bellows systems where CO₂ absorber leaks prevent positive pressure ventilation
  * Can continue mechanical ventilation even with faulty expiratory valve (FGD valve compensates)
* **Limitations and Safety Considerations**
  * Piston retraction issues:
	* Empty reservoir bag can cause negative airway pressures
	* Air entrainment valve opens at -2 cmH₂O, risking gas dilution and awareness
  * Slower response to FGF changes due to:
	* FGF mixing at expiration point
	* Large circuit volume
  * Depends on intact reservoir bag for gas accumulation
  * FGD valve failure prevents mechanical but allows manual ventilation

**Bottom Line:** Electronic piston pump ventilators offer precise, computerized control of ventilation without requiring compressed driving gas. They incorporate a fresh gas decoupling system that prevents barotrauma during inspiration but requires specific safety considerations due to potential negative pressure generation during piston retraction.

## 5.3. FRESH GAS DECOUPLING (FGD) CONCEPT











**Fresh Gas Decoupling (FGD) is a key innovation in modern anesthesia workstations that separates fresh gas flow from the breathing circuit during inspiration to improve ventilation accuracy and safety.**

* **Operational Principles**:
  * **During Inspiration**:
	* Piston delivers tidal volume to lungs
	* Inspiratory one-way valve opens
	* Excess pressure vents through Pmax/PEEP valve
	* Fresh gas is diverted to reservoir bag via decoupling valve
	* Decoupling valve remains closed throughout inspiration
  
  * **Reservoir Bag Function**:
	* Stores patient's exhaled gases
	* Accumulates fresh gas between breaths
	* **Key difference**: Always connected to breathing circuit (vs. traditional bellows where bag is disconnected during mechanical ventilation)
  
  * **During Expiration**:
	* FGD valve opens
	* Fresh gas and exhaled gases mix together
	* Mixed gases refill the piston chamber
	* Excess gases exit through APL bypass valve to scavenging

![image.png](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_image_28.png_9bc303.png_bb0a47.png)

**Bottom Line:** Fresh Gas Decoupling separates fresh gas flow from the breathing circuit during the inspiratory phase, preventing unexpected volume or pressure increases and improving ventilation accuracy. This system allows the reservoir bag to remain connected during mechanical ventilation, accumulating fresh gas that is then incorporated during the expiratory phase.

## 5.4. ELECTRIC TURBINE VENTILATORS











**Electric turbine ventilators use an electrically-powered blower to generate gas flow, offering advanced capabilities and eliminating the need for compressed gas for ventilator driving.**

* **Basic Configuration**:
  * Single circuit ventilator with electrically-powered turbine
  * Electronically controlled with variable speed settings
  * Reservoir bag remains connected to active breathing circuit
* **Operation During Mechanical Ventilation**:
  * **Inspiratory Phase**:
	* Turbine speed increases to draw gas from reservoir bag
	* Gas mixture (patient's exhalation + FGF) passes through CO₂ absorber
	* Gas pushed through inspiratory valve to patient's lungs
	* APL-bypass valve opens → excess gas scavenged
* **Fresh Gas Flow Management**:
  * Continuous FGF injection on machine side of inspiratory valve
  * Gas flows to reservoir bag via turbine when inspiratory valve closes
  * No mechanical FGF decoupling valve present
* **Safety Features**:
  * **Pmax/PEEP valve** (active during mechanical ventilation only):
	* Limits inspiratory pressure
	* Provides PEEP during exhalation
  * **Air entrainment valve** prevents negative pressure injury:
	* Activates if reservoir bag collapses
	* Allows room air entry for safety
* **Manual/Spontaneous Ventilation Mode**:
  * APL-bypass valve closes → manual APL valve becomes active
  * Allows bag-mask ventilation like standard ventilators
  * Turbine assists spontaneous breathing:
	* Reduces circuit resistance
	* Can provide CPAP above ambient pressure
* **Unique Feature**:
  * Only anesthesia ventilator capable of providing Airway Pressure Release Ventilation (APRV)
  * Previously exclusive to ICU ventilators

**Bottom Line:** Electric turbine ventilators use an electronically controlled blower system to move gas through the breathing circuit without compressed driving gas. They offer advanced ventilation modes like APRV while maintaining traditional manual ventilation capabilities, with specialized safety features to prevent patient injury from negative pressure.

## 5.5. VOLUME REFLECTORS











**Volume reflector ventilation systems use a novel approach without traditional bellows, piston, or turbine, employing a coiled tube reservoir to store and direct gases through the breathing circuit.**

* **System Overview**:
  * Used in **Maquet/Getinge FLOW-i** and **Mindray A8/A9** workstations
  * Novel breathing system without bellows, turbine, or piston
  * Similar to ICU ventilators but includes circle system

  ![image.png](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_image_29.png_2d5e89.png_4802cd.png)

* **Key Components**:
  * **Volume Reflector**:
	* 3.6-meter long plastic coil
	* 1.2L internal volume
	* Located between circle system, reservoir bag, and APL/PEEP valve
  * **Reflector Oxygen Module**:
	* Provides oxygen as driving gas during positive-pressure ventilation
	* Directly connected to volume reflector
* **System Classification**:
  * Gas powered
  * Single circuit
  * Electronically controlled
* **Mechanical Ventilation Process**:
  * **During Inspiration**:
	* APL and PEEP valves close
	* FGF delivered via compressed gas module (O₂/air/N₂O)
	* Gases pass through electronic vaporizer
	* Added to inspiratory side of circle system
	* Combines with oxygen flow from reflector gas module
	* Achieves set FiO₂ and anesthetic concentration
  
  * **Gas Exchange**:
	* Additional oxygen driving gas added to volume reflector
	* Compensates for difference between set tidal volume and FGF
	* Pushes stored exhaled gas through CO₂ absorber to patient's lungs
	* Minimal gas mixing despite no barrier between oxygen and exhaled gas
  
  * **During Exhalation**:
	* PEEP valve opens to set value
	* Patient exhales into volume reflector
	* Excess gas vented through PEEP valve to scavenging system

**Bottom Line:** Volume reflector systems represent an innovative approach to anesthesia ventilation, using a long coiled tube instead of traditional bellows or piston mechanisms. This design directly combines fresh gas with exhaled gases and uses controlled oxygen flow to deliver precise tidal volumes through electronic management of gas pathways.

## 5.6. PROBLEMS AND HAZARDS WITH VENTILATORS











**Anesthesia ventilators can develop various problems related to breathing circuits, bellows assemblies, and control systems that pose significant patient safety risks if not properly identified and managed.**

* **Common Hazards Overview**:
  1. Problems with breathing circuits
  2. Issues with bellows assembly
  3. Control assembly complications

### 5.6.2. BREATHING CIRCUIT PROBLEMS

* **Disconnections and Misconnections**:
  * Most common disconnection site: Y-piece
  * Types of disconnections:
	* Complete disconnections
	* Partial disconnections (leaks)
  * Preexisting leaks may occur in:
	* Compressed circuits
	* Corrugated circuits
	* Disposable anesthetic circuits
* **Monitoring Systems**:
  * Disconnection monitors available but should not replace provider vigilance
  * Monitoring methods:
	* Chest wall excursion observation
	* Breath sounds monitoring
	* Pneumatic and electronic pressure monitors
* **Pressure Monitoring Considerations**:
  * Factors affecting monitor effectiveness:
	* Disconnection site location
	* Pressure sensor location
	* Threshold pressure alarm limit
	* Inspiratory flow rate
	* Breathing circuit resistance
  * Alarm settings:
	* Set pressure alarm limit within 5 cm H₂O of peak inspiratory pressure
	* "Auto-set" feature: 3-5 cm H₂O below current peak pressure
	* Reset to default values for each new patient

	![image.png](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_image_30.png_49ea11.png_11d78b.png)

* **Volume Monitoring Systems**:
  * **Volume Monitor Types**:
	* Measurement capabilities:
	  * Exhaled tidal volume
	  * Inhaled tidal volume
	  * Minute volume
	* Recommended alarm settings: bracket 8-12 L/min for 10 L/min minute volume
  * **Technology Types**:
	* *Infrared light/turbine technology* (older Datex-Ohmeda)
	  * Located in expiratory limb
	  * ⚠️ Caution: Surgical lights can cause interference
	* *D-Lite spirometry connector* (GE workstations)
	  * Measures both inhaled and exhaled volumes
	  * Measures airway pressures
	* *Other systems*:
	  * Differential pressure transduction (GE Datex)
	  * Ultrasonic flow sensor (Dräger)
	  * "Hot wire" sensor technology (Dräger)
* **Circuit Monitoring and Safety**:
  * **Capnography (CO₂ Monitoring)**:
	* Most effective for detecting disconnections
	* Measurement methods:
	  * Mainstream (direct)
	  * Side stream (continuous aspiration)
	* Important indicators:
	  * Sudden changes in CO₂ concentrations
	  * Absence of measured CO₂
	  * Note: May also indicate cardiac arrest
  * **Circuit Occlusion Risks**:
	* Common causes:
	  * Kinked tracheal tubes under drapes
	  * Internal obstruction
	  * External mechanical forces
	* Serious complications:
	  * Bilateral tension pneumothorax (from blocked bacterial filter)
	  * No-flow states from incorrect component insertion
* **Pressure and Ventilation Safety**:
  * **Barotrauma Prevention**:
	* Risk factors:
	  * Excess inflow during inspiration
	  * Oxygen flushing complications
	* Safety features:
	  * High-pressure alarms
	  * Automatic phase switching (GE)
	  * Adjustable pressure limiters

### 5.6.3. BELLOWS ASSEMBLY PROBLEMS

* **Common Issues**:
  * Bellows leaks:
	* Improper housing seating
	* Holes leading to hyperinflation
  * Ventilator relief valve problems:
	* Incompetent valve causing hypoventilation
	* Stuck valve causing barotrauma or PEEP

### 5.6.4. CONTROL AND POWER SUPPLY ISSUES

* **Electrical Concerns**:
  * Types of failures:
	* Total electrical failure
	* Partial electrical failure
  * Computer system dependencies:
	* Battery backup systems for essential functions
	* Reboot time considerations
	* Variable feature availability during outages
* **Physiological Effects of Positive Pressure Ventilation**
  * **Respiratory Effects**:
	* Increased functional residual capacity (FRC)
	* Alveolar recruitment and improved gas exchange
	* Improved V/Q matching
	* Improved lung compliance
	* Reduced work of breathing
	* Risks of barotrauma, volutrauma, and biotrauma
	* Impaired lymphatic drainage
  
  * **Cardiovascular Effects**:
	* Overall decreased cardiac output and myocardial oxygen demand
	* Right ventricle:
	  * Decreased preload (SVC compression)
	  * Increased pulmonary vascular resistance
	  * Increased afterload
	* Left ventricle:
	  * Reduced preload (pulmonary vein pressure)
	  * Reduced afterload (transmural pressure)
  
  * **Other System Effects**:
	* Neurological: Raised intracranial pressure with high PEEP
	* Renal:
	  * Decreased perfusion (reduced CO, increased CVP)
	  * Increased ADH and aldosterone (water retention)
	  * Decreased ANP (sodium retention)
	* Hepatic/GI:
	  * Reduced perfusion affecting drug metabolism
	  * Ileus from decreased perfusion

**Bottom Line:** Ventilator problems can arise from various components including breathing circuits, bellows assemblies, and control systems. Vigilant monitoring using multiple modalities (visual, auditory, pressure, volume, and capnography) is essential for patient safety. Understanding the physiological effects of positive pressure ventilation is crucial for optimizing ventilator settings while minimizing potential adverse effects across multiple organ systems.

# 6. WASTE GAS SCAVENGING SYSTEMS

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Anesthesia Equipment|Part A]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2: Equipment and Monitoring|Part B]]

## 6.1. SCAVENGING PRINCIPLES AND GUIDELINES











**Waste gas scavenging systems collect and remove excess anesthetic gases from the operating room to minimize occupational exposure and environmental contamination.**

* **Basic Principles**:
  * Scavenging refers to collection and removal of waste anesthetic gases from operating room
  * Excess gas removal necessary as anesthetic gas used typically exceeds minimum required amounts
  * Primary purpose: Minimize operating room contamination
* **Regulatory Guidelines**:
  * **NIOSH Guidelines (1977)**:
	* Published "Criteria for Recommended Standard: Occupational Exposure to Waste Anesthetic Gases and Vapors"
	* No defined minimum safe exposure level
	* Guidelines include volatile anesthetics (desflurane and sevoflurane not included initially)
	* Note: Recommendations not legally enforceable

	![image.png](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_image_31.png_5771ad.png_b5520a.png)

  * **ASTM Standard F1343-91 (1991)**:
	* Provides guidelines for safe and effective scavenging devices
	* Focus on reducing contamination in anesthetizing areas
  
  * **NIOSH Update (2006)**:
	* Requested data on newer halogenated anesthetic agents
	* Goal: Establish recommended exposure levels (REL)
  
  * **ASA Task Force Publication**:
	* Topic: Management of waste anesthetic gases
	* Covers: Literature analysis, regulatory roles, equipment guidelines
* **Occupational Exposure Regulations**
  * **COSHH** (Control of Substances Hazardous to Health) regulation:
	* Requires employers to protect employees from hazards
  
  * **Maximum Permissible Levels**:
	* Nitrous oxide: 100 ppm
	* Isoflurane/enflurane: 50 ppm (after 8 hours)
	* Halothane: 10 ppm
	* (Some countries use 25 ppm for volatile agents and 2 ppm for halothane)
* **Major Causes of Contamination**:
  * **Anesthetic Technique Issues**:
	* Failure to turn off gas flow control valves post-anesthetic
	* Poor mask fit
	* Circuit flushing
	* Vaporizer filling
	* Use of uncuffed tracheal tubes
	* Difficult-to-scavenge breathing circuits (e.g., Jackson-Rees)
  
  * **Equipment-Related Issues**:
	* High-pressure hose leaks
	* Nitrous oxide tank mounting problems
	* High-pressure circuit and LPC leaks
	* Circle system leaks (especially CO₂ absorber assembly)
* **Prevention and Management**:
  * Ensure operational scavenging system with proper adjustment
  * Side-stream CO₂/multigas analyzer management:
	* Gas withdrawal rate: 50-250 cc/min
	* Must direct to scavenging system or return to breathing system
* **Pollution Reduction Options**
  * Adequate ventilation (15-20 air changes per hour)
  * Circle breathing system use
  * Total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA)
  * Regional anesthesia
  * Effective scavenging systems
* **Concerns of Chronic Exposure**
  * Leukaemia
  * Lymphoma
  * Infertility and spontaneous abortion
  * Congenital abnormalities
  * Some evidence suggests sevoflurane exposure increases likelihood of female offspring
* **Pollution in the Operating Theatre** 
  * Since the late 1960s, speculation about harmful effects of trace anesthetic gases/vapors on operating theatre personnel 
  * Current conclusion: no association between occupational exposure to trace levels in scavenged operating theatres and adverse health effects 
  * Nevertheless, it remains desirable to vent exhaled anesthetic vapors and maintain a vapor-free theatre environment 
  * Regular monitoring and testing (Planned Preventative Maintenance) required 
  * Note that inhalational anesthetic agents (chlorofluorocarbons, hydrofluorocarbons, nitrous oxide) are greenhouse gases affecting climate 
* **Major Causes of Operating Theatre Pollution** 
  * **Anesthetic techniques**:
	* Incomplete scavenging from ventilator/APL valve
	* Poorly fitting face mask
	* Pediatric T-piece systems
	* Failure to turn off gases/vaporizers after anesthesia
	* Uncuffed tracheal tubes
	* Vaporizer filling
	* Patient exhalation during recovery
  * **Anesthetic machine**: Leaks from connections (O-rings, soda lime canister)
  * **Others**: Cryosurgery units and cardiopulmonary bypass circuits using volatile agents

**Bottom Line:** Waste gas scavenging systems are essential to minimize occupational exposure to anesthetic gases, which have been associated with various health concerns. Regulatory guidelines establish exposure limits, while proper equipment design, maintenance, and operational protocols help minimize contamination in operating rooms.

## 6.2. COMPONENTS OF SCAVENGING SYSTEMS











**Scavenging systems consist of five main components that collect, transfer, regulate, and dispose of waste anesthetic gases, with various designs optimized for different clinical settings.**

* **Five Main Components**:
  1. Gas-collecting assembly
  2. Transfer means
  3. Scavenging interface
  4. Gas-disposal assembly tubing
  5. Active or passive gas-disposal assembly

![image.png](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_image_32.png_c2050b.png_6314e3.png)

* **System Types**:
  * "Active system": Uses central vacuum system for waste gas removal
  * "Passive system": Utilizes waste gas weight/pressure for flow
* **Gas-Collecting Assembly**:
  * Function: Captures and delivers excess anesthetic gas to transfer tubing
  * Gas sources:
	* APL valve
	* Ventilator relief valve
  * Special considerations for ventilators:
	* GE Healthcare (7100/7900): Ventilator driving gas exhausts into scavenging
	* Risk: High FGFs + high minute ventilation may overwhelm system
	* Other ventilators (GE Datex-Ohmeda, Dräger Narkomed): Exhaust to room
* **Transfer Means**:
  * Specifications:
	* Diameter: 19 or 30 mm (ASTM F1343-91 standard)
	* Must be rigid to prevent kinking
	* Should be short to minimize occlusion risk
  * Features:
	* Yellow bands for identification
	* Separate tubes for APL and ventilator relief valves
	* Merge into single hose before interface
  * Safety concern: Occlusion can cause barotrauma
* **Scavenging Interface**:
  * Critical function: Protects breathing circuit/ventilator from pressure extremes
  * Pressure limits: -0.5 to +10 cm H₂O
  * Requirements:
	* Mandatory positive-pressure relief
	* Negative-pressure relief for active systems
	* Reservoir recommended for active systems
  
  * **Interface Types**:
	* **Open Interfaces**:
	  * No valves, open to atmosphere
	  * Used with active disposal systems only
	  * Features canister reservoir
	  * Efficiency factors:
		* Vacuum flow rate must exceed excess gas volume
		* Reservoir capacity
		* Flow characteristics

	  ![image.png](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_image_33.png_83579b.png_81517c.png)

	* **Closed Interfaces**:
	  * Communicates through valves
	  * Types:
		* Positive-pressure relief only (for passive systems)
		* Positive and negative-pressure relief (for active systems)
	  * Operation parameters:
		* Positive relief: Opens at 5 cm water
		* Negative relief: Activates at -0.5 cm H₂O
		* Backup negative relief: Opens at -1.8 cm H₂O

	  ![image.png](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_image_34.png_08c25e.png_53ef4c.png)

* **Gas-Disposal Assembly Components**:
  * Conduit:
	* Must be collapse-proof
	* Preferably overhead placement
  * Assembly Types:
	* Active:
	  * Uses central evacuation system
	  * Requires negative-pressure relief valve
	  * Larger reservoir = lower suction flow rate needed
	* Passive:
	  * No mechanical flow device
	  * Uses gas weight/pressure for flow
	  * Requires only positive-pressure relief
	  * Venting options: wall, ceiling, floor, or room exhaust
* **Types of Scavenging Systems**
  * **Charcoal Canisters**:
	* Cardiff Aldasorber (doesn't scavenge nitrous oxide)
	* Replaced every 12 hours
	* Mobile with no setup cost
	* Doesn't remove nitrous oxide
	* ⚠️ If canister is heated, inhalational agents are released back into atmosphere
  
  * **Passive Systems**:
	* Connected to APL valves
	* Essentially a large bore tube ducted outside
	* Cheap and simple
	* Needs access to outside; can be affected by wind
	* Risk of obstruction
  
  * **Active Systems**:
	* Similar to passive but has active vacuum to maintain constant pressure
	* Needs to achieve 75 liters per minute with peak flow of 120-140 liters/min
	* Some employ an ejector to use venturi effect for high flows
* **General Design Features**:
  * Uses plastic tubing with 30 mm connections to ensure incompatibility with breathing circuit (preventing accidental connection to patient)
  * Requires receiving unit with reservoir and pressure relieving valves to avoid excessive pressure
  * Challenges with certain types of circuits:
	* Pediatric semi-closed breathing systems
	* Open or semi-open systems
	* Obstetrics and emergency department with Entonox

**Bottom Line:** Scavenging systems use specialized components to safely collect and remove waste anesthetic gases while preventing excessive pressure or vacuum effects on the breathing circuit. Multiple system types exist, including active (vacuum-driven) and passive systems, with specific interface designs to accommodate different clinical requirements and maintain patient safety.

## 6.3. PROBLEMS AND HAZARDS OF SCAVENGING SYSTEMS











**Scavenging systems, while essential for reducing gas contamination, introduce their own potential hazards that must be understood and managed for patient safety.**

* **General Concerns**:
  * While scavenging systems reduce OR contamination, they increase system complexity
  * System extends from anesthesia machine to disposal site
  * Key risks:
	* Obstruction of pathways → excessive positive pressure → barotrauma
	* Excessive vacuum → undesirable negative pressures in breathing system
  * ⚠️ Important: Preuse checkout must be performed manually per manufacturer instructions
* **Fire Hazards in Equipment Rooms**:
  * Risk scenario:
	* Waste gases vented into machine rooms with outside vents
	* Ventilator driving gas (oxygen) also scavenged → oxygen-enriched environment
	* Presence of petroleum distillates (pumps/oil/grease) creates fire hazard
* **Common Operational Issues**:
  * Inadequate flow in active systems causing backup of gases
  * Excessive vacuum in active systems causing negative pressure in breathing circuit
  * Kinking or occlusion of transfer tubing
  * Disconnections at various junction points
  * Improper interface valve function
* **Preventive Measures**:
  * Regular inspection of all connections
  * Proper adjustment of vacuum flow rates in active systems
  * Ensuring adequate positive and negative pressure relief valve function
  * Appropriate reservoir sizing for interface
* **Safe Scavenging System Design** 
  * Ideal scavenging system should not affect patient ventilation and oxygenation 
  * Should not affect the dynamics of the breathing system 
  * Must be able to handle wide range of expiratory flow rates (30-130 L/min) 
  * ISO standard 80601-2-13 states: "Under single fault conditions, the pressure at the inlet to the anaesthetic gas scavenging system shall not exceed 20 cm H₂O with an exhaust flow rate of 75 L/min" 
  * Safety valve prevents pressure buildup in breathing system if transfer system/tubing is occluded 

**Bottom Line:** Scavenging systems introduce potential hazards including obstruction-related barotrauma, excessive negative pressure effects, and fire risks in equipment rooms. Regular inspection, proper adjustment, and thorough preuse checkout are essential to minimize these risks while maintaining the benefits of waste gas removal.

## 6.4. LOW-FLOW SCAVENGING SYSTEMS











**Low-flow scavenging systems represent an advancement in waste gas management, reducing energy consumption and facilitating potential anesthetic agent recovery while minimizing environmental impact.**

* **Traditional Active Systems**:
  * Gas volume: 25-75 L/min per OR (waste + entrained air)
  * Challenges:
	* Requires large vacuum pumps
	* High energy costs
	* Continuous operation needed
* **Dynamic Gas Scavenging System (DGSS)**:
  * Features:
	* Gas-tight metal container
	* 3-L reservoir bag (OSHA compliant)
	* Pressure-sensitive operation:
	  * Opens at 0.5 cm H₂O pressure from workstation exhaust
	  * Closes at -0.5 cm H₂O
  * Benefits:
	* Continuous flow titration as needed
	* More concentrated waste gas flow
	* Facilitates anesthetic agent recovery
	* Reduces environmental impact (important as inhaled anesthetics are greenhouse gases)

![image.png](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_image_35.png_ba7fa2.png_e70598.png)

* **Environmental Considerations**
  * Inhaled anesthetics contribute to greenhouse gas effect
  * Nitrous oxide responsible for approximately 1% of global greenhouse effect
  * Low-flow systems reduce total gas waste volumes
  * Concentrated waste streams more amenable to filtering or recapture
* **Advanced Gas Capture Systems** 
  * ContrafluranTM Anaesthetic Gas Capture System:
	* Designed to reduce environmental impact of minimal-metabolism anesthetic agents
	* Uses highly porous granules in special canisters to capture sevoflurane and desflurane
	* One canister can hold about 240 mL of desflurane (equivalent to one bottle)
	* Requires separate canisters for sevoflurane and desflurane
	* Visual indicator and audible alarm signal when canister is full
	* Manufacturers collect full canisters to extract, separate and sterilize captured vapors for potential reuse
	* License approval for recycled vapor use pending in UK

**Bottom Line:** Low-flow scavenging systems represent an environmentally and economically superior approach to waste gas management, using pressure-sensitive mechanisms to collect gases only when needed. This reduces energy consumption while creating more concentrated waste streams that could potentially allow anesthetic agent recovery and recycling.

## 6.5. THEATRE POLLUTION SOURCES AND STANDARDS











**Operating theatre pollution from anesthetic gases comes from multiple sources, with established exposure limits and monitoring requirements to minimize potential health effects.**

* **Recommended Maximum Accepted Concentrations (UK, 8-hour time-weighted average)** 
  * Nitrous oxide: 100 ppm
  * Enflurane: 50 ppm
  * Isoflurane: 50 ppm
  * Halothane: 10 ppm
  * Sevoflurane: 20 ppm (recommended by Abbott Laboratories)
  * Desflurane: No set limit, but 50 ppm advisable due to similarity to enflurane
* **International Standards** 
  * **United States**:
	* Halogenated agents: <2 ppm
	* When combined with N₂O: <0.5 ppm
	* N₂O as sole agent: <25 ppm (8-hour time-weighted average)
  * **Netherlands**: N₂O limit of 25 ppm
  * **Italy, Sweden, Norway, Denmark**: N₂O limit of 100 ppm
  * Differences illustrate difficulty in setting standards without adequate data
* **Methods to Decrease Theatre Pollution** 
  1. Adequate theatre ventilation and air conditioning (15-20 air changes/hour)
	 * Unventilated theatres are four times as contaminated compared to those with proper ventilation
	 * Non-recirculating ventilation system preferred
	 * Labor wards using Entonox need minimum 5 air changes/hour
  2. Circle breathing systems (recycle exhaled anesthetics, absorb CO₂, require low fresh gas flow)
  3. Total intravenous anesthesia
  4. Regional anesthesia
  5. Vaporizer filling using fume cupboards to avoid spillage
  6. Scavenging systems

* **Required Monitoring Procedures** 
  * Sampling for waste anesthetic vapor concentrations yearly in UK, quarterly in US
  * Should include:
	* Leak testing of equipment
	* Sampling air in theatre personnel breathing zone
  * Equipment subject to planned preventative maintenance (PPM) program
  * Annual examination of ventilation system and scavenging equipment

**Bottom Line:** Operating theatre pollution comes from multiple sources including technique issues, equipment leaks, and recovery exhalation. Maximum exposure limits vary internationally, with multiple methods available to reduce contamination, particularly adequate ventilation with 15-20 air changes per hour. Regular monitoring and testing of equipment are essential parts of maintaining a safe working environment.

## 6.6. PASSIVE SCAVENGING SYSTEMS











**Passive scavenging systems provide a simple, zero-running-cost approach to waste gas removal using the gas's own pressure and weight to drive flow.**

* **Basic Design** 
  * Simple construction with zero running cost
  * Uses gas's own pressure/weight to drive flow
  * No active vacuum or pump required
* **Components** 
  1. **Collecting and Transfer System**:
	 * Shroud connected to APL valve or ventilator expiratory valve
	 * 30-mm connector attached to transfer tubing (safety measure preventing accidental misconnection)
	 * Leads to receiving system
  
  2. **Receiving System**:
	 * Reservoir bag with two spring-loaded valves:
	   * Positive-pressure relief valve (opens at 1000 Pa) protects against distal obstruction
	   * Negative-pressure relief valve (opens at -50 Pa) protects against excessive vacuum
	 * Without these valves:
	   * Excessive positive pressure could cause barotrauma
	   * Excessive negative pressure could collapse breathing system reservoir bag, risking rebreathing
  
  3. **Disposal System**:
	 * Wide-bore copper pipe leading directly to atmosphere or via theatre ventilation system
* **Operating Principle** 
  * Exhaled gases driven by patient's respiratory effort or ventilator
  * Receiving system mounted on anesthesia machine to minimize transfer tubing length and resistance
* **Limitations and Problems** 
  * Risk of recirculation or flow reversal if connected to theatre ventilation exit grille
  * Wind at outlet may affect performance or reverse flow
  * Outlet should have wire mesh to protect against insects
  * Compression/occlusion of hose may lead to gas escape into theatre
  * Disposal hose should be non-compressible and kept off floor

![Diagram of a passive scavenging system](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_pdf_WORKSTATION.pdf_p137_img1_4616b6.jpeg)

**Bottom Line:** Passive scavenging systems offer a simple, cost-effective approach to waste gas removal without requiring powered components. While straightforward in design, they require careful installation to prevent flow reversal and occlusion issues. Safety valves provide critical protection against both excessive positive and negative pressures that could harm patients.

## 6.7. ACTIVE SCAVENGING SYSTEMS











**Active scavenging systems use a low-pressure vacuum system to actively draw waste anesthetic gases through the scavenging circuit, providing more reliable extraction than passive systems.**

* **Basic Design** 
  * Uses vacuum system to actively move gases through scavenging circuit
  * Can handle wide range of expiratory flow rates (30-130 L/min)
* **Components** 
  1. **Collecting and Transfer System**:
	 * Similar to passive system
	 * 30-mm connector for safety against misconnection
  
  2. **Receiving System**:
	 * Options include:
	   * Valveless, open-ended reservoir between receiving and disposal components
	   * Bacterial filter downstream
	   * Visual flow indicator
	   * Reservoir bag with two spring-loaded safety valves
  
  3. **Active Disposal System**:
	 * Fan or pump to generate vacuum
* **Operating Principle** 
  * Low-pressure vacuum system drives gases through system
  * Receiving system manages variable gas flow rates:
	* During increased demand (excessive negative pressure), ambient air is entrained to maintain pressure
	* During excessive positive pressure, excess gas is released
  * Result is uniform gas flow to disposal system
* **Safety Features** 
  * Reservoir designed to prevent excessive negative/positive pressures affecting patient
  * Excessive negative pressure could collapse breathing system reservoir bag, causing rebreathing
  * Excessive positive pressure could cause barotrauma if obstruction occurs

![Diagram of an active scavenging system](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_pdf_WORKSTATION.pdf_p140_img1_c5f4dc.jpeg)

**Bottom Line:** Active scavenging systems provide more reliable waste gas extraction than passive systems by using a vacuum source to draw gases through the circuit. They incorporate safety features to manage variable flow rates and prevent pressure extremes from affecting the patient breathing system.

## 6.8. CHARCOAL CANISTERS (CARDIFF ALDASORBER)











**Charcoal canisters provide a compact, passive scavenging method for volatile anesthetic agents using activated charcoal to absorb waste gases.**

* **Basic Design** 
  * Compact passive scavenging system
  * Contains activated charcoal particles
  * Connected via transfer tubing to APL valve or ventilator expiratory valve
* **Operating Principle** 
  * Charcoal particles absorb halogenated inhalational agents (halothane, enflurane, isoflurane)
  * Canister weight increases as it absorbs agents
  * Typically replaced after 12 hours of use
* **Limitations** 
  * Cannot absorb nitrous oxide
  * Heating the canister causes release of trapped inhalational agents
  * Weight is the only indicator of saturation/exhaustion

![Image of charcoal canister connected to APL valve](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_pdf_WORKSTATION.pdf_p145_img1_1f3853.jpeg)

**Bottom Line:** Charcoal canisters (Cardiff Aldasorber) offer a simple solution for absorbing halogenated anesthetic agents but cannot absorb nitrous oxide. Their increasing weight indicates progressive saturation, requiring replacement after approximately 12 hours of use. They must be handled carefully as heating can release trapped agents back into the atmosphere.

## 6.9. CONTRAFLURAN ANAESTHETIC GAS CAPTURE SYSTEM











**The Contrafluran system represents an advanced approach to anesthetic gas management, focusing on environmental sustainability by capturing, recycling, and potentially reusing anesthetic agents.**

* **System Concept** 
  * Pioneered by Baxter
  * Designed to address environmental impact of anesthetic agents (which undergo minimal metabolism and are exhaled unchanged)
  * Captures sevoflurane and desflurane for potential reuse
  * Reduces contribution to greenhouse gas effect
* **System Components** 
  * Compact canisters with highly porous granules
  * Connected to expiratory limb of breathing system
  * Attached to anesthetic machine
  * Separate canisters required for sevoflurane and desflurane
  * Visual indicator and audible alarm signal when canister is full
* **Capacity and Use** 
  * One canister can hold approximately 240 mL of desflurane (equivalent to one bottle)
  * Manufacturers collect full canisters to:
	* Extract captured vapors
	* Separate components
	* Sterilize agents
	* Potentially reuse as active ingredients in new anesthetic vapors
* **Regulatory Status** 
  * As of publication, license approval for recycled vapor use was pending in UK

**Bottom Line:** The Contrafluran Anaesthetic Gas Capture System represents an environmentally focused approach to waste anesthetic management, capturing agents that would otherwise be released as greenhouse gases. The system creates potential for recycling and reuse, though regulatory approval for recycled anesthetic agents is still developing.

## 7.1. EQUIPMENT FAILURE STUDIES











**Analyses of anesthesia equipment failures reveal patterns that inform safety improvements, with the bulk of incidents attributable to user error rather than device malfunction.**

* **Key Findings from Decade-Long Study (1,000 incidents)**:
  * Most common failure: Equipment leaks (61/1,000 cases)
  * Primary causes:
	* Design weakness (e.g., disconnected push-on tapers in breathing circuits)
	* Poor equipment maintenance
	* Inadequate setup
  * Detection: Pulse oximeter alarm most commonly alerted to problems
* **Equipment Ventilation**:
  * Proper ventilation critical for function
  * Risk: Entrapped cables can hinder ventilation
  * Prevention: Maintain organized, tidy workstation environment
* **ASA "Closed Claims" Analysis**:
  * **1997 Review Findings**:
	* Medical gas-delivery system claims: Rare but severe (76% death/permanent brain injury)
	* Equipment malfunction distribution:
	  * Breathing circuit: 39%
	  * Vaporizers: 21%
	  * Ventilators: 17%
	  * Gas tanks/lines: 11%
	  * Anesthesia machine: 7%
	* Cause breakdown: 75% use error, 25% equipment failure
* **2013 Update (1990-2011 incidents)**:
  * 40 most recent claims analysis:
	* Provider error: 68%
	* Equipment failure: 13%
	* Combined factors: 18%
  * 35% of incidents preventable with proper preanesthesia checkout
* **2017 Study**:
  * 130 anesthesia machines evaluated
  * 14% failed to meet requirements
  * Recommendation: Regular tracking of device performance

**Bottom Line:** Analysis of anesthesia equipment failures consistently shows that user error is the predominant cause rather than equipment malfunction. Many incidents could be prevented through proper preanesthesia checkout procedures, regular maintenance, and thorough understanding of equipment function. Vigilant monitoring, particularly pulse oximetry, remains crucial for early detection of problems.

## 7.2. SAFETY FEATURES OF MODERN WORKSTATIONS











**Modern anesthesia workstations incorporate numerous safety improvements to address limitations of conventional machines, though increased complexity creates new challenges.**

* **Conventional Machine Limitations**:
  * Lack of barotrauma prevention during oxygen flush
  * No automated preuse checkout capability
  * Multiple external connections
  * Gas-driven ventilator bellows issues:
	* Incomplete emptying
	* Risk of "breath stacking"
	* Inaccurate tidal volume delivery
* **Modern Workstation Improvements**:
  * Fresh Gas Decoupling (FGD) for barotrauma prevention
  * Integrated software-driven self-checkout
  * Limited external connections
  * Electronic, piston-driven ventilators for accurate tidal volumes
* **Current Challenges**:
  * Increased system complexity reduces troubleshooting ability
  * Greater training requirements for maintenance personnel
  * *Critical safety measure*: Maintain immediate access to manual resuscitation equipment:
	* Portable oxygen tank
	* Self-inflating bag
	* Mask
* **Multiple Safety Features in Contemporary Machines**
  * Gas supply safety features:
	* Copper reinforced hoses to prevent compression and kinking
	* Color-coded equipment for each gas
	* Schrader valves at wall end unique to each gas
	* Non-interlocking screw thread system at machine end
	* Pin index system for cylinders
	* Bodok seal and yoke with one-way pressure valves and pop-off valves
	* High-flow oxygen flush bypassing flowmeters and vaporizers
	* Alternative backup supply of wall oxygen and cylinder oxygen
	* Supply failure alarms
	* Pressure regulators and valves to prevent damage
  
  * Vaporizer safety features:
	* Temperature compensation (bellows, bimetallic strip, thermal properties)
	* Anti-spillage mechanisms
	* Back pressure protection
	* Interlock systems preventing simultaneous use
	* Keyed filling systems and color coding
  * Flowmeter safety features:
	* Oxygen as the last gas in sequence
	* Oxygen dial distinctive (serrated, projecting, left position)
	* Hypoxic guard mechanisms
	* Anti-static coatings
	* Mechanical stops
	* Flow restrictors

## 7.3. COMMON GAS OUTLET











**The common gas outlet marks the transition point where the anesthesia machine ends and the breathing system begins, with standardized connections for reliable attachment.**

* **Definition and Function** 
  * Point where anesthesia machine "ends"
  * Where gas mixture from flowmeters plus anesthetic agent from vaporizer exits the machine
  * Entry point to fresh gas tubing that connects to breathing system
* **Design Specifications** 
  * Conically tapered pipe with 22-mm male/15-mm female connector fitting
  * Can be fixed or on a swivelling connector
  * Must be strong enough to withstand torque of up to 10 Nm to support heavy attached equipment

![Common gas outlet](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_pdf_WORKSTATION.pdf_p102_img1_e2b31a.jpeg)

**Bottom Line:** The common gas outlet serves as the critical interface between the anesthesia machine and breathing circuit, featuring standardized dimensions to ensure reliable connections. Its robust design accommodates the weight of attached components while maintaining a secure gas-tight connection.

## 7.4. EMERGENCY OXYGEN FLUSH











**The emergency oxygen flush provides immediate high-flow oxygen delivery, bypassing machine controls to rapidly fill the breathing circuit or deliver emergency oxygen.**

* **Function and Design** 
  * Usually activated by a non-locking button
  * When pressed, delivers pure oxygen from outlet of anesthetic machine
  * Bypasses flowmeters and vaporizers
  * Provides flow of 35-75 L/min at pressure of about 400 kPa
  * Button is recessed in housing to prevent accidental depression
* **Clinical Uses** 
  * Rapid filling of breathing circuit
  * Emergency oxygen delivery
  * Clearing anesthetic gases from circuit
* **Safety Considerations** 
  * High operating pressure and flow increases risk of barotrauma
  * Inappropriate use leads to dilution of anesthetic gases and possible awareness
  * Should not be activated while ventilating patient using minute volume divider ventilator

![Emergency oxygen flush button](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_pdf_WORKSTATION.pdf_p97_img1_788212.jpeg)

**Bottom Line:** The emergency oxygen flush delivers a high flow of 100% oxygen directly to the breathing circuit, bypassing all controls. While valuable for rapid circuit filling or emergency oxygenation, it carries risks of barotrauma, anesthetic dilution, and ventilator interference if used inappropriately.

## 7.5. COMPRESSED OXYGEN OUTLETS











**Anesthesia machines include one or more compressed oxygen outlets that provide oxygen at pipeline pressure for powering external devices or delivering supplemental oxygen.**

* **Basic Function** 
  * Provides oxygen at approximately 400 kPa (4 bar)
  * Can drive ventilators or manually controlled jet injectors
  * May be used for supplemental oxygen delivery systems
* **Applications**:
  * Power source for pneumatic devices
  * Emergency oxygen delivery outside the main breathing circuit
  * Supplemental oxygen for procedures under regional anesthesia

![Compressed oxygen outlet](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_pdf_WORKSTATION.pdf_p98_img1_fbe37d.jpeg)

**Bottom Line:** Compressed oxygen outlets provide regulated oxygen at pipeline pressure for powering pneumatic devices or delivering auxiliary oxygen. These outlets serve critical functions when oxygen is needed outside the main breathing circuit or for driving specialized equipment.

## 7.6. OXYGEN SUPPLY FAILURE ALARM











**Oxygen supply failure alarms provide critical warning when oxygen pressure falls below safe levels, with integrated systems to prevent delivery of hypoxic gas mixtures.**

* **Ideal Warning Device Characteristics** 
  1. Activation dependent on oxygen pressure itself
  2. No batteries or mains power required
  3. Audible signal of special character with sufficient duration and volume
  4. Warning of both impending failure and actual failure
  5. Pressure-linked controls that interrupt other gas flows when activated
  6. Allow atmospheric air delivery without CO₂ accumulation
  7. Prevent resumption of anesthesia until oxygen supply restored
  8. Positioned on reduced pressure side of oxygen supply line
  9. Tamper-proof design
  10. Not affected by ventilator back pressure

* **Operation in Modern Machines** 
  * Low-pressure supply alarm sounds when oxygen pressure falls below 200 kPa
  * At supply pressures below 137 kPa, "fail-safe" valve interrupts flow of other gases to flowmeters
  * Oxygen flow from flowmeter will not decrease until oxygen supply pressure falls below 100 kPa
* **Response System** 
  * Alarm triggers at initial pressure drop
  * Progressive pressure decrease activates gas shutoff systems
  * Systems prevent delivery of hypoxic mixtures when oxygen supply compromised

![Oxygen supply failure alarm](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_pdf_WORKSTATION.pdf_p99_img1_648210.jpeg)

**Bottom Line:** Oxygen supply failure alarms provide essential warning of decreasing oxygen pressure, with integrated systems that progressively shut off other gases as pressure falls. This critical safety feature helps prevent delivery of hypoxic mixtures during oxygen supply failures, while allowing continued ventilation with atmospheric air.

## 7.7. ANTI-HYPOXIC SAFETY FEATURES











**Modern anesthesia machines incorporate multiple overlapping safety systems to prevent the delivery of hypoxic gas mixtures to patients.**

* **Types of Anti-Hypoxic Systems** 
  1. **Mechanical Means**:
	 * Chain linkage between oxygen and nitrous oxide flow control valves
	 * Increasing nitrous oxide flow leads to proportional oxygen flow increase
	 * Oxygen flowmeter control valve has mechanical stop ensuring minimum flow of 175-250 mL/min even when apparently closed
  
  2. **Pneumatic Means**:
	 * Pressure-sensitive diaphragm measures changes in oxygen and nitrous oxide concentrations
	 * System designed to ensure oxygen flow increases by 25% of any nitrous oxide flow increase
  
  3. **Electronic Monitoring**:
	 * Paramagnetic oxygen analyzer continuously measures oxygen concentration
	 * Automatically switches off nitrous oxide when oxygen concentration falls below 25%
* **European Standard Requirements** 
  * EN 740 requires anesthesia machines to have means to prevent delivery of gas mixtures with oxygen concentration below 25%
  * Modern designs make it impossible to deliver nitrous oxide without adding a fixed percentage of oxygen
* **Oxygen Failure Response System** 
  * When oxygen pressure falls below 200 kPa, low-pressure supply alarm activates
  * With supply pressures below 137 kPa, "fail-safe" valve interrupts flow of other gases
  * Oxygen flow set on flowmeter remains until pressure falls below 100 kPa

![Oxygen supply failure mechanism](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_pdf_WORKSTATION.pdf_p101_img1_1762c0.jpeg)

**Bottom Line:** Anti-hypoxic safety features use mechanical linkages, pneumatic systems, or electronic monitoring to ensure adequate oxygen concentration in delivered gas mixtures. These systems prevent delivery of hypoxic mixtures by either increasing oxygen flow proportionally with other gases or shutting off other gases when oxygen levels fall too low.

## 8.2. OXYGEN ANALYZER CALIBRATION











**Oxygen analyzers are critical safety devices that monitor delivered oxygen concentration and serve as the only continuous monitors of low-pressure circuit integrity.**

* **Critical Safety Features**:
  * Only continuous monitor of Low-Pressure Circuit (LPC) integrity
  * Detects problems downstream from flow control valves
* **Calibration Process**:
  * *Traditional machines (fuel cell)*:
	* Expose to room air (21% at sea level)
	* Manual dial adjustment may be needed
  * *Modern workstations*:
	* Temporary sensor removal
	* Menu-guided calibration
	* Some use paramagnetic analyzers with automatic calibration
	  * Some measure PO₂ and express it as a percentage of 1 atm at sea level
	  * ⚠️ If used at altitude where total air pressure is reduced, would read less than 21% even if composition unchanged
* **Hypoxic Mixture Prevention**
  * Oxygen analyzer is essential backup to mechanical proportioning systems
  * Alarms for low FiO₂ provide critical warning of potential hypoxic mixtures
  * Only reliable method to detect wrong gas in oxygen pipeline

**Bottom Line:** Oxygen analyzers are critical safety devices that must be properly calibrated before each clinical day. They provide the only continuous monitoring of the low-pressure circuit integrity and serve as the final safeguard against delivery of hypoxic gas mixtures, regardless of the cause.

## 8.3. LOW-PRESSURE CIRCUIT (LPC) LEAK TEST











**Low-pressure circuit leak testing is essential for detecting leaks in the vulnerable area between flow control valves and the common gas outlet, which could lead to hypoxic mixtures or inadequate anesthetic delivery.**

* **Purpose and Importance**:
  * Checks machine integrity from flow control valves to common gas outlet
  * Evaluates components most prone to breakage
  * Leaks can cause:
	* Hypoxia
	* Patient awareness
* **Common Leak Sources**:
  * Flow tubes (most delicate component)
  * 16 O-rings in typical three-gas machine
  * Glass flow tube-manifold interface
  * Vaporizer O-ring junctions
  * Loose vaporizer filler caps
* **Testing Methods**:
  * *Machine-specific approaches*:
	* Positive-pressure test for machines without check valves
	* Negative-pressure test for machines with check valves
  * *FDA Universal negative-pressure test*:
	* Uses 15-cc suction bulb
	* Generates -65 mmHg pressure
	* Must remain collapsed for 10 seconds
	* ⚠️ Test each vaporizer individually when turned "on"

## 8.4. EVALUATION OF THE CIRCLE SYSTEM











**Circle system evaluation tests both pressure integrity and proper gas flow, ensuring no leaks or obstructions exist that could compromise ventilation.**

* **Components and Coverage**:
  * Tests evaluate circle breathing system from machine common gas outlet to Y-piece
  * Two main components:
	* Breathing system pressure and leak testing
	* Verification of proper gas flow during inspiration/expiration
* **Testing Requirements**:
  * Both tests must be performed preoperatively
  * ASA 2008 recommendations:
	* Perform before each case
	* Test during both manual/bag and automatic/mechanical ventilation
  * Modern workstations feature automated leak testing
	* System compliance calculated for volume delivery adjustment
* **Additional Testing Methods**:
  * Test lung or second reservoir bag at Y-piece needed because:
	* Pressure/leak tests cannot identify all obstructions
	* Cannot confirm unidirectional valve function
  * Daily visual inspection of unidirectional valves required
	* *Note*: Subtle valve damage may be difficult to detect
  * 1993 FDA checkout procedures available but complex for daily use
* **1993 FDA Leak Test Procedure**:
  * Steps:
	* Close APL (pop-off) valve
	* Occlude Y-piece
	* Pressurize circuit to 30 cm H₂O using oxygen flush
  * *Limitation*: Does not assure unidirectional valve integrity
* **Flow Test Protocol**:
  * Purpose: Checks unidirectional valves and detects circuit obstruction
  * Method 1 (affects circuit sterility):
	* Remove Y-piece from circle system
	* Test breathing through corrugated hoses individually
	* Verify one-way valve function:
	  * Inspiratory limb: Can inhale, cannot exhale
	  * Expiratory limb: Can exhale, cannot inhale
	* *Important*: Ensure no anesthetic gas in circuit before testing
  * Method 2 (safer):
	* Use ventilator with reservoir bag at Y-piece
	* Follow 1993 FDA Recommendations (Steps 11 and 12)
* **Carbon Dioxide Absorption**
  * Essential component of circle system - permits safe rebreathing without CO₂ rebreathing
  * **Soda lime composition**:
	* 75% Calcium hydroxide
	* 20% Water (encourages CO₂ absorption, discourages anesthetic gas absorption)
	* 3% Sodium hydroxide
	* 1% Potassium hydroxide
	* 1% Binding agents (silicates)
  
  * **Chemical reaction**:
	* Initially: CO₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O and CO₂ + 2KOH → K₂CO₃ + H₂O
	* Then: K₂CO₃ + Ca(OH)₂ → 2KOH + CaCO₃ and Na₂CO₃ + Ca(OH)₂ → 2NaOH + CaCO₃
	* Overall: CO₂ + Ca(OH)₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O (exothermic)
	* 1kg soda lime absorbs up to 250 liters of CO₂
	* Canister typically contains ~2kg granules (4-8 mesh size)
  
  * **Color change indicators**:
	* Phenolphthalein: Pink to white
	* Ethyl violet: White to purple
  
  * **Potential issues**:
	* Carbon monoxide formation with isoflurane/desflurane through dry, hot absorbent (agents with CHF₂ complex)
	* Sevoflurane can produce compounds A, B, D, E, G (theoretical nephrotoxicity risk with compound A)
	* Soda lime dust can damage airways
	* Resistance to gas flow

**Bottom Line:** Comprehensive circle system evaluation requires both pressure/leak testing and flow verification to ensure proper function of all components, especially unidirectional valves. Modern workstations automate some testing, but visual inspection of valves and verification of CO₂ absorbent condition remain essential manual checks before each case.

## 8.5. WORKSTATION SELF-TESTS











**Modern anesthesia workstations include automated self-testing capabilities, but these have limitations and do not replace the need for manual checks of certain components.**

* **Modern Features**:
  * Integrated electronic, mechanical, and pneumatic testing

	* Gas supply system
	* Flow control valves
	* Circle system
	* Ventilator
	* Integrated vaporizers
* **Important Considerations**:
  * No fully automated checkouts available

	* Automated checkout may miss leaks if vaporizer is off

* **Supplementary Manual Checks**:
  * Visual inspection of unidirectional valves

* **Limitations of Self-Tests**
  * May not detect subtle valve damage

**Bottom Line:** While automated workstation self-tests improve efficiency and standardization, they have significant limitations and do not replace the need for manual checks of critical components. Understanding what is and isn't tested automatically is essential for comprehensive machine preparation.

### 8.6.2. SYSTEMATIC CHECKOUT PROCEDURE

1. **Emergency Equipment**:
   * Verify backup ventilation equipment availability and function

2. **High-Pressure System**:
   * Oxygen cylinder check:
	 * Open O₂ cylinder - verify ≥1,000 psi (half full)
	 * Close cylinder after check
   * Central pipeline verification:
	 * Confirm hose connections
	 * Verify ~50 psi on pipeline gauges

3. **Low-Pressure System**:
   * Initial status check:
	 * Close all flow control valves
	 * Turn off vaporizers
	 * Check vaporizer fill levels and cap tightness
   * Leak test procedure:
	 * Verify master switch and flow controls OFF
	 * Attach suction bulb to fresh gas outlet
	 * Collapse bulb and maintain for 10 seconds
	 * Test each vaporizer individually
	 * Reconnect fresh gas hose

4. **Electrical Systems**:
   * Activate master switch and all necessary electrical equipment

5. **Flow Systems**:
   * Flowmeter testing:
	 * Check full range gas flow
	 * Verify float operation
	 * Inspect flow tubes for damage
	 * Test hypoxic mixture safeguards

6. **Scavenging System**:
   * Connection verification:
	 * Check APL valve connections
	 * Verify ventilator relief valve connections
   * System testing:
	 * Adjust waste gas vacuum
	 * Test reservoir bag collapse (pressure ~0)
	 * Verify pressure <10 cm H₂O during O₂ flush

7. **Breathing System**:
   * O₂ monitor calibration:
	 * Verify 21% in room air
	 * Test low O₂ alarm
	 * Confirm >90% with O₂ flush
   * Circuit verification:
	 * Set to "Bag" mode
	 * Check circuit integrity
	 * Verify CO₂ absorbent
	 * Install necessary accessories

8. **Leak Testing**:
   * Breathing system check:
	 * Zero all gas flows
	 * Close APL valve and occlude Y-piece
	 * Pressurize to 30 cm H₂O
	 * Maintain pressure for 10 seconds
	 * Verify pressure release with APL valve

9. **Ventilation Systems**:
   * Complete testing protocol:
	 * Install test breathing bag
	 * Configure ventilator settings
	 * Test automatic and manual modes
	 * Verify proper bellows function
	 * Check unidirectional valves
	 * Test circuit accessories

10. **Monitor Setup**:
	* Calibrate and set alarms:
	  * Capnometer
	  * Oxygen analyzer
	  * Pressure monitors
	  * Pulse oximeter
	  * Spirometer

11. **Final Configuration**:
	* Verify machine status:
	  * Vaporizers OFF
	  * APL valve OPEN
	  * Selector on "bag" mode
	  * Flowmeters at minimum
	  * Suction adequate
	  * Breathing system ready

**Bottom Line:** The 1993 FDA checkout recommendations provide a comprehensive approach for verifying anesthesia machine functionality. While these procedures were designed for conventional anesthesia machines and may require adaptation for modern workstations, they establish crucial safety verification principles that remain relevant regardless of equipment generation.

# 9. MEDICAL GAS SYSTEMS

## 9.1. OXYGEN SYSTEMS











**Oxygen supply systems range from large-scale liquid storage facilities to individual cylinders, each with specific operating characteristics and safety features.**

* **Sources of Medical Oxygen**:
  * **Manufacturing**:
	* Medical grade oxygen (99% or 99.5% pure) manufactured by fractional distillation of liquefied air
	* Adiabatic expansion during fractional distillation of air (most common)
	* Heating barium oxide to 800°C (inefficient)
	* Oxygen concentrators using zeolite to adsorb nitrogen from air (up to 93% oxygen)
  
  * **Cylinder Supply**:
	* Small hospitals store oxygen in two separate banks of high-pressure cylinders (H-cylinders) connected by manifold
	* Only one bank used at time; number of cylinders depends on anticipated daily demand
	* Manifold contains valves that reduce cylinder pressure (~2000 psig) to line pressure (55 ± 5 psig)
	* Automatic bank switching when one group is exhausted

	![Untitled](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_Untitled.png_5496c4.png_89a2a1.png)

	![Untitled](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_Untitled_1.png_587df7.png_46e711.png)

  * **Liquid Oxygen Storage**:
	* More economical system
	* Liquid oxygen stored below critical temperature of -119°C
	* Gases can only be liquefied by pressure when below their critical temperature
	* Vacuum Insulated Evaporator (VIE) system:
	  * Essentially an enormous thermos flask storing liquid oxygen
	  * Vacuum insulating layer around container
	  * Uses latent heat of vaporization to maintain -150°C
	  * No active cooling mechanism required
	  * Maintains internal pressure up to 10 bar
	  * 840 liters of gaseous oxygen per liter of liquid oxygen

	![Untitled](ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_ANESTHESIA_WORKSTATION_Untitled_2.png_306d7f.png_bbaada.png)

* **E-Cylinders of Oxygen**:
  * Pressure of 1000 psig in half-full E-cylinder provides 330L of O₂ at atmospheric pressure and 20°C
  * If O₂ consumed at 3L/min, half-full cylinder will empty in 110 min
  * Pin index safety system prevents inadvertent crossover and connections
  * Safety features:
	* "Plug" made from Wood's metal with low melting point allows pressure dissipation in fire
	* Pressure relief valve ruptures at 3300 psig (below 5000 psig cylinder wall tolerance) to prevent overfilling
* **Oxygen Cylinder Characteristics**
  * Boiling point: -185°C
  * Critical temperature: -118°C (above this temperature, oxygen cannot be compressed to liquid form)
  * Physical properties:
	* Stored as gas in cylinders at 13,700 kPa (137 bar)
	* Stored as liquid in VIE at -150°C and 1000 kPa (10 bar)
  
  * Testing and maintenance:
	* Made from molybdenum steel
	* Inspected inside and outside annually
	* Hydraulic testing to 50% above working pressure:
	  * Every 5 years for composite cylinders
	  * Every 10 years for steel cylinders
	* Plastic ring around neck indicates next test date
	* British Compressed Gases Association recommends pressure regulators be refurbished/replaced every 5 years
* **Liquid Oxygen Storage Systems** 
  * Vacuum Insulated Evaporator (VIE):
	* A thermally insulated, double-walled steel tank with perlite in vacuum layer for insulation 
	* Functions like a giant thermos flask 
	* Stores liquid oxygen at -150°C to -170°C (below critical temperature of -119°C) at pressure of 1000 kPa 
	* No active cooling - temperature maintained by vacuum insulation and latent heat of vaporization 
	* Weight monitoring:
	  * VIE rests on weighing balance to measure liquid oxygen mass 
	  * Alternative differential pressure gauge measures height of liquid column 
	  * Data connects to hospital alarm system for supply monitoring 
	* At 15°C and atmospheric pressure, liquid oxygen gives 842 times its volume as gas 
	* Safety considerations:
	  * Located away from main buildings due to fire hazard 
	  * Increased fire risk with liquid spillage 
	  * Cryogenic liquid can cause cold burns, frostbite, and hypothermia 
* **Oxygen Concentrators** 
  * Also known as pressure swing adsorption systems 
  * Extract oxygen from air by differential adsorption 
  * Available in sizes from single-patient supply to hospital pipeline system supply 
  * Operating mechanism:
	* Uses zeolite molecular sieve columns 
	* Ambient air filtered and pressurized to 137 kPa 
	* Zeolite selectively retains nitrogen, water vapor, and unwanted components 
	* Sequential column switching (typically 20-second cycles) provides continuous oxygen supply 
	* Unused columns heated to release nitrogen and water vapor 
	* Maximum oxygen concentration of 95% (remaining 5% mostly argon) 
	* Zeolite crystal life approximately 20,000 hours (10 years) 
	* Note: When used with circle systems, higher fresh gas flows needed to prevent argon accumulation 

**Bottom Line:** Oxygen supply systems include cylinder banks, liquid storage facilities, and concentrators, each with specific operating characteristics. Understanding pressure, volume, and temperature relationships is critical for estimating available oxygen. Safety features prevent misconnections, overpressurization, and hazards during fires.

## 9.2. NITROUS OXIDE SYSTEMS











**Nitrous oxide has unique storage characteristics due to its physical properties, with specific safety considerations and clinical applications.**

* **Storage Systems**:
  * Almost always stored in large H cylinders connected by manifold with automatic crossover feature
  * Bulk liquid storage only economical in very large institutions
  * Can be kept liquefied without elaborate refrigeration system
* **Cylinder Properties**:
  * E-cylinders contain nitrous oxide in liquid state; volume remaining not proportional to cylinder pressure
  * When liquid nitrous oxide is expended and pressure begins to fall, only about 400L remains
  * At constant temperature (20°C), liquid vaporizes at same rate as consumption, maintaining constant pressure (745 psig) until liquid is exhausted
  * ⚠️ Only reliable way to determine residual volume is to weigh the cylinder
	* Empty weight (tare weight) often stamped on cylinder shoulder
  * Pressure gauge should not exceed 745 psig at 20°C; higher reading suggests gauge malfunction, tank overfill, or wrong gas
* **Physical and Chemical Properties**
  * **Manufacturing**: Ammonium nitrate heated to 250°C: NH₄NO₃ → N₂O + 2H₂O
	* Produces unwanted byproducts (N₂, NH₃, NO, NO₂) which are removed through washing processes
  
  * **Physical characteristics**:
	* Boiling point: -88.5°C
	* Critical temperature: 36.5°C (above this temperature it cannot be compressed to liquid)
	* Molecular weight: 44 (facilitates rapid diffusion)
	* Saturated vapor pressure at 20°C: 5200 kPa (39,000 mmHg)
	* Blood:gas coefficient: 0.47 (provides relatively rapid onset)
	* Oil:gas coefficient: 1.4 (very low potency compared to volatiles)
	* MAC: 103% (requires hyperbaric conditions for sole agent anesthesia)
  
  * **Storage characteristics**:
	* Stored in French blue cylinders as liquid with vapor phase above
	* Pressure remains at SVP (saturated vapor pressure) until all liquid evaporates
	* Gauge shows constant pressure until liquid is gone, then drops rapidly
	* Cylinder must be weighed to determine remaining content
	* Filling ratio in UK: 0.75 (ratio of liquefied gas weight to weight of water that would fill cylinder at 15°C)
* **Clinical Uses and Effects**
  * **Applications**:
	* Adjunct in general anesthesia (reduces volatile requirements, adds analgesia)
	* Analgesia in labor and procedures (joint relocation, dental extractions)
	* Topical dermatology therapy (cryotherapy)
  
  * **Mechanism of action**:
	* Positive allosteric modulator of GABA₍ₐ₎ receptor
	* Antagonist at NMDA receptor
	* Some opioid receptor agonism (explains analgesic effect)
  
  * **Physiological effects**:
	* **Cardiovascular**:
	  * Mild myocardial depression
	  * Increased pulmonary vascular resistance (avoid in pulmonary hypertension)
	  * Minimal blood pressure effect (sympathetic activation maintains SVR)
	* **Respiratory**:
	  * Minor tidal volume reduction but increased rate (stable minute ventilation)
	  * Concentration effect, second gas effect, diffusion hypoxia
	* **Neurological**:
	  * Analgesia (particularly above 20-25% concentration)
	  * Hypnosis (above 70%)
	* **Metabolic**:
	  * Oxidizes cobalt in vitamin B₁₂, preventing its action as methionine synthetase cofactor
	  * Can lead to megaloblastic anemia and bone marrow dysfunction
	  * Teratogenic effects
	* **GI/GU**:
	  * Emetogenic (avoid if known PONV risk)
	  * Expansion of gas-filled spaces (bowel distention during GA)
	  * Non-tocolytic (can be used in emergency C-section GA)
  
  * **Clinical advantages and disadvantages**:
	* **Advantages**:
	  * Potent analgesic
	  * Reduces MAC of volatiles
	  * Rapid onset (low blood:gas coefficient)
	  * Accelerates induction via second gas effect
	* **Disadvantages**:
	  * High diffusion capacity (25× nitrogen): expands air-filled spaces
	  * Diffusion hypoxia at end of use
	  * Emetogenic properties
	  * Bone marrow suppression (vitamin B₁₂ inactivation)
	  * Potential neurotoxicity (subacute combined degeneration)
	  * Environmental impact (1% of global greenhouse effect)
* **Special Phenomena**:
  * **Diffusion Hypoxia**:
	* When N₂O discontinued, rapid diffusion into alveoli dilutes oxygen
	* Opposite of second gas effect
	* Prevented by adequate oxygen administration after discontinuation
  
  * **Concentration Effect**:
	* Higher FiO₂ with nitrous leads to faster alveolar concentration increase
	* Large N₂O volumes rapidly diffuse from alveolus to blood
	* Creates negative pressure drawing more gas/N₂O from conducting airways
	* Most significant at high N₂O concentrations
  
  * **Second Gas Effect**:
	* Volatile agent concentration increases more rapidly than expected when used with N₂O
	* Same mechanism as concentration effect but affecting the co-administered agent
* **Entonox**:
  * 50/50 mixture of oxygen and nitrous oxide for analgesia/mild hypnosis
  * Produced by bubbling oxygen through liquid nitrous oxide (Poynting effect)
  * Stored at 13,700 kPa in cylinders with white and blue shoulders as a gas mixture
  * Has pseudocritical temperature of -6°C:
	* Below this temperature, gases separate via lamination
	* Risks delivering pure oxygen initially, then hypoxic nitrous oxide
	* Cylinders should be stored horizontally above 5°C
	* Some cylinders have dip tubes to prevent hypoxic mixture development
* **Detailed Entonox Characteristics** 
  * Compressed gas mixture with 50% oxygen and 50% nitrous oxide by volume 
  * Created by bubbling oxygen through liquid nitrous oxide (Poynting effect) 
  * Supplied in cylinders at 13,700 kPa and pipeline at 400 kPa 
  * Typically used with two-stage pressure demand regulator 
  * Liquefication and separation occurs in cylinders at temperatures below -5.5°C (pseudo-critical temperature) 
  * Prevention of separation problems:
	* Store cylinders horizontally for 24 hours at temperatures at/above 5°C before use 
	* Horizontal position increases diffusion area 
	* Mixing by repeated inversion allows earlier use 
	* Large cylinders equipped with dip tube ending in liquid phase 
  * Clinical caution:
	* Prolonged use should be avoided due to bone marrow effects 
	* Adequate scavenging needed to protect staff 

**Bottom Line:** Nitrous oxide has unique physical properties that affect its storage and use. Its presence as a liquid in cylinders means pressure gauges are unreliable for determining remaining content. While providing useful analgesia and reducing volatile requirements, it has significant side effects including gas expansion, diffusion hypoxia, emetogenic properties, and potential toxicity with prolonged use. Understanding phenomena like the second gas effect and concentration effect is important for clinical practice.

## 9.3. GAS SUPPLY SAFETY FEATURES









**Multiple safety features are incorporated into medical gas supply systems to prevent incorrect gas delivery, misconnections, and ensure reliable operation.**

* **Cylinder Safety Systems**:
  * **Color coding**:
	* Oxygen: White
	* Nitrous oxide: Blue
	* Medical air: Black/white collar
	* Entonox: Blue/white
	* Helium: Brown
	* Heliox: Brown/white
	* CO₂: Grey
  
  * **Pin indexing system**:
	* Prevents wrong cylinder attachment to yoke
	* Six possible pin positions with unique configurations for each gas:
	  * Oxygen: positions 2 and 5
	  * Air: positions 1 and 5
	  * Nitrous oxide: positions 3 and 5
* **Pressure Systems**:
  * **Cylinder pressures**:
	* Standard gases: 13,700 kPa (137 bar)
	* Nitrous oxide and CO₂: Stored as liquid/vapor (nitrous at 4,400 kPa, CO₂ at 5,700 kPa)
  
  * **Pipeline pressures**:
	* Standard medical gases: 400 kPa (4 bar)
	* Medical air for surgical equipment: 700 kPa (7 bar)
  
  * **Regulation systems**:
	* Pressure regulating valves and flow restrictors upstream of anesthesia machine
	* Non-return pressure relief valves after vaporizers
	* Bodok seals and yokes with one-way pressure valves
* **Oxygen Flush System**:
  * Delivers 100% oxygen at up to 75 liters per minute
  * Operates at pressure of 400 kPa
  * Bypasses flowmeters and vaporizers
* **Hypoxic Mixture Prevention**:
  * Oxygen failure alarm activates if pipeline supply drops below 200 kPa
  * Multiple oxygen supply sources:
	* Wall oxygen (primary)
	* Cylinder on anesthesia machine (backup)
  * Dual monitoring of FiO₂ (monitor and ventilator screens)
  * Proportioning systems linking O₂ and N₂O flows

  * A reserve bank of cylinders available if primary supply fails

  * Tug test performed to detect misconnection 
  * Strictly enforced regulations for installation, repair, and modification 
  * Safety responsibilities:
	* Anesthetists responsible for gases from terminal outlet to machine 
	* Pharmacy, supplies, and engineering departments responsible for pipeline systems "behind the wall" 
  * Regular inspection and replacement (every 2-5 years) of medical gas hoses due to fire risk from wear and rupture 
* **Cylinder Safety and Maintenance** 
  * Markings engraved on cylinder include test pressure, test dates, chemical formula, and tare weight 
  * Careful handling requirements:
	* Store in purpose-built, dry, well-ventilated, fireproof room 
	* Avoid storage near flammable materials (oil, grease), heat sources, dampness, or corrosive chemicals 
	* Separate storage of full and empty cylinders 
	* Store larger cylinders (F, G, J) upright to protect valves 
	* Store smaller cylinders (C, D, E) horizontally on appropriate shelving 
  * Before use:
	* Remove plastic wrapping just before use 
	* Slightly open and close valve ("crack" it) before connecting to clear particles 
	* Open slowly when attached to prevent rapid pressure/temperature rise 
	* Fully open when in use (turn two full revolutions) 
	* Avoid overtightening during closure

## 9.4. ANESTHESIA IN REMOTE AREAS











**Specialized anesthesia equipment designed for remote areas prioritizes compactness, portability, and robustness while maintaining essential functionality.**

* **Triservice Apparatus** 
  * Designed for use in remote areas where compressed gas and vapor supply is difficult 
  * Named for the three military service branches (Army, Navy, Air Force) 
  * Components:
	* Face mask with non-rebreathing valve 
	* Short tubing leading to self-inflating bag 
	* Second tubing from bag to two Oxford Miniature Vaporizers (OMVs) 
	* Optional oxygen cylinder connection upstream of vaporizers 
	* Third length of tubing as oxygen reservoir during expiration 
* **Operating Principles** 
  * Usable for both spontaneous and controlled ventilation:
	* Patient can draw air through vaporizers; exhaled gases vented via non-rebreathing valve 
	* Self-inflating bag can provide controlled or assisted ventilation 
  * Oxford Miniature Vaporizer (OMV) features:
	* Variable bypass, low-resistance draw-over vaporizer with 50 mL capacity 
	* Metal wick without temperature compensation 
	* Ethylene glycol jacket as heat sink to stabilize temperature 
	* Detachable calibration scale allowing use with different agents 
	* Agent change possible after 10 minutes air blowing and wick rinsing 
	* Extendable feet 
  * Traditional configuration:
	* Downstream vaporizer filled with trichloroethylene to compensate for absence of nitrous oxide analgesia 
* **Limitations and Safety Considerations** 
  * Heat sink inadequate for prolonged use at high gas flows; vapor concentration decreases as temperature falls 
  * Accidental tipping can spill liquid agent into breathing system; vaporizer is spill-proof when turned off 
* **Glostavent Helix Duo** 
  * Modern system developed for low-resource environments with unreliable compressed gas and electricity 
  * Features:
	* Two low-resistance draw-over vaporizers (sevoflurane for induction, isoflurane for maintenance)

	* Mains and battery power with integral oxygen concentrator (10 L/min) 
	* Built-in ventilator (pressure and volume controlled) 
	* Fuel cell oxygen analyzer 
	* Built-in uninterruptible power supply (30 minutes operation) 
	* Suitable for adults, pediatrics, and neonates 

**Bottom Line:** Specialized anesthesia equipment for remote areas focuses on simplicity, portability, and independence from compressed gas and reliable electricity sources. These systems, from the historical Triservice apparatus to the modern Glostavent Helix Duo, allow safe anesthesia delivery in resource-limited settings through innovative designs that maintain essential functionality despite challenging conditions.

## 9.5. SPECIALIZED ANESTHESIA SYSTEMS











**Various specialized anesthesia machines have been developed to address specific clinical scenarios or environments requiring unique features or designs.**

  * Special machines made of non-ferrous materials solve this problem 
* **Compact Anesthesia Machines** 
  * Small, compact designs available for use in restricted spaces 
  * Maintain essential functions in reduced footprint

  * Specialized features:

	* Design prevents delivery of hypoxic mixtures 
	* Primarily used in dental anesthesia 
* **Auxiliary Oxygen Supply Features** 
  * Some modern machines include extra outlet with dedicated flowmeter 
  * Designed to deliver oxygen to conscious or lightly sedated patients via face mask 
  * Useful for patients undergoing surgery under regional anesthesia with sedation 

**Bottom Line:** Specialized anesthesia machines address unique clinical needs beyond standard operating room applications. These include MRI-compatible models made of non-ferrous materials, compact units for restricted spaces, systems with simplified controls for specific applications like dental anesthesia, and machines with dedicated auxiliary oxygen delivery for regional anesthesia cases.
