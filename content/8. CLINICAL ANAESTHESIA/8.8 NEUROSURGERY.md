## 1.1. DEFINITION AND SCOPE

**Neuroanesthesia is the practice of perioperative medicine related to the treatment of diseases or injury to the central nervous system (CNS) or peripheral nervous system (PNS).**

- The **CNS encompasses the brain and spinal cord**
- The **PNS includes all peripheral nerves** emanating from the brain and spinal cord
- Neuroanesthesia provides anesthesia and analgesia for multiple procedures:
  - Invasive procedures
  - Minimally invasive procedures  
  - Neurodiagnostic procedures
  - Neurointerventional procedures
- Involves procedures on brain, spinal cord, and peripheral nerves

**Competency Focus:** `[COMP: MK]`, `[COMP: PC]`

**Bottom Line:** Neuroanesthesia encompasses perioperative care for procedures involving the entire nervous system, requiring specialized knowledge of neurophysiology and neuropharmacology.

# 3. CENTRAL NERVOUS SYSTEM STRUCTURE

## 3.1. BRAIN COMPARTMENTS

**The CNS is composed of the brain and spinal cord, with the brain enclosed by the cranium, a fixed bony cavity.**

- **Supratentorium:** Contains paired cerebral hemispheres and diencephalon
  - Diencephalon composed of thalamus and hypothalamus
- **Infratentorium:** Encompasses brainstem and cerebellum

![Gross anatomy of the brain](https://images-na.ssl-images-amazon.com/images/G/01/books/sample/B01MXPXAIB/B01MXPXAIB_sample.png)

## 3.2. CEREBRAL HEMISPHERES AND LOBES

**Each cerebral hemisphere is divided into four lobes: frontal, temporal, parietal, and occipital.**

- **Frontal lobe:** Primary motor cortex, personality, executive function
- **Parietal lobe:** Primary somatosensory cortex  
- **Temporal lobe:** Primary auditory cortex, limbic structures
- **Occipital lobe:** Primary visual cortex

## 3.3. ELOQUENT CORTICAL AREAS

**Eloquent cortical areas are regions responsible for gross motor function and language.**

- **Primary somatosensory and motor cortex strips:**
  - Located adjacent to central sulcus
  - Extend inferiorly to Sylvian fissure
- **Language regions:** Located in left hemisphere in almost all right-handed and majority of left-handed people
  - **Broca area:** Premotor frontal cortex, responsible for language formation
  - **Wernicke area:** Posterior superior temporal cortex, responsible for language acquisition
- **Clinical significance:**
  - Broca area lesions → expressive aphasia
  - Wernicke area lesions → receptive aphasia

## 3.4. EXTRAPYRAMIDAL SYSTEM

**Group of brain structures that modify motor function but are not components of the corticospinal tract and primary motor cortex.**

**Components include:**
- **Basal ganglia:** Caudate nucleus, globus pallidus, putamen, substantia nigra, red nucleus
- **Cerebellum**
- **Components of auditory and vestibular pathways**

**Dysfunction results in:**
- Difficulty with motor control without frank weakness
- Parkinson disease
- Essential tremor  
- Ataxia

## 3.5. DIENCEPHALON

**The diencephalon lies cephalad to the midbrain and is composed of thalamus and hypothalamus.**

- **Thalamus:** Acts as sensory and motor "relay station"
  - Functionally and physically connects cortex with rest of nervous system
- **Hypothalamus:** Located below thalamus
  - Autonomic and endocrine functions
  - Connected to pituitary gland via infundibulum

## 3.6. LIMBIC SYSTEM

**Group of structures playing roles in cognitive function, memory consolidation, and emotional responses.**

**Components include:**
- Hippocampus
- Amygdala
- Part of hypothalamus
- Some cortical regions (e.g., insular region)

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The hypothalamus functions in both diencephalon and limbic system due to its role in autonomic/endocrine function and behavior/sexual function modulation.

## 3.7. INFRATENTORIUM AND BRAINSTEM

**The infratentorium encompasses the brainstem and cerebellum.**

**Brainstem components:**
- **Midbrain**
- **Pons** 
- **Medulla**

**Brainstem functions:**
- Consciousness via reticular activating system
- Autonomic functions (respiratory and cardiovascular control)
- Reflexes (cough/gag, pupillary reflexes)
- Contains nuclei for cranial nerves III-XII

**Cerebellum:**
- Located in posterior fossa
- Among most rudimentary brain parts
- **Functions:**
  - Processing proprioceptive input
  - Establishing axial posture and gait

**Bottom Line:** The brain is anatomically and functionally divided into supratentorium and infratentorium, with specific regions responsible for distinct neurological functions critical for anesthetic planning.

# 4. CEREBROVASCULAR ANATOMY

## 4.1. ARTERIAL BLOOD SUPPLY

**🔑 KEY POINT 1:** The brain receives approximately 70% of its blood supply from two internal carotid arteries anteriorly and 30% from two vertebral arteries posteriorly forming the basilar artery, that subsequently converge to form the circle of Willis, an anastomotic ring at the base of the skull.

**Anterior circulation:**
- **Common carotid artery:** Originates from aortic arch
- Divides at thyroid cartilage level into:
  - Internal carotid artery
  - External carotid artery
- **Internal carotid artery pathway:**
  - Traverses skull base through foramen lacerum
  - Travels through cavernous sinus into carotid groove
  - Divides into: posterior communicating artery (PCOM), anterior cerebral artery (ACA), middle cerebral artery (MCA)

**Posterior circulation:**
- **Bilateral vertebral arteries:** Originate from subclavian arteries
- Converge to form basilar artery at pontomedullary junction
- **Basilar artery:** At midbrain level
  - Divides into posterior cerebral arteries (PCAs)
  - Anastomoses with PCOMs completing circle of Willis

## 4.2. CIRCLE OF WILLIS

![The circle of Willis showing anterior and posterior blood supply](https://example.com/circle-of-willis.jpg)

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Less than 50% of people demonstrate a "complete" circle of Willis, which has important implications for collateral circulation during vascular procedures.

## 4.3. VENOUS SYSTEM

**The venous system consists of deep and superficial veins that drain into dural venous sinuses.**

![Gross anatomy of venous drainage system showing major venous sinuses](https://example.com/venous-system.jpg)

**Characteristics:**
- **Dural venous sinuses:** Valveless endothelialized channels
- Located between dura mater and skull periosteum
- All sinuses eventually drain into sigmoid sinus → internal jugular veins

**Bottom Line:** The brain's dual arterial supply (anterior 70%, posterior 30%) converges at the circle of Willis, with venous drainage through valveless dural sinuses into internal jugular veins.

# 5. CEREBROSPINAL FLUID SYSTEM

**The entire CNS is insulated from bony encasement by cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).**

**CSF characteristics:**
- **Production:** Primarily by choroid plexus of lateral and third ventricles
- **Volume:** Approximately 150 mL in adults
- **Production rate:** 15-20 mL/h

**CSF flow pathway:**
1. **Origin:** Lateral and third ventricles
2. **Aqueduct of Sylvius** → fourth ventricle
3. **Exit fourth ventricle via:**
   - Foramen of Magendie
   - Lateral foramina of Luschka
4. **Subarachnoid space** in cranium via interconnected cisterns at brain base
5. **Reabsorption:** Into dural venous sinuses (primarily superior sagittal sinus) via arachnoid villi and granulations
6. **Spinal component:** Some CSF traverses foramen magnum entering spinal subarachnoid space

![CSF compartments and flow patterns within and around the brain](https://example.com/csf-flow.jpg)

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** CSF movement is important for acute and chronic compensation in elevated ICP states through redistribution between cranial and spinal compartments.

**Bottom Line:** CSF is produced at 15-20 mL/h, flows through a specific pathway, and provides crucial volume compensation mechanisms during elevated ICP states.

# 6. SPINAL CORD ANATOMY

## 6.1. SPINAL COLUMN STRUCTURE

**The spinal column is composed of 33 vertebrae:**
- **7 cervical vertebrae**
- **12 thoracic vertebrae**
- **5 lumbar vertebrae**
- **9 fused sacral and coccygeal vertebrae**

**Nerve roots exit through corresponding intervertebral foramina.**

**Spinal ligamentous structures:**
- **Anterior longitudinal ligament (ALL):** Runs along anterior surface of vertebral bodies
- **Posterior longitudinal ligament (PLL):** Runs along posterior surface of vertebral bodies within spinal canal
- **Posterior ligamentous complex:**
  - Ligamentum flavum: Connects adjacent laminae
  - Interspinous ligaments: Connect adjacent spinous processes
  - Supraspinous ligaments: Run along tips of spinous processes

**Facet joints:** Paired synovial joints between adjacent vertebrae providing stability and guiding spinal movement

**Common sites of spinal injury:**
- **Transitional junctions** are particularly vulnerable to injury:
  - Craniocervical junction
  - Cervicothoracic junction
  - Thoracolumbar junction
  - Lumbosacral junction

## 6.2. SPINAL CORD ANATOMY AND TRACTS

**The spinal cord consists of:**
- **Central gray matter**
- **Dorsal columns:** Tracts for proprioception and light touch
- **Lateral spinothalamic tracts:** Pain and temperature
- **Outer white matter:** Lateral corticospinal tracts
- **Lateral gray columns:** Cell bodies of preganglionic neurons for sympathetic chain (T1-L2 or L3)

![Major ascending and descending spinal cord tracts (cross-section)](https://example.com/spinal-tracts.jpg)

**Spinal canal anatomy:**
- **Narrowest region:** Mid-thoracic spinal canal
- **Clinical significance:** Unstable injuries in mid-thoracic region can easily impact cord, leading to higher chance of complete cord injury

**Spinal cord termination:**
- **Adults:** L1 or L2 level
- **Neonates:** L3 or L4 level (descends with development)
- **Ending structures:** Conus medullaris terminus and filum terminale

## 6.3. SPINAL VASCULAR SUPPLY

**🔑 KEY POINT 2:** The spinal cord receives its blood supply from one anterior spinal artery and two posterior spinal arteries. The anterior spinal artery originates from 6 to 8 major radicular arteries derived from the aorta, with the largest one being the artery of Adamkiewicz (usually occurring at T11 or T12 and generally supplying T8 to the conus medullaris terminus).

**Radicular arteries:**
- Multiple segmental arteries arise from the aorta
- Reinforce the anterior and posterior spinal arteries
- Variable number and location between individuals

**Anterior spinal artery:**
- Supplies anterior two-thirds of spinal cord
- **Artery of Adamkiewicz:** Largest and most clinically significant radicular contribution
  - Usually at T11 or T12 level
  - Supplies T8 to conus medullaris terminus

**Posterior spinal arteries:**
- Originate from posterior cerebral circulation
- Supply dorsal horns and white matter (posterior third of spinal cord)

![Spinal cord blood supply showing cervical spine served by posterior circulation](https://example.com/spinal-blood-supply.jpg)

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The artery of Adamkiewicz is critical for spinal cord perfusion and is at risk during thoracoabdominal aortic procedures.

**Bottom Line:** The spinal cord has a dual blood supply with the anterior spinal artery (via artery of Adamkiewicz) supplying the anterior two-thirds and posterior arteries supplying the posterior third.

# 8. BRAIN METABOLIC REQUIREMENTS

**Although the adult brain accounts for only 2% of total body weight, it is responsible for 20% of total body oxygen consumption and 25% of total body glucose consumption.**

**Normal brain metabolism:**
- **Cerebral metabolic rate of oxygen consumption (CMRO2):** 3-3.8 mL/100 g/min
- **Brain glucose consumption:** ~5 mg/100 g/min  
- **Normal cerebral blood flow (CBF):** 50 mL/100 g/min or 750 mL/min
- **Cardiac output requirement:** ~15% to meet high metabolic demands

⚠️ **Critical Point:** The brain depends on continuous oxygen and glucose supply, with irreversible injury potentially occurring after only 4-5 minutes of global ischemia.

**Bottom Line:** The brain's disproportionate metabolic demands (20% O2, 25% glucose consumption) relative to its size (2% body weight) necessitate continuous substrate delivery.

# 9. CEREBRAL BLOOD FLOW REGULATION

## 9.1. FLOW-METABOLISM COUPLING

**🔑 KEY POINT 3:** CBF is regulated by "flow-metabolism coupling," whereby increases in regional neuronal electrical activity require corresponding increases in regional blood flow. Such coupling occurs on the order of seconds, with very little variation in the amount of oxygen extraction by the brain tissue (i.e., CBF matches CMRO2 very quickly and efficiently in the healthy brain).

**Characteristics of flow-metabolism coupling:**
- **Response time:** Seconds
- **Efficiency:** CBF matches CMRO2 very quickly in healthy brain
- **Oxygen extraction:** Minimal variation in extraction ratio
- **Regional specificity:** Increases in regional neuronal activity → corresponding regional CBF increases

## 9.2. CEREBRAL AUTOREGULATION

**🔑 KEY POINT 4:** Moderate changes in mean arterial pressure (MAP) (or cerebral perfusion pressure) will yield a consistent CBF of 50 mL/100 g/min, due to the normal brain's ability to autoregulate its blood flow. Cerebral autoregulation of blood flow is thought to remain intact between a MAP of approximately 60 and 160 mmHg and functions by altering cerebrovascular resistance (CVR) on the order of 5 to 60 seconds. The alteration in CVR is accomplished in both a rapid phase ("dynamic autoregulation") and a slow phase ("static autoregulation").

**Key autoregulation concepts:**
- **Cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP):** MAP - ICP (or CVP if higher)
- **CBF formula:** CBF = CPP/CVR
- **Autoregulation range:** MAP 60-160 mmHg (traditional)
- **Lower limit of autoregulation (LLA):** May be as high as 80 mmHg MAP in some healthy individuals
- **Time course:** CVR changes occur within 5-60 seconds

**Phases of autoregulation:**
- **Dynamic autoregulation:** Rapid phase responding to pulsatility changes (systolic BP)
- **Static autoregulation:** Slow phase accommodating MAP changes over longer intervals

![Autoregulation curves showing CBF vs MAP, PaCO2, and PaO2 relationships](https://example.com/autoregulation-curves.jpg)

**Pathological states:**
- **Below LLA:** Maximal cerebral vasodilation → cerebral ischemia risk
- **Above upper limit:** Maximal vasoconstriction → BBB disruption, edema, hemorrhage risk
- **Chronic hypertension:** Autoregulatory curve shifted rightward
- **Anesthetics:** Dose-dependent decrease in autoregulation extent

**Mechanisms of CVR regulation:**
- **Myogenic tone:** Response to transmural pressure changes in small arteries/arterioles
- **Neurogenic forces:** Neuroglial cell neurotransmitter secretion affecting vessel diameter
- **Metabolic responses:** Local microenvironmental changes altering vasomotor response
- **Endothelial responses:** Paracrine signals impacting cerebrovascular tone

## 9.3. CARBON DIOXIDE EFFECTS

**🔑 KEY POINT 5:** CBF is linearly associated with arterial carbon dioxide tension (PaCO2) between 20 and 80 mmHg. Hyperand hypoventilation, both patient-determined and iatrogenic, play critical roles in decreasing or increasing CBF, respectively. A change in PaCO2 of 1 mmHg roughly correlates to a similar change in CBF of 1 to 2 mL/100 g/min. Below the lower limit of this linear effect (i.e., with PaCO2 below 20 mmHg), maximal cerebral vasoconstriction leads to tissue hypoxia and a reflex vasodilation.

**PaCO2-CBF relationship:**
- **Linear range:** 20-80 mmHg PaCO2
- **Quantitative relationship:** 1 mmHg PaCO2 change ≈ 1-2 mL/100 g/min CBF change
- **Below 20 mmHg PaCO2:** Maximal vasoconstriction → tissue hypoxia → reflex vasodilation

**Clinical implications:**
- **Hyperventilation mechanism:** Spontaneous hyperventilation develops in response to acutely elevated ICP
- **Beneficial but potentially detrimental:** Low regional CBF and ischemia can occur
- **Time limitation:** Effect sustainable for only ~6 hours as CSF pH renormalizes

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Hyperventilation is a double-edged sword - beneficial for acute ICP control but potentially harmful due to cerebral ischemia from excessive vasoconstriction.

## 9.4. OXYGEN EFFECTS

**Oxygen tension (PaO2) has minimal effect on CBF unless marked hypoxemia (PaO2 <50 mmHg) occurs, below which CBF increases dramatically.**

**PaO2-CBF relationship:**
- **Above 50 mmHg:** Minimal CBF effect
- **Below 50 mmHg:** Dramatic CBF increase
- **Above 350 mmHg:** Slight cerebral vasoconstriction (possible oxygen toxicity protection mechanism)

## 9.5. TEMPERATURE EFFECTS

**Temperature is an important determinant of CBF:**
- **6-7% decrease in CBF per 1°C decrease in core temperature**
- This relationship is important for understanding hypothermia effects on cerebral metabolism

**Bottom Line:** Cerebral blood flow is tightly regulated through autoregulation (MAP 60-160 mmHg), closely coupled to CO2 (linear 20-80 mmHg), minimally affected by O2 unless <50 mmHg, and decreases 6-7% per 1°C temperature drop.

# 10. REGIONAL BLOOD FLOW CONTROL

**Regional CBF is more complex than global CBF and is governed by both humoral and neurogenic factors.**

**Vasoconstricting factors:**
- α1-adrenergic agonists
- Ionic calcium
- Endothelin
- Thromboxane A2

**Vasodilating factors:**  
- β2-adrenergic agonists
- Nitric oxide
- Adenosine
- Prostaglandins

**Neurogenic influences on local CBF regulation:**
- Acetylcholine release
- Dopamine
- Serotonin  
- Substance P

**Bottom Line:** Regional cerebral blood flow is controlled by complex interactions of humoral mediators and neurogenic factors that can cause vasoconstriction or vasodilation.

# 11. ANESTHETIC EFFECTS ON CEREBRAL PHYSIOLOGY

## 11.1. INTRAVENOUS AGENTS

**🔑 KEY POINT 6:** Intravenous drugs, such as propofol, etomidate, benzodiazepines, and barbiturates, decrease CBF by virtue of a drug-induced decrease in CMRO2 and subsequent flow-metabolism coupling. Autoregulation and PaCO2 responsiveness remain intact with these agents.

**IV agent effects on CBF:**
- **Mechanism:** Drug-induced ↓CMRO2 → flow-metabolism coupling → ↓CBF
- **Preserved functions:**
  - Autoregulation remains intact
  - PaCO2 responsiveness maintained

**Specific agents:**
- **Propofol, etomidate, benzodiazepines, barbiturates:** ↓CBF via ↓CMRO2
- **Opioids:** Very little effect on CMRO2, CBF, autoregulation, or PaCO2 responsiveness
- **Ketamine:** Unique - increases both CBF and CMRO2, little effect on autoregulation or PaCO2 responsiveness

## 11.2. VOLATILE ANESTHETICS

**🔑 KEY POINT 7:** Potent volatile anesthetics, such as isoflurane, sevoflurane, and desflurane, are direct cerebral vasodilators. However, this direct vasodilation is offset by a drug-induced decrease in CMRO2, and via flow-metabolism coupling, an attenuation of the direct effect on CBF. This leads to minimal, if any, increase in CBF at lower doses. However, at high doses, where maximal suppression of CMRO2 has occurred, are direct vasodilatory effects observed leading to a dose-dependent increase in CBF. Furthermore, autoregulation is inhibited with potent volatile anesthetic drugs in a dose-dependent fashion, although the cerebral vasculature remains responsive to changes in PaCO2.

**Volatile anesthetic effects:**
- **Dual mechanism:**
  1. Direct cerebral vasodilation
  2. Drug-induced ↓CMRO2 with flow-metabolism coupling

**Dose-dependent effects:**
- **Lower doses:** Minimal CBF increase (offsetting mechanisms balanced)
- **High doses:** Maximal CMRO2 suppression achieved → direct vasodilatory effects predominate → dose-dependent CBF increase

**Functional changes:**
- **Autoregulation:** Dose-dependently inhibited
- **PaCO2 responsiveness:** Remains intact

**Nitrous oxide:**
- Direct cerebral vasodilator
- Minimal effect on CMRO2
- Variable autoregulation effects (preserved with propofol, impaired with sevoflurane)

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** At low doses (<0.5 MAC), volatile anesthetics have minimal CBF effects due to balanced vasodilation and metabolism coupling. At higher doses, direct vasodilation predominates.

**Bottom Line:** IV agents decrease CBF by reducing metabolism while preserving autoregulation; volatile anesthetics have dose-dependent effects with direct vasodilation offset by reduced metabolism at low doses but predominant vasodilation at high doses.

# 12. SPINAL CORD PHYSIOLOGY

**Spinal cord physiology is very similar to brain physiology:**
- **Autoregulation maintained**
- **Spinal cord perfusion pressure (SCPP) = MAP - SSSP (spinal subarachnoid space pressure)**

⚠️ **Knowledge Gap:** The specific effects of different anesthetic drugs on spinal cord physiology are not as well characterized as their effects on the brain.

**Bottom Line:** Spinal cord physiology parallels brain physiology with maintained autoregulation, but specific anesthetic drug effects are less well characterized.

# 14. INTRACRANIAL PRESSURE

## 14.1. MONRO-KELLIE DOCTRINE

**🔑 KEY POINT 8:** ICP is the pressure within the intracranial cavity, which is a closed vault containing the brain parenchyma (1,400 mL), CSF (150 mL), and the cerebral blood volume (CBV) (150 mL). The Monro–Kellie doctrine states that "an increase in the volume of one intracranial compartment will lead to a rise in ICP unless it is matched by an equal reduction in the volume of another compartment." Since the brain parenchyma is relatively incompressible, CSF and CBV play an integral role in accommodating increases in ICP.

**Intracranial compartments:**
- **Brain parenchyma:** 1,400 mL (relatively incompressible)
- **CSF:** 150 mL (compressible/redistributable)
- **Cerebral blood volume:** 150 mL (compressible)

**Compensatory mechanisms:**
- **CSF:** Egresses from cranial cavity into spinal subarachnoid space
- **CBV:** Decreased by:
  - Reflex arterial vasoconstriction
  - Increased venous efflux from brain and venous sinuses

⚠️ **Critical Limitation:** Since CSF and CBV compartments are relatively low in volume, their ability to compensate for large ICP increases is greatly limited.

## 14.2. INTRACRANIAL ELASTANCE CURVE

**When compensatory effects are exhausted, small volume increases can lead to dramatic pressure increases.**

![Intracranial elastance curve showing three phases of ICP-volume relationship](https://example.com/icp-curve.jpg)

**Curve characteristics:**
- **"Flat part":** Low intracranial elastance, easy compensation via CSF and CBV buffering
- **"Elbow":** Compensation exhausted, elastance increases
- **Steep portion:** Small volume increases → rapid ICP rises

**Clinical formula:** Cerebral elastance (E = dP/dV) can be estimated with invasive monitoring devices

## 14.3. NORMAL VALUES AND CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE

**Normal ICP:** 7-15 mmHg  
**Pathological threshold:** Poor neurologic outcome associated with prolonged ICP >20-25 mmHg

**ICP fluctuations occur with:**
- Valsalva maneuver
- Patient position changes
- Various activities

**Meticulous care required when elevated ICP suspected:**
- Avoid hypoventilation
- Maintain adequate CPP
- Implement volume reduction techniques (mannitol, CSF diversion, cerebral vasoconstricting anesthetics)
- Consider decompressive craniectomy

**Bottom Line:** The Monro-Kellie doctrine governs ICP dynamics in the closed cranial vault, with limited compensatory capacity leading to precipitous pressure rises when compensation fails.

# 15. CEREBRAL EDEMA TYPES

**The most common contributing factor to increased ICP is cerebral edema, classified into three major types:**

## 15.1. CYTOTOXIC EDEMA

**Characterized by increased intracellular water.**
- **Mechanism:** Failure of membrane ionic pumps
- **Common cause:** Cerebral ischemia
- **Process:** Ion accumulation → water accumulation within cells
- **Treatment response:** Not responsive to dexamethasone

## 15.2. VASOGENIC EDEMA  

**Occurs when there is loss of blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity.**
- **Mechanism:** BBB breakdown → extracellular water accumulation
- **Common causes:** 
  - Brain tumors
  - Abscesses  
  - Contusions
- **Treatment:** **Dexamethasone is effective** - upregulates proteins responsible for tight junction integrity between endothelial cells

## 15.3. INTERSTITIAL EDEMA

**Characterized by increased extracellular fluid with intact BBB.**
- **Mechanism:** Osmotic/pressure gradient-driven fluid accumulation
- **Common causes:**
  - Hydrocephalus (CSF permeation into interstitial space)
  - Acute hyponatremia (osmotic difference between brain and intravascular space)

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** In many circumstances, cerebral edema may be due to a combination of cytotoxic, vasogenic, and interstitial edema.

**Bottom Line:** Three distinct edema types (cytotoxic, vasogenic, interstitial) have different mechanisms and treatment responses, with dexamethasone effective only for vasogenic edema.

# 16. ADDITIONAL CAUSES OF INTRACRANIAL HYPERTENSION

**Beyond cerebral edema, multiple factors can contribute to intracranial hypertension:**

## 16.1. INCREASED CEREBRAL BLOOD VOLUME

**Increased arterial inflow causes:**
- Cerebral arterial vasodilation from:
  - Vasodilatory drugs
  - Hypercapnia
  - Severe hypoxemia
  - Acidosis

**Decreased venous efflux causes:**
- Jugular venous obstruction
- Elevated airway pressures

## 16.2. CSF ABNORMALITIES

**Inadequate CSF absorption:**
- Diminished absorption at arachnoid villi (subarachnoid hemorrhage)
- Obstructive hydrocephalus (tumor)

## 16.3. MASS EFFECT

**Direct space-occupying lesions:**
- Tumors
- Hematomas
- Abscesses

**Bottom Line:** Multiple pathophysiological mechanisms beyond edema can cause intracranial hypertension, requiring comprehensive evaluation and targeted treatment approaches.

# 17. CLINICAL MANIFESTATIONS

## 17.1. SYMPTOMS OF INTRACRANIAL HYPERTENSION

**Early symptoms:**
- Headache
- Nausea
- Vomiting
- Papilledema

**Physical examination findings:**
- Altered level of consciousness
- Confusion
- Loss of strength or sensation
- Cranial nerve dysfunction

## 17.2. CUSHING TRIAD

**As ICP continues to increase, Cushing triad may appear:**
- **Hypertension**
- **Bradycardia**  
- **Irregular respiration**

## 17.3. SIGNS OF BRAIN HERNIATION

⚠️ **Life-threatening emergency:** Patients risk brain herniation and death with severe intracranial hypertension.

**Herniation signs (depend on structures involved):**
- Pupillary dilation
- Oculomotor weakness
- Absent pupillary light reflex
- Cardiorespiratory arrest

## 17.4. RADIOLOGICAL MANIFESTATIONS

**CT findings of elevated ICP:**
- Effacement of sulci
- Compression of ventricles
- Midline shift
- Ventriculomegaly (depending on etiology)

**Bottom Line:** Clinical recognition progresses from early symptoms (headache, nausea) to Cushing triad to herniation signs, with radiological confirmation via CT imaging.

# 18. SPINAL CORD INJURY PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

**Acute spinal cord injury is distinctly different from chronic spinal cord injury.**

## 18.1. ACUTE VS CHRONIC PHASES

**Acute phase characteristics:**
- Flaccid paralysis
- Hypotension
- Loss of sensory, motor, possibly autonomic function below injury level

**Chronic phase characteristics:**
- Spastic paralysis
- Pain
- Possible risk for autonomic hyperreflexia

### 18.1.1. Neurogenic Shock vs Spinal Shock

**These are two distinct phenomena that occur in acute spinal cord injury:**

**Neurogenic shock:**
- **Definition:** Interruption of autonomic pathways in injuries above T6 level (including cardiac sympathetics T1-T4)
- **Clinical features:**
  - Hypotension (loss of vasomotor tone)
  - Peripheral vasodilation
  - Bradycardia (unopposed vagal activity)
  - Hypothermia (impaired thermoregulation)
- **Mechanism:** Loss of sympathetic outflow below injury level with intact vagal activity
- **Duration:** Variable, typically hours to weeks

**Spinal shock:**
- **Definition:** Loss of all reflexes below the level of spinal cord injury
- **Onset:** Usually develops 4-6 weeks post-injury (may present as early as 4 days)
- **Duration:** Lasts 24 hours to several weeks
- **Phases of recovery:**
  - **Days 0-1:** Flaccid areflexia
  - **Days 1-3:** Gradual return of reflexes
  - **Days 4+:** Development of spasticity and autonomic hyperreflexia
- **Clinical significance:** Recovery from spinal shock does not indicate neurologic recovery

### 18.1.2. Primary vs Secondary Injury

**Primary injury:**
- **Mechanism:** Direct mechanical trauma to spinal cord
- **Types of trauma:**
  - Direct cord compression
  - Cord hemorrhage
  - Traction injury
  - Axial loading forces
- **Causes:**
  - Subluxation of vertebral elements
  - Hyperextension injuries
  - Retropulsion of bone or disc fragments
  - Penetrating injuries (stab wounds, gunshot injuries)

**Secondary injury:**
- **Onset:** Begins within minutes after primary injury
- **Mechanism:** Local and spreading changes at the cellular level
- **Pathophysiological cascade:**
  - Spinal cord hemorrhage
  - Loss of autoregulation
  - Neurogenic shock
  - Worsened edema and ischemia
- **Clinical manifestation:** Ascending level of spinal cord injury → clinical deterioration
- **Therapeutic target:** Need for anti-inflammatory interventions and prevention strategies

### 18.1.3. Temporal Phases of Spinal Cord Injury

**Immediate phase (seconds to minutes):**
- **Initial sympathetic discharge**
- Massive catecholamine release
- **Clinical effects:**
  - Hypertension
  - Tachycardia

**Acute phase (hours to weeks):**
- **Central nervous system:**
  - Spinal shock
  - Ongoing secondary cord injury
- **Cardiovascular:**
  - Neurogenic shock (injuries above T4)
- **Respiratory:**
  - Respiratory muscle weakness
  - Impaired cough and secretion clearance
- **Gastrointestinal:**
  - Delayed gastric emptying
  - Stress ulceration
- **Renal:**
  - Acute retention of urine
  - Obstructive nephropathy → acute kidney injury
- **Infectious:**
  - Immobility-related infections
  - Pneumonia (weak cough mechanism)
- **Thrombotic:**
  - Venous thromboembolism
- **Thermoregulation:**
  - Impaired temperature control

**Subacute and chronic phases (later):**
- Development of long-term complications
- Transition to chronic spinal cord injury state
- Emergence of autonomic dysreflexia (injuries above T6)

## 18.2. EMERGENCY CONSIDERATIONS

⚠️ **Surgical Emergency:** Acute spinal cord compression (trauma or tumor) - time to decompression correlates with functional outcome in some populations.

**Controversial treatments:**
- **Steroids:** Role in preventing secondary injury much more controversial

## 18.3. INJURY LEVEL IMPLICATIONS

**Injury level determination:**
- **Definition:** Level = lowest level of spinal cord with normal sensation and motor function on both sides
- **ASIA score timing:**
  - ASIA score at 72 hours post-injury is the most sensitive predictor of long-term prognosis
  - Requires awake, cooperative patient for accurate assessment
  - May be limited in application during severe trauma, sedation, or intubation

**Cervical injuries:**
- Most devastating from neurologic perspective
- More physiologic perturbations than lower injuries:
  - Diaphragmatic paralysis
  - Cardiac disturbances
  - Death risk
- **Airway management:** Requires extremely careful approach

## 18.4. COMPLETE VS INCOMPLETE INJURIES

**Complete spinal cord injury:**
- **Definition:** Complete loss of motor and sensory function in the anal and perianal region (S4, S5 segments)
- **Implications:**
  - Total loss of function below injury level
  - Poor prognosis for recovery
  - Higher risk of complications

**Incomplete spinal cord injury:**
- **Definition:** Some preserved neurological function below the level of injury
- **Better prognosis:** Greater potential for recovery compared to complete injuries
- **Common incomplete injury syndromes:**
  - **Anterior spinal artery syndrome:** Loss of motor function and pain/temperature sensation with preserved proprioception and light touch
  - **Brown-Séquard syndrome:** Hemisection of cord resulting in ipsilateral motor weakness and loss of proprioception, with contralateral loss of pain and temperature sensation
  - **Central cord syndrome:** Motor weakness greater in upper extremities than lower extremities, typically from hyperextension injuries
  - **Posterior cord syndrome:** Loss of proprioception and vibratory sense with preserved motor function and pain/temperature sensation
  - **Cauda equina syndrome:** Injury to lumbosacral nerve roots below L1/L2
- **ASIA Impairment Scale (AIS):** Standardized grading system for incomplete injuries

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The presence of any sacral sparing (sensation or voluntary anal contraction) indicates an incomplete injury with better potential for recovery.

**Bottom Line:** Acute spinal cord injury is a surgical emergency with injury level determining physiologic impact, requiring immediate decompression and careful airway management for cervical injuries. Complete vs incomplete injury classification guides prognosis and treatment planning.

# 20. CENTRAL NERVOUS SYSTEM FUNCTION MONITORING

## 20.1. BASIC PRINCIPLES

**The most important monitor of CNS function is the neurologic examination of an awake and responsive patient.**

**When general anesthesia required:** Other monitoring modalities necessary for assessing neurologic function integrity

**Electrophysiologic monitoring rationale:**
- Can detect reversible changes in neurologic function during general anesthesia
- Allows surgical or physiologic plan changes to avert permanent neurologic injury
- ⚠️ **Evidence limitation:** Paucity of definitive randomized controlled trial data proving prevention of neurologic injury and improved outcomes

## 20.2. EVOKED POTENTIALS OVERVIEW

**🔑 KEY POINT 9:** The most commonly used modalities of intraoperative neurophysiologic monitoring are somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEPs), motor evoked potentials (MEPs), and electromyography (EMG), with brainstem auditory evoked potentials (BAEPs) and visual evoked potentials (VEPs) being less commonly used.

![Representative BAEP, SSEP, and MEP tracings showing characteristic waveform patterns](https://example.com/evoked-potentials.jpg)

**Waveform parameters:**
- **Amplitude:** Measured in microvolts (SSEPs, BAEPs) or microvolts/millivolts (MEPs)
  - Signal voltage of peak relative to baseline or adjacent peak
- **Latency:** Usually measured in milliseconds
  - Delay in peak signal following stimulation
  - Reflects transit time along neural pathway

**Bottom Line:** Evoked potentials monitoring uses amplitude and latency changes to detect reversible neurologic function changes during surgery, though definitive outcome benefit data is limited.

# 21. SPECIFIC EVOKED POTENTIAL MODALITIES

## 21.1. SOMATOSENSORY EVOKED POTENTIALS (SSEPS)

**Technical aspects:**
- **Stimulation:** Cyclical, repetitive from peripheral nerves (median, ulnar, posterior tibial)
- **Recording sites:** Subcortex (upper cervical spine, inion) and cortex (scalp)
- **Pathway:** Predominantly posterior column/medial lemniscus pathway

**Monitoring capabilities:**
- Peripheral nerve integrity
- Dorsal columns of spinal cord
- Brainstem
- Subcortex
- Sensory cortex of brain

**Technical requirements:**
- Inherently small amplitude → prolonged stimulation time required
- Signal averaging needed
- Analog to digital conversion
- Signal filtration
- **Time requirement:** Minutes to obtain stable waveform

**Clinical applications:**
- **Spine surgery:** Especially when posterolateral sensory elements at ischemia risk from surgical distraction
- **Neurovascular brain surgery:** Ensuring sufficient somatosensory cortex perfusion during procedures (e.g., cerebral aneurysm clipping)

**Anatomical correlations:**
- **Lower extremity SSEPs:** Correlate with ACA-supplied cortex integrity
- **Upper extremity SSEPs:** Correlate with MCA distribution cortex

**Significant change definitions:**
- **Amplitude decrease:** 50%
- **Latency increase:** 10%
- **Experienced practitioner considerations:** Baseline drift and reproducibility

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Muscle relaxants beneficial for SSEP monitoring as they eliminate myogenic interference artifact.

## 21.2. MOTOR EVOKED POTENTIALS (MEPS)

**Technical aspects:**
- **Stimulation:** Direct cortical stimulation or indirect scalp stimulation
- **Recording:** Compound muscle action potentials (CMAPs) at muscular level
- **Methods:** Magnetic or electrical (electrical more common)

**Stimulation pathway:**
1. Electrical stimulation via scalp electrodes
2. Single pulse or train of pulses travels caudad
3. Depolarizes upper motor neurons in spinal cord
4. Summation in ventral horn
5. Signal travels to α-motor neurons via internuncial pathways
6. Descends to motor endplates → measurable muscle action potentials

**Advantages over SSEPs:**
- **No signal averaging required**
- **No filtration needed**
- **Rapid acquisition:** Seconds for response (single pulse or few pulses)

⚠️ **Critical Limitation:** Measurement severely inhibited by muscle relaxants

**Clinical applications:**
- **Spine surgery:** Especially when anterior elements at risk
- **Intracranial surgery:** When motor cortex or descending motor pathways at injury/ischemia risk

**Significant change criteria:**
- **Amplitude decrease:** 50% considered significant
- **Stimulation intensity:** Need to increase intensity to maintain reproducible signal
- **Latency:** Much less role than with SSEPs

## 21.3. ELECTROMYOGRAPHY (EMG)

**Monitoring capability:**
- Continual assessment of distinct peripheral or cranial nerves/nerve roots
- **Types:** Spontaneous neural electrical activity or stimulated EMG

**Sensitivity:**
- Mechanical injury
- Thermal injury
- **Not an ischemia monitor** (unlike SSEPs and MEPs)

**Technique:**
- Needle electrodes placed in muscles innervated by specific nerve root
- If nerve root disturbed → EMG activity recorded from that muscle

**Clinical applications:**
- **Spine surgery:** Muscles innervated by spinal nerves during procedures
- **Intracranial procedures:** Muscles innervated by cranial nerves (e.g., acoustic neuroma resection)
- **Surgeon identification:** Stimulated EMG to identify cranial nerves during surgery

**"Triggered EMG" (Pedicle Screw Testing):**
- Direct stimulation of screws placed within bony pedicle
- **Intact pedicle:** High current required for nerve root stimulation
- **Disrupted pedicle:** Much less current needed (screw-neural contact/near contact)

⚠️ **Muscle relaxant sensitivity:** Like MEPs, particularly sensitive to muscle relaxant effects

## 21.4. BRAINSTEM AUDITORY EVOKED POTENTIALS (BAEPS)

**Assessment pathway:**
- Auditory canal
- Tympanic membrane  
- Hair cells
- Spiral ganglion
- Vestibulocochlear nerve (cranial nerve VIII)
- Cochlear nuclei
- Superior olivary complex
- Lateral lemniscus
- Inferior colliculus
- Medial geniculate thalamic nuclei

**Technique:**
- Auditory stimuli (clicking sounds) placed in external auditory canal
- Scalp recording electrodes
- Thousands of signals averaged → typical six-wave waveform

**Injury patterns:**
- **Cranial nerve VIII injury:** Affects all waves after wave I (decreased amplitude, prolonged latency)
- **Cerebellar retraction:** Prolongs latency between waves I and V

**Clinical applications:**
- Surgery at or near brainstem (microvascular decompression of CN V or VII)
- Acoustic neuroma resection

💡 **Robustness:** Extremely robust with little effect from any anesthetic regimen

## 21.5. VISUAL EVOKED POTENTIALS (VEPS)

**Assessment pathway:**
- Eye
- Optic nerve
- Optic chiasm  
- Visual cortex (occipital lobe)

**Technique:**
- Bright stimulus applied via special goggles or contact lenses
- Scalp electrode recording

**Clinical applications:**
- Surgery at or near optic chiasm
- Occipital cortex procedures

⚠️ **Major limitations:** 
- Exquisitely sensitive to almost any anesthetic regimen
- Difficulty in signal acquisition and interpretation
- Very infrequently used (despite technologic advances improving successful intraoperative use)

**Bottom Line:** Each evoked potential modality monitors specific neural pathways with unique technical requirements and clinical applications, requiring careful anesthetic planning to optimize signal quality.

# 22. ANESTHETIC EFFECTS ON EVOKED POTENTIALS

## 22.1. SSEP-ANESTHETIC INTERACTIONS

**Inhibitory agents (greatest effect):**
- **Potent volatile anesthetics:** Decrease amplitude, increase latency in nearly linear dose-dependent fashion
- **Nitrous oxide:** Greater effect on amplitude than latency
- **Clinical threshold:** Robust signals usually obtainable up to 0.5 MAC in neurologically intact patients

**Minimal effect agents:**
- **Propofol:** Very limited effect unless very high doses
- **Opioids:** Increase latency and decrease amplitude (especially bolus administration), but minimal clinically significant impact

**Enhancing agents:**
- **Etomidate and ketamine:** Can actually increase cortical amplitudes at clinical doses, used to enhance SSEP waveforms

**TIVA considerations:**
- Commonly performed with hypnotic (propofol) and opioid infusion
- Required in neurologically impaired patients (e.g., peripheral neuropathy)

**Regional effects:**
- Anesthetic effects much less prominent for subcortical, cervical, and peripheral signal acquisition
- These areas much more resistant to anesthetic inhibitory effects

## 22.2. MEP-ANESTHETIC INTERACTIONS

**Extreme sensitivity:** MEPs elicited from scalp are exquisitely sensitive to anesthetic effects

**Volatile anesthetic effects:**
- Greatly inhibitory to MEP acquisition
- **Clinical threshold:** 0.5 MAC can still be used in most cases
- **Above 0.5 MAC:** Nonlinear and greatly accelerated suppression of amplitudes

**Intravenous anesthetics:**
- Generally conducive to MEP acquisition except at very high doses
- **TIVA commonly employed** when MEPs monitored
- **Especially recommended:** When difficulty obtaining adequate baseline MEPs anticipated (patients with neuromuscular deficits)

**Enhancing agents:**
- **Ketamine and etomidate:** May improve amplitudes and lower electrical threshold required for response
- **Higher bolus doses:** Reported to decrease MEP amplitudes

**Muscle relaxant considerations:**
- Must be given very judiciously or avoided completely
- Risk of abolishing MEP response or prohibitively increasing variability

## 22.3. EMG-ANESTHETIC INTERACTIONS

**Muscle relaxant effects:**
- Can impair or (with deep blockade) abolish EMG signals
- **Recommendation:** Avoid muscle relaxation or reverse effects prior to pedicle screw testing or cranial nerve identification

**Anesthetic effects:**
- **Inhaled anesthetics:** Very little effect on spontaneous or triggered EMG acquisition
- **Intravenous anesthetics:** Very little effect on EMG acquisition

## 22.4. BAEP-ANESTHETIC INTERACTIONS

**Robustness:** Quite robust regardless of anesthetic regimen  
**Minimal effects:** Small latency increases with deep inhalational or intravenous anesthesia  
**Environmental factors:** Cold irrigation fluids at brainstem cause latency increases

## 22.5. VEP-ANESTHETIC INTERACTIONS

**Maximum sensitivity:** Most sensitive neuromonitoring modality to anesthesia  
**Anesthetic preferences:**
- **Inhalational-based anesthetics:** More inhibitory to VEPs than TIVA
- **TIVA techniques:** Generally less inhibitory

**Proposed technique:**
- Opioid-based TIVA
- Muscle relaxants
- BIS monitoring
- Other techniques may also be used

**Bottom Line:** Anesthetic choice significantly impacts evoked potential monitoring, with TIVA generally preferred for MEPs and VEPs, while SSEPs and BAEPs are more robust to anesthetic effects.

# 23. CEREBRAL PERFUSION MONITORING

## 23.1. TRANSCRANIAL DOPPLER ULTRASONOGRAPHY (TCD)

**Technology and principles:**
- Ultrasound probe placed over thinner cranial "windows" (e.g., temporal bone above zygomatic arch)
- Measures blood flow velocities in major cerebral vessels (MCA, PCA, ACA, basilar artery)
- **Doppler Effect:** High-frequency sound wave reflects off red blood cells, returns at different frequency

**Doppler shift interpretation:**
- **Positive (frequency increase):** Blood moving toward probe
- **Negative (frequency decrease):** Blood moving away from probe
- Shift proportional to blood velocity

**Clinical interpretations:**
- **Higher than expected velocity:** May indicate stenosis, emboli, or vasospasm
- **Velocity vs. flow distinction:** TCD measures velocity (m/s), not actual flow (volume/time)

⚠️ **Critical distinction:** Decreased arterial diameter → increased velocity but decreased flow volume. Thoughtful interpretation required considering CBF vs. velocity relationship.

**Variables requiring control:**
- PaCO2
- Systemic blood pressure
- Angle of insonation
- Anesthetic drug doses affecting cerebral hemodynamics

**Additional parameters:**
- **Pulsatility index:** Reflects distal CVR
- **Index of autoregulation:** Quantifies static autoregulation status
- **Rate of restoration of flow velocity:** Quantifies dynamic autoregulation

## 23.2. LASER DOPPLER FLOWMETRY (LDF)

**Technology:**
- Small fiberoptic laser (0.5-1 mm diameter) implanted into brain
- Measures Doppler shift from passing red blood cells in microscopic vessels
- **Real-time measurement** of regional CBF
- Based on Doppler shift in light frequency (vs. ultrasound using sound waves)

**Validation:** Yields CBF measurements similar to Xenon (133Xe) washout technique (established gold standard)

**Clinical utility:**
- Detecting effects of physiologic changes (anemia, hyperventilation) on CBF
- Real-time regional CBF monitoring

**Bottom Line:** TCD measures blood flow velocity in major vessels requiring careful interpretation, while LDF provides real-time regional CBF measurement in brain tissue.

# 24. INTRACRANIAL PRESSURE MONITORING

## 24.1. INDICATIONS AND NORMAL VALUES

**Clinical utility:** Useful for patients with any cause of elevated ICP, most studied in traumatic brain injury (TBI) patients

**Monitoring thresholds:**
- **Normal ICP:** 7-15 mmHg
- **Initiation threshold:** Generally when ICP thought to be >20 mmHg or unable to assess clinically
- **Severe, life-threatening:** ICP >40 mmHg

**Information value:**
- ICP alone can be useful
- **Lundberg waves:** Provide valuable information about brain state over time

## 24.2. LUNDBERG WAVES

![Lundberg A, B, and C wave morphologies showing characteristic ICP patterns](https://example.com/lundberg-waves.jpg)

**"A waves" (plateau waves):**
- ICP increases to 50-100 mmHg, remains elevated for minutes
- Occur in severely exhausted compensatory mechanisms
- Due to intense vasodilation in response to decreased cerebral perfusion
- **Always pathologic**

**"B waves":**
- ICP elevations of 20-30 mmHg above baseline
- Occur once or twice per minute
- Reflect vascular tone changes when CPP at LLA

**"C waves":**
- Small oscillations 4-8 times per minute
- ICP in normal range
- Reflect systemic vasomotor tone changes
- Little pathologic significance

## 24.3. MONITORING DEVICES

**Invasive modalities (allow actual pressure measurement):**
- Associated risks but provide accurate measurements

**Noninvasive modalities:**
- Fewer risks but rely on surrogate variables → prone to inaccuracy
- Examples: CT scan, optic nerve sheath diameter

## 24.4. EXTERNAL VENTRICULAR DRAIN (EVD) - GOLD STANDARD

**Current gold standard of ICP monitoring**

**Technical setup:**
- Ventriculostomy with transducer connection
- Tubing placed in lateral ventricle
- Zeroed at external auditory meatus

**Advantages:**
- Pressure transduction
- **CSF drainage capability** for elevated ICP attenuation
- CSF sampling for diagnostic purposes
- Drug delivery capability (antibiotics, thrombolytics)

**Disadvantages and complications:**
- Difficulty in placement
- Fluid leaks, clots, air bubbles → inaccurate recording
- ⚠️ **Infection risk:** Major concern - CSF sepsis increases morbidity/mortality
- **Aseptic technique critical** during placement

⚠️ **Clinical vigilance:** Required when managing EVD patients due to potential risks - clinicians must be competent in setup, assessment, and maintenance.

## 24.5. ALTERNATIVE MONITORING SYSTEMS

**Intraparenchymal devices:**
- **Microtransducer system:** Attached to flexible wire
- **Fiberoptic system:** Pressure transducer at tip
- Both require hollow screw insertion through skull
- **Advantages:** High accuracy (second to EVDs), easier placement, fewer infectious/hemorrhagic risks
- **Disadvantages:** Cannot remove CSF or administer drugs

**Subarachnoid "bolts":**
- Rarely used anymore
- Small bolt threaded through burr hole, tip 1 mm beneath dura
- **Advantages:** CSF diversion capability, lower infectious/hemorrhagic risks
- **Major disadvantage:** Notoriously inaccurate

**Epidural sensor/transducer systems:**
- Lower infectious/hemorrhagic risks
- **Major limitation:** Unacceptably inaccurate (reflects only epidural space, not entire intracranial compartment)

**Bottom Line:** EVD remains the gold standard for ICP monitoring despite infection risks, while alternative systems offer different risk-benefit profiles with varying accuracy and capabilities.

# 25. CEREBRAL OXYGENATION AND METABOLISM MONITORS

## 25.1. JUGULAR BULB VENOUS OXIMETRY

**Technical setup:**
- Fiberoptic catheter placed retrograde into internal jugular vein
- Advanced cephalad beyond common facial vein into jugular bulb at skull base
- **Proper placement:** X-ray confirmation at mastoid process level

**Measurement capabilities:**
- **Primary:** Mixed cerebral/jugular bulb venous oxygen saturation (SjVO2)
- Indicates global brain oxygen consumption/extraction
- **Additional metabolites:** Lactate and glucose concentrations

**Measurement methods:**
- **Serial sampling:** Single time points (risk of extracranial blood contamination)
- **Continuous fiberoptic measurement:** More useful for ongoing monitoring

**Clinical interpretation:**
- **High SjVO2 (80-85%):** May reflect high oxygen delivery or low CMRO2
- **Low SjVO2:** Usually indicates low oxygen delivery (low CBF or hypoxemia) or high CMRO2
- **Critical threshold:** SjVO2 <55% commonly defines critical jugular bulb desaturation

**Clinical applications:**
- Cerebral ischemia indication in systemic hypotension or markedly elevated ICP
- **Hyperventilation guidance:** Goal of maintaining saturation >55% during vasoconstriction

## 25.2. BRAIN TISSUE OXYGEN MONITORS

**Technology:**
- Direct, invasive measurement of partial pressure of oxygen (PbtO2) in brain interstitium
- Clark-type electrode measuring 15-20 mm wide tissue region

**Normal values:** PbtO2 normally 25-48 mmHg

**Clinical significance:**
- **Values reflect balance** between oxygen supply and demand in electrode region
- **Influencing factors:** 
  - Fraction of inspired oxygen
  - Cardiac output
  - Hemoglobin concentration
  - Local oxygen extraction
  - PaO2
  - CBF

**Pathological threshold:** Values <20 mmHg in pathologic brain states considered significant, may portend secondary injury to healthy tissue

**Primary indications:** TBI and SAH (experience growing)

## 25.3. CEREBRAL MICRODIALYSIS

**Growing popularity in neurocritical care units**

**Technical setup:**
- Filamentous catheter placed into brain parenchyma
- Outer and inner tubes with semipermeable membrane at tip
- Pump perfuses inner catheter with crystalloid solution iso-osmolar with CSF

**Mechanism:**
- Metabolites in extracellular fluid driven by concentration gradient into outer tubing
- Collected into microvial for analysis

**Metabolite analysis:**
- **Adequate aerobic metabolism indicators:** Glucose, pyruvate, lactate concentrations
- **Ischemic stress indicators:** Glutamate and glycerol levels (neuronal stress and cell membrane degradation)

**Clinical considerations:**
- **Location dependency:** Findings depend on catheter tip location
- **Comparison requirement:** Values over time needed
- **Best measurement sites:** "At risk" brain regions rather than remote from injury

## 25.4. CEREBRAL OXIMETRY (NEAR-INFRARED SPECTROSCOPY)

**Technology and growing clinical prevalence:**
- Noninvasive, transcutaneous measurement
- Regional cerebral blood oxygenation (rSO2) over frontal cortices bilaterally

**Mechanism:**
- Light transmitted through skull from forehead probe
- Hemoglobin oxygen saturation determined by absorbance ratio of different wavelengths
- Allows determination of oxyhemoglobin and deoxyhemoglobin

**Key differences from pulse oximetry:**
- **Two photodetectors:** Allows subtraction of scalp and skull absorbance
- **Near-field vs. far-field photodetection:** Yields brain tissue oxygenation value
- **No pulsatile flow dependence:** Unlike pulse oximetry

**Clinical interpretation:**
- **Oxygenation percentage:** Maximum hemoglobin saturation percentage
- **Contribution:** Both arterial and venous blood
- **Significant decrease threshold:** ≥20% from baseline values (though definitive data lacking)

**Clinical utility:**
- **Sensitive detection:** Changes in oxygen delivery due to limited oxygen reserve in highly metabolic frontal cortex
- **Intervention guidance:** For low cerebral oximetry values:
  - Increase systemic blood pressure
  - Increase cardiac output
  - Increase supplemental oxygen
  - Increase PaCO2 to ameliorate cerebral vasoconstriction
  - Red blood cell transfusion to increase oxygen-carrying capacity

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Regional frontal cortex estimates provide sensitive method for detecting oxygen delivery changes in this highly metabolic area with limited oxygen reserves.

**Bottom Line:** Multiple cerebral oxygenation and metabolism monitors provide different perspectives on brain tissue health, from global (jugular bulb) to regional (tissue oxygen, microdialysis) to cortical (cerebral oximetry) assessment.

# 27. ISCHEMIA AND REPERFUSION

## 27.1. BRAIN VULNERABILITY TO ISCHEMIA

**The brain is especially susceptible to rapid ischemic injury due to:**
- High rate of oxygen and glucose consumption
- Inability to store substrate
- Inability to dispose of toxic metabolites quickly

**Ischemic injury mechanisms:**
- **Intracellular calcium (Ca2+) accumulation** under ischemic conditions
- Rapid neuronal damage
- **Compounding factor:** Lactic acid accumulation

## 27.2. TYPES OF ISCHEMIA

**Global ischemia:**
- **Causes:** Severe hypotension, severe anemia
- **Treatment approach:** Restore total cerebral perfusion and oxygen-carrying capacity
  - Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR)
  - Red blood cell transfusion

**Focal ischemia:**
- **Causes:** Regional insults (embolus, arterial disruption)
- **Anatomy:** Penumbra of salvageable tissue usually surrounds necrotic core
- **Treatment focus:** 
  - Restore perfusion to ischemic brain regions
  - Restore oxygen and substrate delivery to ischemic penumbra supplied by collateral circulation

💡 **Research Focus:** Much current cerebral protection research deals with "saving the penumbra" through practical methods like CPP augmentation and brain edema reduction.

## 27.3. REPERFUSION INJURY

**Reperfusion of previously ischemic brain tissue can actually worsen neurologic outcome.**

**Mechanisms:**
- **Free radical production** derived from oxygen
- **Inflammatory mediators**
- **Microvasculature injury exacerbation** upon flow restoration

**Clinical significance:** Treatment restoration may paradoxically worsen outcomes in some cases.

**Bottom Line:** Brain ischemia vulnerability stems from high metabolic demands and limited substrate storage, with both global and focal ischemia requiring different treatment approaches and reperfusion potentially causing additional injury.

# 28. HYPOTHERMIA

## 28.1. THEORETICAL BENEFITS

**Induced systemic hypothermia regarded as potentially beneficial intervention in certain neurologically injured subgroups, though definitive human efficacy studies lacking.**

**Theoretical protective mechanisms:**
- **Metabolic protection:** Lowers CMRO2 much more than anesthetics
- **Beyond metabolism:** Animal models show protection with even 1°C decrease, suggesting other mechanisms involved

**Comparison with anesthetics:**
- **Anesthetics:** Can cause isoelectric EEG, reducing brain metabolic activity up to 60%
- **Hypothermia:** Can reduce even homeostatic oxygen need required for basic neuronal survival (far exceeds anesthetic capability)

## 28.2. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS - GLOBAL ISCHEMIA

**Profound hypothermia applications:**
- **Cold-cardiopulmonary bypass:** 27°C in humans - shown to provide cerebral protection
- **Deep hypothermia with circulatory arrest:** 12-18°C - provides cerebral protection

**Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest:**
- **Earlier data:** Adults with return of spontaneous circulation cooled to 32-34°C for 12-24 hours showed possible neurologic improvement
- **Recent data:** Do not support robust benefit from hypothermia in this cohort

**Neonatal applications:**
- **Hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy:** Clinical improvement seen with mild hypothermia within 6 hours of delivery

## 28.3. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS - FOCAL ISCHEMIA

**Less promising results for focal ischemia**

**Intraoperative Hypothermia for Aneurysm Surgery Trial (IHAST):**
- **No improvement** in neurologic outcome
- **Higher rates** of infectious complications
- **Comparison:** Mild total-body hypothermia vs. normothermic management during cerebral aneurysm clipping

**Traumatic brain injury:**
- **Mixed results** with mild hypothermia

## 28.4. HYPERTHERMIA - DETRIMENTAL EFFECTS

⚠️ **Critical concept:** Cerebral hyperthermia is clearly detrimental in both humans and animals.

**Quantitative impact:**
- **Focal cerebral ischemic infarct size triples for each 1°C rise in core temperature** in animals
- **Clinical imperative:** Hyperthermia must be avoided in cerebral ischemia setting

**Bottom Line:** While hypothermia shows theoretical neuroprotective benefits and some clinical applications, definitive human studies are lacking for focal ischemia, but hyperthermia is clearly detrimental and must be avoided.

# 29. PHARMACOLOGIC THERAPY FOR CEREBRAL PROTECTION

## 29.1. GENERAL LIMITATIONS

**Promise exists for cerebral protection by both anesthetic and nonanesthetic drugs, but most data comes from animal stroke models, not definitive human studies.**

## 29.2. ANESTHETIC AGENTS

**Neuroprotective mechanisms:**
- **CMRO2 reduction** effects
- **Other molecular mechanisms**

**Volatile anesthetics may:**
- Reduce ischemia-induced glutamate release
- Increase availability of antiapoptotic mediators
- Activate adenosine triphosphate (ATP)-dependent potassium channels
- Reduce excitotoxic stressors
- Augment CBF

**Agents with beneficial effects in animal/preclinical studies:**
- **Barbiturates:** Proven benefit in focal ischemia in animal studies
- Nitrous oxide
- **Propofol**
- **Etomidate**
- **Ketamine**
- **Lidocaine**

**Ketamine considerations:**
- **Traditional concern:** Generally avoided in neurosurgical patients over ICP elevation concerns
- **Mechanically ventilated patients:** This concern appears unfounded
- **Recent focus:** Neuroprotective ability
- **Clinical use:** Remains rare pending more randomized controlled clinical data

## 29.3. NONANESTHETIC AGENTS

**Calcium-channel blockers:**
- **Mechanism:** Inhibit voltage-gated calcium channels effects
- **Clinical utility:** Do not seem clinically useful for neuroprotection

**Magnesium:**
- **Mechanism:** Antagonistic effect on voltage-gated and transmitter-activated channels
- **Human studies:** Disappointing results
- **Exception:** Antenatal magnesium may decrease cerebral palsy risk in premature births

**Agents under investigation:**
- **Free radical scavengers**
- **Erythropoietin:** Activates antiapoptotic pathways, decreases inflammation
- **Statins:** Upregulation of nitric oxide synthase, anti-inflammatory and antioxidative effects

## 29.4. CLINICAL REALITY

⚠️ **Important limitation:** Despite ongoing and often promising work, a single definitive neuroprotective agent in human clinical studies has never been identified.

**Bottom Line:** While multiple anesthetic and nonanesthetic agents show neuroprotective promise in animal studies, no single agent has proven definitively beneficial in human clinical studies.

# 30. GLUCOSE AND CEREBRAL ISCHEMIA

## 30.1. GLUCOSE AS ESSENTIAL SUBSTRATE

**Ischemia is rapidly detrimental to nervous system due to:**
- **Oxygen starvation**
- **Glucose as only substrate** for aerobic brain metabolism under normal conditions

**Glucose characteristics in nervous system:**
- **Not stored** in nervous system
- When reduced/absent due to limited cerebral circulation → ATP no longer produced at rate supplying neuronal energy requirements
- **Cellular injury quickly ensues**

## 30.2. GLUCOSE VS. OXYGEN CONSUMPTION

**Quantitative relationship:**
- **Cerebral glucose consumption (5 mg/100 g/min) parallels CMRO2**
- **Clinical significance:** Hypoxemia and hypoglycemia roughly equally detrimental to brain

## 30.3. ALTERNATIVE METABOLISM

**Lactate metabolism:**
- With cerebral ischemia and hypoglycemia, lactate metabolized to some extent
- **Efficiency:** Much less effective than glucose

## 30.4. HYPERGLYCEMIA CONCERNS

⚠️ **Detrimental effects:** Hyperglycemia (serum glucose >180 mg/dL) in cerebral ischemia setting worsens neurologic outcomes.

**Mechanism:**
- **Presumed cause:** Worsening cerebral acidosis in anaerobic setting
- **Process:** Glucose converted to lactic acid without oxygen

**Clinical implications:**
- Glucose management critical in cerebral ischemia
- Balance needed: avoid both hypoglycemia and excessive hyperglycemia

**Bottom Line:** Glucose is the essential substrate for brain aerobic metabolism, with both hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia (>180 mg/dL) being detrimental to ischemic brain tissue.

# 31. A PRACTICAL APPROACH

## 31.1. CURRENT CLINICAL REALITY

**🔑 KEY POINT 10:** Reliable pharmacologic and nonpharmacologic therapies to prevent neuronal ischemic injury are currently not readily available for use in the perioperative period. At the present time, one can only hope to attenuate injury by preventing secondary insults to surrounding neuronal tissue (e.g., ensuring adequate oxygen and substrate delivery).

## 31.2. PRACTICAL NEUROPROTECTIVE STRATEGIES

**General principle:** With some exceptions, inhaled and intravenous anesthetics may protect brain from ischemic injury.

**Planned regional ischemia (e.g., temporary clipping during aneurysm surgery):**
- **Propofol regimen:**
  - Large bolus: 1-2 mg/kg
  - High-dose infusion: 150 μg/kg/min
  - **Goal:** Titrated to induce burst suppression prior to planned ischemia
- **Vasopressor:** May be required to maintain CPP during high-dose propofol

**Cardiac or neurologic surgeries with planned circulatory/pulmonary arrest:**
- **Examples:** Aortic arch repair, giant basilar aneurysm clipping
- **Technique:** Deep hypothermia (12-18°C) to protect nervous system

**Thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm repair:**
- **Lumbar CSF drain placement** prior to surgery
- **Purpose:** Lower CSF pressure, maintain spinal cord perfusion when aortic radicular arteries at surgical risk

## 31.3. SECONDARY INJURY PREVENTION FOCUS

**Current best practice:** Focus on preventing secondary insults rather than primary neuroprotection:
- Ensure adequate oxygen delivery
- Maintain substrate delivery
- Optimize cerebral perfusion pressure
- Prevent hyperthermia
- Manage glucose appropriately

**Bottom Line:** Current neuroprotective strategies focus on preventing secondary injury through optimized physiologic management rather than proven pharmacologic neuroprotection, with specific techniques for planned ischemia scenarios.

# 33. PREOPERATIVE EVALUATION

## 33.1. PARAMOUNT IMPORTANCE

**🔑 KEY POINT 11:** The preoperative evaluation of the neurosurgical patient is of paramount importance to ensure a safe and successful anesthetic. Specific problems must be identified so as to formulate appropriate plans for intraoperative and postoperative management. For patients with intracranial mass lesions, the most important fact to ascertain is the presence and extent of intracranial hypertension and this should be assumed until information proves otherwise. Choice of drugs for maintenance of general anesthesia depends to a great extent on the ICP and whether neuromonitoring is being employed.

## 33.2. INTRACRANIAL MASS LESIONS

**Primary assessment goal:** Presence and extent of intracranial hypertension  
**Clinical approach:** Should be assumed until information proves otherwise

**Information sources:**
- **History and physical examination**
- **CT and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans**
- **ICP measurements** (if available)

**Clinical symptoms of elevated ICP:**
- Headaches
- Dizziness
- Visual or gait disturbances
- Nausea or vomiting
- Seizures

**Physical examination findings:**
- Altered level of consciousness
- Confusion
- Papilledema
- Loss of strength or sensation
- Cranial nerve dysfunction

**Radiologic indicators of advanced pathology:**
- **"Slit ventricles"**
- **Midline brain structure shift >5 mm**

## 33.3. LABORATORY EVALUATION

**Routine blood tests utility:**
- May demonstrate electrolyte disturbances

**Causes of electrolyte abnormalities:**
- **Pituitary pathology** (e.g., SIADH)
- Diuretics
- Steroids
- Anticonvulsants

## 33.4. PREMEDICATION CONSIDERATIONS

**Patients with elevated ICP:**
- **Premedication must be carefully titrated or avoided completely**
- **Rationale:** Benzodiazepines and opioids (even small doses) can depress respiration → elevated PaCO2 → exacerbated intracranial hypertension

**Patients with motor deficits:**
- **Risk:** Even sedative doses of anesthetic drugs (especially significant GABA-ergic activity) can exacerbate or "unmask" preexisting or resolved motor deficits

**Medications to continue:**
- **Corticosteroids** (e.g., dexamethasone)
- **Anticonvulsants**

## 33.5. SPINE SURGERY EVALUATION

**Preoperative focus (especially acute setting):**
- **Airway examination**
- Current hemodynamic conditions
- Level of injury
- Degree of injury (complete vs. incomplete)
- Timing of injury
- Complete neurologic examination

**Planning requirements:**
- **Endotracheal intubation:** Careful planning essential
- **Hemodynamic management:** May require advanced airway techniques and careful fluid management with concomitant vasopressor use

**Bottom Line:** Preoperative evaluation must identify elevated ICP and plan anesthetic approach accordingly, with particular attention to premedication risks and medication continuation needs.

# 34. INDUCTION AND AIRWAY MANAGEMENT

## 34.1. CRITICAL PERIODS

**Induction of general anesthesia and airway management are critically important periods, especially in patients with:**
- Elevated ICP
- Unsecured aneurysm
- Cervical spinal cord injury

## 34.2. ELEVATED ICP CONSIDERATIONS

**Hemodynamic management:**
- **Constant attention to hemodynamics** critical to maintain CPP
- **"Slow and controlled" induction** with constant blood pressure attention throughout process
- ⚠️ **Hypotension must be avoided**

**Preparatory measures:**
- **Preinduction arterial catheterization** (may be helpful)
- **Osmotic diuresis** (may be helpful)
- **CSF drainage** (may be helpful)

**Pharmacological adjuncts:**
- **Short-acting opioid**
- **Lidocaine (1.5 mg/kg IV)** to blunt sympathetic response to laryngoscopy
- **Muscle relaxant** administered following induction

**Ventilation management:**
- ⚠️ **Avoid hypoventilation and hypercapnia**

## 34.3. MUSCLE RELAXANT CONSIDERATIONS

**Succinylcholine concerns:**
- ⚠️ **Use with caution** in patients with preexisting motor deficits
- **Mechanism:** Upregulation of nicotinic receptors at neuromuscular junction → increased hyperkalemia risk
- **ICP effects:** Can increase ICP but effect is short duration

## 34.4. BLOOD PRESSURE CONTROL DURING INTUBATION

**Critical importance:** Strict control during intubation
- **Hypertension risks:**
  - Worsens ICP (especially with autoregulatory failure)
  - Puts unsecured aneurysm at rupture risk
- **Hypotension risks:** Decreased CPP can be detrimental

## 34.5. CERVICAL SPINAL CORD INJURY

**Primary concerns:**
- **MAP maintenance:** Very important during induction
- **Intubation technique:** May require complex approaches
  - Awake fiberoptic intubation
  - Midline and sagittal stabilization
- **Goal:** Ensure spinal cord not further compromised

**Bottom Line:** Induction and airway management require meticulous hemodynamic control to maintain CPP while avoiding hypertension, with specific techniques needed for cervical spine injuries.

# 35. MAINTENANCE OF ANESTHESIA

## 35.1. VARIABLE REGIMENS BASED ON GOALS

**Maintenance regimens vary depending on:**
- Hemodynamic goals for procedure
- Monitoring goals for procedure

## 35.2. INTRACRANIAL SURGERY MANAGEMENT

**ICP control paramount until dura mater opened**

**Standard interventions after positioning:**
- **Mannitol (0.5-1.5 g/kg)** if ICP control needed
- **Steroids** (e.g., dexamethasone 10 mg)
- **Prophylactic anticonvulsant** (in some cases)

**Anesthetic choice factors:**
- **ICP level**
- **Whether neuromonitoring employed**

## 35.3. DRUG SELECTION FOR ELEVATED ICP

**Volatile anesthetic limitations:**
- **Often limited to 0.5 MAC** (if used at all)
- **Rationale:** Minimize cerebral vasodilation and autoregulation inhibition

**Preferred maintenance techniques:**
- **Propofol infusion** ± short-acting opioid (remifentanil or sufentanil)
- **This regimen works well for neuromonitoring cases** (>0.5 MAC volatile agent may interfere with SSEP and MEP monitoring)

**Muscle relaxants:**
- **Generally used** unless limited by MEP or EMG monitoring

**Nitrous oxide considerations:**
- **May be considered** in select neurosurgical cases
- **Avoid when:**
  - Patient has significantly increased ICP (mild vasodilating effects)
  - Potential for intradural air
  - Will interfere with monitoring modalities

**Immobility without muscle relaxant:**
- **Remifentanil infusions** can achieve immobility

## 35.4. HEMODYNAMIC MANAGEMENT

**CPP maintenance:**
- Must be maintained throughout procedure
- May require vasopressor

**Vasopressor considerations:**
- **Current evidence:** Insufficient evidence to recommend certain vasopressors over others for most neurosurgical patients
- **Potential considerations:** Vasopressor choice may affect intracranial capillary flow and brain oxygenation beyond arterial tone impact (especially with disrupted BBB)

**Autoregulation impairment:**
- **If greatly inhibited:** CBF becomes directly dependent on MAP
- **Clinical implication:** Hypertension should be avoided

## 35.5. SPINAL CORD INJURY CONSIDERATIONS

**Similar principles apply:**
- Spinal cord perfusion (especially cervical spine surgery)
- Ability to perform neuromonitoring are major concerns

**Bottom Line:** Maintenance anesthesia requires balancing ICP control, hemodynamic stability, and neuromonitoring requirements, with volatile agents limited and TIVA often preferred for complex cases.

# 36. VENTILATION MANAGEMENT

## 36.1. KEY CONSIDERATIONS

**Ventilatory management is a key consideration for patients undergoing neurosurgery.**

## 36.2. INTRACRANIAL PROCEDURE GUIDELINES

**Tidal volume management:**
- **Maintain at 6-8 mL/kg** to minimize potential inflammatory lung injury
- **Peak pressures:** Keep <40 cmH2O

**Special populations:**
- **SAH or TBI patients:** May already exhibit acute lung injury (ALI) or adult respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)
- **Principles especially important** for these patients

## 36.3. PEEP CONSIDERATIONS

**General approach:**
- **Minimize PEEP** unless needed to improve oxygenation
- **Rationale:** Increases intrathoracic pressure → may impede cerebral venous drainage

**Clinical threshold:**
- **Low PEEP levels** appear well tolerated for most patients
- Balance oxygenation needs with cerebral venous drainage concerns

## 36.4. POSITIVE PRESSURE VENTILATION BENEFITS

**PPV generally used for neurosurgical procedures:**
- **Allows direct control of PaCO2**
- **Especially beneficial during sitting craniotomies:** Negative intrathoracic pressure may contribute to venous air embolism (VAE) development

**Bottom Line:** Ventilation management emphasizes lung-protective strategies with careful PEEP use to balance oxygenation needs with cerebral venous drainage requirements.

# 37. FLUID AND ELECTROLYTE MANAGEMENT

## 37.1. EVOLUTION OF FLUID MANAGEMENT PHILOSOPHY

**Historical teaching:** Keep patient "dry" to minimize reactive cerebral edema  
**Current approach:** Not considered optimal

**Primary goal:** Maintain cerebral perfusion
- **More important consideration** than minimizing edema
- **Actually lessens cerebral edema production**

## 37.2. CURRENT FLUID MANAGEMENT STRATEGY

**Optimal approach:** Keep patient euvolemic

**Fluid selection:**
- **Preferred:** Isotonic or slightly hypertonic solutions (e.g., 0.9% sodium chloride)
- **When needed:** Large amounts of fluid administration

**Fluids to avoid:**
- **Hypotonic solutions** (e.g., Ringer lactate) in large amounts → can contribute to cerebral edema
- **Glucose-containing solutions** for multiple reasons:
  - Hyperglycemia detrimental to cerebral metabolism
  - Glucose quickly metabolized and not osmotically active
  - Leaves free water that can worsen brain edema

## 37.3. ELECTROLYTE MONITORING

**Risk factors for derangements:**
- Patient comorbidities
- Length of surgery

**Specific conditions requiring monitoring:**
- **SIADH**
- **Diabetes insipidus (DI)**
- **Cerebral salt wasting syndrome**

## 37.4. SODIUM MANAGEMENT

**Hyponatremic states:**
- **Hypertonic saline (3%) supplementation** may be required
- **Administration rate:** Slowly at 50-100 mL/h
- **Monitoring:** Serum sodium checked hourly

⚠️ **Critical safety:** Rapid rises in serum sodium (>3-4 mEq/L/h) must be avoided → central pontine myelinolysis risk

## 37.5. DIABETES INSIPIDUS MANAGEMENT

**Fluid requirements:**
- **Hypotonic solutions:** Ringer lactate or 0.45% sodium chloride might be required
- **Administration:** Carefully titrated based on volume status and serum electrolyte concentrations

## 37.6. MANNITOL-RELATED EFFECTS

**Electrolyte effects:**
- Can cause mild, generally short-lived electrolyte derangements:
  - Hyponatremia
  - Hyperkalemia
- **Management:** Should be monitored and possibly corrected

**Volume effects:**
- **Diuresis risk:** Can result in dehydration impairing cerebral perfusion

## 37.7. SODIUM CHLORIDE COMPLICATIONS

**Large-volume 0.9% sodium chloride risks:**
- **Hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis**
- **Subsequent acute kidney injury** due to renal tubular acidosis

**Bottom Line:** Modern fluid management emphasizes euvolemia with isotonic/hypertonic solutions while avoiding hypotonic and glucose-containing fluids, with careful electrolyte monitoring and correction.

# 38. TRANSFUSION THERAPY

## 38.1. PREOPERATIVE CONSIDERATIONS

**Coagulation studies review:**
- Should be carefully noted if available
- **Required for patients receiving anticoagulants**

**Preoperative correction:**
- **Coagulopathies should be corrected**
- **Rationale:** Intraoperative/postoperative bleeding can significantly adversely impact outcome

**Platelet threshold:**
- **Neurosurgical patients:** Nonurgent surgery should have platelet count >100,000/mm³

## 38.2. BLOOD PREPARATION STRATEGY

**Low bleeding risk procedures:**
- **"Type and screen"** with negative antibody screen may be adequate

**High bleeding risk procedures:**
- **Red blood cells "typed and crossed"** should be available
- **Examples:** Neurovascular procedures (aneurysm clipping, AVM resection), tumor craniotomies invading cranial sinuses

## 38.3. COAGULOPATHY MANAGEMENT

**Tissue thromboplastin release:**
- **Source:** Brain tissue thromboplastin
- **Products needed:** Fresh frozen plasma, platelets, cryoprecipitate

## 38.4. COMPLEX SPINE SURGERY

**Characteristics:**
- Usually associated with more profound blood loss
- Volume shifts
- Need for transfusion therapy

**Preparation requirements:**
- **Multiple units of blood products** immediately available
- **Frequent monitoring:** Complete blood count (CBC) and coagulation studies

**Blood products:**
- **Planned osteotomies or tumor cases:** Associated with higher transfusion needs

**Bottom Line:** Transfusion planning depends on bleeding risk assessment, with complex spine and neurovascular procedures requiring more extensive blood product preparation and monitoring.

# 39. GLUCOSE MANAGEMENT

## 39.1. IMPORTANCE IN NEUROSURGICAL CASES

**Critical for prevention of:**
- Hypoglycemia
- Hyperglycemia

## 39.2. HISTORICAL PERSPECTIVE

**2001 van den Berghe study:**
- Showed strict glycemic control (target 80-110 mg/dL) in critically ill surgical patients associated with improved outcome

**Subsequent concerns:**
- **Strict glycemic control** associated with increased hypoglycemia risk
- **Hypoglycemia** can be detrimental to brain

## 39.3. CURRENT RECOMMENDATIONS

**Target range:** Most authors agree serum glucose should be maintained in **90-180 mg/dL range**

**Clinical thresholds:**
- **Hyperglycemia >180 mg/dL:** Associated with increased postoperative infections after craniotomy

## 39.4. HYPERGLYCEMIA MANAGEMENT

**Treatment approach:**
- **Short-acting insulin** readily available
- Can be given intravenously as bolus ± infusion

**Monitoring requirement:**
- **Frequent glucose monitoring** to capture hypoglycemic episodes

## 39.5. HYPOGLYCEMIA MANAGEMENT

**Treatment:**
- **50% dextrose** should be available
- **Dosing:** 20-50 mL administered
- **Titration:** Based on continued close glucose monitoring

**Bottom Line:** Glucose management targets 90-180 mg/dL range with readily available insulin for hyperglycemia and dextrose for hypoglycemia, requiring frequent monitoring.

# 40. EMERGENCE

## 40.1. CRITICAL PARAMETERS

**Emergence from general anesthesia following neurosurgical procedures requires meticulous attention to:**
- Hemodynamic parameters
- Ventilatory parameters
- **While ensuring prompt neurologic examination**

## 40.2. POSTCRANIOTOMY HYPERTENSION

**Well-described phenomenon:**
- Poorly understood mechanism
- **Can be detrimental:**
  - May increase bleeding from surgical site
  - Worsen cerebral edema

**Management approach:**
- **Careful analgesia:** Helpful but should not obtund patient postoperatively
- **Antihypertensive medications usually required:**
  - Labetalol
  - Esmolol
  - Nicardipine
  - Clevidipine

## 40.3. SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS - AVM RESECTION

**Control of hypertension especially important:**
- **Risk:** Bleeding from resection bed may be significant
- Requires particularly vigilant blood pressure management

## 40.4. POSTERIOR FOSSA SURGERY

**Unique emergence challenges:**
- **Slower emergence** in patients with:
  - Posterior fossa surgery
  - Preexisting ventilatory disturbance due to brainstem compromise
- **Prolonged time to safe extubation**

## 40.5. COMPLICATIONS TO AVOID

**Coughing on emergence:**
- **Should be avoided** due to:
  - Risk of bleeding
  - Elevation in ICP

**Management strategies:**
- **Low-dose remifentanil infusion**
- **Intravenous or intratracheal lidocaine**

## 40.6. POSTOPERATIVE NAUSEA AND VOMITING (PONV)

**Should be prophylactically treated:**
- **Reasons:** Same as coughing (bleeding risk, ICP elevation)

**Dexamethasone considerations:**
- ⚠️ **Avoid in lymphoma diagnosis cases** (or delay until adequate pathology specimen obtained)
  - **Rationale:** Steroids can cause tumor lysis → interfere with diagnosis
- ⚠️ **Avoid following pituitary surgery**
  - **Rationale:** Can suppress hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis → significantly increase false positive rate for postoperative hypopituitarism diagnosis

**Bottom Line:** Emergence management focuses on controlling hypertension while avoiding coughing and vomiting, with special considerations for posterior fossa cases and careful dexamethasone use.

# 42. BRAIN TUMOR SURGERY

## 42.1. TUMOR TYPES AND LOCATIONS

**Adult supratentorial lesions (more common):**
- **Support cell derived:** Gliomas, astrocytomas, oligodendrogliomas
- **Meningiomas**
- **Metastases:** Most common from melanoma, lung, breast, kidney

**Pediatric infratentorial tumors (posterior fossa, more common in children):**
- Medulloblastoma
- Pilocytic astrocytoma
- Ependymoma
- Brainstem glioma

## 42.2. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

**Independent of histology, brain tumor morbidity associated with:**
- Tumor size
- Rate of growth
- Proximity to and invasion of nearby structures

**Vascular effects:**
- **BBB disruption:** May cause significant vasogenic edema (can persist after resection)
- **Autoregulation impairment:** Usually impaired in parenchyma surrounding tumors

**Compensatory mechanisms:**
- **Most brain tumors grow slowly** → adaptive/compensatory mechanisms accommodate ICP increases
- **Delayed diagnosis:** Often results from slow growth allowing adaptation

## 42.3. ANESTHETIC MANAGEMENT CONSIDERATIONS

**Preoperative assessment factors:**
- Lesion site and dimensions
- Plan for surgical approach
- Neurologic symptomatology
- ICP concerns
- Medical comorbidities

**Premedication caution:**
- **Anxiolysis with benzodiazepines:** Must be carefully considered
- **Risk:** Even small PaCO2 increases in asymptomatic patients can quickly lead to dangerous ICP elevation due to increased intracranial elastance

**Medication management:**
- **Continue:** Steroids and anticonvulsants
- **Often supplement:** Prior to and during craniotomy

## 42.4. HEMODYNAMIC CONSIDERATIONS

**Throughout anesthetic:**
- **Minimize hemodynamic instability** due to possible impaired autoregulation in peritumoral brain
- **Hypertension risks:**
  - Increase bleeding risk
  - Exacerbate brain edema

## 42.5. INDUCTION PROTOCOL

**General approach:**
- **Propofol** administered for induction
- **Hemodynamic blunting:**
  - Lidocaine (1-1.5 mg/kg)
  - Fentanyl (1-2 μg/kg) IV to blunt hemodynamic and ICP response to laryngoscopy

## 42.6. VASCULAR ACCESS REQUIREMENTS

**Mandatory for brain tumor resection:**
- **Two large-bore peripheral IV catheters**
- **Arterial catheter**
- **Possibly central venous catheter**

**Arterial catheter benefits:**
- Monitor CPP closely
- Allow titration of PaCO2 by revealing gradient with end-expired CO2 via arterial blood gas measurement

## 42.7. HEAD FIXATION AND POSITIONING

**Head fixation device application:**
- **Hemodynamic response similar to laryngoscopy**
- **Control requirements:** Optimal blood pressure control using:
  - Propofol
  - Opioids
  - Short-acting β-adrenergic antagonists (esmolol)

**Positioning considerations:**
- **Avoid:** Excessive flexion, extension, or rotation (may impair cerebral venous drainage via jugular vein compression)
- **Head-up position favorable:** Promotes venous drainage

## 42.8. PEEP MANAGEMENT

**Conservative approach:**
- **Use cautiously** (may impair venous drainage)
- **If oxygenation impaired:** PEEP up to 10 cmH2O can be considered

## 42.9. BRAIN VOLUME REDUCTION

**Indications:** Large tumors or known significant intracranial hypertension

**Methods:**
- **Mild hyperventilation**
- **Mannitol (0.5-1.5 g/kg)**
- **Hypertonic saline (3%):** Starting at 50-100 mL/h with hourly sodium surveillance

**Osmolarity comparison:**
- **20% mannitol and 3% hypertonic saline:** Similar volumes have nearly equivalent osmolarity
- **Clinical trials:** Hypertonic saline at least as effective, possibly more effective than mannitol for:
  - Treating elevated ICP
  - Brain relaxation in elective craniotomy

## 42.10. MANNITOL CONSIDERATIONS

**Administration:**
- **Give as bolus** to achieve full effect

**Contraindications/cautions:**
- **Congestive heart failure**
- **Pulmonary edema**  
- **Renal failure**
- **Rationale:** Initial elevation in central circulatory volume may be detrimental before diuretic phase

**Dosing concerns:**
- **Higher-end doses:** May be associated with increased rebound intracranial edema risk

## 42.11. MAINTENANCE ANESTHESIA SELECTION

**Choice heavily dictated by:**
- Avoiding ICP increases (until dura open)
- Maintaining CPP
- Neuromonitoring requirements
- Ensuring rapid emergence

**Preferred agents (short-acting, easily titratable):**
- **Propofol**
- **Remifentanil**
- **Sevoflurane**
- **Desflurane**
- **Nitrous oxide** (unless contraindicated)

**Muscle relaxants:**
- **Beneficial** if not precluded by neuromonitoring

## 42.12. FLUID MANAGEMENT

**Goal:** Euvolemia

**Preferred fluids:**
- **Dextrose-free iso-osmolar crystalloids**
- **Colloids**

## 42.13. EXTUBATION CRITERIA

**Generally extubated at case conclusion:**
- **Patients with normal preoperative consciousness level** (GCS 13-15)

**Hemodynamic stability paramount:**
- **Aberrations (usually hypertension) can lead to:**
  - Worsening cerebral edema
  - Tumor resection bed bleeding postoperatively

**Immediately available agents:**
- **Labetalol**
- **Nicardipine** 
- **Esmolol**

## 42.14. COMPLICATION PREVENTION

**Minimize coughing/vomiting after extubation:**
- **Both may worsen ICP**
- **Management:**
  - Careful opioid titration (remifentanil IV infusion 0.05 mcg/kg/min)
  - Prophylactic antiemetic

## 42.15. PAIN MANAGEMENT BALANCE

**Dual requirements:**
- **Adequate analgesia:** Craniotomy associated with significant pain
- **Avoid obtundation:** Preclude careful postoperative neurologic assessment

**Options:**
- **Short-acting opioids:** Remifentanil, fentanyl
- **Local anesthetic infiltration**
- **Intravenous acetaminophen**
- **Dexmedetomidine**

## 42.16. POSTERIOR FOSSA CONSIDERATIONS

**Increased risks due to brainstem proximity:**
- **More hemodynamic perturbations:** Intraoperatively and postoperatively
- **Respiratory control changes** postoperatively
- **Arousal level changes** postoperatively
- **More frequent requirement:** Postoperative intubation and mechanical ventilation due to altered consciousness or delayed emergence

## 42.17. ICU ADMISSION CRITERIA

**Wide institutional variation for post-craniotomy ICU admission**

**General considerations for ICU admission:**
- **New neurologic deficits:** Particularly reduced consciousness or lower cranial nerve deficits
- **Delayed extubation indications**

## 42.18. ADULT POSTERIOR FOSSA TUMORS

**Types:**
- Acoustic neuromas
- Metastases
- Meningiomas
- Hemangioblastomas

**Clinical effects (brainstem/cranial nerve proximity):**
- Altered respiratory patterns
- Cardiac dysrhythmias
- Cranial nerve dysfunction

**Surgical positions:**
- **Prone**
- **Lateral**
- **Sitting**

**Monitoring requirements:**
- **Specific cranial nerve monitoring:** EMG or BAERs of cranial nerves
- **Sitting position:** Requires special attention due to increased VAE risk

**Bottom Line:** Brain tumor surgery requires comprehensive preoperative evaluation, careful ICP management, optimal positioning, and balanced emergence to ensure neurologic assessment while preventing complications.

# 43. VENOUS AIR EMBOLISM IN SITTING POSITION

## 43.1. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

**VAE occurs when:**
- **Operative site above right atrium level**
- **Open, noncollapsible venous channels present**

## 43.2. INCIDENCE

**Sitting posterior fossa surgery:**
- **VAE occurs:** 30-75% of all cases
- **Usually hemodynamically insignificant**
- **Hemodynamically significant:** Estimated 8-15% of episodes

## 43.3. PATHOPHYSIOLOGICAL EFFECTS

**Air entrainment into heart and pulmonary circulation can lead to:**
- Impaired gas exchange
- Intrapulmonary shunting
- Hypoxemia
- **Concomitant decrease in end-expired CO2**

**Severe air entrainment:**
- Arrhythmias
- Decreased cardiac output
- Severe pulmonary hypertension
- Hemodynamic collapse

## 43.4. MONITORING

**Precordial Doppler ultrasonography:**
- **Can detect:** 0.25 mL of air in heart
- **Characteristics:** Qualitative monitor (indicates presence, not quantity)

**Transesophageal echocardiography:**
- **More sensitive** than precordial Doppler
- **More cumbersome and invasive**
- **Requires:** Observer familiar with technique
- **Limitations:** May cease working when probe temperature rises → not continuous
- **Advantage:** Allows quantitative assessment of intracardiac air

## 43.5. PREVENTION

**Strategies:**
- **Decrease height difference** between operative site and heart as much as possible
- **Maintain euvolemia**
- **Surgeon use of bone wax** to occlude visibly open dural venous sinuses or larger veins

## 43.6. TREATMENT

**Immediate measures:**
- **Notify surgeon** to flood surgical field
- **Administer 100% oxygen**
- **Aspirate air** through multiorifice central venous catheter positioned at superior vena cava-right atrium junction

**Supportive hemodynamic care:**
- **Vasopressors**
- **Fluids**
- **Inotropes**
- **Adjust OR table position** (head at heart level)

**Position change considerations:**
- **Reserved for severe/unremitting VAE manifestations**
- **Likely to disrupt surgical field**
- **Retrospective evidence:** Found effective for treating hemodynamic and respiratory changes from VAE

**Nitrous oxide management:**
- **If being used:** Should be stopped
- **Rationale:** N2O may increase air bubble volume → potentially exacerbate clinical effects

**PEEP application:**
- **Theoretically favorable**
- **Can exacerbate systemic hypotension**

**Bottom Line:** VAE is common but usually insignificant in sitting craniotomies, requiring vigilant monitoring and immediate treatment protocols including surgical field flooding and supportive care.

# 44. PITUITARY SURGERY

## 44.1. SURGICAL APPROACH

**Usually performed:**
- **Endoscopically**
- **Transnasally**

## 44.2. ANESTHETIC CONCERNS

**Primary considerations:**
- **Systemic manifestations** of associated endocrinopathy
- **Electrolyte and fluid disturbances** from endocrine disease
- **SIADH or DI**
- **Inadvertent surgical trespass** into cavernous sinus or internal carotid artery

**Visual considerations:**
- **Patients with sellar mass:** May exhibit visual field defects
- **Important:** Careful preoperative history/physical to differentiate organic vs. anesthetic causes of postoperative visual problems

## 44.3. GROWTH HORMONE-SECRETING TUMORS (ACROMEGALY)

**Anatomical changes:**
- **Large mandible**
- **Tissue hypertrophy** → decreased airway aperture

**Clinical consequences:**
- **Predisposes to:**
  - Obstructive sleep apnea
  - Difficulty with mask ventilation and intubation

**Airway management considerations:**
- **Smaller-sized endotracheal tube** may be needed
- **Additional airway tools/strategies:**
  - Awake fiberoptic intubation
  - Video laryngoscope-based intubation

**Cardiac effects:**
- **Longstanding acromegalics prone to:**
  - Cardiac rhythm disturbances
  - Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
- **Caution warranted** with cardiac depressant medications

## 44.4. ACTH-SECRETING TUMORS (CUSHING SYNDROME)

**Caused by hypercortisolism, associated with:**
- **Glucose intolerance or diabetes mellitus**
- **Increased skin fragility** (potentially difficult peripheral IV access)
- **Impaired wound healing**
- **Secondary hypertension**

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Data suggest Cushing syndrome not significantly associated with airway difficulty.

## 44.5. TSH-SECRETING PITUITARY ADENOMAS

**Management approach:**
- **Should be rendered euthyroid** before surgery unless vision threatened

**Risk of prior treatment:**
- **Patients may have been treated for Graves disease** preoperatively
- **Consequence:** Decreased free T4/T3 production → reduced negative feedback to adenoma → may predispose to rapid tumor growth

## 44.6. SIADH MANAGEMENT

**Common in sellar tumors due to:**
- **Posterior pituitary compression**
- **Excess circulating ADH**

**Clinical effects:**
- **Intravascular volume overload**
- **Hyponatremia**
- **Extracellular body water usually normal** (edema/hypertension not characteristic)

**Treatment:**
- **Water restriction** (to extent safe)
- **Treat underlying cause**
- **Demeclocycline:** Tetracycline antibiotic inhibiting ADH action in renal tubules
- **Desmopressin:** For persistent or severe cases

## 44.7. DIABETES INSIPIDUS MANAGEMENT

**Hallmark:** Polyuria with dilute urine  
**Treatment:** Fluid replacement required

## 44.8. SURGICAL COMPLICATION CONSIDERATIONS

**Accidental surgical entry (infrequent complication):**
- **Cavernous sinus**
- **Internal carotid artery**

**Preparation:**
- **Adequate vascular access** recommended
- **Arterial catheter** recommended

## 44.9. LUMBAR SUBARACHNOID CATHETER

**Sometimes placed** before or after pituitary surgery

**Intraoperative uses:**
- **Air or sterile saline injection:** Facilitate exposure by moving sellar structures
- **CSF withdrawal:** Same goal through volume changes

**Postoperative uses:**
- **CSF drainage:** When dural sealant or fat graft used
- **Purpose:** Reduce chronic CSF leakage risk

**Bottom Line:** Pituitary surgery presents unique challenges related to endocrine effects, airway considerations in acromegaly, fluid/electrolyte management, and potential vascular complications requiring careful preparation.

# 45. CEREBRAL ANEURYSM SURGERY AND ENDOVASCULAR TREATMENT

## 45.1. ANEURYSM DEVELOPMENT AND RUPTURE RISK

**Risk factors for development and rupture:**
- Age >40 years
- Female sex
- Cigarette smoking
- Systemic hypertension
- Connective tissue disorders

## 45.2. CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE

**Ruptured aneurysms:**
- **Most common cause** of spontaneous SAH (80% of nontraumatic SAH)

**Aneurysm formation:**
- **Thought to arise from:** Turbulent blood flow at arterial branching points
- **Results in:** "Sac-like" or "fusiform" dilatations

## 45.3. LOCATION DISTRIBUTION

**Most common locations:**
- **Anterior communicating arteries:** 40%
- **Posterior communicating arteries (PCOMs):** 25%
- **Middle cerebral artery (MCA):** 25%
- **Vertebrobasilar system:** Only 10%

## 45.4. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY FOLLOWING SAH

**Acute changes:**
- **Acute ICP increase** accompanied by **CPP decrease**
- **Compensatory increase in systemic blood pressure** to maintain CBF

## 45.5. CLINICAL PRESENTATION

**Symptoms:**
- **Severe headache:** Classically "worst headache of my life"
- Nausea and vomiting
- Photophobia
- Seizures
- Focal neurologic deficits
- Altered consciousness

**Diagnostic imaging:**
- **Noncontrast head CT:** Usually sufficient to diagnose SAH
- **More sophisticated imaging required** for aneurysm location/morphology:
  - Digital-subtraction angiography
  - CT angiography
  - MRI angiography

## 45.6. GRADING SYSTEMS

**Hunt and Hess Grading Scale (clinical symptoms):**

| Grade | Clinical Symptoms |
|-------|------------------|
| 0 | Unruptured aneurysm/asymptomatic |
| 1 | Minimal headache, slight nuchal rigidity |
| 2 | Moderate to severe headache, nuchal rigidity, cranial nerve palsy |
| 3 | Mildly obtunded, confused, focal deficits |
| 4 | Severely obtunded, hemiparesis, early decerebrate posturing |
| 5 | Coma, decerebrate posturing |

**World Federation of Neurological Surgeons (WFNS) Grading Scale:**

| Grade | Glasgow Coma Scale Score | Motor Deficit |
|-------|-------------------------|---------------|
| 1 | 15 | No |
| 2 | 14-13 | No |
| 3 | 14-13 | Yes |
| 4 | 12-7 | Yes or no |
| 5 | 6-3 | Yes or no |

**Fisher Grade System (radiologic bleeding):**

| Grade | Blood on CT Scan |
|-------|------------------|
| 1 | No subarachnoid blood seen |
| 2 | Diffuse vertical layers of blood <1 mm thick |
| 3 | Localized clot and/or vertical layer of blood ≥1 mm thick |
| 4 | Intracerebral or intraventricular clot with diffuse or absent SAH |

## 45.7. ANEURYSM SIZE CLASSIFICATION

**Size categories (important for management):**
- **"Small":** <10 mm diameter
- **"Large":** 10-24 mm diameter
- **"Giant":** >24 mm diameter

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Rupture risk increases with diameter, with those >6 mm generally requiring treatment.

## 45.8. PREOPERATIVE ASSESSMENT - RUPTURED ANEURYSMS

**Must consider presence and possibility of:**
- **Rebleeding**
- **Vasospasm** of cerebral arteries (rarely occurs within 72 hours of rupture)
- **Hydrocephalus**
- **Cardiac dysfunction**
- **Neurogenic pulmonary edema**
- **Seizures**

**Treatment timing:**
- **Generally undertaken within first 48 hours** after SAH presentation
- **Goals:**
  - Minimize rebleeding risk
  - Prior to increased vasospasm risk

## 45.9. OPTIMIZATION REQUIREMENTS

**May require:**
- **Inotropes**
- **Antiarrhythmics**
- **Hypertonic saline supplementation** (if SIADH or cerebral salt wasting present)
- **Euvolemia maintenance**
- **Tissue oxygenation maintenance**
- **All without delaying surgical treatment**

**Medications:**
- **Enteral nimodipine:** Only intervention decreasing vasospasm risk
- **Statin drug:** Via pleotropic effects, may also decrease vasospasm risk

## 45.10. SURGICAL CLIPPING CONSIDERATIONS

**Aneurysm morphology:**
- **Amenable to clipping:** Usually saccular (vs. fusiform)

**Rupture prevention:**
- **Great caution** to minimize rupture risk
- **Avoid hypertension during:**
  - Intubation
  - Head fixation
  - Surgical procedure

**ICP considerations:**
- **Prior rupture patients:** Adequate CPP must be maintained (ICP may be elevated)

## 45.11. ANESTHETIC INDUCTION

**Controlled approach:**
- **ICP considerations** in mind
- **CPP maintained** (sometimes requiring careful vasopressor use)
- **Prevent hypertension** (increased rerupture risk)

**Unruptured aneurysms with normal ICP:**
- **Excessive hypertension should be avoided**

## 45.12. MAINTENANCE TECHNIQUE

**Similar to brain tumor maintenance**
- **Neuromonitoring may be employed** to detect regional ischemia
- **No optimal regimen identified** for improved neurologic outcome
- **Balanced anesthetic technique** appropriate considering above factors

## 45.13. BURST SUPPRESSION DURING TEMPORARY CLIPPING

**During aneurysm exposure:**
- **Burst suppression on EEG may be desired** to decrease ischemic burden from temporary large vessel occlusion
- **Not fully supported by human clinical data**

**Technique:**
- **Propofol:** 1-2 mg/kg bolus followed by 100-150 μg/kg/min infusion
- **Additional vasopressor** may be required to maintain CPP

## 45.14. TEMPORARY CLIPPING STRATEGIES

**Prior to direct aneurysmal neck clipping:**
- **Temporary clips on parent/feeding arteries** to "soften" neck
- **Make more amenable to direct clipping** while minimizing rupture chances

**Alternative when temporary clips anatomically difficult:**
- **Adenosine 0.3-0.4 mg/kg bolus** → transient (3-5 second) circulatory arrest allowing safe permanent clip application

**Other techniques (more logistically difficult):**
- Deep hypothermic circulatory arrest (DHCA)
- Retrograde suction decompression
- Rapid ventricular pacing

**Enhanced monitoring:**
- **SSEP and MEP monitoring** may be performed more frequently during clipping (greatest ischemic risk time)

## 45.15. INTRAOPERATIVE RUPTURE MANAGEMENT

**Inadvertent rupture possible** during aneurysm dissection

**Required preparation:**
- **Plan must be in place** for this potentially devastating complication
- **Availability:**
  - Blood products
  - **Adenosine (0.3-0.4 mg/kg)**
  - **Rapidly acting antihypertensive** (e.g., sodium nitroprusside)
- **Large-bore intravenous access required** (especially large aneurysms >10 mm and ruptured aneurysms)
- **Central venous access recommended**

## 45.16. ENDOVASCULAR TREATMENT

**Technique:**
- **Groin arterial access**
- **Deployment of coils** into aneurysmal sac to occlude blood flow

**Efficacy evidence:**
- **Cochrane review (including ISAT):** Concluded outcome better in patients in good clinical condition post-rupture with coiling vs. clipping
- **However:** Both techniques have undergone advances; appropriateness still being determined
- **Some aneurysms not amenable to coiling** due to morphology

**Major disadvantage:**
- **Incomplete obliteration** requiring recoiling (necessary in up to 30% of cases)

## 45.17. ANESTHETIC MANAGEMENT FOR COILING

**Technique:**
- **General anesthesia with muscle relaxation** (movement prevention essential)
- **Arterial catheter needed:**
  - Monitor blood pressure closely
  - Obtain blood samples for repeated coagulation measurements (heparin administered periodically)

**Communication requirements:**
- **Close anesthesiologist-interventionalist communication** throughout procedure
- **Dye extravasation** into brain parenchyma may indicate aneurysmal or feeding vessel rupture

**Emergency preparedness:**
- **Protamine immediately available** to reverse heparin
- **Emergency interventions available:**
  - Hyperventilation
  - Mannitol
  - Burst suppression
  - EVD placement
- **Emergency craniotomy likely intervention**

**Complications:**
- **Coil embolism** to unintended brain locations possible
- **Prompt neurologic examination** important at procedure conclusion

**Bottom Line:** Cerebral aneurysm management requires understanding of grading systems, careful hemodynamic control to prevent rupture while maintaining CPP, and preparation for emergency complications in both surgical and endovascular approaches.

# 46. ARTERIOVENOUS MALFORMATIONS

## 46.1. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND CLINICAL PRESENTATION

**Cerebral AVMs are congenital abnormalities:**
- **Structure:** Plexus of arteries and "arterialized" veins forming a nidus
- **Characteristic:** No capillary bed between arteries and veins within AVMs

**Clinical consequences:**
- **Cerebral hemorrhage**
- **Headaches**
- **Seizures**
- **Signs of cerebral ischemia** due to "steal" effect

**Demographics:**
- **Usually detected:** Ages 10-40 years
- **Location:** ~70% supratentorial

## 46.2. MORPHOLOGY AND DIAGNOSIS

**Structural characteristics:**
- **Nidus:** Dysplastic vessels of varying size
- **Drainage:** One or more superficial or deep veins

**Diagnostic methods:**
- **MRI**
- **Cerebral angiography**

**Associated findings:**
- **~7% of cerebral AVMs** include flow-related cerebral aneurysm

## 46.3. TREATMENT MODALITIES

**Available treatments:**
- **Stereotactic radiosurgery**
- **Embolization:** As stand-alone therapy or prior to planned surgical resection
  - **Goals:** Minimize intraoperative bleeding, reduce burden, improve curativity

## 46.4. SPETZLER-MARTIN GRADING SYSTEM

**Used to predict surgical outcome based on:**

| Feature | Score |
|---------|-------|
| **Size** ||
| Small (<3 cm) | 1 |
| Medium (3-6 cm) | 2 |
| Large (>6 cm) | 3 |
| **Adjacent Brain** ||
| Noneloquent | 0 |
| Eloquent | 1 |
| **Venous Drainage Pattern** ||
| Superficial | 0 |
| Deep | 1 |

## 46.5. ANESTHETIC MANAGEMENT - VASCULAR ACCESS

**Vascular access more important than any other intracranial neurosurgical procedure:**
- **Central venous catheter highly recommended**

**Greatest risk:** Bleeding (intraoperatively and postoperatively)

## 46.6. HEMODYNAMIC MANAGEMENT

**Complex balance required:**
- **Blood pressure control:**
  - **Maintain CPP** without worsening blood loss from resection bed
  - **Avoid hypotension:** Crucial due to seizures/focal deficits from ischemic "steal" phenomenon

**Rupture considerations:**
- **Most AVMs high-flow, low-resistance shunts**
- **Low rupture incidence** with acute BP rises (unless accompanying aneurysm present)

**Required preparations:**
- **Blood products immediately available**
- **Antihypertensives often needed** (especially during emergence)

## 46.7. SURGICAL HEMOSTASIS CHALLENGES

**Difficulty factors:**
- **Poor visualization** of arterial and especially venous vessels
- **Postoperative bleeding risk**

## 46.8. NORMAL PERFUSION PRESSURE BREAKTHROUGH (NPPB)

**Controversial mechanism but clinically relevant:**
- **Theory:** Autoregulatory inhibition caused by AVM affecting surrounding "normal" brain
- **Process:** Previously normal cerebral vessels maximally vasodilated due to longstanding "steal"
- **Post-resection:** "Vasoparalyzed" vessels unable to vasoconstrict

**Clinical consequences:**
- **Cerebral hyperemia**
- **Cerebral edema**
- **Headache**
- **Possibly increased postoperative bleeding risk**

## 46.9. OCCLUSIVE HYPEREMIA

**Alternative phenomenon potentially leading to perioperative hyperemia:**
- **Primarily related to:** Arterial and venous obstruction in normal brain tissue surrounding AVM
- **Cause:** Surgical disruption of vessels
- **Impact:** Flow impairment in brain parenchyma that surrounded AVM

**Consequences:**
- **Ischemia:** From poor arterial flow
- **Edema**
- **Hemorrhage risk:** From impaired venous outflow

## 46.10. POSTOPERATIVE CONSIDERATIONS

**Seizure management:**
- **Up to 50% of patients** may experience postoperative seizures
- **Prophylactic anticonvulsants typically administered**

**Neuromonitoring:**
- **Increasingly used** during cerebral AVM resections
- **Maintenance anesthetic regimen** allowing neuromonitoring may be required

**Standard procedures:**
- **Arterial catheterization**
- **Careful induction and intubation** (similar to cerebral aneurysms)

**Bottom Line:** AVM resection presents unique challenges with bleeding risk, complex hemodynamic management to balance perfusion and hemorrhage prevention, and potential for hyperemia-related complications requiring careful monitoring and prophylactic measures.

# 47. CAROTID SURGERY

## 47.1. CLINICAL INDICATIONS

**Carotid endarterectomy generally indicated when:**
- **Plaque burden >70% occlusive** in internal carotid artery (ICA)
- **Clinical significance:** Up to 20% of strokes may be due to cerebral ischemia from ICA intimal narrowing (unilateral or bilateral)

**Evidence-based benefits:**
- **Proven useful** for reducing stroke risk in severe ICA occlusion (70-99% occlusion) with ischemic symptoms
- **Less severe occlusion or asymptomatic patients:** Benefit may not outweigh risks → medical management may be preferred

## 47.2. TREATMENT OPTIONS

**Procedural treatments:**
- **Carotid endarterectomy**
- **Carotid artery stenting**

**Carotid endarterectomy advantages:**
- **Lower overall incidence:** Postoperative stroke (particularly older patients) and restenosis

**Potential disadvantages:**
- **General or regional anesthetic requirement**
- **Possible increased cardiac event risk**
- **Higher cranial nerve dysfunction incidence**

**Carotid artery stenting advantages:**
- **Very minimal sedation possible**
- **Lower cranial nerve injury incidence**

**Stenting disadvantages:**
- **Higher risk:** Restenosis and stroke in postprocedural period

## 47.3. ANESTHETIC TECHNIQUE - "AWAKE" SURGERY

**Regional anesthesia approach:**
- **Technique:** Superficial and sometimes deep cervical plexus block (C2-C4 dermatomes)
- **Adjuncts:** Low-dose analgesia and sedation with remifentanil or propofol may be considered
- **Critical requirement:** Patient responsive to commands and able to perform contralateral manual tasks

**Patient selection criteria:**
- **Cooperative patient** able to tolerate lying flat for prolonged period
- **Unsuitable candidates:** Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or uncompensated congestive heart failure

**Advantages of awake technique:**
- **Direct neurologic status monitoring**
- **Better hemodynamic stability**
- **Shorter hospital length of stay**
- **Decreased bleeding complications**

**Ischemia management:**
- **If patient becomes agitated, confused, or unresponsive** following carotid occlusion
- **Assumption:** Cerebral ischemia has ensued
- **Management:** Ensure adequate perfusion and oxygenation by increasing systemic BP up to 20% greater than preoperative values

## 47.4. ANESTHETIC TECHNIQUE - "ASLEEP" SURGERY

**General endotracheal anesthesia:**
- **Frequently employs** some form of cerebral ischemia monitoring

**Monitoring modalities:**
- **EEG:** Most common (ischemia presents as ipsilateral slowing)
- **SSEPs and MEPs:** Ischemia as decreased amplitude or increased latency
- **Carotid stump pressure:** Pressure in ICA distal to cross-clamp
  - **Reflects:** Adequate collateral flow via circle of Willis
  - **Desirable threshold:** >50 mmHg
- **Cerebral oximetry or TCD:** Less commonly employed

⚠️ **Monitoring limitations:** No modality has been shown to reliably ensure adequate collateral blood flow.

**Advantages of general anesthesia:**
- **Motionless patient**
- **Ability to ensure eucapnia**
- **Airway control at all times**

**Eucapnia preference:**
- **Hyperventilation:** Cerebral vasoconstriction and decreased CBF
- **Hypoventilation/hypercapnia:** May lead to "steal" phenomenon from watershed areas

## 47.5. BLOOD PRESSURE MANAGEMENT

**Invasive arterial monitoring preferred:**
- **Operative morbidity:** Generally due to neurologic complications
- **Operative mortality:** Usually due to cardiac complications

**Patient characteristics:**
- **Generally chronically hypertensive** preoperatively
- **May have cardiovascular disease** and other significant comorbidities

**Pre-cross-clamp management:**
- **Blood pressure maintained at baseline levels**
- **Regional anesthesia:** Often maintains baseline BP naturally
- **General anesthesia:** May require pharmacologic manipulation

## 47.6. CROSS-CLAMPING MANAGEMENT

**Cross-clamp placement:**
- **Usually at levels:** Common carotid artery below, ICA above plaque area
- **Heparinization commonly performed** prior to cross-clamping

**Upon cross-clamp occlusion:**
- **Blood pressure should be augmented** to improve collateral flow from contralateral side
- **Often requiring vasopressor**

**Carotid baroreceptor manipulation:**
- **Bradycardia and possibly hypotension** not uncommon
- **Surgeon may infiltrate carotid sinus with lidocaine** to prevent this response

## 47.7. FLOW RESTORATION EFFECTS

**Following carotid artery flow restoration:**
- **Hypertension may persist** (probably due to surgical denervation of carotid baroreceptor)
- **Autoregulation concerns:** Cerebral vessels distal to stenotic artery have been maximally vasodilated → autoregulation may not be intact

**Vasomotor paralysis consequences:**
- **Hypertension with cerebral vasomotor paralysis** can lead to:
  - Cerebral edema
  - Increased cerebral hemorrhage risk

## 47.8. POSTOPERATIVE COMPLICATIONS

**Neurologic compromise may indicate:**
- **Cerebral emboli**
- **ICA thrombosis**
- **Requires close monitoring**

**Neck hematoma:**
- **Anesthesiologist must be keenly aware** of this potential complication
- **Can quickly compromise airway**
- **Management:** Immediate intubation (may be more difficult) and surgical wound exploration

**Airway emergency:**
- **When complicated by expanding neck hematoma**
- **Consider immediate decompression** by surgical incision opening

## 47.9. ALTERNATIVE TREATMENT MODALITIES

**Other ICA stenosis treatments gaining popularity:**
- **Carotid angioplasty**
- **Transcarotid artery revascularization**
- **Evidence supporting safety and efficacy** over traditional endarterectomy

**Bottom Line:** Carotid surgery can be performed awake or asleep with different advantages, requires meticulous blood pressure management throughout all phases, and carries risks of neurologic and airway complications requiring immediate recognition and management.

# 48. EPILEPSY SURGERY

## 48.1. EPIDEMIOLOGY AND CHARACTERISTICS

**General population impact:**
- **Epilepsy affects ~1%** of general population
- **Characterized by:** Recurrent seizure activity (generalized and partial varieties)

**Most common type:**
- **Complex partial seizures** (including temporal lobe epilepsy)
- **Characteristics:** Initial focus of abnormal neuronal discharge → spreads with subsequent loss of consciousness

## 48.2. ETIOLOGY

**Epilepsy may occur:**
- **Idiopathically**
- **Secondary to:**
  - Head injury
  - Tumors
  - Neurovascular disease
  - Metabolic derangement
  - Infection

## 48.3. SURGICAL INDICATIONS

**Surgical treatment:**
- **~30% of patients with drug-resistant epilepsy** undergo surgical treatment
- **Generally indicated when:** Discrete epileptic focus identified (most frequently temporal lobe)

**Common procedure:**
- **Temporal lobectomy with amygdalohippocampectomy**

## 48.4. MODERN SURGICAL TECHNIQUES

**Stereoencephalography (SEEG):**
- **Less invasive technique** to identify potentially resectable epileptogenic foci
- **Process:** Electrodes implanted into brain via burr holes with stereotactic guidance and general anesthesia
- **Postoperative:** Patients admitted to epilepsy monitoring unit to attempt focus identification

**Focus identification outcomes:**
- **If focus identified:** Return to OR for craniotomy for electrode removal and focus resection
- **If focus not identified:** Electrodes removed with sedation

**Laser Interstitial Thermal Therapy (LITT):**
- **For identified focus candidates**
- **Technique:** Fiberoptic probe placed stereotactically via burr hole into epileptogenic focus
- **Method:** Laser beam directed into lesion for thermal ablation with real-time MRI guidance
- **Advantage:** Avoids need for craniotomy
- **Additional applications:** Also used to ablate brain tumors

## 48.5. PREOPERATIVE EVALUATION

**Critical assessment areas:**
- **Patient's preoperative antiepileptic regimen**
- **Known side effects** of medications
- **Plasma concentrations** of drugs with known therapeutic windows (in some cases)

**Medication management:**
- **Generally continued** throughout perioperative period unless seizure focus mapping intended

**Drug interaction considerations:**
- **Antiepileptics can induce liver enzymes**
- **Increases metabolism of:** Muscle relaxants, opioids, dexmedetomidine
- **Clinical consequence:** May need higher dosages

## 48.6. ANESTHETIC EFFECTS ON SEIZURE ACTIVITY

**Each anesthetic drug has own profile:**
- **Excitatory effects** on seizure activity
- **Inhibitory effects** on seizure activity

**Clinical significance:**
- **If used improperly:** Anesthetic drugs can be detrimental to seizure focus mapping

## 48.7. ELECTROCORTICOGRAPHY (ECOG) MANAGEMENT

**Benzodiazepines:**
- ⚠️ **Should be avoided** when ECoG planned

**Induction:**
- **Acceptable:** Propofol, muscle relaxant, and opioid

**Maintenance prior to ECoG:**
- **Any regimen conducive for craniotomy** can be used

**30 minutes prior to ECoG initiation:**
- **Propofol infusion should be stopped**
- **Potent volatile anesthetic:** Decreased to 0.5 MAC or stopped

**Awareness prevention during ECoG:**
- **Scopolamine**
- **Nitrous oxide**
- **High-dose opioid infusion**
- **Dexmedetomidine** may also be useful
- **All have very little adverse effect on ECoG**

**Patient counseling:**
- **Should be counseled** about possibility of intraoperative awareness

**Enhancing agents (in some cases):**
- **Methohexital**
- **Etomidate**
- **Alfentanil (50 μg/kg)**
- **Purpose:** Enhance epileptiform activity and assist in mapping

**Post-ECoG management:**
- **Once ECoG complete:** General anesthetic technique used prior to ECoG can be resumed during focus resection

## 48.8. POSTOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT

**Close monitoring required:**
- **For seizure activity**

**Seizures may signify:**
- **Postoperative bleeding**
- **Metabolic derangements**
- **Hypercapnia**
- **Hypoxemia**

**Treatment approach:**
- **Treat aggressively** to avoid:
  - Cerebral damage
  - Status epilepticus

**Bottom Line:** Epilepsy surgery requires understanding of antiepileptic drug interactions, careful anesthetic planning for electrocorticography to avoid interference while preventing awareness, and vigilant postoperative seizure monitoring.

# 49. AWAKE CRANIOTOMY

## 49.1. INDICATIONS AND PURPOSE

**Also known as "awake craniotomy":**
- **Craniotomy performed with monitored anesthesia care**
- **Gaining popularity** in some institutions

**Clinical indications:**
- **Lesions lying adjacent to primary cortex** controlling:
  - Motor function
  - Speech
  - Sensory function

**Clinical advantage:**
- **Allows real-time cortical mapping:** Speech, motor, or sensory
- **Facilitates more aggressive tumor resection** while minimizing functional risk

## 49.2. PATIENT SELECTION

**Critical requirement:**
- **Motivated patient** is critical to procedure success

**Preoperative assessment:**
- **Detailed explanation of anesthetic** to ensure cooperation and allay anxiety

## 49.3. ANESTHETIC TECHNIQUES

**Two main approaches:**

**Continuous sedation technique:**
- **Patient sedated for procedure duration**

**"Asleep-awake-asleep" technique:**
- **Supraglottic airway device general anesthesia** prior to and following awake mapping
- **During awake mapping:** Airway device removed for patient communication
- **After mapping:** Airway device replaced after anesthesia induction
- **Alternative:** Most patients tolerate sedation for surgical closure

## 49.4. MONITORING AND VASCULAR ACCESS

**Standard monitoring:**
- **Arterial catheter placed**

**Sedation options:**
- **Prior to and following awake mapping:**
  - Propofol infusions
  - Remifentanil infusions
  - Dexmedetomidine infusions

**Comparative study findings:**
- **Propofol-remifentanil vs. dexmedetomidine:**
  - Similar sedation efficacy
  - Similar intraoperative brain mapping efficacy
  - **Dexmedetomidine group:** Lower respiratory depression rates

## 49.5. REGIONAL ANESTHESIA

**Selective scalp nerve block:**
- **May be performed preoperatively** (unilaterally or bilaterally)
- **Six nerves innervating scalp and dura mater:**
  - Supratrochlear
  - Supraorbital
  - Zygomaticotemporal
  - Auriculotemporal
  - Lesser occipital
  - Great occipital

**Alternative:**
- **Surgeon may perform field block**

**Safety consideration:**
- ⚠️ **Avoid local anesthetic toxicity** (particularly as surgeon infiltrates dura mater with additional local anesthetic)

## 49.6. CORTICAL MAPPING PROCESS

**During cortical mapping:**
- **Surgeons use electrical stimulation** with probe stimulator
- **Conjunction with neuropsychologic testing** to map critical cortical regions
- **Purpose:** Determine safe resection margins (particularly near major language areas in cortex, usually left hemisphere)

## 49.7. AIRWAY MANAGEMENT CONSIDERATIONS

**Critical attention to airway:**
- **Hypoventilation and hypercapnia detrimental** to surgical exposure
- **Apnea requiring emergency intubation** may be very difficult with:
  - Patient's head turned
  - Head fixed in holder

**Surgical drape positioning:**
- **Should allow direct and constant access** to patient's face

## 49.8. POSITIONING AND COMFORT

**Patient positioning:**
- **Careful padding and positioning** accomplished before any sedation
- **Ensure patient comfort** for surgery duration

**Communication:**
- **Constant communication with patient** important for:
  - Facilitating speech or motor testing
  - Comforting anxious patient

## 49.9. INTRAOPERATIVE COMPLICATIONS

**Potential complications:**
- **Disinhibited or uncooperative patient**
- **Oversedation** prohibitive to neuropsychologic testing
- **Respiratory depression**
- **Airway obstruction**
- **Intractable brain edema**
- **Seizures**
- **Uncontrollable pain**

**Seizure management:**
- **Cold saline applied to brain surface** by surgeon
- **Small propofol bolus (20 mg)** intravenously

## 49.10. POSTOPERATIVE CARE

**Observation location:**
- **PACU or ICU** with similar considerations as any craniotomy
- **Include adequate analgesia**

## 49.11. ALTERNATIVE FOR POOR CANDIDATES

**Patients who are poor awake craniotomy candidates:**
- **Surrogate testing during general anesthesia:**
  - Motor or sensory mapping possible
  - **Language function cannot be monitored**

**Future developments:**
- **Recent advances in mapping and monitoring speech function** occurred
- **May be available as monitoring option** in future

**Bottom Line:** Awake craniotomy enables real-time functional mapping for safer resection of lesions near eloquent cortex, requiring careful patient selection, specialized anesthetic techniques, and management of unique complications while maintaining patient cooperation.

# 51. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND CLASSIFICATION

## 51.1. PUBLIC HEALTH IMPACT

**TBI is a significant public health problem:**
- **Developed and underdeveloped world impact**
- **Accounts for 15-20% of mortality** in people 5-35 years old
- **Often associated with other trauma:** Thoracic, abdominal, orthopedic injuries

## 51.2. PRIMARY VS SECONDARY INJURY

**Primary insult:**
- **Often irreversible** 
- **Direct trauma to brain tissue**

**Secondary insults (often preventable):**
- **Death often associated with secondary insults from:**
  - Hypotension
  - Hypoxemia
  - Malignantly increased ICP (due to hematoma or edema)

💡 **Management Focus:** Emergency and anesthetic management should focus on minimizing secondary insults to optimize perfusion of injured brain.

## 51.3. GLASGOW COMA SCALE STRATIFICATION

**TBI patients stratified by GCS on presentation (3-15):**
- **GCS 13-15:** Mild head injury
- **GCS 9-12:** Moderate head injury  
- **GCS <9:** Severe head injury

**Clinical correlations:**
- **GCS ≤8:** Often intubated due to high airway protection impairment risk
- **GCS ≤8:** Corresponds to high mortality

## 51.4. ICP MONITORING

**Frequently used in acute TBI patients:**
- **Can be instituted in emergency department** with EVD
- **Available treatments:**
  - Mannitol
  - Hyperventilation
  - Propofol or barbiturates

## 51.5. OPERATIVE INDICATIONS

**Operative management normally indicated for:**
- **Depressed skull fractures**
- **Dural breach**
- **Midline shift >5 mm**
- **Basal cistern compression**
- **Refractory ICP elevation**
- **Acutely expanding intracranial hemorrhage:** Subdural and epidural hematomas

**Bottom Line:** TBI management focuses on preventing secondary injury through optimal resuscitation and ICP control, with GCS stratification guiding treatment intensity.

# 52. ANESTHETIC MANAGEMENT

## 52.1. INITIAL ASSESSMENT AND ASSUMPTIONS

**Cervical spine injury:**
- **Patients with TBI assumed to have concomitant cervical spine injury**
- **Intubation considerations** must account for this

**Associated injuries:**
- **40% of patients have associated life-threatening injury**

**Assessment priorities:**
- **Detailed but rapid primary survey**
- **Focused neurologic examination**

## 52.2. RESPIRATORY MANAGEMENT

**Hypoxemia considerations:**
- **Common occurrence**
- **Treatment complicated by possible pulmonary injury**

## 52.3. HEMODYNAMIC MANAGEMENT

**Drug administration considerations:**
- **Must be tailored to avoid systemic hypotension**
- **Patients may have hypovolemia** from associated injury

**Blood pressure targets:**
- **Systolic BP maintained at:**
  - **≥100 mmHg:** Patients aged 50-69 years
  - **≥110 mmHg:** Patients aged 15-49 years or >70 years

**Resuscitation requirements:**
- **Fluid resuscitation and vasopressors** often needed to ensure adequate:
  - Systemic perfusion pressure
  - Cerebral perfusion pressure

**Hypertonic saline benefits:**
- **Evidence suggests achieving euvolemia with hypertonic saline may be beneficial**
- **Especially pediatric patients:**
  - Intravascular volume repleted
  - CPP maintained with less cerebral edema

## 52.4. METABOLIC MANAGEMENT

**Glucose control:**
- **Euglycemia should be maintained**

## 52.5. VASCULAR ACCESS AND MONITORING

**Required monitoring/access:**
- **Arterial catheter**
- **Large-bore intravenous cannulae**
- **Possibly central venous catheter**

**Laboratory preparations:**
- **Cross-matched red blood cells** and other blood products available
- **Results review:** Hemoglobin concentration and clotting function

## 52.6. AIRWAY MANAGEMENT

**Succinylcholine considerations:**
- **Controversial following acute closed head injury**
- **May raise ICP transiently**
- **Can be used if need to rapidly secure airway** and no other contraindications exist

**Nasal intubation:**
- ⚠️ **Contraindicated if basilar skull fracture present or presumed**

## 52.7. ANESTHETIC MAINTENANCE

**Choice depends on understanding ICP management**

**Available interventions:**
- **Mannitol or hypertonic saline** may be required for edema control
- **Hyperventilation considerations:**
  - **Should not continue beyond 2-6 hours** (effect may not be durable)
  - **After acute phase:** PaCO2 of 30-35 mmHg desirable

## 52.8. CEREBRAL PERFUSION PRESSURE MANAGEMENT

**Critical for TBI management:**
- **CPP kept above 60 mmHg** (perhaps above 70 mmHg) to maintain cerebral perfusion
- **Avoid excessive hypertension:** Reduce risk of exacerbating edema or increasing hemorrhage risk

## 52.9. COAGULOPATHY MANAGEMENT

**Brain tissue thromboplastin release:**
- **May lead to disseminated intravascular coagulation**
- **Coagulopathy must be aggressively sought and treated**

## 52.10. RESPIRATORY COMPLICATIONS

**ARDS or neurogenic pulmonary edema:**
- **May be present**
- **Lung-protective ventilation strategy needed:**
  - PEEP utilization
  - Low tidal volumes
  - Maintain oxygenation

## 52.11. INTRAOPERATIVE AND POSTOPERATIVE COMPLICATIONS

**Potential complications:**
- **Seizures**
- **Intracranial hemorrhage**
- **Sudden and profound hypotension** following decompression of large hematomas
- **Cerebral edema**

**Cerebral edema treatment:**
- **Propofol**
- **Mannitol and/or hypertonic saline**
- **Temporary hyperventilation**
- **CSF diversion**
- **Possibly avoiding bone flap replacement** at surgery conclusion

## 52.12. EXTUBATION CONSIDERATIONS

**Extubation at surgery conclusion depends on:**
- **Degree of ICP elevation**
- **Severity of injury**
- **Concomitant injuries**

**Postoperative management:**
- **Most patients admitted to neurointensive care unit**
- **Require continued:**
  - Intubation
  - Mechanical ventilation
  - Sedation

**Bottom Line:** TBI anesthetic management requires assuming cervical spine injury, maintaining adequate perfusion pressures, managing associated injuries, and preparing for multiple potential complications while focusing on secondary injury prevention.

# 54. SPINAL CORD INJURY

## 54.1. INJURY PATTERNS AND SEVERITY

**Spinal canal anatomy and injury vulnerability:**
- **Mid-thoracic region:** Spinal canal is narrowest
- **Clinical significance:** Unstable injuries in mid-thoracic region can easily impact cord
- **Outcome:** Higher chance of complete cord injury in mid-thoracic trauma

**Cervical spine injuries:**
- **Most common** (most mobile spine part)
- **Most devastating** from neurologic perspective
- **More functional deficits than lower injuries:**
  - Motor deficits
  - Sensory deficits
  - Autonomic deficits

## 54.2. RESPIRATORY IMPACT

**Impact on respiratory muscle function:**
- **Cervical injuries necessitate extremely careful airway management**
- **Higher cord injuries impact respiratory function more:**
  - **Intercostal function:** Impacted by thoracic and cervical injuries
  - **Phrenic function:** Impaired by lesions at C5 or higher

**Functional consequences:**
- **C4 level injury:** Voluntary respiratory effort remains but vital capacity may be diminished by up to 25%

## 54.3. AUTONOMIC EFFECTS

**Higher thoracic and cervical cord injuries:**
- **Sympathetic nervous system function usually impaired**
- **Cardiac accelerator nerve function** (T1-T5 segments, particularly T1-T4) impairment → profound hypotension and bradycardia
- **Injuries above T6:** Particularly at risk for autonomic instability and dysreflexia

**Temperature control:**
- **Lost with SCI** due to:
  - Loss of sweating function (impaired heat dissipation)
  - Cutaneous vasodilation from sympathetic function loss (heat loss)
  - Compromised ability to shiver or vasoconstrict (heat conservation)
- **Clinical implications:** Patients prone to hypothermia in acute setting, require active warming

## 54.4. ASIA CLASSIFICATION SYSTEM

**American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) Impairment Scale:**

| ASIA Class | Deficit | Description |
|-----------|---------|-------------|
| A | Complete | No motor or sensory function |
| B | Incomplete | Sensory but no motor function below neurologic level including S4-5 |
| C | Incomplete | Motor function preserved below neurologic level + ≥50% key muscles have severe weakness |
| D | Incomplete | Motor function preserved below neurologic level + ≥50% key muscles have mild weakness |
| E | None/Intact | Normal motor and sensory function |

**Prognostic value:**
- **ASIA score at 72 hours post-injury** is the most sensitive predictor of long-term prognosis
- **Assessment requirements:** Awake, cooperative patient capable of following commands
- **Limitations:** May be difficult to assess accurately in:
  - Sedated or intubated patients
  - Patients with severe polytrauma
  - Those with altered mental status

## 54.5. ACUTE VS CHRONIC SPINAL CORD INJURY

**Following acute spinal cord injury:**
- **Spinal cord autoregulation impaired**
- **Both neurogenic shock and spinal shock phenomena may be present** (distinct entities)

### Neurogenic Shock
**Definition:** Interruption of autonomic pathways in injuries above T6 level
- **Mechanism:** Loss of sympathetic outflow (including cardiac sympathetics T1-T4) with intact vagal activity
- **Clinical features:**
  - Hypotension from loss of vasomotor tone
  - Peripheral vasodilation
  - Bradycardia from unopposed vagal activity
  - Hypothermia from impaired thermoregulation
- **Duration:** Variable, typically hours to weeks

### Spinal Shock
**Definition:** Loss of all reflexes below the level of spinal cord injury
- **Onset:** Usually develops 4-6 weeks post-injury (may present as early as 4 days)
- **Duration:** Lasts 24 hours to several weeks
- **Clinical features:**
  - Sensory-motor deficits below injury level
  - Flaccid paralysis
  - Areflexia
- **Phases of recovery:**
  - **Days 0-1:** Flaccid areflexia
  - **Days 1-3:** Gradual return of reflexes
  - **Days 4+:** Development of spasticity and autonomic hyperreflexia
- **After initial period:** Spasticity of affected muscles tends to occur
- **Important distinction:** Recovery from spinal shock does not indicate neurologic recovery

## 54.6. HYPOTENSION CAUSES

**Contributors to hypotension:**
- **Autonomic impairment**
- **Hypovolemia associated with other injuries:**
  - Long bone fractures
  - Intra-abdominal bleeding

## 54.7. PRIMARY VS SECONDARY INJURY

**Primary injury mechanisms:**
- **Direct mechanical trauma to spinal cord:**
  - Stretching of spinal cord or blood supply
  - Compression of spinal cord or blood supply
  - Direct cord hemorrhage
  - Traction injury
  - Transection of spinal cord or blood supply
  - Axial loading forces

**Causes of primary injury:**
- **Hyperflexion**
- **Hyperextension**
- **Subluxation of vertebral elements**
- **Impingement of fractured spinal column**
- **Retropulsion of bone or disc fragments** into spinal canal
- **Penetrating injuries:** Stab wounds, gunshot injuries

**Secondary injury:**
- **Onset:** Begins within minutes after primary injury
- **More complicated biochemical phenomenon:**
  - Spinal cord hemorrhage progression
  - Loss of autoregulation
  - Neurogenic shock
  - Inflammatory mediator release
  - Cytokine release
  - Excitatory amino acid release
  - → Worsened edema and ischemia
  - → Cellular edema, apoptosis, free radical formation
- **Clinical manifestation:** Ascending level of spinal cord injury → clinical deterioration
- **Therapeutic implications:** Need for anti-inflammatory interventions and aggressive prevention strategies

## 54.8. SECONDARY INJURY PREVENTION

**Paramount importance:** Mitigating secondary insults by avoiding:
- **Hypoxemia**
- **Hypotension**
- **Worsening cord edema**

**Comorbid injuries common:**
- **Traumatic brain injury**
- **Nonvertebral fractures**
- **Facial injuries**
- **Chest or abdominal injuries:** Including pneumothorax and pulmonary contusion
- **Traumatic amputations**

**Clinical imperative:**
- **Life-threatening injuries must be addressed** while ensuring spinal alignment maintained to avoid secondary injury

## 54.9. CHRONIC SPINAL CORD INJURY PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

**Chronic SCI is characterized by multi-system complications:**

### Cardiovascular Complications
- **Autonomic dysreflexia:** Episodes of severe hypertension in response to stimuli below injury level (injuries above T6)
- **Cardiac arrhythmias**
- **Cardiovascular risk factors:** Obesity, metabolic syndrome
- **Reduced blood volume:** Chronic relative hypovolemia
- **Anemia of chronic disease**
- **Postural hypotension:** Impaired baroreceptor reflexes
- **Altered baroreceptor reflex function**
- **Difficult intravenous access:** From previous cannulation, muscle atrophy

### Respiratory Complications
- **Impaired respiratory mechanics:** Paralysis of intercostal and/or abdominal muscles
- **Need for ventilatory support:** Some patients require long-term mechanical ventilation
- **Blunted CO₂ response:** Reduced ventilatory drive
- **Ventilator dependence:** Especially high cervical injuries
- **Recurrent pneumonias:** Weak cough, impaired secretion clearance
- **Atelectasis:** Poor lung expansion
- **Thromboembolism:** Increased VTE risk
- **Temperature regulation issues:** Inability to increase ventilation for heat dissipation

### Neuromuscular and Musculoskeletal
- **Spasticity:** Involuntary muscle contractions
- **Contractures:** Limited joint mobility from chronic spasticity
- **Extrajunctional acetylcholine receptors:** Upregulation increases succinylcholine risk (hyperkalemia)
- **Spinal fixation hardware:** May complicate regional anesthesia
- **Osteoporosis:** Increased fracture risk from immobility
- **Pressure ulcers:** Chronic skin breakdown from immobility
- **Chronic pain syndromes:** Neuropathic and nociceptive pain
- **Psychological complications:** Depression, anxiety, PTSD

### Gastrointestinal Complications
- **Stress ulcers:** Increased gastric acid secretion from unopposed vagal activity
- **Delayed gastric emptying:** Gastroparesis
- **Gastric retention:** Increased aspiration risk
- **Increased anal sphincter tone**
- **Constipation:** Common and severe
- **Overflow incontinence:** From chronic retention
- **Gallstones:** Increased incidence

### Renal and Genitourinary Complications
- **Neurogenic bladder:** Loss of voluntary bladder control
- **Detrusor sphincter dyssynergia:** Uncoordinated bladder function
- **Chronic renal impairment:** From recurrent infections and obstruction
- **Nosocomial bacterial colonization:** Chronic catheterization
- **Recurrent urinary tract infections**
- **Hydronephrosis:** From chronic obstruction

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Chronic SCI patients have unique anesthetic challenges requiring careful preoperative assessment of all organ systems, not just the neurologic injury itself.

**Bottom Line:** Spinal cord injury severity correlates with neurologic level, with cervical injuries being most devastating due to respiratory and autonomic effects, requiring prevention of secondary injury through optimal physiologic support. Chronic SCI results in progressive multi-system complications requiring comprehensive perioperative management.

# 55. INITIAL MANAGEMENT

## 55.1. IMMEDIATE ASSESSMENT

**Patients with acute spinal cord injury may be compromised:**
- **Ventilatory standpoint** (depending on injury level and extent)
- **Hemodynamic standpoint** (depending on injury level and extent)

**Critical priority:**
- **Immediate control of these parameters crucial**

### 55.1.1. ATLS Approach to Acute Spinal Cord Injury

**Systematic approach essential:**
- **ABC + ATLS protocol** (polytrauma is common in spinal cord injury)
- **Spinal immobilization:**
  - Head blocks and spinal board preferred over log rolling
  - Maintain alignment until cleared or stabilized
  - ⚠️ **Remove spinal board as soon as possible** to prevent pressure sores

**Additional considerations in acute SCI:**
- **Difficult airway management:**
  - Full stomach risk (gastroparesis)
  - C-spine injury limiting neck extension
  - Uncooperative patient (intoxication, pain, TBI)
- **Hemodynamic instability:**
  - May have both neurogenic shock AND hypovolemic shock
  - Distinguish between causes to guide treatment
- **Hypothermia:** Impaired thermoregulation
- **Coagulopathy:** From trauma
- **Acidosis:** From hypoperfusion
- **Intoxication:** Substance use common in trauma

**Balance of priorities:**
- **Prevent secondary spinal cord injury** while addressing life-threatening injuries
- **Maintain spinal cord perfusion pressure** (MAP >85-90 mmHg)
- **Avoid hypoxia, hypercarbia, hyper/hypoglycemia**
- **Risk vs benefit of imaging:** Balance need for spinal imaging with patient stability (unstable patients, ventilated patients, those on vasopressors/inotropes)

## 55.2. NEUROLOGIC EXAMINATION

**Detailed neurologic examination critical to:**
- **Establish operative plan**
- **Subsequent prognostication**

## 55.3. AIRWAY MANAGEMENT

**Oxygen administration priority:**
- **Administer O₂ immediately** to prevent secondary cord damage from hypoxia
- **Low threshold for intubation,** especially in high cervical spine injuries

**Cervical spinal injury focus:**
- **Maintaining in-line stabilization (MILS)** throughout airway manipulation
- **MILS technique:**
  - Requires removal of hard collar and head blocks
  - Manual stabilization by trained assistant
  - Maintain neutral alignment, avoid flexion/extension
- **May require use of:**
  - Fiberoptic intubation
  - Other adjuncts to secure airway
  - **KIV awake fiberoptic intubation (AFOI)** for high-risk cases

**Aspiration risk considerations:**
- **High aspiration risk due to:**
  - Paralytic ileus from spinal cord injury
  - Loss of lower esophageal sphincter (LES) tone
  - Full stomach assumption in trauma
- **Rapid sequence induction (RSI) preferred**
- **Caution with cricoid pressure:** May cause spinal movement

**Potentially difficult airway scenarios:**
- **Maxillofacial trauma**
- **Blood or vomit in airway**
- **Airway edema** from direct trauma
- **Patient on halo traction**
- **Cervical spine instability limiting positioning**

**Equipment preparation:**
- **Difficult airway equipment readily available**
- **Fiberoptic bronchoscope (FOB)**
- **KIV awake fiberoptic intubation (AFOI)**
- **No single technique superior:** Use familiar techniques, avoid hypoxia

**Hemodynamic considerations:**
- ⚠️ **Monitor for hemodynamic swings during induction and intubation**
- **Vagal stimulation from laryngoscopy/suctioning** may cause severe bradycardia or asystole
- **KIV anticholinergic premedication:** Atropine or glycopyrrolate

**Stable patient considerations:**
- **Radiographic studies helpful** in assessing cervical injury degree and intubation options
- ⚠️ **Plain x-ray imaging not sensitive** for ligamentous injury → underestimates risk

**Spinal cord compression setting:**
- **Awake fiberoptic intubation may be preferred**
- **Allows neurologic examination immediately post-intubation**

## 55.4. SUCCINYLCHOLINE SAFETY

**Time-dependent safety window:**
- ✅ **SAFE:** Within first 24-72 hours following spinal cord injury
- ⚠️ **CONTRAINDICATED:** From approximately 72 hours to 6 months post-injury
- ✅ **Likely safe again:** After 6 months (receptor normalization)

**Rationale for contraindication:**
- **Extrajunctional acetylcholine receptor upregulation** begins after 72 hours
- **Life-threatening hyperkalemia** may result from succinylcholine use
- **New junctional and extrajunctional nicotinic receptors** massively increase K⁺ release

**Acute phase RSI considerations:**
- **RSI with in-line stabilization thought to be safe acutely**
- **Use succinylcholine within first 72 hours if indicated**
- **After 72 hours:** Use alternative non-depolarizing neuromuscular blocking agents

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The hyperkalemic response to succinylcholine is one of the most dangerous complications in subacute SCI patients, making careful timing of injury critical to drug selection.

### 55.4.1. Respiratory Management

**Hypoventilation is common in acute SCI:**

**Causes of respiratory compromise:**
- **Rib fractures** from trauma
- **Traumatic brain injury** affecting respiratory drive
- **Pain** limiting chest wall expansion
- **Pneumothorax or hemothorax**
- **Neurological disruption to muscles of ventilation:**
  - Intercostal muscle paralysis (thoracic injuries)
  - Diaphragmatic paralysis (C5 or higher injuries)
  - Abdominal muscle paralysis

**Monitoring requirements:**
- **Monitor in higher acuity setting** (ICU or high-dependency unit)
- ⚠️ **Patients may deteriorate in first 72 hours post-injury**
- **Serial respiratory assessments:**
  - Vital capacity measurements
  - Arterial blood gases
  - Work of breathing assessment

**Positioning considerations:**
- ⚠️ **KEEP SUPINE for diaphragm to have greater excursion** in inspiration
- **DO NOT sit acute high-thoracic SCI patients up:**
  - They breathe better lying flat
  - Sitting upright reduces diaphragmatic efficiency
  - Also at risk of cardiovascular instability from neurogenic shock when upright

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Unlike chronic SCI patients with autonomic dysreflexia (who benefit from sitting up), acute high-thoracic SCI patients should remain supine for optimal respiratory mechanics and hemodynamic stability.

## 55.5. HEMODYNAMIC SUPPORT

**Support requirements:**
- **Intravenous fluids and blood products**
- **Vasopressors and inotropes** often required to support blood pressure
- **May be fluid-resistant** requiring vasopressor support

**Rationale:**
- **Hypotension and anemia** can contribute to secondary spinal cord injury

**Vagal stimulation risks:**
- ⚠️ **Beware of vagally stimulating procedures:**
  - Laryngoscopy
  - Suctioning
  - Other airway manipulation
- **Risk of severe bradycardia or asystole** from unopposed vagal activity
- **Mitigation strategies:**
  - **KIV atropine or glycopyrrolate** premedication
  - **Consider external or pharmacological pacing** availability

**Acute pulmonary edema risk:**
- ⚠️ **Beware of acute pulmonary edema (APO)**
- **Mechanism:** Pulmonary capillary integrity may be impaired from catecholamine surge during initial injury
- **Management:** Judicious fluid resuscitation, monitor for signs of pulmonary edema

**Thermoregulation:**
- **Keep patient warm:** Patients are vasodilated and prone to hypothermia
- **Active warming measures:**
  - Forced air warming devices
  - Warmed intravenous fluids
  - Increased ambient temperature

## 55.6. MONITORING AND VASCULAR ACCESS

**Required monitoring:**
- **Arterial blood pressure monitoring** (invasive arterial line)
- **Continuous ECG monitoring** (risk of bradyarrhythmias)
- **Temperature monitoring** (impaired thermoregulation)

**Vascular access:**
- **Large-bore intravenous access** (preferably central venous access)
- **Invasive monitors indicated if neurogenic shock present**

**Urinary catheterization:**
- **Indwelling urinary catheter (IDC) should be placed early:**
  - Marker of renal perfusion and resuscitation adequacy
  - Avoids bladder overdistension (which acts as a vagal stimulus)
  - Monitors urine output for goal-directed therapy

## 55.7. BLOOD PRESSURE TARGETS

**American Association of Neurological Surgeons (AANS) Guidelines:**
- ⚠️ **Avoid systolic BP <90 mmHg** at all costs
- **Target MAP >85-90 mmHg** to ensure adequate spinal cord perfusion
- **Duration:** Maintain for first 5-7 days post-injury
- **Goal:** Optimize spinal cord perfusion pressure and prevent secondary injury

**Clinical approach:**
- **Early aggressive blood pressure support**
- **Invasive arterial monitoring recommended**
- **Vasopressor support often required** (neurogenic shock may be fluid-resistant)

## 55.8. CONTROVERSIAL NEUROPROTECTIVE STRATEGIES

**Evidence-based neuroprotection (PROVEN):**
- ✅ **Avoid hypoxia:** Maintain adequate oxygenation
- ✅ **Avoid hypotension:** Aggressive MAP support (>85-90 mmHg)
- ✅ **Avoid hypercarbia:** Maintain normocarbia
- ✅ **Avoid hyper/hypoglycemia:** Maintain euglycemia

**Strategies receiving attention but lacking convincing human data:**

**Therapeutic hypothermia:**
- ❌ **Not currently recommended**
- Insufficient evidence for benefit
- May increase complications

**Steroids:**
- ❌ **Not routine due to concerns over mortality and sepsis**
- See Section 55.9 for detailed discussion

**Naloxone:**
- Insufficient evidence for routine use

**Early decompression and stabilization:**
- **May reduce length of hospital stay**
- **May reduce days of mechanical ventilation**
- **Does NOT improve neurologic injury level**
- **Decision must balance:**
  - Potential benefits of early surgery
  - Risk of transporting unstable patient to OR
  - Risk of imaging studies in ventilated, vasopressor-dependent patients

## 55.9. CORTICOSTEROID CONTROVERSY

**Corticosteroids have received particular attention:**

**Potential neuroprotective mechanisms:**
- **Reduction of vasogenic edema**
- **Improvement of spinal cord perfusion**
- **Anti-inflammatory effects**
- **Free radical scavenging**

**National Acute Spinal Cord Injury Studies (NASCIS) I-III:**
- **Demonstrated early steroid intervention** may improve neurologic outcome in SCI patients
- ⚠️ **Studies highly suspect:**
  - Results not reproducible
  - Populations skewed
  - Survival or quality of life did not improve with intervention (methylprednisolone)

**Corticosteroid complications:**
- **Predisposition to infection**
- **Glucose dysregulation**
- **Must be weighed against potential benefits**

**Guideline evolution:**
- **2013 Guidelines:** Endorsed by American Association of Neurological Surgeons and Congress of Neurological Surgeons → did not recommend corticosteroid use
- **2017 Guidelines:** Recommend high-dose methylprednisolone for 24 hours be offered in acute spinal cord injury for patients presenting within 8 hours of injury

## 55.10. ADDITIONAL MANAGEMENT CONSIDERATIONS

**Comprehensive acute SCI care requires attention to multiple systems:**

### Thromboprophylaxis
**Rationale:**
- **High VTE risk:** Immobility combined with increased thrombogenicity from trauma

**Approach:**
- **Mechanical prophylaxis:**
  - Sequential compression devices
  - Thromboembolism-deterrent stockings
- **Pharmacological prophylaxis:**
  - **Prophylactic LMWH** (low molecular weight heparin)
  - ⚠️ **Start after 72 hours post-injury** due to initial bleeding risk
- **IVC filter:** Consider if anticoagulation contraindicated

### Gut Protection
**Pathophysiology:**
- **Unopposed vagal activity** increases gastric acid secretion
- **Risk of peptic ulcer disease (PUD)**

**Management:**
- **Prophylactic acid suppression:**
  - H₂ antagonists OR
  - Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs)
- **Duration:** Minimum 6 weeks post-injury

### Nutrition and Glycemic Control
**Gastroparesis considerations:**
- **Unopposed vagal activity** → gastroparesis
- **Complications:**
  - Nausea and vomiting
  - Aspiration risk
  - Abdominal distension impairing respiration

**Nutritional support:**
- **Early feeding within 24 hours** decreases mortality in polytrauma patients
- **Rationale:** SCI patients are in hypermetabolic state
- **Route:** Consider post-pyloric feeding if gastroparesis severe

**Glycemic control:**
- **Maintain euglycemia**
- **Avoid hyperglycemia:** Worsens secondary neurologic injury
- **Monitor closely:** Especially if steroids administered

### Pressure Sore Prevention
**High risk in SCI patients:**

**Prevention strategies:**
- **Appropriate pressure-relieving mattresses**
- **Good nursing care with meticulous skin inspection**
- **Regular repositioning** (every 2 hours minimum)
- ⚠️ **Remove spinal board as soon as possible** after spinal clearance or stabilization
- **Early spinal fixation** allows mobilization and reduces pressure injury risk
- **Nutritional optimization**
- **Keep skin clean and dry**

### Thermoregulation
**Impaired temperature control mechanisms:**
- **Vasodilation** below injury level
- **Compromised ability to sweat**
- **Loss of shivering response** below injury level

**Management:**
- **Active warming in acute phase**
- **Monitor core temperature**
- **Warm ambient environment**
- **Warmed IV fluids**

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Comprehensive SCI care addresses all body systems, not just the spinal cord injury itself. Preventing avoidable complications (pressure sores, VTE, stress ulcers) significantly impacts long-term outcomes.

**Bottom Line:** Initial SCI management requires immediate ventilatory and hemodynamic support, careful airway management with spine stabilization, and MAP maintenance above 85-90 mmHg, with corticosteroid use remaining controversial. Comprehensive care must address thromboprophylaxis, gastroprotection, nutrition, pressure area care, and thermoregulation to prevent avoidable secondary complications.

# 56. INTRAOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT

## 56.1. ANESTHETIC TECHNIQUE CONSIDERATIONS

**Choice of maintenance anesthesia technique should focus on:**
- **Maintaining blood pressure**
- **Allowing for intraoperative neuromonitoring**

## 56.2. COMPLEX SPINE SURGERY CONSIDERATIONS

**Often involves:**
- **Multiple level fusions**
- **Osteotomies**

**Should account for:**
- **Real possibility of significant, sometimes profound, surgical bleeding**
- **Need for postoperative mechanical ventilation** following massive transfusion

## 56.3. POSITIONING MANAGEMENT

**Lengthy and extensive surgeries:**
- **Great care must be paid to surgical prone positioning**
- **Recognition:** Potential for hypotension with position change to prone
- **Proactive management** necessary

**Abdominal compression considerations:**
- **Allowing abdomen to be free from compression** during prone positioning important
- **Benefits:**
  - Decrease abdominal pressure
  - Decrease epidural venous plexus pressure
  - Decrease blood loss during thoracolumbar surgery

## 56.4. HEAD FIXATION

**Depending on spinal levels and surgery extent:**
- **Patient may be placed in head-fixation device**
- **Adequate intravascular access important** to establish before prone positioning

## 56.5. LABORATORY MONITORING

**Frequent measurements required:**
- **Arterial blood gas**
- **Coagulation parameters**
- **Hemoglobin concentration**
- **Platelet counts**

**Clinical significance:**
- **Coagulopathy and anemia quite common**
- **Need rapid correction** as can contribute to secondary injury

## 56.6. SURGICAL COMMUNICATION

**Close communication with surgeon important:**
- **Large operations may benefit from:**
  - Early closure
  - Staging of procedure

## 56.7. BLOOD CONSERVATION STRATEGIES

**Antifibrinolytics:**
- **Tranexamic acid:** Can decrease bleeding risk with minimal (if any) increased thrombotic complication risk

**Intraoperative cell salvage:**
- **Quite helpful** in reducing total allogeneic blood transfusion
- **Appropriate for:** Noninfectious, nontumor cases

**Techniques largely fallen out of favor:**
- **Acute normovolemic hemodilution**
- **Deliberate hypotension**
- **Reason:** Known harmful effects of anemia and hypotension on neurologic and cardiovascular systems

## 56.8. PAIN CONTROL PLANNING

**Postoperative pain control:**
- **Particularly challenging** in complex spine surgery patients

**Intraoperative methadone:**
- **Single bolus dose** shown to:
  - Reduce postoperative analgesic requirements
  - Improve pain scores
  - Provide analgesic benefit lasting months

**Multimodal management emphasis:**
- **Including less traditional methods:**
  - Regional blocks
  - Showing promising results

## 56.9. CHRONIC SCI ANESTHETIC CONSIDERATIONS

**Anesthetic management for patients with chronic spinal cord injury requires special considerations:**

### Preoperative Assessment

**Comprehensive history and examination:**
- **Level and completeness of injury:**
  - Document neurologic level
  - Complete vs incomplete injury
  - ASIA classification if known
- **Time since injury:**
  - Critical for succinylcholine safety (contraindicated 72h-6 months post-injury)
- **Autonomic dysreflexia history:**
  - Previous episodes and triggers
  - Frequency and severity of episodes
  - Typical presenting symptoms
  - Previous management strategies
- **Muscle spasms and contractures:**
  - May complicate positioning
  - May increase surgical complexity
  - May require adjustment of drug dosing

**Respiratory assessment:**
- **Baseline ventilatory function:**
  - Home NIV (non-invasive ventilation) use
  - Obstructive sleep apnea history
  - Recent community-acquired pneumonia
  - Previous ICU admissions for respiratory failure
- **Pulmonary function tests** if available
- **Baseline oxygen requirements**

**Airway assessment:**
- **Previous cervical spine operations:**
  - May limit neck mobility
  - Previous difficult intubation history
- **Tracheal stenosis:** From previous tracheostomy
- **Difficult airway anticipated:** Plan accordingly

**Other considerations:**
- **Pressure areas:** Inspect skin carefully, avoid further injury
- **Cardiovascular status:** Autonomic dysreflexia, postural hypotension, arrhythmias
- **Renal function:** Chronic renal impairment from neurogenic bladder
- **Current medications:** Antispasmodics, analgesics, autonomic medications

### Anesthetic Technique Selection

**Choice depends on multiple factors:**
- **Patient preference**
- **Level and completeness of lesion relative to surgical site**
- **Previous anesthetic experience and management**
- **Presence and severity of autonomic dysreflexia**
- **Degree of spasticity**

### Monitored Anesthesia Care (MAC)

**Indications:**
- Operations below the level of neurologic injury
- Incomplete lesions with some sensation
- Chronic pain syndromes

**Advantages:**
- Avoids airway manipulation
- Patient can report symptoms of autonomic dysreflexia

**Cautions:**
- ⚠️ **Sedation may mask symptoms of autonomic dysreflexia**
- ⚠️ **May cause ventilatory depression** in patients with marginal respiratory function
- **Patients must be cooperative and agreeable**
- **Must be able to report symptoms immediately**

### Regional Anesthesia

**Spinal anesthesia:**
- **Safe and effective** for chronic SCI patients
- **Advantages:**
  - Abolishes autonomic dysreflexia
  - Reduces muscle spasms
  - Provides excellent postoperative analgesia

**Challenges:**
- **Difficult to site:**
  - Poor positioning from contractures
  - Previous spinal instrumentation
  - Bony deformities
  - Calcified ligaments
- **Difficult to assess:**
  - Cannot assess sensory level in areas without baseline sensation
  - ⚠️ **Beware of total spinal** - may be difficult to detect
  - Nerve stimulators may be unreliable

**Epidural anesthesia:**
- Similar considerations to spinal
- May be easier to titrate
- Same challenges with placement and assessment

### General Anesthesia

**Indications:**
- Surgery above level of injury
- Patient preference
- Contraindications to regional techniques

**Specific considerations:**

**Autonomic dysreflexia management:**
- **Need to actively prevent and manage AD**
- **Risk of malignant hypertension** with surgical stimuli below injury level
- **Deep general anesthesia** usually prevents AD
- **Have antihypertensive medications ready:**
  - GTN (glyceryl trinitrate)
  - Short-acting vasodilators
  - Labetalol, hydralazine

**Hemodynamic management:**
- ⚠️ **Prone to post-induction hypotension:**
  - Loss of sympathetic tone
  - Reduced intravascular volume
  - Altered baroreceptor reflexes
- **Optimize preload before induction**
- **Vasopressors readily available**

**Airway management:**
- **May be difficult:** Previous C-spine surgery, limited mobility
- **Aspiration risk:** Gastroparesis
- **KIV awake fiberoptic intubation (AFOI)**
- **Caution with succinylcholine** (see timeframe considerations in Section 55.4)

**Postoperative considerations:**
- ⚠️ **Beware of postoperative hypoventilation:**
  - Baseline respiratory compromise
  - Opioid sensitivity
  - Residual neuromuscular blockade
- **May require prolonged ventilatory support**
- **Plan for higher level of postoperative care**

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Anesthetic technique for chronic SCI patients must be individualized based on injury level, completeness, time since injury, and risk of autonomic dysreflexia. No single technique is universally superior - choose based on patient factors and institutional expertise.

**Bottom Line:** Intraoperative management focuses on blood pressure maintenance, careful positioning, aggressive monitoring and correction of coagulopathy/anemia, and multimodal pain management planning. Chronic SCI patients require specialized anesthetic planning accounting for autonomic dysreflexia risk, respiratory compromise, difficult airway potential, and technique-specific challenges.

# 57. COMPLICATIONS OF SPINE SURGERY

## 57.1. GENERAL RISK PROFILE

**Complications specifically related to anesthesia for spine surgery:**
- **Fortunately rare**
- ⚠️ **Often devastating when they occur**

## 57.2. POSTOPERATIVE VISUAL LOSS (POVL)

**Incidence:**
- **0.088 to 1.2 per 1,000** after spine surgery

**Etiology breakdown:**
- **Most cases:** Posterior ischemic optic neuropathy
- **Less common:** Central retinal artery occlusion and cortical blindness

**Risk factors:**
- **Male sex**
- **Obesity**
- **Use of Wilson frame**
- **Longer surgery duration**
- **High estimated blood loss**

**Protective factor:**
- **Use of colloid** may be protective

**Management:**
- **ASA Practice Advisory:** Recommends informing high-risk patients preoperatively about POVL risk
- **Ophthalmologic consultation** immediately undertaken if suspected

## 57.3. ANTERIOR SPINAL ARTERY SYNDROME

**Pathophysiology:**
- **Caused by sustained hypoperfusion** of anterior spinal artery
- **Due to:** Surgical distraction or hypotension
- **Results in:** Motor weakness

**Detection:**
- **MEPs may be helpful** in detecting and averting this devastating complication

## 57.4. THROMBOEMBOLIC COMPLICATIONS

**Predisposing factors:**
- **Deliberate hypotension**
- **Hypothermia**
- **Hypovolemia**

**Incidence:**
- **Lumbar fusion associated with:**
  - **Symptomatic DVT:** Up to 4%
  - **PE:** 2%

**Prevention challenges:**
- **Prophylaxis with anticoagulant often impossible** prior to spine surgery
- **Fear of:** Worsening blood loss and epidural hematoma formation

**High-risk patient management:**
- **Inferior vena cava filter consideration** prior to surgery

## 57.5. AUTONOMIC DYSREFLEXIA (AD)

⚠️ **MEDICAL EMERGENCY/URGENCY**

**Not a surgical complication but important late SCI complication:**
- **Occurs weeks to months** after complete SCI
- **60-80% of SCI patients with injuries above T6** affected
- **Physiologic derangement** in injuries above T6 level

**Pathophysiology:**
- **Inappropriate autonomic response to stimuli below level of injury**
- **Disruption of descending inhibitory tracts** with intact sympathetic reflex arcs below injury level
- **Results in:**
  - Episodes of malignant hypertension/sympathetic activity below lesion
  - Compensatory parasympathetic response above lesion

**Triggering stimuli:**
- **Even minor stimuli below injury level can trigger AD:**
  - **Bladder distension** (most common trigger)
  - **Constipation or fecal impaction**
  - **Pressure sores**
  - Hollow organ distention
  - Pain or noxious stimuli
  - Surgical procedures

**Clinical manifestations:**
- **Characterized by:**
  - **Headache** (severe, pounding)
  - **Flushing, pallor, and sweating** above the level of the lesion
  - **Profuse sweating** (especially face and neck)
  - Piloerection (goosebumps) above lesion
- **Most common:** Profound systemic hypertension (malignant HTN)
- **Additional symptoms:**
  - Often bradycardia (compensatory)
  - Cardiac dysrhythmias
  - Cardiac dysfunction
  - Anxiety, sense of impending doom

**Complications of untreated AD:**
- ⚠️ **Hypertensive emergency:**
  - **Stroke** (intracerebral hemorrhage)
  - **Seizures**
  - **Severe headache**
  - Myocardial infarction
  - Pulmonary edema
  - Retinal hemorrhage
  - Death

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The triad of severe hypertension, headache, and sweating above the injury level in a chronic SCI patient should immediately trigger consideration of autonomic dysreflexia.

## 57.6. AD PREVENTION AND MANAGEMENT

**Primary goal:** Prevention
- **Minimize triggers:** Appropriate bowel and bladder care
- **Anesthesia should still be used** for painful or stimulating procedures in insensate regions
- **Adequate anesthetic depth** prevents AD during surgery

**Immediate management of acute AD episode:**

**Step 1: Remove the stimulus**
- **Identify and eliminate trigger:**
  - Check for bladder distension → catheterize or irrigate catheter
  - Check for fecal impaction → gentle disimpaction
  - Check surgical stimulation → request pause or deepen anesthesia
  - Remove tight clothing or restraints
  - Reposition to relieve pressure areas

**Step 2: Positional management**
- **Sit patient up** (elevate head of bed to 90°)
- **Benefit:** Reduces cerebral perfusion pressure and promotes venous pooling in lower extremities
- ⚠️ **CRITICAL EXCEPTION:** Do NOT sit up acute high-thoracic SCI patients (see Section 55.4.1)
  - Acute SCI patients breathe better lying flat
  - Risk of cardiovascular instability from neurogenic shock when upright
  - **This guideline applies ONLY to chronic SCI with established AD**

**Step 3: Pharmacological management**
- **Administer short-acting vasodilators if hypertension persists:**
  - **GTN (glyceryl trinitrate):** First-line, rapid onset
  - **Nitrates:** Sublingual or spray
  - **Calcium-channel blockers:** Nifedipine
  - **Hydralazine:** Effective but slower onset
  - **Alpha-blockers:** Prazosin, terazosin
  - **Other agents:** Labetalol, sodium nitroprusside (ICU setting)

**Step 4: Ongoing monitoring**
- **Continuous BP monitoring**
- **ECG monitoring**
- **Watch for rebound hypotension** after stimulus removed
- **May need to lighten anesthesia once AD controlled**

**Intraoperative prevention:**
- **General anesthesia:** Usually prevents AD if adequate depth
- **Regional anesthesia:** Spinal or epidural effective in blocking afferent stimulus
- **Ensure adequate anesthetic level** before surgical stimulation

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The key to AD management is rapid identification and removal of the triggering stimulus. Pharmacological treatment is adjunctive - if the stimulus persists, medications alone may not control the hypertension.

## 57.7. ANESTHETIC TECHNIQUE CONSIDERATIONS

**Technique selection depends on:**
- **Patient preference**
- **Level and completeness of lesion relative to operation site**
- **Previous anesthetic management experience**
- **Presence and severity of autonomic dysreflexia**
- **Degree of spasticity**

**Monitored Anesthesia Care (MAC):**
- **Appropriate for:**
  - Operations below the lesion level
  - Incomplete lesions with chronic pain
- **Requirements:**
  - Patient must be agreeable and cooperative
  - Must be able to report symptoms of AD immediately
- **Cautions:**
  - ⚠️ **Sedation may mask symptoms of autonomic dysreflexia**
  - ⚠️ **May cause ventilatory depression** in patients with marginal respiratory reserve
- **Advantage:** Patient can report early AD symptoms

**Regional Anesthesia (Preferred by many):**

**Spinal anesthesia:**
- **Safe and effective** in abolishing autonomic dysreflexia and spasms
- **Advantages:**
  - Usually denser block than epidural
  - Does not risk sparing of sacral segments (may occur with epidural)
  - Excellent postoperative pain control
- **Challenges:**
  - **Difficult to site:** Poor positioning from contractures, previous spinal instrumentation, bony deformities
  - **Difficult to assess effectiveness and level:**
    - Cannot test sensation in insensate areas
    - ⚠️ **Beware of total spinal** - may be difficult to detect
  - **Nerve stimulators may be unreliable** for motor assessment

**Epidural anesthesia:**
- **Less preferred than spinal** due to:
  - Risk of sacral segment sparing
  - Variable block density
  - May not reliably prevent AD
- **Similar placement challenges** as spinal

**General Anesthesia:**
- **Effective when adequate depth maintained**
- **Specific considerations:**
  - **Need to address autonomic dysreflexia:** Adequate depth usually prevents AD
  - **Risk of malignant hypertension** on surgical stimuli if anesthesia inadequate
  - ⚠️ **Prone to post-induction hypotension:**
    - Loss of sympathetic tone
    - Reduced intravascular volume
    - Altered baroreceptor reflexes
  - **Difficult airway potential:** Previous C-spine operations, tracheal stenosis
  - **Aspiration risk:** Gastroparesis
  - **KIV awake fiberoptic intubation**
  - ⚠️ **Caution with succinylcholine** (see Section 57.8)
  - ⚠️ **Beware postoperative hypoventilation:** Baseline respiratory compromise, opioid sensitivity

**Technique preference summary:**
- **Usually preferred:**
  - General anesthesia (adequate depth)
  - Spinal anesthesia
- **Less preferred:**
  - Epidural anesthesia (sacral sparing risk)
  - Mild-to-moderate sedation (may mask AD)

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** For chronic SCI patients at risk of AD, both deep general anesthesia and neuraxial techniques (especially spinal) are effective. The choice should be individualized based on surgical site, patient factors, and institutional expertise. See Section 56.9 for comprehensive chronic SCI anesthetic planning.

## 57.8. MUSCLE RELAXANT CONSIDERATIONS

**Succinylcholine and chronic SCI:**

**Time-dependent risk:**
- ✅ **SAFE:** Within first 24-72 hours following acute spinal cord injury
- ⚠️ **ABSOLUTELY CONTRAINDICATED:** From approximately 72 hours to 6 months post-injury
- **Likely safe again:** After 6 months (though many avoid indefinitely)

**Mechanism of hyperkalemia:**
- **Extrajunctional acetylcholine receptor upregulation** develops after 72 hours
- **Massive K⁺ release** from muscle depolarization
- ⚠️ **May precipitate life-threatening hyperkalemia:**
  - Cardiac arrest
  - Ventricular fibrillation
  - Severe dysrhythmias
  - Can be rapidly fatal

**Alternative neuromuscular blocking agents:**
- **Use non-depolarizing agents for RSI in subacute/chronic SCI:**
  - Rocuronium (1.0-1.2 mg/kg for RSI)
  - Vecuronium
  - Cisatracurium
- **Sugammadex available** for rocuronium reversal if needed

**Acute phase considerations:**
- **RSI with in-line stabilization thought to be safe acutely**
- **Succinylcholine acceptable within first 72 hours** if no other contraindications
- **Document time of injury carefully** to guide drug selection

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The hyperkalemic cardiac arrest from succinylcholine in chronic SCI patients is often refractory to resuscitation. Prevention through strict avoidance is essential. When in doubt about timing of injury, use an alternative agent.

## 57.9. POSTOPERATIVE MONITORING

**Critical for all neurosurgical patients:**
- **Careful monitoring in postoperative period**
- **AD may manifest at this time**

**Bottom Line:** Spine surgery complications include rare but devastating POVL, anterior spinal artery syndrome, thromboembolic events, and late autonomic dysreflexia in SCI patients, all requiring specific prevention strategies and vigilant monitoring.

# 58. CONCLUSION

The perioperative care of neurosurgical patients requires a sound understanding of neurophysiologic and neuropharmacologic principles, the timely application of these principles, and vigilance to often rapidly changing clinical conditions. At the core of neuroanesthesia practice are the ideas of maintaining cerebral oxygen and substrate delivery, facilitating intraoperative neuromonitoring, and assuring for a rapid emergence to facilitate neurologic examination in appropriate patients. Expert application of the requisite knowledge to achieve these goals, along with efficient resource utilization, will provide the safest neurologic outcome possible for this vulnerable patient population.
