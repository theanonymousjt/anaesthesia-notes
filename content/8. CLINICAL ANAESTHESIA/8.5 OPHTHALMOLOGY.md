# 1. OCULAR ANATOMY AND PHYSIOLOGY



---

**Comprehensive understanding of ocular anatomy and physiology forms the foundation for safe anesthetic management in ophthalmic surgery.**

## 1.2. ANATOMY OF THE EYE

### 1.2.1. ORBITAL STRUCTURE [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

**The orbit is a pyramidal bony cavity housing the globe and associated structures.**

* **Orbital walls composed of multiple bones:**
  * Frontal bone (superior wall)
  * Zygomatic bone (lateral wall)
  * Greater wing of sphenoid (lateral wall)
  * Maxilla (inferior and medial walls)
  * Palatine bone (inferior wall)
  * Lacrimal bone (medial wall)
  * Ethmoid bone (medial wall)

* **Important anatomical landmarks for regional blocks:**
  * **Optic foramen:** Transmits optic nerve, ophthalmic artery, and sympathetic nerves from carotid plexus
  * **Superior orbital fissure:** Contains oculomotor nerve branches, trigeminal nerve branches, trochlear nerve, abducens nerve, and ophthalmic veins
  * **Inferior orbital fissure:** Contains infraorbital and zygomatic nerves
  * **Infraorbital foramen:** Located 4mm below orbital rim, transmits infraorbital nerve, artery, and vein
  * **Supraorbital notch:** At junction of medial one-third and temporal two-thirds of superior orbital rim

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The supraorbital notch, infraorbital foramen, and lacrimal fossa are all clinically palpable landmarks essential for regional block placement.

### 1.2.2. GLOBE ANATOMY [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

**The eye consists of two spheres with different radii of curvature, creating a complex optical system.**

* **Three-layer structure:**
  * **Sclera (outer fibrous layer):** Provides rigidity and maintains eye shape
  * **Uveal tract (middle vascular layer):** Composed of iris, ciliary body, and choroid
  * **Retina (inner neural layer):** Ten-layer neurosensory membrane

* **Anterior segment components:**
  * **Cornea:** Highly avascular and transparent, steeper curvature than sclera
  * **Iris:** Contains pupil with three muscle sets (dilator sympathetically innervated, sphincter and ciliary parasympathetically innervated)
  * **Crystalline lens:** Posterior to pupil, regulated by ciliary muscle
  * **Anterior and posterior chambers:** Contain aqueous humor

* **Posterior segment components:**
  * **Vitreous cavity:** Filled with gelatinous vitreous humor
  * **Choroid:** Vascular network supplying outer retina
  * **Retina:** Converts light to neural impulses

⚠️ **Safety Consideration:** The suprachoroidal space can become blood-filled during expulsive hemorrhage, creating a surgical emergency.

### 1.2.3. EXTRAOCULAR MUSCLES [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

**Six extraocular muscles control eye movement within the orbital cone.**

* **Four rectus muscles plus superior and inferior oblique muscles**
* **Form the intraconal compartment:** Extends from muscle origins at orbital apex to globe attachments
* **Clinical significance:** 
  * Intraconal (retrobulbar) blocks place anesthetic within this cone
  * Extraconal (peribulbar) blocks remain outside the cone
  * Muscle traction triggers oculocardiac reflex

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The muscle cone concept is essential for understanding regional anesthesia techniques and their complications.

### 1.2.4. VASCULAR SUPPLY [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

**Complex vascular network with dual arterial supply and venous drainage to cavernous sinus.**

* **Arterial supply:**
  * Internal carotid artery branches (primary)
  * External carotid artery branches
  * Ophthalmic artery (main branch)

* **Venous drainage:**
  * Superior and inferior ophthalmic veins
  * Central retinal vein
  * **All drain directly to cavernous sinus**

⚠️ **Critical Safety Point:** Venous drainage obstruction from orbit to right atrium impedes aqueous drainage and elevates IOP.

### 1.2.5. NEURAL INNERVATION [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

**Multiple cranial nerves provide complex sensory and motor innervation.**

* **Motor innervation:**
  * **CN III (Oculomotor):** Most extraocular muscles, pupillary constriction, accommodation
  * **CN IV (Trochlear):** Superior oblique muscle
  * **CN VI (Abducens):** Lateral rectus muscle
  * **CN VII (Facial):** Orbicularis oculi, eyelid muscles

* **Sensory innervation:**
  * **CN V (Trigeminal):** Multiple branches providing orbital and facial sensation
  * Forms afferent limb of oculocardiac reflex

* **Autonomic innervation:**
  * **Sympathetic:** Iris dilator, superior tarsal muscle
  * **Parasympathetic:** Iris sphincter, ciliary muscle

**Bottom Line:** Thorough understanding of ocular anatomy is essential for safe regional anesthesia and anticipating surgical complications. The orbital cone concept differentiates intraconal from extraconal blocks, while the complex innervation explains reflex responses and autonomic effects.

## 1.3. PHYSIOLOGY OF INTRAOCULAR PRESSURE



### 1.3.2. AQUEOUS HUMOR FORMATION AND DRAINAGE [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]

**Aqueous humor production and drainage maintains normal intraocular pressure through active and passive mechanisms.**

* **Aqueous humor formation:**
  * **Two-thirds:** Active secretory process by ciliary body in posterior chamber
  * **One-third:** Passive filtration from iris vessels in anterior chamber
  * **Carbonic anhydrase and cytochrome oxidase systems involved**
  * **Production rate:** Approximately 2 μL/min

* **Formation mechanism:**
  * Sodium actively transported into posterior chamber
  * Bicarbonate and chloride ions follow passively
  * Creates osmotic pressure gradient many times greater than plasma
  * Results in continuous aqueous humor production

* **Drainage pathway:**
  * Posterior chamber → through pupil → anterior chamber
  * Peripheral anterior chamber → trabecular meshwork
  * Schlemm canal → episcleral venous system
  * Connects to superior vena cava and right atrium

⚠️ **Clinical Significance:** Obstruction anywhere from eye to right atrium elevates IOP, including Trendelenburg position, cervical collars, and tight neckties.

### 1.3.3. IOP REGULATION MECHANISMS [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]

**Normal IOP ranges 10-21.7 mmHg with multiple regulatory factors.**

* **Normal variations:**
  * 1-2 mmHg with cardiac contractions
  * Diurnal variation: 2-5 mmHg (higher on awakening)
  * Higher morning pressure from vascular congestion, lid pressure, mydriasis during sleep

* **Three main factors influencing IOP:**
  1. **External pressure on eye**
  2. **Scleral rigidity** 
  3. **Changes in intraocular contents**

* **Primary control mechanism:**
  * Rate of aqueous humor formation
  * Rate of aqueous humor outflow
  * **Formation equation:** Rate ∝ K(OPaq - OPpl + CP)
	* K = coefficient of outflow
	* OPaq = osmotic pressure of aqueous humor
	* OPpl = osmotic pressure of plasma
	* CP = capillary pressure

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Small changes in plasma osmolality (e.g., mannitol administration) markedly influence aqueous humor formation and IOP.

* **Outflow equation:** A = (πr⁴/8ηL)(Piop - Pv)
  * A = volume of aqueous outflow per unit time
  * r = radius of Fontana spaces in trabecular meshwork
  * Piop = intraocular pressure
  * Pv = venous pressure
  * η = viscosity
  * L = length of Fontana spaces

⚠️ **Critical Concept:** Mydriasis narrows Fontana spaces, increases outflow resistance, and raises IOP - explaining why miotics are used in glaucoma patients.

### 1.3.4. GLAUCOMA PATHOPHYSIOLOGY [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]

**Dysregulation of aqueous humor homeostasis leads to optic nerve dysfunction and vision loss.**

* **Classification systems:**
  * **Acquired vs. congenital**
  * **High-IOP vs. normal pressure**
  * **Acute vs. chronic**
  * **Open-angle vs. closed-angle**

* **Open-angle glaucoma:**
  * Elevated IOP with anatomically open anterior chamber angle
  * Sclerosis of trabecular tissue impairs filtration and drainage
  * Treatment targets: decrease production, increase outflow, decrease episcleral venous pressure

* **Closed-angle glaucoma:**
  * Peripheral iris contacts posterior corneal surface
  * Mechanically obstructs aqueous humor outflow
  * **Acute angle-closure:** Urgent condition with tense, painful eye
  * **Chronic angle-closure:** More common, often asymptomatic

* **Precipitating mechanisms:**
  * Mydriasis in narrow-angle patients
  * Crystalline lens swelling (pupillary block)
  * Traumatic lens dislocation

⚠️ **Anesthetic Implications:** Systemic anticholinergics in usual clinical doses are safe for glaucomatous eyes. Atropine 0.4mg systemically results in only 0.0001mg ocular absorption.

**Bottom Line:** IOP regulation depends on balanced aqueous humor formation and drainage. Understanding these mechanisms guides perioperative management strategies including positioning, fluid therapy, and medication choices to prevent dangerous IOP elevations that could cause permanent visual loss.

# 2. FACTORS INFLUENCING INTRAOCULAR PRESSURE


Medical Knowledge - Drug Effects Integration


---

## 2.2. PHYSIOLOGICAL FACTORS

### 2.2.1. VENTILATION AND TEMPERATURE EFFECTS [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]

**Ventilation and temperature significantly influence intraocular pressure through multiple mechanisms.**

* **Hyperventilation effects:**
  * Decreases IOP through reduced PaCO2
  * Mechanism: Decreased choroidal blood volume

* **Hypoventilation/CO2 retention effects:**
  * Elevates IOP significantly
  * Asphyxia and CO2 administration increase IOP
  * Mechanism: Choroidal vascular congestion

* **Temperature effects:**
  * **Hypothermia lowers IOP** despite increased aqueous humor viscosity
  * Mechanisms: Decreased aqueous humor formation, vasoconstriction
  * Net result: IOP reduction

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The counterintuitive effect of hypothermia lowering IOP (despite increased viscosity) demonstrates the predominant role of aqueous humor formation over viscosity changes.

### 2.2.2. AIRWAY MANAGEMENT EFFECTS [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]

**Direct laryngoscopy, intubation, and extubation significantly impact IOP.**

* **Laryngoscopy and intubation:**
  * Can raise IOP severalfold
  * Occurs even without visible reaction
  * Particularly concerning in traumatic eye injuries and glaucoma
  * ⚠️ Topical laryngeal anesthesia may attenuate hypertensive response but doesn't reliably prevent IOP increases

* **Coughing, straining, and vomiting:**
  * Dramatically increase venous pressure
  * Raise IOP up to 40 mmHg or more
  * **Disastrous consequences if eye is surgically open:**
	* Expulsive hemorrhage
	* Vitreous loss
	* Iris/lens prolapse

* **Video laryngoscopy advantages:**
  * May raise IOP less than direct laryngoscopy
  * Preferred technique when IOP control is critical

* **Supraglottic airway considerations:**
  * Minimal effect on IOP when inserted at appropriate depth
  * Often preferred for ophthalmic surgery
  * Avoids intubation-related IOP spikes

⚠️ **Critical Safety Point:** Ensure sufficient anesthetic depth before airway manipulation and plan for smooth, non-eventful emergence to prevent dangerous IOP spikes.

**Bottom Line:** Physiological factors including ventilation, temperature, and airway management profoundly affect IOP. Hyperventilation and hypothermia decrease IOP, while hypoventilation, coughing, straining, and airway manipulation significantly increase IOP with potentially catastrophic consequences in open-eye surgery.

## 2.3. PHARMACOLOGICAL EFFECTS [PART A/B]

### 2.3.1. ANESTHETIC AGENTS [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**Most anesthetic agents produce dose-related reductions in IOP through multiple mechanisms.**

* **Inhalational anesthetics:**
  * Cause dose-related IOP decreases
  * Sevoflurane and propofol decrease IOP equally during non-ophthalmic surgery
  * Nitrous oxide: No independent effect when combined with sevoflurane and remifentanil

* **Intravenous anesthetics:**
  * **Propofol, etomidate, dexmedetomidine:** Lower IOP at sedation and GA doses
  * **Barbiturates, neuroleptics, opioids, hypnotics:** All reduce IOP
  * Effect seen in both normal and glaucomatous eyes

* **Proposed mechanisms for IOP reduction:**
  * Depression of diencephalon control center
  * Reduction of aqueous humor production
  * Enhancement of aqueous humor outflow
  * Relaxation of extraocular muscles

#### 2.3.1.1. KETAMINE CONTROVERSY [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**Historical controversy regarding ketamine's effect on IOP with conflicting evidence.**

* **Initial concerns:**
  * Early studies suggested significant IOP increase with IV/IM ketamine
  * Measurements complicated by ketamine-induced nystagmus
  * Less accurate indentation tonometry used

* **Current evidence:**
  * 2 mg/kg IV ketamine failed to significantly affect IOP in adults
  * Multiple pediatric studies show no IOP increase
  * More accurate applanation tonometry shows no convincing increase

* **Clinical considerations:**
  * Insufficient evidence to support significant IOP increase
  * Nystagmus and blepharospasm make ketamine less optimal for ophthalmic surgery
  * May be useful for examination under anesthesia in suspected infantile glaucoma

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The ketamine-IOP controversy likely resulted from measurement difficulties and study methodology rather than true significant IOP elevation.

### 2.3.2. NEUROMUSCULAR BLOCKING DRUGS [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**Neuromuscular blocking agents affect IOP through direct muscle effects and indirect respiratory consequences.**

#### 2.3.2.1. NONDEPOLARIZING NEUROMUSCULAR BLOCKING AGENTS

**All nondepolarizing agents in equipotent ED95 doses directly lower IOP by relaxing extraocular muscles.**

* **Mechanism:** Direct extraocular muscle relaxation
* **Clinical benefit:** Consistent IOP reduction
* ⚠️ **Important caveat:** Inadequate ventilation with muscle paralysis can increase PaCO2 and secondarily increase IOP

#### 2.3.2.2. SUCCINYLCHOLINE EFFECTS

**Succinylcholine uniquely increases IOP through multiple mechanisms.**

* **IOP increase characteristics:**
  * Average peak increase: 9 mmHg
  * Onset: 1-4 minutes after administration
  * Duration: Returns to baseline within 7 minutes
  * Clinically relevant in compromised globe integrity

* **Proposed mechanisms:**
  * Tonic contraction of extraocular muscles (fasciculation)
  * Choroidal vascular dilation
  * Orbital smooth muscle relaxation
  * **Primary mechanism:** Cycloplegic action deepening anterior chamber and increasing outflow resistance

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The primary IOP increase mechanism appears to be cycloplegic rather than simply extraocular muscle fasciculation, as the effect persists even with detached extraocular muscles.

#### 2.3.2.3. PREVENTION OF SUCCINYLCHOLINE-INDUCED IOP INCREASE

**Multiple methods attempted but none consistently and completely prevent IOP elevation.**

* **Attempted preventive measures:**
  * Prior acetazolamide administration
  * Pretreatment with narcotics, β-blockers
  * Defasciculating dose of nondepolarizing relaxants
  * IV lidocaine 1-2 mg/kg
  * "Self-taming" with small succinylcholine dose

* **Clinical reality:**
  * Some attenuation possible but unreliable
  * Pretreatment with defasciculating doses may be most effective
  * **Grover et al.** reported lidocaine 1.5 mg/kg IV may provide protection for rapid-sequence induction in open-eye injuries

⚠️ **Modern Clinical Context:** The historical concern about succinylcholine in open-eye injuries is being reconsidered. Few reports clearly attribute vitreous loss to succinylcholine versus other factors in desperate clinical circumstances.

### 2.3.3. OTHER MEDICATIONS [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 2.3.3.1. HYPERTONIC SOLUTIONS

**Intravenous hypertonic solutions reduce IOP by elevating plasma osmotic pressure.**

* **Effective agents:**
  * Mannitol (most commonly used)
  * Urea, sorbitol, dextran

* **Mechanism:** Decreased aqueous humor formation via osmotic gradient

* **Pharmacokinetics:**
  * **Onset:** Rapid
  * **Peak effect:** 30-45 minutes
  * **Duration:** 5-6 hours

* **Clinical considerations:**
  * **Standard dose:** Up to 1 g/kg IV (typical clinical dose: 12.5g slowly)
  * Mannitol preferred over urea due to fewer side effects

⚠️ **Serious complications from rapid large-dose infusion:**
* Acute intravascular volume overload
* Hypertension, congestive heart failure
* Plasma sodium dilution
* Subsequent diuresis-induced hypotension
* Renal failure

#### 2.3.3.2. ACETAZOLAMIDE [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**Carbonic anhydrase inhibitor that reduces aqueous humor formation.**

* **Mechanism:**
  * Inactivates carbonic anhydrase
  * Interferes with sodium pump
  * Decreases aqueous humor formation

* **Advantages:**
  * Easy administration from single vial
  * Available orally and topically
  * More convenient than large-volume hypertonic solutions

* **Systemic effects:**
  * Loss of sodium, potassium, and water (renal tubular effects)
  * Risk of electrolyte imbalances
  * Potential for cardiac dysrhythmias during general anesthesia

* **Contraindications:**
  * Marked hepatic or renal dysfunction
  * Low sodium or abnormal potassium levels
  * **Caution in chronic lung disease:** Risk of severe acidosis with long-term therapy

**Bottom Line:** Anesthetic agents generally reduce IOP through multiple mechanisms, while succinylcholine uniquely increases IOP. Hypertonic solutions and acetazolamide effectively reduce IOP but carry significant systemic risks requiring careful patient selection and monitoring. Understanding these pharmacological effects is essential for safe ophthalmic anesthesia management.

# 3. OCULOCARDIAC REFLEX

Medical Knowledge - Reflex Physiology and Management


---

**The oculocardiac reflex is a potentially dangerous bradycardic response triggered by ocular or orbital manipulation.**

## 3.2. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND TRIGGERS

### 3.2.1. REFLEX ARC [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]

**Classic reflex pathway first described by Bernard Aschner and Giuseppe Dagnini in 1908.**

* **Afferent limb:** Trigeminal nerve (CN V)
* **Efferent limb:** Vagal nerve (CN X)
* **Central integration:** Brainstem cardioinhibitory center

### 3.2.2. TRIGGERING STIMULI [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]

**Multiple stimuli can elicit the oculocardiac reflex:**

* **Direct globe pressure**
* **Traction on extraocular muscles**
* **Manipulation of conjunctiva or orbital structures**
* **Performance of regional eye blocks**
* **Ocular trauma**
* **Direct pressure on tissue remaining after enucleation**

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The reflex can be triggered during both local and general anesthesia, making it a universal concern in ophthalmic procedures.

## 3.3. CLINICAL MANIFESTATIONS [PART A/B]

### 3.3.1. CARDIAC ARRHYTHMIAS [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]

**Wide spectrum of cardiac dysrhythmias possible, not just bradycardia.**

* **Most common:** Sinus bradycardia
* **Other manifestations:**
  * Junctional rhythm
  * Ectopic atrial rhythm
  * Atrioventricular blockade
  * Ventricular bigeminy
  * Multifocal premature ventricular contractions
  * Wandering pacemaker
  * Idioventricular rhythm
  * **Severe complications:** Asystole, ventricular tachycardia

### 3.3.2. RISK FACTORS FOR INCREASED SEVERITY [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]

**Certain conditions augment reflex incidence and severity:**

* **Hypercarbia and hypoxemia**
* **Shallow anesthetic depth**
* **Pediatric patients** (higher vagal tone)
* **Age factor:** Children tend to have more pronounced responses

### 3.3.3. INCIDENCE VARIABILITY

**Reported incidence varies dramatically in literature:**

* **Range:** 16% to 82%
* **Berler study:** 50% incidence
* **Higher rates typically in pediatric populations**

## 3.4. PREVENTION AND MANAGEMENT [PART B/C]

### 3.4.1. PROPHYLACTIC MEASURES [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C4 Trauma Resuscitation and Crisis Management|ⓘ]]

**Various prevention strategies with limited reliability and inherent risks.**

#### 3.4.1.1. REGIONAL ANESTHESIA

* Can block afferent limb of reflex
* Associated with its own complications
* Not consistently effective

#### 3.4.1.2. ANTICHOLINERGIC PREMEDICATION

* **IM atropine or glycopyrrolate:** Ineffective in usual premedication regimens
* **IV atropine within 30 minutes of surgery:** May reduce incidence
* **Pediatric dosing:** 0.02 mg/kg IV just before surgery
* **Glycopyrrolate alternative:** 0.01 mg/kg IV (less tachycardia than atropine)

⚠️ **Controversial Risk:** Some anesthesiologists believe prophylactic IV atropine may cause more serious and refractory dysrhythmias than the reflex itself.

#### 3.4.1.3. ATROPINE-RELATED COMPLICATIONS

* **Reported cardiac effects:**
  * Ventricular fibrillation
  * Ventricular tachycardia
  * Left bundle branch block
  * Various other dysrhythmias

### 3.4.2. ACUTE MANAGEMENT [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C4 Trauma Resuscitation and Crisis Management|ⓘ]] `[COMP: PC]`

**Standard approach when oculocardiac reflex occurs:**

1. **Immediate response:**
   * Ask surgeon to cease operative manipulation immediately
   * Assess anesthetic depth and ventilatory status
   * **Typical resolution:** Heart rate and rhythm return to baseline within 20 seconds

2. **Reflex attenuation phenomenon:**
   * Reflex weakens with repeated manipulation
   * Bradycardia less likely to recur (tachyphylaxis)
   * Due to fatigue of reflex arc at cardioinhibitory center level

3. **Indications for IV atropine:**
   * Especially serious initial dysrhythmia
   * Tenacious recurrence of reflex
   * Hemodynamic compromise

### 3.4.3. AGE-SPECIFIC CONSIDERATIONS

**Different approaches for adults versus pediatric patients:**

* **Adults:** Prophylactic measures generally NOT indicated due to inherent risks
* **Pediatrics:** Some anesthesiologists routinely use prophylactic IV atropine for strabismus surgery
* **Risk-benefit analysis:** Must weigh reflex prevention against atropine-induced complications

### 3.4.4. SPECIAL SITUATION: SUPERSELECTIVE OPHTHALMIC ARTERY CHEMOTHERAPY

**Trigeminocardiac reflex during retinoblastoma treatment.**

* **Procedure:** Direct intra-arterial chemotherapy for retinoblastoma
* **Incidence:** Approximately 40% overall occurrence
* **Clinical manifestations:**
  * Abrupt drop in end-tidal CO2
  * Chest wall rigidity (reduced lung compliance)
  * Profound hypoxia
  * Systemic hypotension
  * Bradycardia

* **Management approach:**
  * Cease catheter advancement
  * 100% oxygen
  * Higher airway pressures
  * IV epinephrine boluses: 0.5-1.0 mcg/kg

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Bradycardia during ophthalmic artery cannulation may actually indicate successful catheter placement.

⚠️ **Important Recognition:** Preoperative IV atropine does not prevent this trigeminocardiac reflex variant.

**Bottom Line:** The oculocardiac reflex is a potentially serious complication of ophthalmic surgery that can produce various cardiac dysrhythmias. While prophylactic measures exist, they are not consistently effective and carry their own risks. The key to management is immediate recognition, cessation of stimulus, and supportive care with IV atropine reserved for severe or persistent cases.

# 4. OPHTHALMIC PHARMACOLOGY




---

**Topical ophthalmic drugs can produce significant systemic effects and dangerous anesthetic interactions.**

## 4.2. TOPICAL MEDICATIONS [PART A/B]

### 4.2.1. SYSTEMIC ABSORPTION PATHWAYS [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

**Multiple routes allow topical ophthalmic drugs to reach systemic circulation.**

* **Primary absorption sites:**
  * Conjunctival absorption
  * Nasal mucosa after nasolacrimal drainage
  * Percutaneous absorption in premature infants (immature epidermis)

* **Prevention of systemic absorption:**
  * Occlude nasolacrimal duct by pressing inner canthus after instillation
  * Significantly reduces systemic drug levels

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Simple nasolacrimal duct occlusion can dramatically reduce systemic absorption of topical ophthalmic medications.

### 4.2.2. PARASYMPATHOMIMETIC AGENTS [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 4.2.2.1. ECHOTHIOPHATE (PHOSPHOLINE IODIDE)

**Long-acting anticholinesterase with profound anesthetic implications.**

* **Clinical uses:**
  * Glaucoma refractory to other therapies
  * Pediatric accommodative esotropia
  * Lowers IOP by decreasing aqueous humor outflow resistance

* **Mechanism of anesthetic interaction:**
  * Reduces plasma pseudocholinesterase activity by 95%
  * Effect lasts 4-6 weeks after discontinuation

⚠️ **Critical Anesthetic Implications:**
* **Prolonged succinylcholine paralysis:** Expect prolonged apnea
* **Delayed ester local anesthetic metabolism**
* **Duration of effect:** Normal enzyme activity doesn't return for 4-6 weeks after stopping drug

#### 4.2.2.2. PILOCARPINE

**Cholinergic agonist with systemic cardiovascular effects.**

* **Mechanism:** Increases trabecular outflow
* **Systemic effects:**
  * Bronchospasm
  * Hypertension  
  * Tachydysrhythmias
* **Special formulation:** Lower-concentration version for presbyopia treatment may cause headache and increase retinal detachment risk

### 4.2.3. COCAINE [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**Historical local anesthetic with significant toxicity potential.**

* **Historical significance:** Introduced to ophthalmology by Koller in 1884
* **Current limited use:** 
  * Nasal packs for dacryocystorhinostomy
  * Only local anesthetic providing inherent vasoconstriction

* **Pharmacological properties:**
  * Excellent mucosal absorption (plasma levels comparable to IV injection)
  * Interferes with catecholamine uptake
  * Sympathetic nervous system potentiation

#### 4.2.3.1. DOSING AND TOXICITY

* **Maximum safe dose:** 3 mg/kg (200 mg for 70-kg adult)
* **Lethal dose:** ~1 g (but highly variable)
* **Systemic reactions:** Can occur with as little as 20 mg

#### 4.2.3.2. CONTRAINDICATIONS AND INTERACTIONS

⚠️ **Absolute contraindications:**
* Hypertensive patients
* Tricyclic antidepressant use
* Monoamine oxidase inhibitor therapy
* Concurrent sympathomimetics (epinephrine, phenylephrine)

#### 4.2.3.3. TOXICITY MANAGEMENT

* **Cardiovascular complications:** Use labetalol (combined α and β blockade)
* **Avoid pure β-blockers:** Risk of unopposed α-stimulation exacerbating hypertension
* **Chest pain/MI:** Use nitroglycerin, avoid β-blockers acutely

* **Modern alternatives:** "Pseudococaine" solutions (lidocaine 4% + oxymetazoline 0.05% + peppermint oil)

### 4.2.4. CYCLOPENTOLATE [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**Mydriatic agent with CNS toxicity potential.**

* **CNS effects:**
  * Dysarthria
  * Disorientation
  * Frank psychotic reactions
  * Convulsions (especially pediatric)

* **Concentration-dependent toxicity:**
  * 2% solution: Higher CNS dysfunction risk
  * 1% solution: Safer alternative
  * **Pediatric recommendations:** 0.5-1% solutions only

⚠️ **PACU consideration:** Can cause post-operative drowsiness, requiring alert to recovery staff to prevent misdiagnosis.

### 4.2.5. PHENYLEPHRINE [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**α-Adrenergic agonist for pupillary dilation with cardiovascular risks.**

* **Clinical effects:**
  * Pupillary dilation
  * Capillary decongestion

#### 4.2.5.1. SYSTEMIC COMPLICATIONS

* **Cardiovascular effects:**
  * Hypertension, headache
  * Tachycardia, tremulousness
  * **Severe complications:** Cardiac dysrhythmias, myocardial ischemia/infarction
  * Cerebral hemorrhage in patients with aneurysms

#### 4.2.5.2. CONCENTRATION-DEPENDENT RISK

* **10% concentration:** Significant systemic effects possible
* **2.5% concentration:** Recommended for infants and elderly
* **Vulnerable populations:** Children especially susceptible to overdose

⚠️ **Surgical timing:** Never give phenylephrine after surgery begins when venous channels are patent.

### 4.2.6. BETA-ADRENERGIC ANTAGONISTS [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 4.2.6.1. NONSELECTIVE BETA-BLOCKERS

**Timolol, carteolol, levobunolol, metipranolol with significant systemic absorption.**

* **Mechanism:** Antiglaucoma effect through aqueous humor reduction

⚠️ **Life-threatening complications:**
* **Respiratory:** Life-threatening asthmatic crises in stable asthma patients
* **Cardiac:** Severe sinus bradycardia, sick sinus syndrome, cardiac arrest
* **Neuromuscular:** Myasthenia gravis exacerbation
* **Pediatric:** Postoperative apnea in neonates and young infants

#### 4.2.6.2. Β1-SELECTIVE AGENTS

**Betaxolol with improved safety profile.**

* **Advantages:** More oculospecific, minimal systemic effects
* **Interactions:** Additive effects with oral β-blockers
* **Cautions:** Still exercise care in pulmonary disease

**Contraindications:**
* Sinus bradycardia
* Congestive heart failure
* Greater than first-degree heart block
* Cardiogenic shock
* Overt myocardial failure

### 4.2.7. ALPHA-ADRENERGIC AGONISTS [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**Apraclonidine and brimonidine with systemic hypotensive effects.**

* **Mechanism:** Decrease aqueous production or increase uveoscleral outflow
* **Side effects:**
  * Hypotension
  * Headache
  * Somnolence

### 4.2.8. INTRAOCULAR PERFLUOROCARBONS [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**Expandable gases for retinal detachment repair with critical anesthetic implications.**

* **Gas types and duration:**
  * Air: 5-day absorption
  * Sulfur hexafluoride: 10-day absorption  
  * Perfluoropropane: 70-day absorption

#### 4.2.8.1. CRITICAL DRUG INTERACTION

⚠️ **Nitrous oxide contraindication:**
* N2O is manyfold more diffusible than perfluorocarbons
* Can rapidly expand gas bubbles
* **Risk:** Retinal and optic nerve ischemia from central retinal artery occlusion

#### 4.2.8.2. CLINICAL MANAGEMENT

* **Intraoperative:** Discontinue N2O 15 minutes before gas injection
* **Postoperative N2O avoidance periods:**
  * Air injection: 5 days
  * Sulfur hexafluoride: 10 days
  * Perfluoropropane: 70 days
* **Patient safety:** MedicAlert bracelet warns against N2O administration

💡 **Case reports:** Devastating permanent blindness reported when N2O given to patients with intraocular gases, even weeks after injection.

**Bottom Line:** Ophthalmic medications pose significant anesthetic risks through systemic absorption and drug interactions. Key concerns include echothiophate prolonging neuromuscular blockade, sympathomimetics causing cardiovascular complications, and the critical nitrous oxide contraindication with intraocular gases. Understanding these interactions is essential for safe ophthalmic anesthesia.

## 4.3. SYSTEMIC OPHTHALMIC DRUGS [PART A/B]

### 4.3.1. GLYCEROL [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**Oral hyperosmotic agent for IOP reduction with multiple systemic risks.**

* **Mechanism:** Dehydrates vitreous to reduce IOP
* **Complications:**
  * Nausea and vomiting (aspiration risk)
  * Hyperglycemia
  * Mental confusion
  * Seizure activity

⚠️ **Anesthetic implications:** Risk of aspiration from nausea/vomiting, blood glucose management issues.

### 4.3.2. MANNITOL [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**IV hyperosmotic agent requiring careful cardiovascular assessment.**

* **Standard dosing:** Up to 1 g/kg IV (typical clinical dose: 12.5g given slowly)
* **Mechanism:** Vitreous dehydration reduces IOP

#### 4.3.2.1. SERIOUS SYSTEMIC COMPLICATIONS

⚠️ **Cardiovascular complications from rapid large-dose infusion:**
* Renal failure
* Congestive heart failure
* Pulmonary congestion
* Electrolyte imbalances (particularly glucose)
* Hypotension or hypertension
* Myocardial ischemia
* Allergic reactions (rare)

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Patient's renal and cardiovascular status must be thoroughly assessed before mannitol therapy.

### 4.3.3. ACETAZOLAMIDE [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**Carbonic anhydrase inhibitor with renal and metabolic effects.**

* **Mechanism:** Decreases aqueous production through renal tubular effects

#### 4.3.3.1. CONTRAINDICATIONS

* Marked hepatic dysfunction
* Marked renal dysfunction  
* Low sodium levels
* Abnormal potassium values

#### 4.3.3.2. COMPLICATIONS

* **Electrolyte imbalances:** Can trigger serious cardiac dysrhythmias
* **Chronic lung disease:** Risk of severe acidosis with long-term therapy
* **Metabolic effects:** Sodium, potassium, water loss

💡 **Safety advantage:** Topical carbonic anhydrase inhibitors appear relatively free of clinically important systemic effects.

**Bottom Line:** Systemic ophthalmic drugs require careful patient selection and monitoring due to significant cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic effects. Pre-existing organ dysfunction significantly increases complication risks.

### 5.2.1. PATIENT DEMOGRAPHICS AND CHALLENGES [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 5.2.2. COMMUNICATION AND RAPPORT BUILDING `[COMP: ICS]`

**Effective preoperative communication sets expectations and obtains informed consent.**

* **Patient education priorities:**
  * Explain inherent risks of surgery and anesthesia

  * **Reality education:** Field avoidance, surgeon at head, functional airway maintenance required

* **Intraoperative experience preparation:**
  * Explain expected intraoperative awareness

#### 5.2.2.1. MEDICOLEGAL RESPONSIBILITIES `[COMP: PROF]`

* Candid explanation fulfills informed consent requirements
* Document patient understanding and acceptance
* Address specific questions and concerns

### 5.2.3. CLINICAL ASSESSMENT STANDARDS [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] `[COMP: PC]`

#### 5.2.3.1. HISTORY AND PHYSICAL EXAMINATION

⚠️ **Evidence-based requirement:** History and physical examination are the foundation of safe patient care. Questionnaires in lieu of medical evaluation lack sensitivity to detect pertinent medical issues.

* **Essential components:**
  * Complete medication list (systemic and topical)
  * Drug allergy history (medicines, foods, tape)
  * Personal/family anesthetic reaction history
  * Focused physical examination

#### 5.2.3.2. LABORATORY TESTING GUIDELINES

**Evidence-based approach to preoperative testing:**

* **ASA Task Force conclusion:** Routine preoperative tests commonly not useful
* **Schein et al. study:** Routine testing doesn't improve cataract patient safety or outcomes
* **Economic impact:** $150 million annually for unnecessary U.S. cataract surgery testing

💡 **Society for Ambulatory Anesthesia recommendation:** No preoperative testing prior to cataract surgery unless indicated independent of procedure.

**Testing indications:** Based on history and physical examination findings, NOT routine protocols.

### 5.2.4. ANTITHROMBOTIC THERAPY MANAGEMENT [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] `[COMP: SBP]`

**Complex risk-benefit analysis for antiplatelet and anticoagulant continuation.**

#### 5.2.4.1. EVIDENCE-BASED CONSENSUS

**Large-scale studies support continuation of antithrombotic therapy:**

* **McClellan multicenter study:** Nearly 20,000 elderly cataract patients
  * Patients continuing therapy: No increased ocular hemorrhage
  * Patients discontinuing: No decreased medical events
  * Complication rates so low that absolute risk differences minimal

* **Meta-analysis findings:** 11 studies show increased bleeding risk but:
  * Almost all instances self-limiting
  * No clinically relevant visual compromise
  * No bleeding-related visual acuity compromise

#### 5.2.4.2. PROCEDURE-SPECIFIC CONSIDERATIONS

**Retina surgery classification:**

* **Historical concern:** Previously classified as high hemorrhage risk
* **Contemporary evidence:** Equivalent or higher bleeding rates but:
  * No long-term visual sequelae in nearly all cases
  * Most self-limited vitreous hemorrhages
  * Rarely require return to operating room
  * Evolving smaller gauge techniques may further decrease risk

#### 5.2.4.3. REGIONAL ANESTHESIA SAFETY

* **Traditional concern:** Increased hemorrhagic risk with eye blocks
* **Current evidence:** No conclusive data supporting contraindication
* **Recent literature review:** No sight-threatening complications with single antiplatelet agents or anticoagulants

#### 5.2.4.4. NOVEL ANTICOAGULANTS

**Direct antithrombotic agents present monitoring challenges:**

* **Anticoagulants:** Dabigatran, rivaroxaban, apixaban
* **Antiplatelets:** Prasugrel, ticagrelor
* **Challenge:** Efficacy cannot be monitored with conventional clotting assays

#### 5.2.4.5. CLINICAL RECOMMENDATION `[COMP: SBP]`

💡 **Evidence-based guidance:** Most patients should continue antithrombotic medication regimens prior to ophthalmic surgery given current evidence.

### 5.2.5. CORONARY STENT MANAGEMENT [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Specialized considerations for patients with coronary stents.**

#### 5.2.5.1. DRUG-ELUTING VS. BARE-METAL STENTS

* **Bare-metal stents:** Susceptible to in-stent restenosis
* **Drug-eluting stents:** More vulnerable to stent thrombosis (high mortality)
* **Dual antiplatelet therapy:** Extended periods required for drug-eluting stents

#### 5.2.5.2. PERIOPERATIVE RECOMMENDATIONS

**Emerging consensus prioritizes thrombotic over bleeding risk:**

* **Surgery timing:**
  * Bare-metal stents: Delay elective surgery 30 days
  * Drug-eluting stents: Delay elective surgery 6 months

* **Cataract surgery exception:** Minimal stress response and systemic inflammation
  * **SAMBA recommendation:** Surgery possible 30 days post-stent if antiplatelet therapy uninterrupted

### 5.2.6. SPECIAL POPULATION CONSIDERATIONS [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

#### 5.2.6.1. PEDIATRIC PATIENTS

**Age-related pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic considerations:**

* **Premature infants:** Unique physiological challenges
* **Altered drug handling:** Age-appropriate dosing essential
* **Psychological factors:** Separation anxiety, cooperation challenges

#### 5.2.6.2. GERIATRIC PATIENTS

**Multiple age-related physiological changes:**

* **Hypertension:** Present in most geriatric patients
* **Mydriatic cautions:** Poorly controlled hypertension patients shouldn't receive dilating drops without anesthesia consultation
* **High-concentration phenylephrine:** Can precipitate hypertensive crisis

### 5.2.7. CARDIAC IMPLANTABLE ELECTRONIC DEVICES (CIEDS) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Growing population requiring specialized management.**

#### 5.2.7.1. PREOPERATIVE ASSESSMENT

**Essential device information:**

* CIED type and indication for implantation
* Residual battery life
* Magnet response potential
* Pacing/tachydysrhythmic event history
* Shock delivery history

#### 5.2.7.2. PROCEDURAL CONSIDERATIONS

* **Electrocautery use:** Most ophthalmic procedures use minimal/no cautery
* **Small-incision cataract surgery:** Often no cautery required
* **Low electromagnetic interference risk**

**Safety record:** Despite millions of annual procedures, no documented ICD activations during ophthalmic surgery.

#### 5.2.7.3. AMBULATORY SURGERY FACILITY DEBATE

**Controversy regarding CIED patients in freestanding facilities:**

* **Complex devices:** May require real-time cardiac device team access
* **Risk-benefit analysis:** Individual facility capabilities vs. patient needs

**Bottom Line:** Comprehensive preoperative evaluation must address the unique challenges of ophthalmic surgery patients, including advanced age, multiple comorbidities, antithrombotic therapy, and specialized devices. Evidence-based approaches favor continuing most medical therapies while ensuring appropriate risk stratification and safety planning.

## 5.4. REGIONAL ANESTHESIA TECHNIQUES

### 5.4.1. UNIVERSAL PROTOCOL AND SAFETY [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S13 Ophthalmic Procedures|ⓘ]] `[COMP: SBP]`

#### 5.4.1.1. WRONG-SITE SURGERY PREVENTION

**Joint Commission Universal Protocol (2003) - three components:**

1. **Preoperative verification**
2. **Marking of intended site**  
3. **"Time-out" immediately before procedure**

#### 5.4.1.2. OPHTHALMIC SURGERY RISK FACTORS

⚠️ **Higher risk for laterality errors:**
* Patient/staff confusion about side, site, procedure
* Sedation/anesthesia increasing error likelihood
* Pediatric patients lacking intervention competence
* Similar patient names
* Wrong side preparation/draping
* Surgical caps obscuring marked sites

#### 5.4.1.3. HUMAN FACTOR CONSIDERATIONS

* **Communication failures:** Oral and written
* **Protocol non-adherence:** Safety system bypassing
* **Distraction-rich environment**
* **Inadequate cross-checking:** Consent forms, charts, patient verification

### 5.4.2. INTRACONAL (RETROBULBAR) AND EXTRACONAL (PERIBULBAR) BLOCKS

#### 5.4.2.1. HISTORICAL DEVELOPMENT

* **1844:** Knapp described early ophthalmic regional anesthesia
* **Early 20th century:** Atkinson introduced practical retrobulbar block
* **Modern era:** Peribulbar technique developed for improved safety

#### 5.4.2.2. ANATOMICAL BASIS - THE ORBITAL CONE CONCEPT

**Four rectus muscles with connective tissue septae create defined compartment:**

* **Cone extends:** From muscle origins at orbital apex to globe attachments
* **Intraconal space:** Within the muscle cone
* **Extraconal space:** Outside the muscle cone
* **Modern terminology:** 
  * Intraconal injection = Retrobulbar block
  * Extraconal injection = Peribulbar block

💡 **Anatomical reality:** Cadaveric studies show no complete intermuscular septum. Solutions placed in either space eventually distribute to both areas.

#### 5.4.2.3. TECHNIQUE DIFFERENCES

##### 5.4.2.3.1. INTRACONAL (RETROBULBAR) BLOCK [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S13 Ophthalmic Procedures|ⓘ]]

**Needle placement characteristics:**
* **Depth:** Deep within orbit, behind globe
* **Angulation:** Steep angulation toward orbital apex
* **Volume:** Low volume required (2-4 mL)
* **Onset:** Rapid onset
* **Quality:** Intense depth of anesthesia

**Advantages:**
* Rapid onset and dense block
* Lower volume requirements
* Predictable results

##### 5.4.2.3.2. EXTRACONAL (PERIBULBAR) BLOCK [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S13 Ophthalmic Procedures|ⓘ]]

**Needle placement characteristics:**
* **Depth:** Shallower placement, parallel to globe
* **Direction:** Toward greater wing of sphenoid
* **Volume:** Larger volumes required (8-12 mL)
* **Onset:** Longer latency period
* **Distribution:** Gradual spread to neural structures

**Theoretical safety advantages:**
* Greater distance from vital intraorbital structures
* Reduced risk of optic nerve/brain proximity

#### 5.4.2.4. NEEDLE ENTRY POINTS AND SAFETY MODIFICATIONS

**Traditional approach:**
* **Location:** Junction of lateral third and medial two-thirds of inferior orbital rim
* **Alignment:** In line with lateral limbal margin

**Modern safety modifications:**
* **More lateral entry:** Decreases likelihood of muscle injection
* **Rationale:** Intramuscular injection potentially causes postoperative strabismus
* **Medial approaches:** Caruncle entry points popularized

⚠️ **Superior approach caution:** Avoid supplementation above globe due to:
* Preponderance of vessels in superior orbit
* Superior oblique muscle and trochlear muscle encounter risk

#### 5.4.2.5. NEEDLE SELECTION AND SAFETY

**Length considerations:**
* **Standard needles:** 1.5-inch (38-mm) can reach critical orbital apex structures in ~20% of cases
* **Recommended length:** 1.25-inch (31-mm) or shorter
* **Safety rationale:** Reduced risk of apex complications

**Sharp vs. dull needle controversy:**
* **Dull needles:** May require more force, risk of globe penetration if excessive pressure applied
* **Sharp needles:** Less insertion force, potentially less damage if inadvertent globe contact

#### 5.4.2.6. PATIENT POSITIONING DURING BLOCK

**Historical practice:** Patient gaze superonasally
* **Unsold et al. finding:** This stretches optic nerve directly in needle path
* **Increased risk:** Direct optic nerve trauma

**Current recommendation:** Neutral gaze position
* **Benefit:** Keeps optic nerve lax within orbit
* **Safety advantage:** Reduced risk of nerve injury

#### 5.4.2.7. IOP MANAGEMENT POST-BLOCK

**Pressure elevation prevention:**
* **Digital pressure:** Gentle, non-continuous
* **Ocular decompression devices:** Mechanical pressure application
* **Duration:** 5-20 minutes typically sufficient
* **Goal:** Reduce IOP to below baseline levels

### 5.4.3. FACIAL NERVE BLOCKADE FOR EYELID AKINESIA

#### 5.4.3.1. INDICATION AND RATIONALE

* **Extraconal blocks:** Often provide inherent lid akinesia due to larger volumes
* **Intraconal blocks:** Usually require separate facial nerve block
* **Purpose:** Prevent orbicularis oculi contraction during surgery

#### 5.4.3.2. HISTORICAL TECHNIQUES (PROXIMAL TO DISTAL)

1. **Van Lint method (1914):** First described technique
2. **Atkinson method**
3. **O'Brien method**
4. **Nadbath-Rehman method:** Most proximal approach

#### 5.4.3.3. NADBATH-REHMAN TECHNIQUE RISKS

⚠️ **Serious potential complications:**
* **Anatomy:** 27-gauge, 12-mm needle between mastoid process and mandibular ramus
* **Risk factor:** Jugular foramen proximity (10mm medial to stylomastoid foramen)
* **Cranial nerve complications:** CN IX, X, XI paralysis causing:
  * Hoarseness
  * Dysphagia  
  * Secretion pooling
  * Agitation, respiratory distress
  * Laryngospasm

**Contraindication:** Not recommended for outpatients due to complete hemifacial akinesia interfering with oral intake.

### 5.4.4. COMPLICATIONS OF NEEDLE-BASED OPHTHALMIC ANESTHESIA `[COMP: PC]`

#### 5.4.4.1. LOCAL COMPLICATIONS

##### 5.4.4.1.1. HEMORRHAGE - SUPERFICIAL VS. DEEP

**Superficial hemorrhage:**
* **Manifestation:** Circumorbital hematoma
* **Clinical significance:** Cosmetic issue, usually not serious

**Retrobulbar hemorrhage:**
* **Arterial source:** Precipitous bleeding, dramatic IOP increase
* **Clinical signs:** Globe proptosis, upper lid entrapment, palpable pressure increase
* **Vascular compromise:** Long-term visual acuity at risk
* **Emergency management:**
  * Immediate ophthalmology consultation
  * Fundoscopic examination
  * Tonometry (IOP measurement)
  * Ultrasound assessment
  * Possible lateral canthotomy

⚠️ **Monitoring requirement:** Continuous ECG due to oculocardiac reflex risk as blood extravasates.

##### 5.4.4.1.2. GLOBE PENETRATION/PERFORATION

**Definitions:**
* **Penetration:** Single full-thickness entry wound
* **Perforation:** Entry and exit wounds

**Most common location:** Posterior pole

**Risk factors for needle injury:**
* **Elongated globe:** Myopia, scleral buckle presence
* **Recessed orb:** Enophthalmos
* **Atypical eye shape:** Staphyloma (abnormal outpouching)
* **Anesthesiologist experience level:** Inverse relationship with complications

**Consequences:**
* **Mechanical trauma:** Potential retinal detachment
* **Chemical injury:** Local anesthetic toxicity to retinal tissue
* **Visual outcome:** Blindness or severe visual impairment possible

##### 5.4.4.1.3. RISK ASSESSMENT AND PREVENTION

**Preoperative evaluation essentials:**
* **History:** Childhood myopia correction (high sensitivity/specificity for elongated axial length)
* **Physical examination:** Globe position, enophthalmos detection
* **Ultrasound results:** **Critical - axial length >26mm = high risk**

💡 **Essential safety practice:** Always review preoperative ultrasound results before needle-based blocks.

**Future technology:** Real-time ultrasound guidance may reduce penetration risk through direct needle visualization.

#### 5.4.4.2. ULTRASOUND SAFETY CONSIDERATIONS

⚠️ **FDA restrictions for ophthalmic ultrasound:**
* **Mechanical index limit:** ≤0.23
* **Thermal index limit:** <1
* **Rationale:** Prevent thermal and mechanical bioeffects

**Equipment compliance:** Commercial anesthesia ultrasound transducers may not meet ophthalmic safety standards.

#### 5.4.4.3. SYSTEMIC COMPLICATIONS

##### 5.4.4.3.1. INTRA-ARTERIAL INJECTION

**Mechanism:**
* Retrograde flow via ophthalmic artery branch
* Internal carotid artery access
* Circle of Willis redistribution
* **Result:** Immediate seizures, cardiopulmonary instability

##### 5.4.4.3.2. BRAINSTEM ANESTHESIA

**Mechanism:**
* Direct local anesthetic spread along optic nerve meningeal sheath
* **Onset:** Typically not immediate (unlike intra-arterial injection)
* **Severity:** Depends on concentration, volume, and CNS distribution area

**Clinical manifestations:**
* **Contralateral eye examination:** Reveals amaurosis, mydriasis, extraocular muscle paresis
* **Cranial nerve involvement:** CN VI, III more common than CN IV
* **Severe CNS signs:** 
  * Violent shivering
  * Loss of consciousness
  * Apnea
  * Hemiplegia, paraplegia, quadriplegia
  * Hyperreflexia

**Nicoll et al. series:** 16 cases in 6,000 intraconal blocks
* **Respiratory arrest:** 8 patients
* **Critical requirement:** Personnel skilled in airway management, ventilatory support, circulatory support must be immediately available

⚠️ **Lower incidence:** Extraconal blocks associated with less brainstem anesthesia risk than intraconal blocks.

### 5.4.5. CANNULA-BASED TECHNIQUES - SUB-TENON BLOCK

#### 5.4.5.1. HISTORICAL DEVELOPMENT

* **1884:** Turnbull performed first sub-Tenon block (cocaine drops)
* **1956:** Swan formally described cannula-based technique
* **1990s:** Rediscovered and popularized as safer alternative

#### 5.4.5.2. TECHNIQUE OVERVIEW

**Anatomical approach:**
* Incision through conjunctiva and Tenon capsule
* Blunt cannula insertion into episcleral space
* Local anesthetic instillation beneath Tenon capsule

**Imaging evidence:** Local anesthetics spread into posterior orbit from sub-Tenon space.

#### 5.4.5.3. CLINICAL CHARACTERISTICS

* **Onset:** Rapid analgesia
* **Akinesia:** Proportional to volume injected
* **Safety advantage:** Decreased posterior pole penetration risk (especially myopic patients)

#### 5.4.5.4. TECHNIQUE VARIATIONS

**Standard approach:**
* **Quadrant selection:** Most commonly inferonasal
* **Instruments:** Forceps and blunt-tipped scissors
* **Cannula guidance:** Toothless forceps assistance

**Modern variations:**
* **Ultrashort cannulae:** 6-mm length
* **Needle-based episcleral techniques**
* **Incisionless techniques:** Triport cannula without scissors
* **Probe-based methods:** Metal or plastic conjunctival probes

#### 5.4.5.5. ADVANTAGES AND DISADVANTAGES

**Advantages:**
* **High myopia safety:** Reduced penetration risk with elongated axial length
* **Anticoagulated patients:** May be safer than needle-based techniques
* **Guise study:** 6,000 blocks with high effectiveness, <10% minor hemorrhage incidence

**Disadvantages:**
* **Cosmetic issues:** Conjunctival bleeding, chemosis, ballooning
* **Learning curve:** Anesthesiologist reluctance to use scissors
* **Geographic variation:** Less common in North America vs. Europe

#### 5.4.5.6. COMPLICATIONS

**Major complications (rare but serious):**
* Globe perforation
* Hemorrhage
* Rectus muscle trauma
* Postoperative strabismus
* Orbital cellulitis
* Optic nerve neuritis
* Brainstem anesthesia

**Equipment-related factors:**
* **Longer rigid cannulae (18-25mm):** More complications
* **Shorter flexible cannulae (12mm):** Higher incidence of conjunctival bleeding/chemosis

⚠️ **Reported fatality:** One death associated with sub-Tenon block, potentially from central local anesthetic spread (definitive pathogenesis remains unclear).

### 5.4.6. TOPICAL ANALGESIA TECHNIQUES

#### 5.4.6.1. HISTORICAL CONTEXT AND MODERN RESURGENCE

* **Late 1800s/early 1900s:** Popular technique
* **1930s decline:** Improved needle-based and general anesthesia safety
* **Modern advantages:** Clear corneal incision techniques, improved cataract surgery control

#### 5.4.6.2. PATIENT SELECTION CRITERIA

**Ideal candidates:**
* Alert and able to follow instructions
* Capable of refraining from eye movement and lid squeezing
* Anticoagulated patients (no bleeding risk)
* Monocular patients (avoid prolonged local anesthetic amaurosis)

**Exclusion criteria:**
* Dementia or cognitive impairment
* Photophobia
* Communication barriers
* Active infection
* Dense cataracts requiring significant iris manipulation
* Large scleral incisions anticipated

#### 5.4.6.3. APPLICATION METHODS

**Drops vs. gels:**
* **Gels:** Higher anterior chamber drug concentrations, superior surface analgesia
* **Safety consideration:** Potential endophthalmitis barrier to bactericidal agents
* **Timing:** Apply gels after antiseptic solutions, anesthetic drops before bactericidal preparations

#### 5.4.6.4. SUPPLEMENTATION TECHNIQUES

**Intracameral injection:**
* **Volume:** 0.1-0.2 mL preservative-free 1% lidocaine
* **Administration:** Direct anterior chamber injection

**"Shugarcaine" admixture:**
* **Components:** Preservative-free lidocaine + bisulfite-free epinephrine in salt solution
* **Effects:** Analgesia, pupil dilation, iris stabilization
* **Special indication:** Floppy iris syndrome in benign prostatic hyperplasia patients

#### 5.4.6.5. FLOPPY IRIS SYNDROME

**Association:** Strong correlation with α1-selective adrenergic antagonists
* **High-risk medications:** α1a class (tamsulosin, silodosin)
* **Clinical triad:** Poor pupillary dilation, floppy iris tissue, iris prolapse tendency
* **Complication risk:** Higher rate of cataract surgical complications
* **Persistence:** Symptoms continue >1 year after drug discontinuation

### 5.4.7. LOCAL ANESTHETIC SELECTION AND ADJUVANTS

#### 5.4.7.1. ANESTHETIC AGENT SELECTION PRINCIPLES

**Fast-onset, brief-duration agents:**
* **Indications:** Cataract surgery, pterygium excision
* **Examples:** Lidocaine, mepivacaine

**Longer-acting agents:**
* **Indications:** Vitreoretinal surgery, lengthy procedures
* **Examples:** Bupivacaine, ropivacaine

#### 5.4.7.2. MIXING LOCAL ANESTHETICS - EVIDENCE REVIEW

**Traditional practice:** Combine different agents for rapid onset + prolonged duration  
**Clinical reality:** May not provide true advantage
* **Dilution effect:** Each agent's concentration reduced
* **pH interactions:** May lower unionized fraction available for nerve penetration

#### 5.4.7.3. CONCENTRATION-DEPENDENT TOXICITY

⚠️ **Bupivacaine 0.75%:** Demonstrated extraocular muscle toxicity potential  
**Safer alternatives:** Lower concentrations without myotoxicity

**Ropivacaine advantages:**
* **Concentrations:** 0.75% or 1% effective
* **Safety profile:** Less cardiotoxicity than 0.75% bupivacaine
* **Combination potential:** Effective alone or with lidocaine

#### 5.4.7.4. ADJUVANT MEDICATIONS

##### 5.4.7.4.1. EPINEPHRINE

* **Mechanism:** Delays drug washout, prolongs block duration
* **Concern:** May compromise retinal perfusion
* ⚠️ **Contraindication:** Avoid in glaucomatous optic nerve damage patients

##### 5.4.7.4.2. HYALURONIDASE - THE MOST IMPORTANT ADJUVANT

**Historical introduction:** Atkinson 1949  
**Mechanism:** Hydrolyzes hyaluronic acid, increases tissue permeability  
**Dosing range:** 0.75-300 International Units per mL

**Clinical benefits:**
* Promotes local anesthetic dispersion
* Reduces orbital pressure from injection volume
* Enhances blockade quality
* **Safety benefit:** May reduce local anesthetic-induced extraocular muscle injury

**Evidence for safety importance:**
* **1998 and 2000 shortages:** Clustered increases in postoperative diplopia
* **Subsequent studies:** Support protective role against muscle complications

**Quality assurance considerations:**
* **Compounding risks:** Tainted medications from local facilities causing deaths/blindness
* **Current availability:** Human recombinant formulation widely available
* **Recommendation:** Use pharmaceutical-grade rather than compounded formulations

#### 5.4.7.5. PRESSURE REDUCTION DEVICES

**Purpose:** Globe softening before surgery  
**Examples:** 
* Super Pinky ball
* Honan Intraocular Pressure Reducer (pneumatic with safety features)

**Application:** 5-20 minutes post-block, can achieve below-baseline IOP

⚠️ **Serious complication risk:** Excessive pressure causing ischemic optic neuropathy or central retinal artery occlusion leading to blindness.

**Modern relevance:** Less need with smaller incisions, lower-profile lenses, topical analgesia trends.

**Bottom Line:** Regional anesthesia techniques for ophthalmic surgery range from needle-based intraconal and extraconal blocks to cannula-based sub-Tenon blocks and topical methods. Each technique has specific indications, advantages, and complication profiles. Success requires thorough anatomical knowledge, appropriate patient selection, meticulous technique, and readiness for complication management. The choice of local anesthetic and adjuvants should be evidence-based, with hyaluronidase being the most important adjuvant for safety and efficacy.

## 5.5. GENERAL ANESTHESIA CONSIDERATIONS

### 5.5.1. INDICATIONS FOR GENERAL ANESTHESIA [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S13 Ophthalmic Procedures|ⓘ]]

**Primary indications requiring general anesthesia:**

* **Pediatric patients:** Infants and children unable to cooperate
* **Adult incapacity:** Patients unable to communicate, cooperate, or remain stationary
* **Patient preference:** Despite ability to tolerate regional anesthesia
* **Surgical requirements:** Procedures requiring absolute immobility
* **Failed regional anesthesia:** Inadequate block requiring conversion

### 5.5.2. AIRWAY MANAGEMENT OPTIONS

#### 5.5.2.1. SUPRAGLOTTIC AIRWAYS (SGAS)

**Increasing acceptance for ophthalmic surgery with significant advantages:**

**Benefits over endotracheal tubes:**
* **IOP advantages:** Less IOP increase on insertion and removal
* **Emergence benefits:** Reduced bucking and coughing during recovery
* **Insertion:** Possible without neuromuscular blocking agents

**Safety considerations:**
* **Patient selection:** Minimal aspiration risk required
* **Monitoring:** Vigilance for intraoperative displacement
* **Pediatric caution:** Possible laryngospasm in infants and neonates

**Clinical evidence:**
* Safe and effective for eye surgery
* Lower IOP impact compared to endotracheal intubation
* Preferred by many anesthesiologists for routine cases

#### 5.5.2.2. ENDOTRACHEAL INTUBATION

**Reserved for higher-risk patients:**
* **Aspiration risk:** Full stomach, GERD, delayed gastric emptying
* **Airway concerns:** Difficult ventilation anticipated
* **Positioning requirements:** Prone positioning, head inaccessibility
* **Surgeon preference:** Some prefer guaranteed airway security

### 5.5.3. IOP MANAGEMENT DURING GENERAL ANESTHESIA

#### 5.5.3.1. EMERGENCE STRATEGIES

**Critical period:** Transition from anesthesia to awakening with highest IOP risk

**IOP elevation prevention techniques:**
* **Deep extubation:** Remove ETT at surgical depth of anesthesia
* **Pharmacological adjuncts:**
  * IV lidocaine: Reduces cough reflex
  * Opioids: Smooth emergence
  * Dexmedetomidine: Dose-dependent cough and agitation reduction

**Dexmedetomidine protocol:**
* **Dosing:** 0.25-1.0 mcg/kg
* **Timing:** 15 minutes prior to emergence
* **Effect:** Dose-dependent reduction in coughing and agitation

### 5.5.4. HISTORICAL VS. MODERN APPROACHES

#### 5.5.4.1. TRADITIONAL TECHNIQUES

**Historical approach for large incision surgery:**
* General endotracheal anesthesia
* Deep, sustained neuromuscular paralysis
* Sandbag head immobilization
* **Goal:** Absolute perioperative immobility

#### 5.5.4.2. MODERN PREFERENCES

**Current practice evolution:**
* **Smaller incisions:** Reduced immobility requirements
* **SGA preference:** When appropriate for patient safety
* **Regional emphasis:** Most adult cases suitable for regional techniques
* **General anesthesia:** Reserved for specific indications

### 5.5.5. PEDIATRIC ANESTHETIC CONSIDERATIONS

#### 5.5.5.1. AGE-RELATED FACTORS

* **Physiological differences:** Drug metabolism, cardiovascular responses
* **Psychological factors:** Separation anxiety, cooperation challenges
* **Equipment considerations:** Age-appropriate airway devices, monitoring

#### 5.5.5.2. SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS

* **Induction techniques:** Inhalational vs. IV induction preferences
* **Maintenance strategies:** Balanced anesthesia approaches
* **Recovery planning:** PONV prevention, pain management

### 5.5.6. MONITORED ANESTHESIA CARE (MAC) PRINCIPLES

#### 5.5.6.1. DEFINITION AND GOALS

**MAC should represent "Maximum Anesthesia Caution, not Minimal Anesthesiology Care"**

#### 5.5.6.2. SEDATION STRATEGY FOR REGIONAL BLOCKS

**Pre-block sedation considerations:**
* **Comfort and amnesia:** Appropriate agent selection
* **Avoid polypharmacy:** Especially in geriatric patients
* **Prevent deep sedation:** High-dose opioids, benzodiazepines, hypnotics inappropriate

⚠️ **Dangerous practice:** Deep sedation without airway control combines general anesthesia disadvantages without controllability benefits.

#### 5.5.6.3. INTRAOPERATIVE SEDATION GOALS

**Post-block patient state:**
* **Relaxed but responsive:** Avoid head movement from snoring
* **Prevent sudden awakening movements:** Gradual emergence preferred
* **Maintain airway patency:** Patient responsibility for ventilation
* **Rational command response:** Essential for safety

⚠️ **Leading cause of liability:** Patient movement during monitored anesthesia care causes eye injury and anesthesiologist liability in >80% of MAC closed claims.

#### 5.5.6.4. UNDERSEDATION RISKS

**Cardiovascular consequences:**
* Tachycardia and hypertension from anxiety
* **Special concern:** Coronary artery disease patients
* **Balance required:** Adequate anxiolysis without oversedation

### 5.5.7. ENVIRONMENTAL AND SAFETY CONSIDERATIONS

#### 5.5.7.1. OPERATING ROOM ENVIRONMENT

**Unique challenges:**
* **Anesthesiologist positioning:** Remote from patient's airway
* **Surgeon location:** At patient's head, sharing airway access
* **Drape configuration:** May limit access and create claustrophobic environment

#### 5.5.7.2. PATIENT POSITIONING AND COMFORT

**Essential considerations:**
* **Orthopedic limitations:** Arthritis, joint deformities require meticulous positioning
* **Comfort padding:** Prevent pressure points and discomfort
* **Operating table adjustments:** Optimize patient stability and comfort

#### 5.5.7.3. VENTILATION AND FIRE SAFETY

**Face mask ventilation considerations:**
* **CO2 accumulation:** Ensure adequate ventilation around face
* **Oxygen supplementation:** Can delay desaturation signs but may accumulate under drapes

⚠️ **Fire hazards:**
* **Supplemental oxygen:** Contributes to surgical fires, especially with electrocautery
* **Prevention strategies:** Consider air or air/oxygen mixtures for oculoplastic procedures
* **Drape management:** Avoid tightly occluded drapes promoting oxygen accumulation

**MAC fire statistics:** Burn injuries during facial surgery with supplemental oxygen account for nearly one-third of MAC closed claims cases.

#### 5.5.7.4. TEMPERATURE MANAGEMENT

**Importance for ophthalmic surgery:**
* **Shivering prevention:** Dangerous during delicate eye procedures
* **Cardiovascular considerations:** Shivering increases cardiac workload
* **Patient comfort:** Maintain normothermia throughout procedure

#### 5.5.7.5. COMPREHENSIVE MONITORING REQUIREMENTS

**Standard ASA monitoring:**
* **Continuous ECG:** Essential for oculocardiac reflex detection
* **Pulse oximetry:** Mandatory for all patients
* **Blood pressure:** Regular monitoring for cardiovascular stability
* **End-tidal CO2:** Clinical signs plus capnometry for ventilation assessment

### 5.5.8. EVIDENCE FOR MAC IN CATARACT SURGERY

#### 5.5.8.1. ROSENFELD STUDY FINDINGS

**Prospective study of 1,006 consecutive cataract operations:**
* **Intervention rate:** 37% of patients required some type of intervention
* **Unpredictability:** Majority of interventions couldn't be predicted preoperatively
* **Age factor:** Patients <60 years required intervention >60% of cases

#### 5.5.8.2. TYPES OF INTERVENTIONS

**Range from minor to critical:**
* **Minor:** Verbal reassurance, hand holding
* **Moderate:** IV medications (sedation, antihypertensive, antiarrhythmic agents)
* **Major:** Respiratory assistance
* **Critical designation:** 30% considered critical to surgical success

#### 5.5.8.3. RISK FACTORS

**Identified predictors (limited predictive value):**
* Hypertension
* Lung disease  
* Renal disease
* Cancer diagnosis

**Important limitation:** These four conditions combined accounted for only small portion of needed interventions.

#### 5.5.8.4. STUDY CONCLUSIONS

**Evidence supporting MAC:**
* Qualified anesthesia personnel MAC is reasonable and justified
* Contributes to quality of patient care for local anesthesia cataract surgery
* Interventions often unpredictable, requiring anesthesia expertise

#### 5.5.8.5. INTERNATIONAL PRACTICE PATTERNS

**Anesthesia involvement varies globally:**
* **United States:** 97% monitoring equipment use, 78% anesthesiologist presence
* **Australia:** 97% anesthesia-trained personnel
* **Malaysia:** 31% anesthesia monitoring
* **Thailand:** 18% anesthesia monitoring

### 5.5.9. PATIENT SATISFACTION AND VALUE

#### 5.5.9.1. FUNG STUDY RESULTS

**Community-based cataract surgery satisfaction assessment:**
* **Finding:** Patient value and regard for anesthesiologist role increased from preoperative to postoperative interviews
* **Implication:** Even with topical anesthesia (less profound analgesia), patients recognize anesthesia care value

#### 5.5.9.2. SURGICAL FOCUS REQUIREMENTS

**Microsurgical demands:**
* Ophthalmologist attention must remain focused on surgical field
* **Ethical imperative:** Surgeon distraction from microsurgical field inappropriate
* **Patient safety:** Anesthesiologist monitoring allows surgeon concentration

**Bottom Line:** General anesthesia for ophthalmic surgery has evolved from routine endotracheal intubation with paralysis to selective use of SGAs and MAC. The key principles include appropriate patient selection, IOP management during emergence, comprehensive monitoring, and environmental safety considerations. Evidence supports the value of anesthesia care even for procedures under topical anesthesia, with patient satisfaction and safety outcomes supporting continued anesthesia involvement.

**Specific clinical scenarios in ophthalmic anesthesia require specialized knowledge and rapid decision-making to ensure optimal patient outcomes.**

## 6.2. OPEN-EYE, FULL-STOMACH CASES [PART B/C]

### 6.2.1. CLINICAL CHALLENGE DEFINITION [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C4 Trauma Resuscitation and Crisis Management|ⓘ]] `[COMP: PC]`

**Complex risk-benefit analysis balancing aspiration risk against vision-threatening IOP elevation.**

* **Dual threats:**
  * **Aspiration risk:** Full stomach, emergency presentation
  * **Vision loss risk:** IOP elevation causing globe content extrusion

* **Decision complexity:**
  * Weigh aspiration risk against blindness risk
  * Consider airway management options
  * Evaluate polytrauma possibilities

### 6.2.2. REGIONAL ANESTHESIA CONSIDERATIONS

#### 6.2.2.1. HISTORICAL CONTRAINDICATION

**Traditional teaching:**
* Regional anesthesia contraindicated for penetrating eye injuries
* **Rationale:** Local anesthetic injection pressure could extrude intraocular contents
* **Additional concerns:** Needle instrumentation, lid squeezing, bleeding pressure

#### 6.2.2.2. CONTEMPORARY EVIDENCE AND PRACTICE EVOLUTION

**Scott et al. landmark study:**
* **Population:** 220 disrupted eyes over 4-year period
* **Technique:** Regional anesthesia successfully used
* **Injury types:** Intraocular foreign bodies, dehisced cataract/corneal transplant incisions
* **Selection criteria:** More anterior, smaller wounds compared to GA cases
* **Outcomes:** No difference in visual acuity changes between regional vs. general anesthesia

**Additional evidence:**
* **Combined topical anesthesia and sedation:** Successful case reports for selected open-globe injuries
* **Facility considerations:** Ambulatory surgery centers managing trauma cases when hospitals lack ophthalmologist privileges

### 6.2.3. GENERAL ANESTHESIA - STANDARD APPROACH

#### 6.2.3.1. CURRENT PRACTICE PATTERNS

**Retina subspecialist survey findings:**
* **Corneal ruptured globe:** 90% general anesthesia
* **Scleral ruptured globe:** 94% general anesthesia  
* **Intraocular foreign body:** 62% general anesthesia

#### 6.2.3.2. TIMING AND URGENCY CLASSIFICATION

**Most cases allow optimal timing:**
* **Urgent but not emergent:** Majority of open-globe injuries
* **Fasting time:** Allow appropriate NPO periods when possible
* **True emergencies:** Small subset requiring immediate intervention

#### 6.2.3.3. AIRWAY MANAGEMENT STRATEGY

**Adequate fasting time available:**
* **Preferred approach:** Smooth IV induction without paralysis
* **Airway management:** Supraglottic airway insertion
* **Advantage:** Avoids IOP spikes from intubation and neuromuscular blockade

**Emergent cases requiring immediate surgery:**
* **Standard approach:** IV induction with neuromuscular blocking agent
* **Essential requirements:** 
  * Appropriate anesthetic depth confirmation
  * Neuromuscular blockade onset verification with quantitative monitoring
  * Controlled induction preventing coughing/straining

### 6.2.4. NEUROMUSCULAR BLOCKING AGENT SELECTION

#### 6.2.4.1. SUCCINYLCHOLINE CONTROVERSY - HISTORICAL PERSPECTIVE

**Historical concerns:**
* **IOP elevation:** 9 mmHg average increase within 1-4 minutes
* **Theoretical risk:** Vitreous expulsion with compromised globe integrity
* **Confounding factors:** Many reports lack clear causation attribution

**Lincoff et al. 1957 watershed report:**
* **Quote:** "Various communications… reports of cases in which succinylcholine was given to forestall impending vitreous prolapse only to have a prompt expulsion of vitreous occur"
* **Critical analysis:** Extremely difficult to attribute vitreous loss directly to succinylcholine in desperate circumstances

#### 6.2.4.2. MODERN EVIDENCE AND PERSPECTIVE

**Limited causation evidence:**
* **Few clear reports:** Difficult to separate succinylcholine effects from other factors
* **Clinical reality:** Many confounders in emergency open-globe situations
* **Risk-benefit:** May be acceptable when benefits of rapid, reliable intubation outweigh theoretical IOP risks

**Advantages of succinylcholine:**
* **Swift onset:** Rapid securing of airway
* **Maximal intubating conditions:** Superior to other agents
* **Brief duration:** Quick recovery if problems arise

#### 6.2.4.3. ROCURONIUM-SUGAMMADEX ALTERNATIVE

**Modern "open-globe, full-stomach" paradigm:**

**Rocuronium protocol:**
* **Dose:** 1.2 mg/kg IV (rapid onset dose)
* **Onset:** Comparable to succinylcholine at this dose
* **Historical disadvantage:** Prolonged duration

**Sugammadex reversal:**
* **Rapid reversal:** >90% train-of-four recovery in <120 seconds
* **Clinical advantage:** Rapid termination if "can't intubate, can't ventilate" situation

**New paradigm benefits:**
* **Rapid sequence induction:** High-dose rocuronium for swift intubation conditions
* **Emergency reversal option:** Sugammadex available for crisis situations
* **IOP advantage:** Avoids succinylcholine-induced IOP elevation

### 6.2.5. DIFFICULT AIRWAY CONSIDERATIONS

#### 6.2.5.1. PRE-EXISTING AIRWAY ABNORMALITIES

**Risk assessment considerations:**
* **Patient anatomy:** History of difficult intubation
* **Anesthetic history:** Previous airway management problems
* **Current injury:** Facial trauma, airway compromise

#### 6.2.5.2. CONSULTATION AND PLANNING

**Ophthalmologist consultation:**
* **Injury assessment:** Probability of saving vision
* **Surgical urgency:** True emergency vs. delayed approach
* **Risk communication:** Visual prognosis discussion

#### 6.2.5.3. AWAKE FIBEROPTIC INTUBATION

**Indications:**
* **Anticipated difficult airway:** Previous history or anatomical concerns
* **High aspiration risk:** When regional techniques inappropriate
* **Patient cooperation:** Able to tolerate awake approach

**Considerations:**
* **IOP elevation risk:** Patient gagging/coughing during procedure
* **Thorough topical anesthesia:** Essential for minimizing IOP spikes
* **Risk-benefit balance:** Weigh IOP elevation against oxygenation/ventilation failure

### 6.2.6. DECISION-MAKING FRAMEWORK

#### 6.2.6.1. PRIMARY DECISION TREE

1. **Assess fasting status:**
   * Adequate fasting → IV induction + SGA (preferred)
   * Full stomach → Proceed to step 2

2. **Evaluate airway difficulty:**
   * Normal airway → RSI with NMB
   * Difficult airway → Consider awake fiberoptic vs. regional anesthesia

3. **Consider injury characteristics:**
   * Small, anterior wounds → Regional anesthesia possible
   * Large, posterior wounds → General anesthesia preferred

4. **Assess surgical urgency:**
   * True emergency → Immediate GA
   * Urgent but stable → Allow fasting time if possible

#### 6.2.6.2. RISK STRATIFICATION

**Low-risk scenarios:**
* Small anterior wounds
* Stable patient
* Time for fasting
* Normal airway

**High-risk scenarios:**
* Large posterior wounds  
* Unstable polytrauma
* Full stomach + difficult airway
* Vitreous already prolapsing

**Bottom Line:** Open-eye, full-stomach cases require individualized risk-benefit analysis considering aspiration risk versus vision-threatening IOP elevation. Modern approaches include expanded use of regional anesthesia for selected cases and rocuronium-sugammadex as an alternative to succinylcholine for rapid sequence induction. The key is matching the anesthetic technique to the specific clinical scenario while maintaining readiness for emergency airway management.

## 6.3. INTRAOCULAR SURGERY

### 6.3.1. SURGICAL CATEGORIES AND REQUIREMENTS [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S13 Ophthalmic Procedures|ⓘ]]

**Intraocular procedures demand precise IOP control and absolute patient immobility.**

#### 6.3.1.1. PROCEDURE CLASSIFICATIONS

**High-precision intraocular surgeries:**
* **Glaucoma drainage surgery**
* **Open-sky vitrectomy**
* **Penetrating keratoplasty (corneal transplantation)**
* **Traditional intracapsular cataract extraction**

#### 6.3.1.2. CRITICAL IOP MANAGEMENT

**Pre-incision requirements:**
* **Target IOP:** Low-normal levels essential
* **Rationale:** Once scleral incision made, IOP equals atmospheric pressure
* **Risk of elevated IOP:** Abrupt decompression can cause:
  * Iris prolapse
  * Lens prolapse
  * Vitreous loss
  * Expulsive choroidal hemorrhage

### 6.3.2. ANESTHETIC TECHNIQUE COMPARISONS

#### 6.3.2.1. LOCAL VS. GENERAL ANESTHESIA OUTCOMES

**Complication rate analysis:**
* **Available data:** No major difference in complication rates
* **Similar outcomes:** Vitreous loss and iris prolapse rates comparable
* **Technique selection:** Based on patient and surgical factors rather than outcome differences

#### 6.3.2.2. PATIENT SELECTION FACTORS

**Optimal candidates for local anesthesia:**
* Alert and cooperative patients
* Ability to remain motionless
* Absence of claustrophobia
* Appropriate anxiety levels

**General anesthesia indications:**
* Pediatric patients
* Cognitive impairment
* Movement disorders
* Patient preference
* Surgeon preference for complex cases

### 6.3.3. SURGICAL TECHNIQUES REQUIRING SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS

#### 6.3.3.1. PUPILLARY DILATION MANAGEMENT

**Maximal pupillary dilation importance:**
* **Critical for:** Most intraocular surgical procedures
* **Visualization requirements:** Adequate surgical field access
* **Technique limitations:** Poor dilation complicates surgery

#### 6.3.3.2. INTRAOPERATIVE EPINEPHRINE INFUSION

**Continuous epinephrine technique:**
* **Concentration:** 1:200,000 in balanced salt solution
* **Delivery:** Small-gauge needle in anterior chamber
* **Mechanism:** 
  * Immediate iris dilation on contact
  * Simultaneous aspiration removes drug
  * Intense iris/ciliary body vasoconstriction limits systemic uptake

⚠️ **Systemic absorption pathways:**
* **Schlemm canal drainage:** Direct venous system access
* **Conjunctival spillover:** Vessel absorption
* **Nasal mucosa drainage:** Tear duct pathway

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The immediate dilation effect with simultaneous aspiration minimizes drug exposure while maximizing therapeutic effect.

### 6.3.4. ADVANCED SURGICAL TECHNIQUES

#### 6.3.4.1. MICROINCISION SURGERY

**Modern trends:**
* **Smaller incisions:** Reduced tissue trauma
* **Self-sealing wounds:** Decreased closure requirements
* **Faster healing:** Reduced inflammation and recovery time

#### 6.3.4.2. PHACOEMULSIFICATION CONSIDERATIONS

**Energy delivery effects:**
* **Ultrasonic energy:** Tissue heating concerns
* **Irrigation/aspiration:** Fluid dynamics management
* **Chamber stability:** Pressure maintenance critical

### 6.3.5. COMPLICATION PREVENTION AND MANAGEMENT

#### 6.3.5.1. EXPULSIVE CHOROIDAL HEMORRHAGE

**Most catastrophic complication:**
* **Mechanism:** Sudden, massive bleeding into suprachoroidal space
* **Risk factors:** Elevated IOP at incision, elderly patients, myopia
* **Consequences:** Potential complete vision loss, globe rupture

**Prevention strategies:**
* **Pre-incision IOP reduction:** Mannitol, acetazolamide
* **Smooth anesthesia:** Prevent coughing, straining, hypertension
* **Patient positioning:** Avoid excessive Trendelenburg
* **Surgical timing:** Coordinate with anesthesia for optimal conditions

#### 6.3.5.2. VITREOUS LOSS MANAGEMENT

**When vitreous presents at surgical wound:**
* **Immediate recognition:** Stop surgery, assess extent
* **Vitrectomy consultation:** May require specialist intervention
* **Anesthetic considerations:** Maintain stable conditions for extended procedure

### 6.3.6. SPECIAL PATIENT POPULATIONS

#### 6.3.6.1. PEDIATRIC INTRAOCULAR SURGERY

**Unique considerations:**
* **Congenital cataracts:** Time-sensitive for visual development
* **Congenital glaucoma:** IOP measurement under anesthesia
* **Anesthetic effects:** Ketamine may not alter IOP measurements in infants

#### 6.3.6.2. HIGH MYOPIA PATIENTS

**Increased surgical risks:**
* **Globe length:** Increased perforation risk with injections
* **Retinal detachment:** Higher baseline risk
* **Scleral rigidity:** May be decreased

#### 6.3.6.3. GLAUCOMA PATIENTS

**Special anesthetic considerations:**
* **Baseline elevated IOP:** Less tolerance for further increases
* **Medication interactions:** Multiple topical medications
* **Advanced disease:** Higher risk of vision loss from IOP spikes

**Bottom Line:** Intraocular surgery requires meticulous IOP control and patient immobility to prevent catastrophic complications. Success depends on coordinated care between anesthesiologist and surgeon, with emphasis on pre-incision IOP reduction and maintenance of stable surgical conditions throughout the procedure.

## 6.4. RETINAL DETACHMENT SURGERY

### 6.4.1. SURGICAL APPROACHES AND ANESTHETIC IMPLICATIONS [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S13 Ophthalmic Procedures|ⓘ]]

**Retinal detachment repair involves multiple surgical techniques with specific anesthetic considerations.**

#### 6.4.1.1. EXTERNAL APPROACHES

**Scleral buckling procedures:**
* **Technique:** Synthetic silicone band placement around globe
* **Anesthetic concerns:** Globe manipulation and rotation
* **Oculocardiac reflex risk:** Extraocular muscle traction triggers reflex
* **Monitoring requirement:** Vigilant cardiac rhythm surveillance

**Preoperative IOP reduction:**
* **Pharmacological agents:** IV acetazolamide or mannitol
* **Purpose:** Globe softening facilitates buckling procedure
* **Surgeon request:** Anticipate need for IOP-lowering medications

#### 6.4.1.2. INTERNAL APPROACHES - VITREORETINAL SURGERY

**Vitrectomy with fluid/air exchange:**
* **Surgical technique:** Vitreous humor removal with air replacement
* **Gas injection:** Expandable gases for internal tamponade
* **Anesthetic implications:** Critical nitrous oxide interactions

### 6.4.2. GAS-NITROUS OXIDE INTERACTIONS - CRITICAL SAFETY ISSUE

#### 6.4.2.1. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF GAS INTERACTION

**Blood-gas partition coefficient differences create dangerous interactions:**

| Gas | Blood-Gas Partition Coefficient |
|-----|--------------------------------|
| Perfluoropropane | 0.001 |
| Sulfur hexafluoride | 0.004 |
| Nitrogen | 0.015 |
| Nitrous oxide | 0.468 |

**Mechanism of interaction:**
* **N2O diffusibility:** Manyfold greater than perfluorocarbons
* **Rapid bubble expansion:** Can double or triple size within minutes
* **Peak expansion:** Within 20 minutes of gas injection

#### 6.4.2.2. INTRAOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT

**Nitrous oxide discontinuation:**
* **Timing:** Cease N2O 15 minutes before gas injection
* **Rationale:** Prevent significant bubble volume changes
* **Alternative maintenance:** Use air/oxygen mixtures or other agents

**Potential benefits vs. risks:**
* **Intraoperative expansion:** May enhance tamponade effect
* **Postoperative collapse:** Dramatic IOP drop when N2O discontinued
* **Net risk:** IOP instability outweighs potential benefits

### 6.4.3. POSTOPERATIVE NITROUS OXIDE CONTRAINDICATIONS

#### 6.4.3.1. DURATION OF CONTRAINDICATION BY GAS TYPE

**Evidence-based avoidance periods:**
* **Air injection:** 5 days
* **Sulfur hexafluoride:** 10 days  
* **Perfluoropropane:** 70+ days

**Stinson and Donlon recommendations confirmed by multiple studies.**

#### 6.4.3.2. PATIENT SAFETY MEASURES

**MedicAlert bracelet system:**
* **International cooperation:** Gas distributors, FDA, and manufacturers provide warning bracelets
* **Patient identification:** Clear warning against N2O administration
* **Healthcare provider alert:** Emergency and elective surgery teams warned

#### 6.4.3.3. DEVASTATING CASE REPORTS

**Seaberg et al. permanent vision loss cases:**
* **Case 1:** Diabetic 19-year-old, SF6 injection 25 days prior, received intraoperative N2O → CRAO and permanent blindness
* **Case 2:** Diabetic 37-year-old, perfluoropropane 41 days prior, received N2O → CRAO and permanent blindness

**High-risk populations:**
* **Diabetes patients:** Lower retinal arterial pressure
* **Elderly patients:** Reduced vascular reserve  
* **Atherosclerosis:** Compromised circulation
* **Risk mechanism:** Lower baseline retinal artery pressure increases CRAO susceptibility

⚠️ **Critical teaching point:** Resorption time is not always uniform or predictable, emphasizing the importance of patient identification systems.

### 6.4.4. VENOUS AIR EMBOLISM - EMERGING COMPLICATION

#### 6.4.4.1. HISTORICAL CONTEXT AND MODERN EMERGENCE

**Timeline of recognition:**
* **30-year safety record:** 1975-2005 with traditional sutured infusion
* **2002 technique change:** Introduction of unsutured cannulated sclerotomy  
* **2005 first report:** Beginning of venous air embolism case reports

#### 6.4.4.2. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

**Morris et al. proposed mechanism:**
* **Choroidal detachment:** Accidental creation during surgery
* **Cannula malposition:** Displacement into suprachoroidal space
* **Vortex vein entry:** Air under pressure enters torn vortex vein
* **Systemic embolization:** Direct route to systemic venous circulation

**Quantitative analysis:**
* **Infusion pressure:** 40 mmHg standard pressure
* **Flow rate:** 350 cc/min through 25-gauge cannula
* **Globe volume:** Only 5-6 cc total capacity
* **Lethal dose:** 200-300 cc air embolism potentially fatal

#### 6.4.4.3. CLINICAL RECOGNITION

**Early warning signs:**
* **End-tidal CO2:** Precipitous decline (most reliable early sign)
* **Cardiovascular:** Hypotension, dysrhythmias
* **Respiratory:** If using nasal cannula ETCO2, qualitative decrease noted

**Advanced manifestations:**
* **Cardiovascular collapse:** Severe hypotension, cardiac arrest
* **Pulmonary:** Acute right heart failure
* **Neurological:** If paradoxical embolism occurs

#### 6.4.4.4. PREVENTION STRATEGIES

**Team approach essentials:**
* **Formal timeout:** Verbal confirmation of cannula position
* **Continuous verification:** Throughout air infusion process
* **Pressure monitoring:** Anesthesiologist awareness of infusion pressures
* **Communication protocol:** Clear surgeon-anesthesiologist dialogue

#### 6.4.4.5. MANAGEMENT PROTOCOL

**Immediate interventions:**
* **Stop air infusion:** Cease fluid/air exchange immediately
* **Cardiopulmonary support:** ACLS protocols as needed
* **Patient positioning:** Left lateral decubitus, Trendelenburg if possible
* **High-flow oxygen:** 100% oxygen administration
* **Emergency transfer:** Higher-acuity facility with ECMO capability if available

#### 6.4.4.6. AMBULATORY SURGERY CENTER CONSIDERATIONS

**Limitations of outpatient facilities:**
* **Resuscitation capability:** Limited advanced cardiac life support
* **ECMO access:** Not available in ambulatory settings
* **Transfer protocols:** Must be established for emergency situations

**Risk mitigation:**
* **Case selection:** Consider facility capabilities
* **Emergency planning:** Established protocols for air embolism
* **Equipment availability:** Advanced monitoring, resuscitation drugs

### 6.4.5. SURGICAL URGENCY AND TIMING

#### 6.4.5.1. RHEGMATOGENOUS RETINAL DETACHMENT CLASSIFICATION

**Fovea-sparing detachments:**
* **Characteristics:** Macula remains attached, central vision preserved
* **Prognosis:** High likelihood of excellent visual acuity if repaired
* **Urgency level:** More urgent than fovea-involving
* **Safe delay:** 1-3 days without affecting visual outcome

**Fovea-involving detachments:**
* **Characteristics:** Macula detached, central vision compromised
* **Prognosis:** Lower probability of 20/40 or better vision
* **Timing flexibility:** Duration of detachment has minimal effect on outcome if repaired within ~1 week
* **Scheduling:** Can safely defer to next available elective slot

#### 6.4.5.2. INTRAOCULAR FOREIGN BODY MANAGEMENT

**Traditional teaching:** Surgery within 72 hours reduces endophthalmitis risk

**Contemporary evidence:**
* **Military experience:** Operation Iraqi Freedom and Operation Enduring Freedom
* **Median removal time:** 21 days
* **Complication rate:** Zero cases of endophthalmitis or deleterious effects
* **Practice implications:** More flexibility in scheduling urgent cases

### 6.4.6. ALTERNATIVE TAMPONADE AGENTS

#### 6.4.6.1. SILICONE OIL

**Advantages over gas:**
* **No N2O interaction:** Permanent vitreous substitute
* **Longer duration:** Months to years in place
* **Predictable behavior:** No expansion/contraction issues

**Disadvantages:**
* **Visual quality:** May cause decreased vision
* **Complications:** Cataract formation, glaucoma, corneal changes
* **Removal requirement:** Often needs subsequent surgery

#### 6.4.6.2. PERFLUOROCARBON LIQUIDS

**Intraoperative use:**
* **Heavy liquid advantage:** Sinks to dependent retina
* **Temporary tamponade:** Usually exchanged for gas or oil
* **Surgical tool:** Facilitates retinal manipulation

**Bottom Line:** Retinal detachment surgery presents unique anesthetic challenges including oculocardiac reflex management, critical gas-nitrous oxide interactions, and emerging venous air embolism risks. Understanding gas absorption timelines and implementing appropriate safety measures through MedicAlert bracelets is essential for preventing devastating postoperative complications. Modern surgical techniques require enhanced vigilance for air embolism prevention and management protocols.

## 6.5. STRABISMUS SURGERY

### 6.5.1. DEMOGRAPHICS AND PATHOPHYSIOLOGY [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S13 Ophthalmic Procedures|ⓘ]]

**Strabismus affects approximately 3% of the population and represents the most common pediatric ocular operation in the United States.**

#### 6.5.1.1. TYPES AND ETIOLOGY

**Infantile strabismus:**
* **Onset:** Within first 6 months of life, often noted neonatally
* **Associated conditions:** Higher incidence with CNS dysfunction
  * Cerebral palsy
  * Meningomyelocele with hydrocephalus

**Acquired strabismus:**
* **Causes:** 
  * Oculomotor nerve trauma
  * Sensory abnormalities (cataracts, refractive errors)
  * Adult-onset forms

#### 6.5.1.2. SURGICAL TECHNIQUES

**Muscle weakening (recession):**
* **Procedure:** Moving muscle insertion posteriorly on globe
* **Effect:** Reduces muscle action/strength

**Muscle strengthening (resection):**
* **Procedure:** Removing section of muscle tendon
* **Effect:** Increases muscle tension and action

### 6.5.2. ANESTHETIC COMPLICATIONS SPECIFIC TO STRABISMUS SURGERY

#### 6.5.2.1. OCULOCARDIAC REFLEX - ENHANCED RISK

**Higher incidence in pediatric strabismus:**
* **Population factor:** Children have higher baseline vagal tone
* **Trigger mechanism:** Direct extraocular muscle manipulation
* **Severity:** Can be more pronounced than in adult procedures

**Management strategies:**
* **Prophylactic atropine:** Some anesthesiologists use 0.02 mg/kg IV before surgery
* **Alternative:** Glycopyrrolate 0.01 mg/kg IV (less tachycardia)
* **Controversy:** Risk of atropine-induced dysrhythmias vs. reflex prevention

#### 6.5.2.2. MALIGNANT HYPERTHERMIA ASSOCIATION

**Historical concern - recent challenges:**
* **Traditional belief:** Strabismus/ptosis patients have increased MH risk
* **Rationale:** Susceptible individuals often have localized skeletal muscle weakness
* **Recent studies:** Challenge this association
* **Clinical approach:** Maintain awareness but recent evidence suggests lower risk than previously thought

#### 6.5.2.3. POSTOPERATIVE NAUSEA AND VOMITING (PONV)

**Oculogastric reflex:**
* **Mechanism:** Eye manipulation triggers nausea/vomiting
* **High incidence:** Without prophylaxis, very common complication

**Eberhart pediatric PONV risk score:**
* **Risk factors (4-point scale):**
  1. Surgery duration >30 minutes
  2. Age >3 years
  3. History of PONV/motion sickness
  4. Strabismus surgery

**Prevention strategies:**

##### 6.5.2.3.1. DROPERIDOL

* **High-dose:** 0.075 mg/kg at induction → ~10% PONV incidence
* **Low-dose:** 0.02 mg/kg immediately after induction
* **Effectiveness:** Reduces both incidence and severity
* **FDA controversy:** "Black box" warning for QT prolongation
  * **Dosing consideration:** PONV doses extremely low, unlikely cardiovascular risk
  * **Evidence quality concerns:** Validity of FDA conclusion questioned

##### 6.5.2.3.2. SEROTONIN RECEPTOR ANTAGONISTS

* **Options:** Ondansetron, dolasetron, granisetron, palonosetron
* **Efficacy:** Proven effective for PONV prevention
* **Standard practice:** Widely used first-line agents

##### 6.5.2.3.3. NEWER ANTIEMETIC CLASSES

* **NK1 receptor antagonists:** Aprepitant (40-hour half-life)
  * **Administration:** Oral preoperatively or IV at induction
* **Amisulpride:** Non-sedating dopamine receptor antagonist
  * **Advantages:** No QTc prolongation, no extrapyramidal effects
  * **Current approval:** Adults only

##### 6.5.2.3.4. COMBINATION THERAPY

* **Multi-modal approach:** Two different mechanism antiemetics plus glucocorticoid
* **Dexamethasone:** Commonly added to combination regimens
* **High-risk patients:** Multiple antiemetic approach recommended

**Anesthetic technique considerations:**
* **Total IV anesthesia (TIVA):** Lower PONV incidence with propofol
* **Trade-off:** Higher oculocardiac reflex incidence and bradycardia with TIVA
* **Narcotic avoidance:** May reduce PONV but evidence mixed

### 6.5.3. SUCCINYLCHOLINE AND FORCED DUCTION TEST INTERACTION

#### 6.5.3.1. FORCED DUCTION TEST (FDT) PURPOSE

**Clinical utility:**
* **Diagnostic tool:** Differentiates muscle paresis from restrictive forces
* **Surgical planning:** Informs treatment approach selection
* **Technique:** Forceps grasp of sclera near corneal limbus, manual eye movement assessment

#### 6.5.3.2. SUCCINYLCHOLINE INTERFERENCE

**France et al. quantitative study findings:**
* **IOP elevation duration:** <5 minutes typical
* **Muscle paralysis duration:** <5 minutes typical  
* **FDT effect duration:** 20-30 minutes prolonged return to baseline
* **Mechanism:** Unknown why FDT changes outlast other succinylcholine effects

**Clinical recommendations:**
* **Avoid succinylcholine:** When FDT planned, especially repeat strabismus surgery
* **Alternative approach:** If succinylcholine used, delay FDT ≥20 minutes
* **Modern practice:** Succinylcholine rarely needed for pediatric strabismus surgery

#### 6.5.3.3. CONTEMPORARY ANESTHETIC MANAGEMENT

**Current standard approaches:**
* **No paralytic technique:** Most cases don't require neuromuscular blockade
* **SGA preferred:** Laryngeal mask airway without muscle relaxants
* **Advantages:** 
  * Less hemodynamic perturbation
  * Reduced straining/coughing on removal
  * Avoids succinylcholine-FDT interaction

**Adult strabismus considerations:**
* **Regional anesthesia option:** Eye block with sedation for monocular disease
* **General anesthesia:** SGA without paralysis when GA required

### 6.5.4. ANESTHETIC TECHNIQUE SELECTION

#### 6.5.4.1. GENERAL ANESTHESIA APPROACHES

**Supraglottic airway advantages:**
* **No muscle relaxant requirement:** Insertion without paralysis
* **Hemodynamic stability:** Less cardiovascular perturbation than intubation
* **Smooth emergence:** Reduced airway irritation and coughing
* **Patient selection:** Minimal aspiration risk required

**Inhalational vs. TIVA considerations:**
* **Sevoflurane-based:** Traditional approach, higher PONV risk
* **Propofol TIVA:** Lower PONV but increased oculocardiac reflex
* **Balanced technique:** Combine benefits of both approaches

#### 6.5.4.2. REGIONAL ANESTHESIA IN ADULTS

**Appropriate candidates:**
* **Monocular strabismus:** Single eye procedures
* **Cooperative adults:** Able to remain still
* **Surgeon comfort:** Experience with regional techniques
* **Procedure complexity:** Less complex repairs suitable

#### 6.5.4.3. PEDIATRIC ANESTHESIA CONSIDERATIONS

**Induction techniques:**
* **Inhalational induction:** Often preferred in children
* **IV induction:** If IV access already established
* **Premedication:** Anxiolysis may be beneficial

**Maintenance strategies:**
* **Volatile anesthetics:** Traditional approach
* **Propofol infusion:** For PONV reduction
* **Narcotic-sparing:** May reduce PONV (evidence mixed)

### 6.5.5. OPIOID-SPARING ANALGESIA

#### 6.5.5.1. KETOROLAC EVIDENCE

**Munro et al. comparative study:**
* **Dose:** 0.75 mg/kg IV ketorolac
* **Comparison:** Equivalent analgesia to morphine
* **PONV advantage:** Much lower 24-hour nausea/vomiting incidence
* **Clinical application:** Effective opioid-sparing strategy

#### 6.5.5.2. REGIONAL ANESTHESIA FOR PEDIATRIC CASES

**Yao et al. pediatric vitreoretinal study:**
* **Technique:** Retrobulbar block during general anesthesia
* **Outcome:** Eliminated need for intraoperative fentanyl and postoperative analgesia
* **Safety:** Performed safely following induction
* **Application:** Opioid-reducing multimodal approach

### 6.5.6. SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS

#### 6.5.6.1. REPEAT STRABISMUS SURGERY

**Enhanced complexity:**
* **Scar tissue:** Previous surgery creates adhesions
* **FDT importance:** More critical for surgical planning
* **Avoid succinylcholine:** Interferes with diagnostic assessment
* **Longer procedures:** May require adjusted antiemetic strategy

#### 6.5.6.2. ASSOCIATED MEDICAL CONDITIONS

**Cerebral palsy patients:**
* **Seizure disorders:** Medication interactions
* **Gastroesophageal reflux:** Aspiration risk considerations
* **Positioning challenges:** Contractures, spasticity
* **Communication barriers:** Modified consent and cooperation issues

**Hydrocephalus patients:**
* **Intracranial pressure:** Positioning restrictions
* **Shunt presence:** Avoid pressure on shunt sites
* **Associated anomalies:** Multiple system involvement possible

**Bottom Line:** Strabismus surgery presents unique challenges including enhanced oculocardiac reflex risk, high PONV incidence, and potential succinylcholine-FDT interaction. Modern anesthetic management emphasizes SGA techniques without paralysis, multimodal PONV prevention, and opioid-sparing analgesia. Understanding the association with malignant hyperthermia and properly managing patients with associated neurological conditions are essential for optimal outcomes.

# 7. LASER THERAPY PRINCIPLES

Physics and Equipment (Pages 11-13)

**Laser technology revolutionized ophthalmic surgery, requiring comprehensive understanding of physics principles and safety protocols.**

## 7.2. LASER PHYSICS AND SAFETY [PART A/C]

### 7.2.1. HISTORICAL DEVELOPMENT [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#3.1 Physics|ⓘ]]

**The laser invention at Columbia University in 1957 transformed multiple fields:**
* **Industrial applications:** Manufacturing and precision cutting
* **Scientific measurements:** Enhanced precision and accuracy
* **Medical therapy:** Countless surgical and therapeutic conditions
* **Recognition:** 13 Nobel Prizes inspired by laser technology

### 7.2.2. FUNDAMENTAL PHYSICS PRINCIPLES [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#3.1 Physics|ⓘ]]

#### 7.2.2.1. BASIC MECHANISM

**Light Amplified by Stimulated Emission of Radiation (LASER):**
* **Photon-electron interaction:** Excited electron collision produces identical photon
* **Large-scale amplification:** Process repeated to create orderly light beam
* **Energy conversion:** Electrical or chemical energy converted to coherent light

#### 7.2.2.2. UNIQUE PROPERTIES OF LASER LIGHT

**Monochromatic characteristics:**
* All photons have identical wavelength, energy, and frequency
* Specific wavelength determined by lasing medium
* Enables precise tissue targeting and absorption

**Coherent properties:**
* All photons in phase with each other
* Maintains beam integrity over distance
* Allows precise focusing and concentration

**Collimated beam:**
* Non-divergent light beam
* Maintains concentrated energy density
* Enables surgical precision

### 7.2.3. ENERGY AND POWER RELATIONSHIPS [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#3.1 Physics|ⓘ]]

#### 7.2.3.1. ENERGY CALCULATION

**Radiant energy absorption formula:**
* **Energy (joules)** = **Power (watts)** × **Duration (seconds)**
* **Clinical relevance:** Determines tissue effect and damage

#### 7.2.3.2. OPERATIONAL MODES

**Continuous wave mode:**
* **Constant power output**
* **Steady tissue heating**
* **Applications:** Photocoagulation, controlled heating

**Pulsed mode:**
* **Intermittent high-energy bursts**
* **Reduced thermal diffusion**
* **Applications:** Precise cutting, minimized collateral damage

### 7.2.4. TISSUE INTERACTION PRINCIPLES [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#3.1 Physics|ⓘ]]

#### 7.2.4.1. WAVELENGTH-DEPENDENT EFFECTS

**Wavelength-absorption relationship:**
* **Longer wavelengths:** More strongly absorbed, shallow tissue penetration
* **Shorter wavelengths:** More scattered, deeper penetration
* **Clinical application:** Match wavelength to desired tissue interaction

#### 7.2.4.2. POWER DENSITY DETERMINES CLINICAL EFFECT

**High power density applications:**
* **Tissue cutting:** Precise incision with minimal thermal damage
* **Tissue vaporization:** Complete tissue removal
* **Applications:** Surgical excision, precise ablation

**Low power density applications:**
* **Photocoagulation:** Controlled tissue coagulation
* **Hemostasis promotion:** Bleeding control through thermal effects
* **Therapeutic heating:** Controlled temperature elevation

**Variable manipulation:**
* **Power adjustment:** Increase/decrease energy delivery rate
* **Duration control:** Modify exposure time for desired effect
* **Distance variation:** Alter power density through focal changes

### 7.2.5. SAFETY PROTOCOLS AND EQUIPMENT `[COMP: SBP]`

#### 7.2.5.1. PERSONAL PROTECTIVE EQUIPMENT

**Eye protection requirements:**
* **Wavelength-specific goggles:** Must match laser type in use
* **Staff protection:** All personnel in laser vicinity
* **Patient protection:** Moist gauze pads and metal shields for non-target eyes

**Protective goggle specifications:**
* **Clear goggles:** CO2 laser (10,600 nm)
* **Orange goggles:** Argon laser (488-515 nm)
* **Green goggles:** Nd:YAG laser (1,064 nm)
* **Orange-red goggles:** Nd:YAG-KTP laser (532 nm)

⚠️ **Critical safety point:** Wrong goggle color provides no protection and creates false security.

#### 7.2.5.2. ENVIRONMENTAL SAFETY MEASURES

**Operating room modifications:**
* **Warning signs:** Posted at all entrances during laser use
* **Access control:** Limited personnel entry during operation
* **Fire safety:** Enhanced protocols due to ignition risk
* **Ventilation:** Smoke evacuation systems for tissue vaporization

## 7.3. OPHTHALMIC LASER APPLICATIONS

### 7.3.1. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS OVERVIEW [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S13 Ophthalmic Procedures|ⓘ]]

**Lasers treat three most common causes of vision loss in the United States:**
1. **Diabetic retinopathy**
2. **Glaucoma**
3. **Age-related macular degeneration**

**Expanding applications:**
* **Refractive surgery:** Rapidly growing field
* **Retinal disorders:** Multiple therapeutic applications
* **Glaucoma treatment:** Various pressure-reduction techniques

### 7.3.2. ARGON LASER CHARACTERISTICS AND APPLICATIONS [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S13 Ophthalmic Procedures|ⓘ]]

#### 7.3.2.1. PHYSICAL PROPERTIES

**Argon laser specifications:**
* **Wavelength:** 488-515 nm (blue-green light, ~0.5 μm)
* **Power characteristics:** Low maximum power output
* **Delivery system:** Easily transmitted through fiberoptic bundles
* **Tissue interaction:** Strongly absorbed by hemoglobin, melanin, and pigments

#### 7.3.2.2. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS

**Retinal detachment surgery:**
* **Mechanism:** Photocoagulates pigment epithelium and adjacent neurosensory retina
* **Effect:** Creates adhesion between retina and eye wall
* **Goal:** Maintain retinal attachment and prevent re-detachment

**Diabetic retinopathy treatment:**
* **Therapeutic principle:** Focal, controlled necrosis of ischemic retina
* **Mechanism:** Reduces metabolic demand and growth factor production
* **Outcome:** Prevents progression to proliferative disease

**Retinal vein occlusion management:**
* **Application:** Treatment of late complications
* **Efficacy:** Some success in managing secondary changes
* **Timing:** Usually after acute phase resolution

#### 7.3.2.3. SAFETY CONSIDERATIONS

⚠️ **Penetration risk:** Argon laser emissions penetrate cornea and lens, causing severe retinal damage to unprotected eyes.

**Required protection:** Orange protective goggles for all personnel in vicinity.

### 7.3.3. ND:YAG LASER SYSTEM [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S13 Ophthalmic Procedures|ⓘ]]

#### 7.3.3.1. TECHNICAL SPECIFICATIONS

**Neodymium:Yttrium-Aluminum-Garnet laser:**
* **Wavelength:** 1,064 nm (1.06 μm, infrared range)
* **Power characteristics:** High power density capability
* **Tissue interaction:** Infrared absorption properties

#### 7.3.3.2. PRIMARY CLINICAL APPLICATION

**Posterior capsule opacification:**
* **Indication:** Opacified posterior lens capsule after cataract surgery
* **Incidence:** Approximately one-third of extracapsular cataract extractions
* **Mechanism:** High-power density creates opening in membrane
* **Outcome:** Restores visual clarity through capsular window

#### 7.3.3.3. SAFETY REQUIREMENTS

**Personnel protection:**
* **Required goggles:** Green-tinted protective eyewear
* **Visual impairment:** Green goggles impair cyanosis detection ability
* **Clinical awareness:** Staff must recognize visual assessment limitations

### 7.3.4. EXCIMER LASER TECHNOLOGY [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S13 Ophthalmic Procedures|ⓘ]]

#### 7.3.4.1. TECHNICAL INNOVATION

**Excimer/Exciplex laser characteristics:**
* **Name origin:** "Excited dimer" or "excited complex"
* **Gas combination:** Inert gas (argon, krypton, xenon) + reactive gas (fluorine, chlorine)
* **Energy state:** Pseudomolecule exists only in energized state
* **Wavelength:** Ultraviolet range (125-200 nm)

#### 7.3.4.2. OPERATIONAL PARAMETERS

**Typical specifications:**
* **Pulse rate:** ~100 Hz (some operate up to 8 kHz)
* **Pulse duration:** 10 ns (some up to 30 ns)
* **Energy delivery:** Extremely precise, controlled ablation

#### 7.3.4.3. UNIQUE TISSUE INTERACTION

**Molecular bond disruption:**
* **Mechanism:** Energy sufficient to disrupt molecular bonds
* **Tissue removal:** Ablation rather than burning or cutting
* **Precision:** Exceptionally fine layer removal
* **Thermal effect:** Almost no heating or neighboring tissue change

#### 7.3.4.4. REFRACTIVE SURGERY APPLICATIONS

**Laser-Assisted In Situ Keratomileusis (LASIK):**
* **Common term:** "Laser corrective surgery"
* **Precision:** Controlled corneal reshaping
* **Applications:** Myopia, hyperopia, astigmatism correction
* **Advantages:** Minimal thermal damage, precise ablation depth

### 7.3.5. AGE-RELATED MACULAR DEGENERATION TREATMENT EVOLUTION

#### 7.3.5.1. HISTORICAL PROGRESSION

**Initial approach - direct photocoagulation:**
* **Technique:** Laser cauterization of neovascular membrane
* **Problem:** Concurrent damage to adjacent healthy macular tissue
* **Limitation:** Visual improvement limited by collateral damage

#### 7.3.5.2. PHOTODYNAMIC THERAPY

**Verteporfin-based treatment:**
* **Drug administration:** Intravenous verteporfin injection
* **Activation wavelength:** 693 nm cold laser light
* **Mechanism:** Photoactivation in presence of oxygen produces reactive radicals
* **Selectivity:** Theoretically targets diseased tissue preferentially
* **Limitation:** Still caused damage to nearby healthy tissue

#### 7.3.5.3. CURRENT STANDARD OF CARE

**Anti-VEGF therapy supersedes laser treatment:**
* **Ranibizumab:** FDA-approved monoclonal antibody
* **Bevacizumab:** Off-label use of related drug
* **Delivery:** Intravitreous injection
* **Advantages:** More effective, fewer side effects than laser approaches
* **Outcome:** Represents non-laser advancement in treatment

### 7.3.6. ANESTHETIC IMPLICATIONS OF LASER SURGERY

#### 7.3.6.1. PATIENT SELECTION AND PREPARATION

**Cooperation requirements:**
* **Conscious sedation:** Most procedures performed under MAC
* **Patient stillness:** Critical for precise laser delivery
* **Communication:** Must respond to commands during treatment

#### 7.3.6.2. ENVIRONMENTAL CONSIDERATIONS

**Operating room modifications:**
* **Air quality:** Smoke evacuation systems required
* **Fire safety:** Enhanced protocols due to laser ignition risk
* **Access control:** Limited personnel movement during laser use

#### 7.3.6.3. MONITORING CONSIDERATIONS

**Standard monitoring with modifications:**
* **Pulse oximetry:** Ensure adequate oxygenation throughout
* **Blood pressure:** Manage hypertension to reduce bleeding risk
* **ECG monitoring:** Detect dysrhythmias from patient anxiety

#### 7.3.6.4. EMERGENCY PREPAREDNESS

**Laser-specific emergencies:**
* **Retinal burns:** From inadequate eye protection
* **Fire hazards:** Ignition of drapes, oxygen-enriched environment
* **Respiratory issues:** Smoke inhalation from tissue vaporization

**Bottom Line:** Laser therapy in ophthalmology requires understanding of fundamental physics principles, wavelength-specific tissue interactions, and comprehensive safety protocols. Different laser types serve specific therapeutic purposes, from argon laser photocoagulation to excimer laser refractive surgery. Anesthetic management must ensure patient cooperation while maintaining safety in the laser environment through proper protective equipment and emergency preparedness.

# 8. POSTOPERATIVE COMPLICATIONS



---

**Postoperative ocular complications range from minor surface injuries to devastating vision loss, requiring prompt recognition and appropriate management.**

## 8.2. SURFACE INJURIES [PART B/C]

### 8.2.1. CORNEAL ABRASION - MOST COMMON COMPLICATION [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S13 Ophthalmic Procedures|ⓘ]]

#### 8.2.1.1. INCIDENCE AND RISK FACTORS

**Wide variation in reported incidence based on protective measures:**
* **Unprotected eyes:** Batra and Bali reported 44% incidence
* **Protected eyes:** Cucchiara and Black found 0.17% in neurosurgical patients
* **Recent large series:** 0.15% in >100,000 non-ophthalmic procedures, decreased to 0.079% after teaching initiative
* **Duration correlation:** Deljou et al. identified increased risk with longer general anesthesia and non-supine positioning

#### 8.2.1.2. MECHANISMS OF INJURY

**Multiple pathways can result in corneal damage:**
* **Direct trauma:** Face mask contact, surgical drapes, spilled solutions
* **Laryngoscopy-related:** Watch bands, ID badges, lanyards on laryngoscopist
* **Physiological changes:** Loss of pain sensation, obtunded protective reflexes, decreased tear production
* **Patient factors:** Dry eyes, contact lens use, pre-existing ocular surface disease

#### 8.2.1.3. PREVENTION STRATEGIES `[COMP: SBP]`

**Evidence-based protective measures:**
* **Eyelid taping:** Standard of care, apply immediately after induction
* **Timing:** During mask ventilation and laryngoscopy
* **Protective goggles:** For procedures above clavicle
* **Position checks:** Frequent monitoring in prone patients
* **Ointment considerations:** Petroleum-based ointments provide protection but have limitations

**Ointment advantages and disadvantages:**
* **Benefits:** Moisture retention, lubrication, physical barrier
* **Risks:** 
  * Allergic reactions (rare)
  * Flammability concerns (laser surgery contraindicated)
  * Postoperative visual blurring
  * May increase rubbing behavior during emergence

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Simple eyelid taping remains the most effective and safest protection for most procedures.

#### 8.2.1.4. CLINICAL PRESENTATION AND DIAGNOSIS

**Classic symptom complex:**
* **Unilateral eye pain:** Typically sharp, foreign body sensation
* **Tearing (epiphora):** Reflex lacrimation response
* **Photophobia:** Light sensitivity due to ciliary muscle spasm
* **Blepharospasm:** Protective lid closure reflex
* **Pain exacerbation:** Worsened by blinking and eye movement

**Diagnostic confirmation:**
* **Foreign body inspection:** Rule out eyelash or debris
* **Topical anesthetic test:** Single drop of proparacaine provides diagnostic pain relief
* **Ophthalmoscopy:** If available, may reveal corneal defect
* **Fluorescein staining:** Gold standard but requires specialized equipment

⚠️ **Red flag symptoms requiring ophthalmology consultation:**
* **Bilateral symptoms:** Suggests systemic cause
* **Abnormal pupillary reflexes:** May indicate serious underlying pathology
* **No pain relief with topical anesthetic:** Consider acute glaucoma, deeper injury

#### 8.2.1.5. TREATMENT AND MANAGEMENT `[COMP: PC]`

**Evidence-based treatment approach:**
* **Avoid eye patching:** May delay wound healing, no longer recommended
* **Limit topical anesthetics:** Can delay healing and cause keratitis
* **Pain management:** Limited duration topical NSAIDs preferred
* **Antibiotic controversy:** Routine topical antibiotics not clearly beneficial

**Healing timeline:**
* **Typical resolution:** 72 hours for most abrasions
* **Complete epithelial regeneration:** Usually occurs within 24-48 hours
* **Pain resolution:** Often precedes complete healing

### 8.2.2. CHEMICAL INJURY [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S13 Ophthalmic Procedures|ⓘ]] `[COMP: PC]`

#### 8.2.2.1. MECHANISM AND PREVENTION

**Common cause:** Spillage during skin preparation, particularly antiseptic solutions

**FDA warning example:**
* **Hibiclens (4% chlorhexidine gluconate):** Serious corneal damage reported
* **Mechanism:** Detergent formulation causes epithelial damage
* **Prevention:** Meticulous technique during prep, eye protection

#### 8.2.2.2. EMERGENCY MANAGEMENT

**Immediate treatment:**
1. **Copious irrigation:** Balanced salt solution or sterile water
2. **Prolonged flushing:** Continue until chemical removal complete
3. **pH assessment:** Check tear film pH if litmus paper available
4. **Ophthalmology consultation:** Document extent of injury

**Documentation importance:**
* **Baseline assessment:** Establish initial injury extent
* **Legal protection:** Important for liability considerations
* **Treatment planning:** Guide ongoing management

### 8.2.3. PHOTIC INJURY [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S13 Ophthalmic Procedures|ⓘ]]

#### 8.2.3.1. RISK SOURCES

**Direct or reflected light beam exposure can cause permanent eye damage:**
* **Surgical lights:** High-intensity illumination
* **Laser radiation:** Specific wavelength-dependent damage
* **Electrocautery:** Bright light and infrared radiation

#### 8.2.3.2. PREVENTION PROTOCOLS `[COMP: SBP]`

**Patient protection requirements:**
* **Moist gauze pads:** First layer of protection
* **Metal shields:** Secondary protection over gauze
* **Appropriate tinting:** Wavelength-specific protection for different lasers

**Laser-specific protection:**
* **CO2 laser:** Clear goggles adequate
* **Argon laser:** Orange goggles required
* **Nd:YAG laser:** Green goggles required  
* **Nd:YAG-KTP laser:** Orange-red goggles required

⚠️ **Critical safety point:** Wrong goggle color provides NO protection and creates false security.

**Bottom Line:** Surface injuries are the most common ocular complications of anesthesia, with corneal abrasion being most frequent. Prevention through eyelid taping and environmental awareness is more effective than treatment. Chemical and photic injuries require immediate recognition and appropriate emergency management to prevent permanent damage.

## 8.3. POSTOPERATIVE VISION LOSS (POVL) [PART B/C]

### 8.3.1. OVERVIEW AND SIGNIFICANCE `[COMP: PBLI]`

**Postoperative vision loss represents one of the most devastating anesthetic complications, ranging from mild visual disturbances to complete blindness.**

#### 8.3.1.1. SCOPE OF THE PROBLEM

**Vision loss can result from injury at any point along the visual pathway:**
* **Anterior segment:** Cornea to lens
* **Posterior segment:** Vitreous, retina, optic nerve  
* **Central visual pathway:** Optic chiasm to occipital cortex

#### 8.3.1.2. HISTORICAL RECOGNITION AND REGISTRY DEVELOPMENT

**ASA Response to emerging problem:**
* **Increasing reports:** Uptick in POVL cases, especially spine surgery
* **Case volume correlation:** Spinal fusion procedures doubled in preceding decade
* **National incidence:** 0.017% after spine surgery in prone position
* **Registry establishment:** ASA POVL Registry created for risk factor identification

### 8.3.2. POSTSURGICAL ACUTE GLAUCOMA [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S13 Ophthalmic Procedures|ⓘ]]

#### 8.3.2.1. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND RISK FACTORS

**Acute angle-closure glaucoma - rare but serious postoperative complication:**

**Risk factors:**
* **Genetic predisposition:** Family history of angle closure
* **Anatomical factors:** Shallow anterior chamber depth, increased lens thickness, small corneal diameter
* **Demographic factors:** Female gender, advanced age
* **Precipitating events:** Pupillary dilation, stress response

#### 8.3.2.2. CLINICAL PRESENTATION

**Classic triad of symptoms:**
* **Painful red eye:** Severe, often described as boring pain
* **Fixed dilated pupil:** Loss of pupillary light reflex
* **Blurred vision:** Corneal edema causes visual disturbance
* **Additional symptoms:** Headache, nausea, vomiting (often misdiagnosed)

#### 8.3.2.3. DIAGNOSTIC CHALLENGES `[COMP: PC]`

**Differential diagnosis consideration:**
* **Corneal abrasion vs. acute glaucoma:** Key differentiation required
* **Topical anesthetic test:** Glaucoma pain NOT relieved by proparacaine
* **Pupillary assessment:** Fixed dilated pupil suggests glaucoma
* **Vision assessment:** Significant visual impairment with glaucoma

⚠️ **Time-sensitive emergency:** Diagnostic delay may cause permanent optic nerve damage and vision loss.

#### 8.3.2.4. MANAGEMENT PROTOCOL

**Immediate interventions:**
1. **Ophthalmology consultation:** STAT consultation required
2. **IOP reduction:** Systemic and topical agents
   * IV acetazolamide 250-500mg
   * Topical β-blockers, α-agonists, pilocarpine
3. **Pain management:** Systemic analgesics as bridge to definitive treatment
4. **Definitive treatment:** Laser peripheral iridotomy usually required

#### 8.3.2.5. ANESTHETIC RISK FACTOR ANALYSIS

**Surgical and anesthetic factors (limited evidence):**
* **One study findings:** No correlation with anesthetic agent type, surgery duration, fluid volume, or intraoperative blood pressure
* **Implication:** Risk appears more related to patient predisposition than anesthetic management

### 8.3.3. HEMORRHAGIC RETINOPATHY

#### 8.3.3.1. VALSALVA RETINOPATHY [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S13 Ophthalmic Procedures|ⓘ]]

**Retinal hemorrhage from forceful exhalation against closed airway:**

**Triggering mechanisms:**
* **Intraoperative:** Bucking on endotracheal tube, coughing, sneezing
* **Postoperative:** Protracted vomiting, Valsalva maneuvers
* **Emergency situations:** Airway obstruction, laryngospasm

**Clinical characteristics:**
* **Venous hemorrhages:** Usually self-limiting
* **Resolution time:** Days to few months typically
* **Asymptomatic:** Most cases unless macula involved
* **Severe complications:** Massive hemorrhage, optic nerve involvement may cause permanent impairment

**Treatment approach:**
* **Observation:** Most cases resolve spontaneously
* **Vitrectomy:** Considered for massive hemorrhage with visual compromise
* **Prognosis:** Generally good unless complications occur

#### 8.3.3.2. EPIDURAL INJECTION-RELATED RETINOPATHY

**Zhang et al. case series - unique complication:**
* **Trigger:** Large volume epidural injections (≥40 mL)
* **Mechanism:** Rapid injection causes sudden intracranial pressure increase
* **Clinical course:** 8 of 9 patients had complete recovery
* **Prevention:** Slow injection technique, minimal necessary volume

**Risk factors:**
* **Obesity:** May increase baseline pressures
* **Hypertension:** Vascular predisposition
* **Coagulopathies:** Bleeding tendency
* **Pseudotumor cerebri:** Pre-existing elevated cerebrospinal fluid pressure
* **Diabetic retinopathy:** Pre-existing vascular disease

#### 8.3.3.3. PURTSCHER RETINOPATHY

**Arterial retinal bleeding from extraocular trauma:**
* **Mechanism:** Associated with significant head/chest trauma
* **Fundoscopic findings:** Cotton-wool exudates, retinal hemorrhages
* **Prognosis:** Poor - most patients sustain permanent visual impairment
* **Recognition:** Rule out in trauma patients with postanesthetic visual complaints

### 8.3.4. RETINAL ISCHEMIA AND CENTRAL RETINAL ARTERY OCCLUSION (CRAO)

#### 8.3.4.1. MECHANISMS OF RETINAL ISCHEMIA

**Multiple pathways can lead to retinal ischemia:**
* **Direct external pressure:** Ill-fitting anesthesia masks, especially with hypotension
* **Embolic phenomena:** Cardiac surgery, carotid procedures
* **Gas bubble expansion:** Intraocular gases with nitrous oxide administration
* **Elevated IOP:** Impaired venous drainage, increased orbital pressure

#### 8.3.4.2. PREVENTION STRATEGIES - POSITIONING `[COMP: SBP]`

**Critical importance of patient positioning:**
* **Prone position protocols:** Padded foam headrest with eye openings
* **Mayfield tongs:** Alternative head stabilization
* **Frequent assessment:** Check eyes for pressure at regular intervals
* **Avoid combinations:** Steep head-down + deliberate hypotension + large crystalloid volumes

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The combination of head-down position, deliberate hypotension, and massive crystalloid administration creates highest risk for orbital venous congestion and decreased ocular perfusion.

#### 8.3.4.3. CRAO - CLINICAL PRESENTATION AND MANAGEMENT

**Pathognomonic findings:**
* **Clinical signs:** Proptosis, chemosis, hyphema, corneal abrasion, lid bruising
* **Funduscopic examination:** 
  * Pale, edematous retina
  * Cherry-red spot (foveal appearance)
  * Visible emboli in retinal arterioles
* **Embolic types:** Platelet-fibrin, cholesterol, calcific, crystalloid

#### 8.3.4.4. EMERGENCY TREATMENT ATTEMPTS

**Limited efficacy interventions:**
* **Ocular massage:** May dislodge emboli to peripheral sites (contraindicated if glaucoma suspected)
* **IV acetazolamide:** Attempt to increase retinal blood flow
* **5% CO2 inhalation:** Vasodilatory effect
* **Hyperbaric oxygen:** Controversial, limited availability

#### 8.3.4.5. DIAGNOSTIC WORKUP

**Embolic source investigation:**
* **Echocardiogram:** Cardiac source evaluation
* **Carotid ultrasound:** Atherosclerotic disease assessment
* **Temporal artery biopsy:** Rule out arteritic causes
* **CT/MRI:** Usually negative in pure CRAO

#### 8.3.4.6. PREVENTION IN HIGH-RISK PROCEDURES

**Nasal and sinus injection safety:**
* **Topical vasoconstrictors:** Apply to decrease vascular bed
* **Small needle:** 25-gauge to minimize injection pressure
* **Low-volume syringe:** Reduce injection force
* **Avoid corticosteroid combinations:** May predispose to crystal formation

### 8.3.5. ISCHEMIC OPTIC NEUROPATHY (ION) - LEADING CAUSE OF POVL

#### 8.3.5.1. CLASSIFICATION AND EPIDEMIOLOGY

**ION represents the most common cause of POVL:**
* **Age group:** Most common sudden visual loss in patients >50 years
* **Surgical risk:** Highest in CABG and long prone spinal fusion surgeries
* **Classifications:** Arteritic vs. non-arteritic, anterior vs. posterior

#### 8.3.5.2. ANTERIOR ISCHEMIC OPTIC NEUROPATHY (AION)

**Pathophysiology - multifactorial mechanism:**
* **Primary cause:** Temporary low blood flow to anterior optic nerve vessels
* **Additional factors:** Intra-axonal edema, disturbed autoregulation
* **Blood supply:** Short posterior ciliary arteries (vulnerable)

**Risk factors:**
* **Demographics:** Male predominance, cardiovascular disease
* **Surgical procedures:** CABG, thoracovascular operations, spinal surgery
* **Perioperative factors:** Massive bleeding, anemia, hypotension (but correlations inconsistent)

**ASA POVL Registry findings:**
* **Surgeon survey paradox:** Hypotension/anemia equally prevalent in AION and non-AION patients
* **Implication:** Traditional risk factors may be less predictive than assumed

**Clinical presentation:**
* **Painless visual loss:** Usually noted first postoperative day
* **Afferent pupillary defect:** Marcus Gunn pupil
* **Altitudinal field defects:** Characteristic pattern
* **Optic disc changes:** Edema or pallor on fundoscopy
* **MRI findings:** Initial optic nerve enlargement, later atrophy

#### 8.3.5.3. POSTERIOR ISCHEMIC OPTIC NEUROPATHY (PION)

**Anatomical vulnerability:**
* **Blood supply:** Less luxuriant than anterior segment, poor collateral flow
* **Vascular anatomy:** Pial vessels from ophthalmic artery collaterals
* **Clinical distribution:** Most perioperative ION associated with spine surgery

**Risk factors differ from AION:**
* **Male predominance:** Similar to AION
* **CABG association:** Only ~11% of cases (much lower than AION)
* **Cardiovascular disease:** Less correlation than AION
* **Surgical procedures:** Neck, nose, sinus, spine surgeries
* **Facial edema:** Noted in approximately one-third of cases

**Clinical characteristics:**
* **Delayed onset:** Symptom-free period often precedes visual loss
* **Bilateral involvement:** More common than with AION (suggests chiasmal involvement)
* **No disc edema:** Retro-orbital location means normal fundoscopy initially
* **Pupillary findings:** Afferent defect or non-reactive pupils
* **CT findings:** Early optic nerve enlargement in intraorbital portion

#### 8.3.5.4. ASA POVL REGISTRY FINDINGS - LANDMARK STUDY

**First 6-year analysis of spine surgery ION cases (83 cases):**

**Patient characteristics:**
* **No traumatic eye injury:** No cases attributed to edema or direct globe pressure
* **Blood pressure variability:** Mean pressures varied widely
* **Critical hypotension:** 34% had MAP or SBP at least 40% below baseline
* **Moderate hypotension:** Only 6% had pressures <20% below baseline

**Surgical characteristics:**
* **Duration:** Surgery exceeding 6 hours increased risk
* **Blood loss:** >1 liter associated with higher risk
* **Positioning:** No causative evidence for direct pressure

#### 8.3.5.5. RISK FACTOR ANALYSIS - POSTOPERATIVE VISUAL LOSS STUDY GROUP

**2012 first multicenter case-control study comparing ION vs. non-ION patients:**

**Multivariate analysis identified independent risk factors:**
* **Male sex:** Significant association
* **Obesity:** Increased risk
* **Wilson frame use:** Specific equipment association
* **Prolonged anesthesia duration:** Time-dependent risk
* **Greater estimated blood loss:** Volume-dependent risk
* **Lower percent colloid administration:** Fluid type matters

**Notably absent associations:**
* **Age:** No statistically significant effect
* **Hypertension:** No independent effect
* **Atherosclerosis:** No clear association
* **Smoking:** No identified relationship
* **Diabetes:** No statistical correlation

#### 8.3.5.6. PREVENTION STRATEGIES AND PRACTICE ADVISORY

**ASA Practice Advisory for Perioperative Visual Loss (2012, updated 2019):**

**Evidence-based recommendations:**
1. **No established transfusion threshold:** No specific hematocrit level proven protective
2. **Deliberate hypotension:** Not proven contributory but consultant concern for high-risk patients
3. **Head positioning:** Level with or higher than heart when possible
4. **Neck positioning:** Neutral position (avoid flexion, extension, lateral flexion, rotation)
5. **Staged procedures:** Consider for high-risk patients to limit prone time

**Pathophysiology implications:**
* **Intraoperative factors:** May be more important than pre-existing comorbidities
* **Venous congestion hypothesis:** Half of risk factors support optic canal venous congestion theory
* **Inflammatory response:** Potential role in pathogenesis under investigation

### 8.3.6. CORTICAL BLINDNESS

#### 8.3.6.1. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND RISK FACTORS

**Brain injury beyond lateral geniculate nucleus or visual cortex:**
* **Anatomical sites:** Visual pathway beyond optic nerve, visual cortex in occipital lobe
* **High-risk procedures:** CABG surgery, cardiac procedures
* **Associated conditions:** Systemic disease often present

#### 8.3.6.2. MECHANISMS

**Common causes:**
* **Embolic phenomena:** Most frequent mechanism
* **Sustained profound hypotension:** Watershed infarctions
* **Other mechanisms:** Cardiac arrest, hypoxemia, intracranial hypertension, exsanguinating hemorrhage

#### 8.3.6.3. CLINICAL DIFFERENTIATION FROM ION

**Diagnostic features:**
* **Fundoscopy:** Normal optic disc appearance
* **Pupillary responses:** Normal light reflexes
* **Eye movements:** Normal motility
* **Optokinetic nystagmus:** Loss with normal eye movements

#### 8.3.6.4. IMAGING STUDIES

**CT and MRI findings:**
* **Bilateral occipital lesions:** Frequently identified
* **Vascular territories:** Posterior cerebral artery involvement
* **Pattern recognition:** Watershed infarction, basilar artery occlusion
* **CABG-specific:** Often includes parieto-occipital areas

#### 8.3.6.5. PROGNOSIS

**Recovery potential:**
* **ION comparison:** Most ION cases have limited improvement
* **Cortical blindness:** Visual recovery possible but prolonged in previously healthy patients
* **Variable outcome:** Recovery may be considerable but requires extended time

#### 8.3.6.6. PREVENTION STRATEGIES

**Cardiac surgery-specific measures:**
* **Adequate perfusion pressure:** Maintain systemic circulation
* **Aortic manipulation:** Minimize manipulation to reduce emboli
* **Air removal:** Meticulous de-airing during valvular procedures
* **Arterial filtration:** Use filters in selected patients during bypass
* **Particulate matter:** Remove debris from bypass circuit

**Bottom Line:** Postoperative vision loss encompasses a spectrum from minor surface injuries to devastating blindness. Ischemic optic neuropathy, particularly posterior ION, represents the leading cause of severe POVL after spine surgery. Prevention strategies focus on positioning, hemodynamic management, and avoiding known risk factor combinations. When POVL occurs, immediate ophthalmologic consultation and appropriate diagnostic workup are essential for optimal patient care and legal documentation.

## 8.4. OTHER COMPLICATIONS [PART B/C]

### 8.4.1. POSTCATARACT PTOSIS [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S13 Ophthalmic Procedures|ⓘ]]

#### 8.4.1.1. INCIDENCE AND MULTIFACTORIAL ETIOLOGY

**Ptosis after cataract surgery is relatively common with multiple contributing factors:**

**Pre-existing factors:**
* **Baseline ptosis:** May become more apparent postoperatively
* **Age-related changes:** Levator muscle dehiscence, fascial laxity
* **Previous ocular surgery:** Scarring and anatomical distortion

#### 8.4.1.2. PROCEDURAL CONTRIBUTING FACTORS

**Surgical technique-related causes:**
* **Eyelid speculum:** Mechanical trauma to levator complex
* **Superior rectus bridle suture:** Traction on superior rectus-levator complex
* **Conjunctival manipulation:** Large flap creation causing inflammation
* **Surgical duration:** Prolonged procedure time increases risk

#### 8.4.1.3. ANESTHESIA-RELATED FACTORS

**Regional anesthesia contributions:**
* **Upper lid injection:** Local anesthetic delivery through upper eyelid
* **12 o'clock approach:** Injection at superior position increases risk
* **Facial nerve block:** Injection into upper lid orbicularis
* **Volume effects:** Large injection volumes causing tissue distension

**Postoperative factors:**
* **Eyelid edema:** Inflammatory response causing mechanical ptosis
* **Prolonged patching:** Tight or extended patching duration
* **Muscle inflammation:** Direct local anesthetic myotoxicity

#### 8.4.1.4. TAYLOR ET AL. MRI STUDY FINDINGS

**Imaging evidence of muscle damage:**
* **Population:** Four patients with diplopia after extraconal blocks
* **MRI findings:** Peribulbar edema consistent with direct myotoxicity
* **Mechanism:** Presumed inadvertent intramuscular injection
* **Implication:** Local anesthetics clearly myotoxic to extraocular muscles

#### 8.4.1.5. MULTIFACTORIAL NATURE - FEIBEL ET AL. CONCLUSION

**Important study conclusions:**
* **Complex etiology:** No single surgical aspect is sole contributor
* **Multiple factors:** Various surgical and anesthetic elements contribute
* **General anesthesia cases:** Postsurgical ptosis also occurs without regional blocks
* **Clinical approach:** Recognize multifactorial nature for appropriate counseling

#### 8.4.1.6. PREVENTION STRATEGIES `[COMP: SBP]`

**Anesthetic technique modifications:**
* **Avoid upper lid injection:** Use inferotemporal approaches
* **Minimize volume:** Use smallest effective volume
* **Avoid 12 o'clock injection:** Choose lateral entry points
* **Consider topical anesthesia:** For appropriate surgical cases

**Postoperative care:**
* **Gentle patching:** Avoid excessive pressure or duration
* **Early mobilization:** Encourage normal blinking patterns
* **Patient counseling:** Discuss temporary vs. permanent expectations

### 8.4.2. CLOSED CLAIMS ANALYSIS - LIABILITY PATTERNS `[COMP: PBLI]`

#### 8.4.2.1. HISTORICAL TRENDS IN EYE-RELATED CLAIMS

**ASA Closed Claims Project database analysis over several decades:**

**Overall claim patterns:**
* **Current representation:** 4-5% of all surgical anesthesia claims
* **Historical peak:** 5-6% of claims in 1970-80s
* **Recent trend:** Decreasing to 4% since 1990s and 2000s
* **Financial impact:** Some of largest financial recompense in anesthesia claims

#### 8.4.2.2. OPHTHALMIC SURGERY-SPECIFIC CLAIMS

**Needle-based regional anesthesia:**
* **Peak period:** Increased from 2% (1980s) to 7% (1990s)
* **Recent trend:** Decreased in past decade
* **Primary causes:** Needle trauma (29% of claims), patient movement (13% of claims)
* **Correlation:** Decrease parallels adoption of sub-Tenon and topical techniques

#### 8.4.2.3. VISION LOSS OUTCOMES

**Severity of outcomes:**
* **Permanent injury:** More than half resulted in vision loss
* **Financial impact:** High-value settlements/awards
* **Quality of life impact:** Devastating patient consequences
* **Legal significance:** Often involves expert witness testimony

#### 8.4.2.4. WRONG EYE SURGERY CLAIMS

**Encouraging trend:**
* **1990s frequency:** 3% of eye surgery claims
* **Recent data:** None reported since 2002 to closed claims database
* **Contributing factors:** Universal protocol implementation, safety culture improvement
* **Prevention success:** Demonstrates effectiveness of systematic safety interventions

#### 8.4.2.5. GENERAL ANESTHESIA EYE INJURY CLAIMS

**Non-ophthalmic surgery complications:**
* **Frequency:** 4% of all closed claims when excluding ophthalmic needle blocks
* **Common causes:** Corneal abrasions, positioning injuries, chemical exposure
* **Prevention potential:** Many preventable with proper technique and monitoring

### 8.4.3. QUALITY IMPROVEMENT AND RISK MANAGEMENT `[COMP: PBLI]`

#### 8.4.3.1. MARTIN ET AL. PERFORMANCE IMPROVEMENT STUDY

**Large-scale quality improvement initiative:**
* **Population:** Analysis of >100,000 non-ophthalmologic procedures
* **Baseline incidence:** 0.15% perioperative corneal injuries
* **Intervention:** Educational initiative implementation
* **Outcome:** Decreased to 0.079% following teaching program
* **Implication:** Education and awareness significantly reduce complication rates

#### 8.4.3.2. SYSTEM-LEVEL INTERVENTIONS

**Effective quality improvement measures:**
* **Staff education:** Regular training on eye protection techniques
* **Protocol standardization:** Consistent approaches to eye care
* **Equipment availability:** Proper protective devices readily available
* **Culture change:** Emphasis on prevention rather than treatment

#### 8.4.3.3. RISK FACTOR IDENTIFICATION AND MODIFICATION

**Evidence-based risk reduction:**
* **Duration management:** Recognize prolonged procedure risk
* **Positioning protocols:** Standardize prone positioning eye checks
* **Equipment design:** Proper headrests and protective devices
* **Team communication:** Clear handoffs regarding eye protection

### 8.4.4. MEDICOLEGAL CONSIDERATIONS `[COMP: PROF]`

#### 8.4.4.1. DOCUMENTATION REQUIREMENTS

**Essential documentation elements:**
* **Preoperative assessment:** Vision status, risk factors, patient counseling
* **Intraoperative care:** Protective measures taken, positioning checks
* **Postoperative evaluation:** Visual assessment, complication recognition
* **Consultation timing:** When specialists contacted, recommendations followed

#### 8.4.4.2. INFORMED CONSENT CONSIDERATIONS

**Vision-related risks requiring discussion:**
* **POVL risk:** Particularly for high-risk procedures (spine, cardiac)
* **Regional block complications:** Hemorrhage, infection, nerve damage
* **Positioning risks:** Prolonged prone procedures
* **Equipment-related risks:** Laser exposure, chemical injury potential

#### 8.4.4.3. EXPERT TESTIMONY AND STANDARDS OF CARE

**Common liability themes:**
* **Preventable complications:** Cases where proper technique could prevent injury
* **Recognition delays:** Failure to promptly diagnose and treat complications
* **Communication failures:** Inadequate informed consent or complication disclosure
* **System failures:** Inadequate protocols or equipment availability

### 8.4.5. FUTURE DIRECTIONS AND TECHNOLOGY

#### 8.4.5.1. ADVANCED MONITORING TECHNOLOGIES

**Emerging protective technologies:**
* **Automated eye pressure monitoring:** Real-time IOP assessment during positioning
* **Enhanced imaging:** Better visualization of eye protection adequacy
* **Smart positioning systems:** Integrated pressure monitoring in headrests

#### 8.4.5.2. TECHNIQUE EVOLUTION

**Minimally invasive approaches:**
* **Topical anesthesia expansion:** Reduced needle-related complications
* **Improved regional techniques:** Ultra-short cannulae, ultrasound guidance
* **Drug delivery advances:** Sustained-release anesthetics

#### 8.4.5.3. QUALITY METRICS AND OUTCOMES RESEARCH

**Performance measurement:**
* **Complication tracking:** Standardized reporting systems
* **Risk stratification:** Better predictive models
* **Intervention effectiveness:** Evidence-based prevention strategies
* **Patient-reported outcomes:** Vision-specific quality of life measures

**Bottom Line:** Ophthalmic anesthesia complications range from common minor surface injuries to rare but devastating vision loss. Success in complication prevention requires understanding of multifactorial etiologies, implementation of evidence-based protective measures, and systematic quality improvement approaches. Closed claims analysis demonstrates that many complications are preventable through proper technique, positioning, and safety protocols. The trend toward less invasive techniques and enhanced safety protocols has reduced complication rates, but continued vigilance and system-level interventions remain essential for optimal patient care.
