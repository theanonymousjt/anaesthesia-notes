# 1. BASIC MATHEMATICAL CONCEPTS AND SI UNITS

## 1.1. MATHEMATICAL DEFINITIONS AND RELATIONSHIPS

### 1.1.1. AMPLITUDE, FREQUENCY, AND WAVELENGTH

* **Amplitude:** The maximal displacement of a graph or wave from the x axis    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
* **Frequency:** The number of complete cycles in a given time period, usually per second    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
  * Measured in Hertz (Hz)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
  * Frequency is the reciprocal of the period    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
* **Wavelength:** The length from a point on one wave to the same point on the next, usually measured at the peaks or troughs    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
* **Velocity:** The distance a wave travels per unit time    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
  * Equal to frequency × wavelength    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].

💡 These principles are fundamental to understanding waveform displays on monitors, including ECG, arterial line traces, and capnography.

### 1.1.2. DIFFERENTIATION AND INTEGRATION

* **Differentiation:** Differentiating the equation of a graph will produce the equation that calculates the gradient at any given point    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
  * d/dx (x)^n = n(x)^n-1    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
* **Integration:** Integrating the equation for a graph will give the equation that will calculate the area under curve    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].

### 1.1.3. LOGARITHMS AND EXPONENTIAL FUNCTIONS

* **Logarithm:** A logarithm describes the power to which the base number must be raised to give the target number    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
  * If y = x^2, then log(x) y = 2    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
  * Example: log₁₀ 1000 = 3 (10³ = 1000)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
* **Exponential Functions:** During an exponential process, the rate of change of the output value is proportional to the input value    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
  * **Exponential Growth:** Y = ae^kt    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
	* Also called a tearaway exponential function    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
	* Seen with bacterial growth (initially until resources become limiting)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
	* Incompatible with homeostasis    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].

![Untitled](PHYSICS_Untitled_1.png_4cd23c.png)

  * **Exponential Decay:** Y = ae^-kt    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
	* Occurs in drug metabolism, where as plasma concentration decreases, the rate of decrease also reduces    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
	* Terminal elimination of most IV anesthetic agents follows this pattern    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].

![Exponential Decay Curve](physics_exponential_decay_1765679320153.png)

  * **Exponential Build-up:** y = (1 - ae^-kt)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
	* Also called a wash-in curve    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
	* Seen with increasing concentration of volatile anesthetic agents in the lungs    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].

![Untitled](PHYSICS_Untitled_4.png_760e98.png)

💡 Exponential decay explains why the time to halve a drug's plasma concentration (context-sensitive half-time) increases with infusion duration.

### 1.1.4. NEWTON'S LAWS

* **First Law:** A body will remain either at rest or at a constant velocity if no net force acts upon it    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
  * Inertia is resistance to change in velocity    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
  * Momentum is velocity × mass    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
* **Second Law:** Force = Mass × Acceleration    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
  * To accelerate double the mass at the same rate, double the force is required    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
  * The Newton (N) = kg × m/s²    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
  * Energy is force × distance = kg·m²·s⁻²    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
  * Power is energy/time = kg·m²·s⁻³    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
* **Third Law:** For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
  * If a weight applies 10N of force to the ground, the ground applies 10N of force upwards to the weight    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Understanding basic mathematical concepts is essential for interpreting physiological waveforms, drug pharmacokinetics, and equipment function. Exponential functions are particularly important in anesthesia for understanding drug metabolism and volatile agent uptake.

## 1.2. SI UNITS

### 1.2.1. BASE SI UNITS

* **Second (time):** Based on cesium 133 atom    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
* **Metre (distance):** Distance light travels in 1/300,000,000 second    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
* **Ampere (electrical current):** Current producing 1N force between parallel conductors    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]]. Alternatively, 1 coulomb per second    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
* **Candela (light intensity):** Intensity of a monochromatic beam of light    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
* **Kilogram (mass):** Originally the mass of a liter of water, now defined based on Planck's constant    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
* **Mole (amount of substance):** Amount containing Avogadro's number (6.23×10²³) of particles    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]]. Equal to amount in 12g of Carbon-12    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].
* **Kelvin (temperature):** 1/273.16th of the temperature of water's triple point    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]]. Equals -273.15°C    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].

💡 The molar volume of any gas at standard temperature and pressure is 22.4 liters per mole    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]].

💡 Understanding these units is crucial for equipment usage and drug calculations in anesthesia practice    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

### 1.2.3. NON-SI UNITS IN ANESTHESIA PRACTICE [PART A/B]

* **Pressure measurements:**
  * mmHg (millimeters of mercury) - commonly used for blood pressure    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * cmH₂O (centimeters of water) - used for airway pressures    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * bar and atmospheres - used for gas cylinder pressures    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
  * psi (pounds per square inch) - sometimes found on older equipment    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
* **Conversion factors:**
  * 1 atmosphere = 101.3 kPa = 760 mmHg = 1033 cmH₂O = 1.013 bar    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
  * 1 kPa ≈ 7.5 mmHg ≈ 10 cmH₂O    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].

⚠️ Misinterpretation of pressure units can lead to serious equipment-related errors or misunderstood patient parameters    [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].

### 1.2.4. SOLUTION CONCENTRATIONS

* **Weight/Volume (w/v):** Describes grams of solute per 100mL of solution    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * A 1% w/v solution contains 1g of solute per 100mL of solution, or 10mg/mL    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Common examples include propofol and lidocaine, both formulated as 1% w/v (10mg/mL) solutions    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Volume/Volume (v/v):** Used for volatile anesthetics    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Example: 2% v/v sevoflurane means 2mL of liquid sevoflurane per 100mL of carrier    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Weight/Weight (w/w):** Grams of solute per 100g of total mixture    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Less commonly used in anesthesia    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Molar solutions:** Amount of substance in moles per liter of solution    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * 1 molar (1M) = 1 mole/liter    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

⚠️ Drug dose calculation errors are a common source of medication incidents. Always double-check concentration units when preparing medications    [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** SI units form the foundation of scientific measurement in anesthesia. Understanding both SI and non-SI units commonly used in clinical practice is essential for safe equipment operation and accurate drug dosing.

# 2. PRESSURE PRINCIPLES AND GAS LAWS

## 2.1. PRESSURE DEFINITIONS AND MEASUREMENTS

### 2.1.1. ABSOLUTE VS GAUGE PRESSURE

* **Pressure** is a measure of force per unit area    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Diffusion/Hydrostatic Pressure and Osmotic Forces|ⓘ]].
  * 1 pascal = 1 newton per meter squared    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Diffusion/Hydrostatic Pressure and Osmotic Forces|ⓘ]].
  * 1 atmosphere = 101.3 kPa = 760 mmHg = 1033 cmH₂O = 1.013 bar = 14.5 pounds / square inch    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Diffusion/Hydrostatic Pressure and Osmotic Forces|ⓘ]].
  ![[Pasted image 20250609171249.png]]
* **Absolute pressure** is the total pressure including atmospheric pressure    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Diffusion/Hydrostatic Pressure and Osmotic Forces|ⓘ]].
* **Gauge pressure** is the difference between absolute pressure and atmospheric pressure    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Diffusion/Hydrostatic Pressure and Osmotic Forces|ⓘ]].
  * Gauge pressure = Absolute pressure - Atmospheric pressure    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Diffusion/Hydrostatic Pressure and Osmotic Forces|ⓘ]].
    ![Absolute vs Gauge Pressure](physics_absolute_vs_gauge_pressure_7k2m9p_1765697843710.png)
  * An "empty" gas cylinder has a gauge pressure of zero but actually contains air at atmospheric pressure (absolute pressure of 1.013 bar)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Diffusion/Hydrostatic Pressure and Osmotic Forces|ⓘ]].

💡 Most pressure gauges on anesthesia equipment display gauge pressure, not absolute pressure. This is important when troubleshooting cylinder or pipeline issues    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

### 2.1.2. PARTIAL PRESSURE AND GAS TENSION

* **Partial pressure** is the pressure exerted by each component of a gas mixture    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Diffusion/Hydrostatic Pressure and Osmotic Forces|ⓘ]].
  * Example: For a 60:40 mixture of oxygen and nitrous oxide at 100 kPa, oxygen has a partial pressure of 60 kPa and nitrous oxide has a partial pressure of 40 kPa    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Diffusion/Hydrostatic Pressure and Osmotic Forces|ⓘ]].
* **Dalton's law of partial pressures** states that the total pressure exerted by a mixture of gases equals the sum of the component partial pressures    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Diffusion/Hydrostatic Pressure and Osmotic Forces|ⓘ]].
  * P_total = P₁ + P₂ + P₃ + …    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Diffusion/Hydrostatic Pressure and Osmotic Forces|ⓘ]].
* **Gas tension** refers to the partial pressure of a gas in solution, such as CO₂ in blood    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Diffusion/Hydrostatic Pressure and Osmotic Forces|ⓘ]].
  * For each gas tension, there is a corresponding partial pressure of gas above the liquid with which the liquid is in equilibrium    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Diffusion/Hydrostatic Pressure and Osmotic Forces|ⓘ]].
  * This is **Henry's law** - increasing the partial pressure of gas above a liquid increases the amount dissolved in the liquid    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Diffusion/Hydrostatic Pressure and Osmotic Forces|ⓘ]].

💡 Understanding partial pressures is crucial for oxygen delivery. Patients at high altitude receive lower FiO₂ despite the same oxygen percentage due to lower atmospheric pressure    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

### 2.1.3. PRESSURE MEASUREMENT TECHNIQUES [PART A/B]

* **(Anaeroid) Manometer:**
  * Simplest method using a vertical column of liquid with known density (water or mercury)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
  * Pressure = density × height × gravitational acceleration    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
  * Units are mmHg or cmH₂O based on the measuring fluid    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
  * Advantages: No calibration required, accurate    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
  * Disadvantages: Unwieldy, inconvenient for clinical use    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
* **(Hydrostatic) Mechanical gauges:**
  * **Bourdon gauge:** A spiral tube that uncoils as internal pressure increases, moving a pointer along a calibrated scale    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].

![Untitled](PHYSICS_Untitled.png_99144a.png)

  * Advantages: No power supply needed, reliable, durable    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
  * Used in anesthesia machine pressure gauges and cylinder gauges    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Electrical gauges:**
  * Often employ the piezoelectric effect    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
  * Crystals under mechanical stress produce a small electric charge, which is calibrated to measure pressure    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
  * Advantages: Can measure small pressures that mechanical gauges cannot    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
  * Disadvantages: Require power supply, calibration, potential for electrical interference    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
  * Used in many modern pressure transducers for invasive blood pressure monitoring    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
- **Barometer**:
	- Measures atmospheric pressure
	- *"Torricellian vacuum"*: contains mercury vapor at SVP
	- As pressure is exerted, height of the mercury in the tube goes up until the vapour pressure equilibrates to the pressure exerted on it.
	![[Pasted image 20250609171823.png]]
⚠️ Always check pressure gauges during equipment checks. Faulty gauges may lead to undetected pipeline or cylinder pressure issues    [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Pressure principles are fundamental to anesthesia practice. Understanding absolute vs. gauge pressure and methods of measurement is essential for safe equipment operation and patient monitoring.

## 2.2. GAS LAWS

### 2.2.1. BOYLE'S LAW

* At a constant **temperature**, the pressure of a gas is inversely proportional to the volume it occupies    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
  * P₁V₁ = P₂V₂    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
  * As volume decreases, pressure increases proportionally    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
    ![Boyle's Law P-V Curve](physics_boyles_law_pv_curve_4x3n8w_1765697864054.png)
  * Mnemonic: "Boyle is boiling" - temperature is kept constant    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].

💡 Boyle's Law explains why the same amount of gas in a small-volume breathing circuit produces higher pressures than in a larger circuit    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

### 2.2.2. CHARLES' LAW

* At a constant **pressure**, the volume of a gas is directly proportional to its absolute temperature    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
  * V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
  * As temperature increases, volume increases proportionally    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
  * Mnemonic: "Charles in charge is cool under pressure" - pressure is kept constant    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].

💡 Charles' Law explains why heated gases expand and may necessitate adjustment of tidal volumes during ventilation    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

### 2.2.3. GAY-LUSSAC'S LAW

* At a constant **volume**, the pressure of a gas is directly proportional to its absolute temperature    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
  * P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
  * As temperature increases, pressure increases proportionally    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
  * Mnemonic: "Gay-Lussac is a volumous name" - volume is kept constant    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].

💡 Gay-Lussac's Law explains why gas cylinder pressure decreases in cold environments    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

### 2.2.4. UNIVERSAL (IDEAL) GAS LAW

* Combines Boyle's, Charles', and Gay-Lussac's laws into a single equation    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
  * PV = nRT    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
  * Where:
	* P = pressure
	* V = volume
	* n = number of moles of gas
	* R = universal gas constant (approximately 8.13)
	* T = absolute temperature (Kelvin)

### 2.2.5. HENRY'S LAW

* The amount of gas dissolved in a liquid at a given temperature is directly proportional to the partial pressure of that gas in contact with the liquid    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Diffusion/Hydrostatic Pressure and Osmotic Forces|ⓘ]].
  * P = Hv × M    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Diffusion/Hydrostatic Pressure and Osmotic Forces|ⓘ]].
  * Where:
	* P = partial pressure of gas
	* Hv = Henry's proportionality constant (specific to each gas and liquid)
	* M = molar concentration of gas in solution

💡 Henry's Law explains why:

* N₂O dissolves more readily in blood as its partial pressure increases    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* Hyperbaric oxygen therapy increases oxygen dissolved in plasma    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* Decompression sickness occurs when rapid pressure changes cause dissolved gases to form bubbles    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

### 2.2.6. DALTON'S LAW

* The total pressure exerted by a mixture of gases equals the sum of the partial pressures of each individual gas    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Diffusion/Hydrostatic Pressure and Osmotic Forces|ⓘ]].
  * P_total = P₁ + P₂ + P₃ + …    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Diffusion/Hydrostatic Pressure and Osmotic Forces|ⓘ]].

💡 Dalton's Law forms the basis for understanding oxygen concentration and delivery. It explains why increasing altitude decreases the partial pressure of oxygen despite unchanged percentage composition of air    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

### 2.2.7. GRAHAM'S LAW

* The rate of diffusion of a gas is inversely proportional to the square root of its molecular weight    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Diffusion/Hydrostatic Pressure and Osmotic Forces|ⓘ]].
  * Rate₁/Rate₂ = √(MW₂/MW₁)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Diffusion/Hydrostatic Pressure and Osmotic Forces|ⓘ]].
  * Lighter gases diffuse faster than heavier gases    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Diffusion/Hydrostatic Pressure and Osmotic Forces|ⓘ]].

💡 Graham's Law explains why helium (MW=4) diffuses through small airways more easily than nitrogen (MW=28), making heliox useful in upper airway obstruction    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Gas laws are the foundation for understanding ventilator function, gas delivery systems, and respiratory physiology. Knowledge of these laws helps explain equipment behavior under different conditions and physiological responses to changes in pressure, volume, and temperature.

## 2.3. CRITICAL PROPERTIES

### 2.3.1. CRITICAL TEMPERATURE AND PRESSURE

* **Critical temperature** is the temperature above which a gas cannot be compressed back into a liquid, regardless of how much pressure is applied    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
  * Each substance has its own critical temperature    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
  * A substance in gaseous phase but below its critical temperature is described as a **vapor**, as it can be compressed back into a liquid by applying more pressure    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
  * Example: N₂O has a boiling point of -88.5°C but a critical temperature of 36.5°C, making it a vapor from -88.5°C to 36.5°C and a gas above that    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
  * N₂O is technically inhaled by patients as a vapor and exhaled as a gas since body temperature exceeds its critical temperature    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
    ![Critical Temperature Phase Diagram](physics_critical_temperature_phase_8j5v2c_1765697887640.png)
* **Critical pressure** is the pressure required to liquefy a gas at its critical temperature    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
  * Above the critical pressure, the distinction between liquid and gas phases disappears    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].

💡 Understanding critical properties explains why:

* Oxygen (-118.6°C critical temperature) is stored as compressed gas in cylinders at room temperature    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* Nitrous oxide (36.5°C critical temperature) can be stored as a liquid at room temperature, providing more gas per cylinder volume    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

### 2.3.2. PSEUDOCRITICAL TEMPERATURE [PART A/B]

* At any given pressure, there is a temperature above which a mixture of gases will not separate into its component parts    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
* An Entonox cylinder (50% O₂/50% N₂O) at 13,700 kPa has a pseudocritical temperature of -5°C    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].
  * This decreases to -30°C in a pipeline with pressures of 400 kPa    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Behavior of Gases, Liquids and Vapors|ⓘ]].

⚠️ If Entonox cylinders are stored below their pseudocritical temperature, the gases may separate with N₂O liquefying at the bottom of the cylinder. This can lead to delivery of a hypoxic mixture initially. Always warm Entonox cylinders that have been stored in cold environments before use    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Critical properties of gases determine their storage requirements and behavior in anesthesia delivery systems. Understanding these properties is essential for safe handling of medical gases, particularly N₂O and Entonox cylinders in cold environments.

# 3. FLOW AND FLUID DYNAMICS

## 3.1. PRINCIPLES OF FLUID FLOW

### 3.1.1. LAMINAR VS TURBULENT FLOW

* **Flow** is the measure of fluid moving past a given point per unit time    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physics of Flow|ⓘ]].
* Flow can be **laminar** or **turbulent** depending on fluid characteristics and the container    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physics of Flow|ⓘ]].
* **Laminar flow** describes the smooth movement of fluid columns parallel to vessel walls, with no turbulence or eddy currents between layers    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physics of Flow|ⓘ]].
  * Fastest in the center (double the average flow of the fluid as a whole)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physics of Flow|ⓘ]].
  * Slowest at the peripheries (almost zero)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physics of Flow|ⓘ]].
  * Proportional to the viscosity of the fluid    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physics of Flow|ⓘ]].
  * Described by the Hagen-Poiseuille equation    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physics of Flow|ⓘ]].
* **Turbulent flow** is chaotic, unpredictable passage of fluid through a vessel    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physics of Flow|ⓘ]].
  * May be caused by uneven vessel walls or fluid velocity exceeding the critical velocity    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physics of Flow|ⓘ]].
  * Critical velocity is the flow rate above which laminar flow cannot exist    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physics of Flow|ⓘ]].
  * Specific to a given fluid in a tube with a certain radius    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physics of Flow|ⓘ]].
  * Resistance to flow increases with increasing pressure (proportional to the square root of pressure)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physics of Flow|ⓘ]].

![Laminar vs Turbulent Flow Profiles](physics_flow_profiles_1765679344644.png)

💡 In clinical settings, flow is typically laminar in small airways but turbulent in larger airways and endotracheal tubes during peak inspiration    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].

### 3.1.2. REYNOLDS NUMBER

* A dimensionless number that predicts the likelihood of turbulent flow    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physics of Flow|ⓘ]].
* Determined by the following equation:

* If Reynolds number is above 2000, flow is probably turbulent    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physics of Flow|ⓘ]].
* If Reynolds number is below 2000, flow is probably laminar    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physics of Flow|ⓘ]].
* Factors promoting turbulent flow include    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physics of Flow|ⓘ]]:
  * Higher flow velocity
  * Larger diameter tubes
  * Higher fluid density
  * Lower fluid viscosity
  * Sharp corners or sudden changes in vessel diameter

💡 Reynolds number explains why heliox (helium-oxygen mixture) maintains laminar flow at higher flow rates than air due to its lower density    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].

* Describes the relationship between flow rate and pressure in laminar flow    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physics of Flow|ⓘ]]:

* Key implications of this equation    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physics of Flow|ⓘ]]:
  * Flow is proportional to the pressure difference (ΔP)
  * Flow is proportional to radius⁴ - doubling the radius increases flow 16-fold

⚠️ Understanding this equation is crucial for appreciating why:

* Small decreases in endotracheal tube diameter (due to secretions or kinking) dramatically increase airway resistance    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].
* Shorter, wider IV cannulas allow faster fluid administration    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].

**Clinical Significance**

* Critical flow in liters/minute can be approximated to the internal diameter of the tube in mm    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].
  * A size 8.0 ETT will have a critical flow of approximately 8 L/min    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].
  * Peak inspiratory flow typically exceeds this, resulting in turbulent flow    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].
  * Quiet tidal breathing is often laminar    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].
* Small airways in the lungs have smaller diameters, but gas velocity is much slower, resulting in predominantly laminar flow    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].
* In partial upper airway obstruction, laminar flow allows more effective ventilation than turbulent flow    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].
  * By reducing the density of the inspired gas mixture (using heliox), the Reynolds number decreases, increasing the likelihood of maintaining laminar flow    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].
  * Heliox has a density of 0.5g/L compared to room air's 1.25g/L    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Understanding the principles of fluid flow is essential for respiratory management and vascular access. The radius of tubes (airways or cannulas) has the most dramatic impact on flow due to the fourth-power relationship, explaining why small reductions in airway diameter can cause significant airflow obstruction.

## 3.2. BERNOULLI PRINCIPLE AND APPLICATIONS [PART A/B]

### 3.2.1. VENTURI EFFECT

* **Bernoulli Principle:** For an ideal fluid (non-compressible, non-viscous, flowing in a laminar fashion), the sum of pressure, kinetic, and potential energies per unit of fluid remains constant at all points    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physics of Flow|ⓘ]].
  * Simplified for horizontal tubes: P + ρv²/2 = constant    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physics of Flow|ⓘ]].
	* P = Pressure
	* ρ = Density
	* v = Velocity
  * Clinically, this means if fluid velocity increases, pressure must decrease to conserve energy    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physics of Flow|ⓘ]].
* **Applications:**
  * Pressure limiting valves work by passing gas through a narrow orifice, increasing flow velocity and lowering pressure    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physics of Flow|ⓘ]].
  * When fluid passes through a constriction in a tube, velocity increases and pressure decreases    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physics of Flow|ⓘ]].

![nse-777825305307310968-112585.jpg](PHYSICS_nse-777825305307310968-112585.jpg_a0906b.jpg)

* **Venturi Principle:** Utilizes the pressure drop described by the Bernoulli principle to entrain air via holes on the side of a device    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physics of Flow|ⓘ]].
  * This air entrainment means the total flow of gas delivered is higher than the driving flow    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physics of Flow|ⓘ]].

![nse-981665631636497090-112584.jpg](PHYSICS_nse-981665631636497090-112584.jpg_c4206f.jpg)

### 3.2.2. ENTRAINMENT RATIO [PART A/B]

* Describes how much air will be entrained per unit flow of driving gas    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physics of Flow|ⓘ]].
  * ER = Entrained Flow / Driving Flow    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Physics of Flow|ⓘ]].
* Each Venturi device has a fixed entrainment ratio, so increasing the driving flow will not change the FiO₂ but will increase the total flow    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].

![Venturi Principle Diagram](physics_venturi_principle_1765679369248.png)

### 3.2.3. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS

* **Venturi Masks:** Fixed performance oxygen delivery devices that use the venturi principle    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].
  * Also known as High Air Flow Oxygen Enrichment (HAFOE) devices    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].
  * Provide a fixed FiO₂ independent of the patient's breathing pattern as long as the total flow exceeds peak inspiratory flow    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].
  * Useful for    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]:
	* Delivering controlled low FiO₂ to patients relying on hypoxic drive
	* Monitoring response to treatment by holding FiO₂ constant
	* Accurately determining required FiO₂ for a patient's condition
	
* **Other Clinical Applications of the Venturi Principle**    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]:
  * Nebulizers - entrain liquid medication
  * Sanders injector - for jet ventilation
  * Jet ventilation systems - entrain air around a high-pressure jet

💡 Venturi masks are the preferred oxygen delivery device for COPD patients requiring controlled oxygen therapy, as they provide a precise FiO₂ independent of respiratory pattern    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].
![Venturi Mask Color Codes](physics_venturi_mask_fio2_colors_2r8k6t_1765697920054.png)

**Bottom Line:** The Bernoulli principle explains how fluid velocity and pressure are inversely related. This principle powers many critical devices in anesthesia, including Venturi masks, nebulizers, and jet ventilators, which rely on pressure drops at constrictions to entrain additional air or fluid.

## 3.3. THE COANDA EFFECT [PART A/B]

* **Definition:** The tendency of a flowing fluid to change direction by adhering to a surface    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Principle of Measurement|ⓘ]].
* **Mechanism:** When fluid flows through a narrow orifice, the Bernoulli principle states its velocity will increase and pressure decrease    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Principle of Measurement|ⓘ]].
  * If the fluid is contained within a narrow lumen tube instead of entraining another fluid (as in the Venturi effect), this reduced pressure causes it to adhere to the surface of the tube    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Principle of Measurement|ⓘ]].
  * If there is a constriction prior to a division into two tubes, flow will preferentially pass down one division but not the other    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Principle of Measurement|ⓘ]].
* **Clinical Implications:**
  * May explain uneven ventilation where one bronchiole receives less gas than another after a division in the respiratory tree    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].
  * May contribute to the occurrence of myocardial ischemia distal to a division of a coronary artery    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].

💡 The Coanda effect explains why exhaled air tends to follow the contours of the face or deflector when wearing a face mask, rather than projecting straight forward    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** The Coanda effect describes how flowing fluids tend to adhere to adjacent surfaces. This phenomenon may contribute to uneven ventilation in the lungs and has implications for understanding airflow patterns in respiratory equipment.

## 3.4. FLUID LOGIC IN VENTILATORS

* **Definition:** Fluid logic refers to the use of flowing gases to control and direct other gas flows without moving mechanical parts    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Principle of Measurement|ⓘ]].
* **Mechanism in Ventilators:** A ventilator can "choose" which limb of a circuit gas will flow down using a "switching flow" that fires across the main flow, guiding the main gas flow down one or other of the limbs    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Principle of Measurement|ⓘ]].
* **Advantages and Disadvantages:**
  * **Advantages**: Fewer moving parts, potentially more reliable mechanical function    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].
  * **Disadvantages**: Consume more gas, more complex to understand and troubleshoot    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].
* **Examples:** Penlon Nuffield 200 ventilator    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Principle of Measurement|ⓘ]].

💡 Although largely replaced by electronically controlled ventilators, understanding fluid logic principles remains relevant for comprehending gas flow behavior in modern breathing systems and when older equipment may still be encountered    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Fluid logic ventilators use gas flows to control other gas flows without mechanical parts. While these systems have been largely replaced by electronic controls, the principles remain important for understanding gas flow behavior in anesthesia breathing systems.

# 4. TEMPERATURE AND HEAT

## 4.1. BASIC CONCEPTS

### 4.1.1. HEAT VS TEMPERATURE

* **Heat** is the kinetic vibrational energy of molecules within a substance.
  * Given by the equation E = 0.5mv² (where m is mass and v is velocity).
  * Can be transferred from one substance to another.
  * Measured in joules (J) or calories (cal).
* **Temperature** is a quantitative measure of the manifestation of heat energy.
  * Measures the ability of an object to transfer heat to another object.
  * A measure of heat "density".
  * Example: A hot frying pan has less overall heat energy than an iceberg, but the higher temperature means the frying pan will transfer heat energy to the iceberg.
* **SI unit of temperature** is the Kelvin (K).
  * Defined as 1/273.16 of the thermodynamic triple point of water.
  * Equal to -273.15 degrees Celsius.
  * Celsius and Kelvin have the same interval unit.

💡 Understanding this distinction helps explain why objects with different masses but the same temperature (e.g., different volumes of IV fluid at room temperature) contain different amounts of heat energy and thus have different effects when introduced into the patient    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].

### 4.1.2. SPECIFIC HEAT CAPACITY

* The amount of energy needed to increase the temperature of one kilogram of a substance by one kelvin (or one degree Celsius).
* Measured in J/kg·K.
* Water has a high specific heat capacity of 4200 J/kg·K.
* Clinical significance:
  * Body tissues with high water content (like blood) require substantial energy to change temperature    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].
  * Water-based systems are effective for patient warming due to water's high heat capacity    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].

### 4.1.3. LAWS OF THERMODYNAMICS

* **Zeroth Law:** If two systems X and Y are both in thermal equilibrium with a third system Z, then X and Y must be in equilibrium with each other.
  * This is the basis for temperature measurement - the thermometer comes to equilibrium with the patient.
* **First Law:** In a closed, perfect system, the total amount of internal energy remains constant.
  * Energy can neither be created nor destroyed, only transformed.
* **Second Law:** In a natural thermodynamic process, entropy increases.
  * Heat spontaneously flows from hot to cold objects.
  * Clinical implication: "Your patient will get colder if you don't do anything about it!"    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].
* **Third Law:** Entropy approaches a constant as temperature approaches absolute zero.
* **Additional Thermodynamic Concepts:**
  * **Boiling point:** Temperature at which a substance transitions from liquid to gas phase.
	* Temperature at which saturated vapor pressure exceeds ambient pressure.
	* Depends on ambient pressure, increasing as pressure increases.
  * **Freezing point:** Temperature at which a substance transitions from liquid to solid phase.
	* Also correlates with ambient pressure.
  * **Latent heat:** Energy required to change the phase of a substance without changing its temperature.
	* Latent heat of fusion: Energy to convert solid to liquid.
	* Latent heat of vaporization: Energy to convert liquid to gas.
	* Phase changes are endothermic processes requiring energy input.

💡 The evaporation of sweat requires latent heat, which is extracted from the skin, providing a cooling effect. This same principle allows spilled volatile anesthetics to cause frostbite due to their rapid evaporation    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Heat represents the total molecular kinetic energy in a substance, while temperature indicates the intensity of that energy. Understanding thermodynamic principles is essential for managing patient temperature and predicting how anesthetic equipment and drugs behave under different thermal conditions.

## 4.2. TEMPERATURE MEASUREMENT [PART A/B]

### 4.2.1. ELECTRICAL METHODS [PART A/B]

* **Platinum Resistance Thermometer:**
  * Linear increase in resistance as temperature increases.
  * Accurate but has slow response time.
  * Used in laboratory settings rather than clinical monitoring    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].
* **Thermistor:**
  * Metal oxide semiconductor.
  * As temperature increases, resistance decreases exponentially.
  * Used in many clinical temperature probes (e.g., PICCO catheters, pulmonary artery catheters)    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].
    ![RTD vs Thermistor Response](physics_temperature_rtd_thermistor_5w7n1y_1765698039445.png)
  * **Advantages:**
	* Cheap, quick response, portable, can be incorporated into catheters.
	* Can be made linear over a specific clinical temperature range.
  * **Disadvantages:**
	* Calibration can drift over time.
	* Vulnerable to extreme temperatures.
* **Thermocouple:**
  * Utilizes the Seebeck Effect.
  * Two different metals joined together generate a small voltage at the junction point, proportional to temperature.
  * Usually copper and constantan (copper-nickel alloy).
  * Requires one reference junction and one measuring junction.
  * **Advantages:**
	* Small, robust, quick response.
  * **Disadvantages:**
	* Very small output voltage requires amplification.
	* Multiple thermocouples together form a thermopile.
* **Infrared Sensors:**
  * Pyroelectric sensor can detect infrared radiation emitted by the body.
  * Used in tympanic membrane thermometers    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].
  * **Advantages:**
	* Non-invasive, quick    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].
  * **Disadvantages:**
	* Less accurate if not properly positioned    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].
	* Affected by cerumen (earwax)    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].

### 4.2.2. NON-ELECTRICAL METHODS

* **Liquid Expansion Thermometer:**
  * Expansion of fluid is proportional to temperature.
  * Types include:
	* Mercury - solidifies at -39°C.
	* Alcohol - boils at 78.5°C.
	* Gas expansion thermometer.
* **Bimetallic Strip:**
  * Uses two metals with different coefficients of thermal expansion.
  * Bends in proportion to temperature changes.
* **Bourdon Gauge:**
  * Uses Charles' law (volume of gas increases with temperature).
* **Color Change Crystals:**
  * Liquid crystals (FeverScan).
  * Solid crystals (TempaDot).

💡 Most modern anesthesia monitoring systems use thermistors for temperature measurement due to their reliability, small size, and relatively low cost    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].

### 4.2.3. CLINICAL TEMPERATURE MONITORING SITES

* **Tympanic Membrane:**
  * Fast, accurate, reflects brain temperature.
  * Risks include tympanic perforation; earwax can give false readings.
* **Esophageal:**
  * Reliable and relatively non-invasive during anesthesia.
  * Most commonly used site during general anesthesia.
  * Depth of placement affects readings; lower third of esophagus provides most accurate core temperature.
* **Skin:**
  * Not accurate for core temperature assessment.
  * Comparing core and skin temperature gives a measure of peripheral perfusion.
  * Toe-core temperature gradient >4°C suggests significant vasoconstriction.
* **Bladder:**
  * Accurate but invasive.
  * Requires urinary catheter with temperature sensor.
  * Readings may lag behind rapid core temperature changes.
* **Rectal:**
  * Slower response to changes than esophageal or tympanic.
  * Usually up to one degree Celsius higher than core due to bacterial fermentation.
  * Less practical during most surgical procedures.

⚠️ Temperature monitoring site should be selected based on the clinical scenario. Consistent site should be used when tracking temperature changes in a single patient.

**Bottom Line:** Temperature measurement techniques vary in accuracy, response time, and invasiveness. Electrical methods (particularly thermistors) dominate clinical practice due to reliability and ease of integration with monitoring systems. The choice of monitoring site should balance accuracy, accessibility, and patient safety considerations.

## 4.3. BODY TEMPERATURE REGULATION [PART A/B]

### 4.3.1. NORMAL TEMPERATURE AND VARIATION [PART A/B]

* **Normal core temperature:** 36.5-37.5°C.
* **Circadian variation:** Approximately 0.5°C throughout the day, typically lowest in early morning and highest in early evening.
* **Heat Balance:**
  * **Heat generation** determined by:
	* Basal metabolic rate.
	* Heat generated by digestion.
	* Muscle activity (significantly increased during exercise).
  * **Heat loss** governed by:
	* Distribution of blood to the skin.
	* Countercurrent exchange:
	  * Warmer arterial blood transfers heat to nearby veins that return the heat to the core.
	  * Degree of vasoconstriction determines amount of heat retained or lost.
	* Temperature gradient between body and environment.
	* Insulation (body fat, clothing).
	* Evaporation of water (sweat, respiratory moisture).

💡 The main routes of heat loss in humans at room temperature:

* Radiation: 60% (infrared heat waves).
* Conduction and convection: 20-25% (direct contact and air movement).
* Evaporation: 15-20% (from skin and respiratory tract).

### 4.3.2. CENTRAL AND PERIPHERAL THERMORECEPTORS

* **Central thermoreceptors:**
  * Less clearly defined than peripheral receptors.
  * Located in the hypothalamus, spinal cord, and abdominal organs.
  * Primarily detect core temperature changes.
* **Peripheral thermoreceptors:**
  * **Skin thermoreceptors:**
	* Travel in lateral spinothalamic tracts.
	* Two types: warm and cold receptors.
	* Warm receptors act via unmyelinated type C fibers with maximum discharge rate at 50°C.
	* Cold receptors act via Aδ fibers with increasing discharge rate below 25°C.
  * **Deep tissue thermoreceptors:**
	* Found in spinal cord, abdominal organs, and great veins.
	* Monitor core temperature and induce shivering at low temperatures.

💡 The temperature difference between peripheral tissues and the core (gradient) provides information about cardiovascular status. A widening gradient may indicate vasoconstriction due to hypovolemia or hypothermia.

### 4.3.3. HYPOTHALAMIC CONTROL [PART A/B]

* The hypothalamus acts as the body's "thermostat" with a temperature set point.
* **Pre-optic hypothalamus** (anterior) responds to increased temperature by triggering:
  * Vasodilation.
  * Sweating - sweat is hypotonic, 50% in chest and back, greater in men, up to 3L/hr
  * Inhibition of the posterior hypothalamus.
* **Posterior hypothalamus** responds to decreased temperature by triggering (with Ach as the main neurotransmitter):
  * Shivering (medial posterior hypothalamus near third ventricle).
  * Chemical thermogenesis (non-shivering):
	* Sympathetic system (beta-3 activity) uncouples oxidative phosphorylation to increase heat production.
	* Primarily occurs in brown fat, more abundant in neonates and children than adults, found in interscapular, perinephric, around intra-abdominal vessels in newborn.
		* Thermogenic capacity of brown fat is ~150x that of skeletal muscles.
  * Vasoconstriction to conserve heat.
  * Piloerection - traps air adjacent to skin reducing convectional heat loss
* **Special Considerations:**
  * Acclimatization to hot environments takes several weeks, resulting in:
	* Increased aldosterone release.
	* Reduced sodium loss in sweat (from 30g/day to <5g/day).
	* More efficient sweating.
  * Hypothalamic function can be affected by:
	* Tumors and trauma compressing the hypothalamus.
	* Spinal cord injury altering autonomic inputs.
	* Epidural analgesia can paradoxically increase temperature during labor, possibly due to loss of active vasodilation combined with increased metabolic rate,.
 - **Interthreshold range**: range of core temperature over which no autonomic thermoregulatory responses occur - approx. 0.2C at normal temperature (37C) in non anaesthetized state
	 - GA increases this interthreshold range
		 - Decreases the threshold to cold by approximately 2.5C
		 - Increases the threshold to heat by approximately 1.3C
 - **Thermoneutral zone**: range of environmental temperature in which body temperature is maintained while metabolic rate and O2 consumption is minimal and steady.
	 - Critical temperature is 27C (whereby below which extra heat must be generated to prevent a fall in body temperature)
💡 Anesthetic agents typically reset the hypothalamic set point to approximately 34°C, contributing to the development of perioperative hypothermia.

**Bottom Line:** The body maintains temperature through a complex balance of heat production, conservation, and loss, centrally regulated by the hypothalamus. Understanding these mechanisms is crucial for anticipating and managing temperature changes during anesthesia and surgery.

## 4.4. TEMPERATURE MANAGEMENT IN ANESTHESIA

### 4.4.1. PERIOPERATIVE HYPOTHERMIA

* **Definition:** Core temperature <36°C.
* **Causes in anesthetized patients:**
  * **Reduced sensitivity to hypothermia:**
	* Anesthetic agents reset the hypothalamic set point to approximately 34°C.
  * **Reduced heat production:**
	* Decreased muscle tone (respiratory and skeletal).
	* Lack of shivering due to anesthetic effects.
  * **Behavioral factors:**
	* Lack of heat-seeking behavior (unconscious patient).
  * **Increased heat loss:**
	* Vasodilation from anesthetic agents.
	* Conductive loss to IV fluids and cold operating room surfaces.
	* Exposure of body cavities.
	* Cold, dry anesthetic gases.
* **Main routes of heat loss in anesthetized patients:**
  * **Radiation:** 40% - Heat loss by infrared radiation to the environment.
  * **Convection:** 30% - Heat loss to air moving across skin surface.
  * **Evaporation:** 15% - From mucous membranes and exposed surfaces.
  * **Respiration:** 10% - 8% for humidification, 2% for warming inspired gases.
	  * Evaporation + Respiration losses  = insensible losses from skin and respiratory tract (up to 25%)
  * **Conduction:** 5% - Direct contact with cold surfaces or fluids.

💡 Most patients develop hypothermia in a characteristic triphasic pattern during anesthesia:

1. Initial rapid drop (0.5-1°C) in the first 30-60 minutes due to redistribution of heat from core to periphery
2. Slower linear phase as heat loss exceeds production
3. Plateau phase once heat loss equals production at a lower temperature

### 4.4.2. EFFECTS OF HYPOTHERMIA

* **Cardiovascular system:**
  * Arrhythmias and reduced cardiac myocyte function.
  * Vasoconstriction, increased systemic vascular resistance.
  * ECG changes: Prolonged PR and QT intervals, J waves (Osborn waves).
  * Paradoxical vasodilation can occur due to insensitivity to catecholamines
* **Respiratory system:**
  * Reduced respiratory drive.
  * Leftward shift of oxygen dissociation curve (reduced oxygen delivery to tissues).
* **Central nervous system:**
  * Impaired cognition and delirium.
  * Reduced cerebral blood flow.
  * Reduced anesthetic requirements (MAC decreases by ~5% per °C drop).
* **Coagulation:**
  * Impaired platelet function.
  * Reduced enzyme activity in coagulation cascade.
  * Increased blood viscosity and DVT risk.
* **Other effects:**
  * Delayed drug metabolism and clearance.
  * Increased risk of surgical site infection (threefold increase with 1.5°C drop).
  * Shivering increases oxygen consumption by 400-500% during recovery.
  * Hyperglycemia and electrolyte disturbances.
  * Patient discomfort.

⚠️ Even mild hypothermia (35-36°C) is associated with increased surgical site infection, coagulopathy, and cardiac events. Maintaining normothermia should be a priority for all surgical patients unless therapeutic hypothermia is indicated.

* **Effects on Blood Gas Values:**
  * pH increases by approximately 0.02 for each 1°C drop in temperature.
  * Gas solubility increases as temperature drops.
  * Blood gas machines are calibrated to 37°C, giving falsely low readings for pH and high for CO₂ in hypothermic patients unless temperature-corrected.

### 4.4.3. PATIENT WARMING METHODS

* **Environmental Measures:**
  * Target ambient temperature of 22-24°C and humidity 50-60%.
  * Pre-warming the operating room before patient arrival.
  * Minimizing patient exposure during preparation.
* **Patient Warming Devices:**
  * **Forced Air Warmers (FAWs):**
	* Use convection to prevent and treat hypothermia.
	* In children and neonates, placing the patient on the blanket with warm air flowing upward is most effective.
	* Complications include burns and pressure ulcers if not properly applied.
	* Most common and effective method for intraoperative warming.
  * **Water-Filled Mattresses/Blankets:**
	* Provide conductive warming.
	* Risk of pressure injury over bony prominences.
  * **Resistive Polymer Blankets:**
	* Provide even heating without airflow.
	* Useful in settings where forced air is contraindicated (e.g., some orthopedic procedures).
  * **Electric Heating Pads:**
	* May interfere with other monitoring equipment.
	* Risk of burns if not properly monitored.
* **Fluid Warming Devices:**
  * Should be used if more than 500ml of fluid is to be administered.
  * Types include:
	* **Counter-current Heat Exchange Systems:**
	  * Good for low-flow rates (e.g., in neonates).
	* **Water Baths:**
	  * Maintain consistent temperature for infusion lines.
	* **Convective Air Systems:**
	  * Warm fluids as they flow through tubing.
	* **Insulators:**
	  * Reduce heat loss from warmed fluids.
* **Invasive Methods (for severe hypothermia):**
  * Warmed irrigation of body cavities (peritoneal, gastric, bladder).
  * Extracorporeal warming via ECMO or cardiopulmonary bypass.

⚠️ **Hyperthermia Risks:**

* Iatrogenic overwarming can lead to sweating, dehydration, and electrolyte disturbances.
* Other causes of perioperative hyperthermia include infection, malignant hyperthermia, and serotonin syndrome, which require different management approaches.

💡 The most effective approach to perioperative temperature management combines:

1. Pre-warming patients before induction (30 minutes of forced air warming)
2. Active warming during surgery (forced air plus fluid warming)
3. Appropriate ambient temperature maintenance
4. Monitoring to guide interventions

**Bottom Line:** Perioperative hypothermia is common, preventable, and associated with significant complications. A multi-modal approach to temperature management should be employed for all patients undergoing anesthesia unless specific indications for hypothermia exist.

## 5.1. HUMIDITY PRINCIPLES

### 5.1.1. FUNDAMENTAL DEFINITIONS

**Absolute humidity:** The mass of water vapor held by a certain volume of air

* Measured in g/m³ or mg/L
* Independent of temperature
* Examples:
	* Room air at typical conditions: ~17 g/m³
	* Trachea at 37°C: ~44 g/m³ (physiological target)

**Relative humidity:** The amount of water vapor in air expressed as a percentage of the maximum theoretical amount that the air could hold at that temperature

* Temperature-dependent measurement
* Example: 22 g/m³ of water vapor in air that could carry 44 g/m³ = 50% relative humidity
* **Critical relationship:** As air warms, its capacity to hold water vapor increases, so relative humidity decreases even if absolute humidity remains constant
* Conversely, cooling air increases relative humidity until it reaches 100%, beyond which vapor condenses

**Dew point:** The temperature at which air becomes fully saturated with water vapor (100% relative humidity) and condensation begins to occur

* When air cools below its dew point, water vapor condenses into liquid form
* **Clinical relevance:** Explains condensation ("rain-out") in breathing circuits when warm, humid exhaled gases meet cooler circuit components

💡 **Pressure-humidity relationship:** Water vapor (MW=18) has less atomic mass than nitrogen (MW=28) or oxygen (MW=32), so increasing humidity slightly reduces the overall density of a gas mixture, thereby reducing the pressure it generates at the same volume

### 5.1.2. HUMIDITY MEASUREMENT TECHNIQUES

![[Pasted image 20250609144014.png]]

#### 5.1.2.1. HAIR HYGROMETER

* **Principle:** Hair length increases with humidity (hygroscopic properties)
* **Mechanism:** Hair fixed at one end, attached to calibrated graduated scale at the other
* **Accuracy:** Works well between 30-90% relative humidity
* **Limitation:** Simple mechanical method with limited precision

#### 5.1.2.2. WET AND DRY BULB HYGROMETER

* **Components:** Two identical thermometers - one dry bulb (ambient temperature), one wet bulb (with wick connected to water bath)
* **Principle:** Latent heat of vaporization causes wet bulb to cool proportionally to evaporation rate, which depends on air humidity
* **Measurement:** Temperature difference between thermometers correlates to humidity
	* Bigger difference = less humid air (greater evaporation from wet bulb)
* **Output:** Relative humidity calculated using pre-calibrated reference tables
* **Limitation:** Only provides relative humidity, not absolute humidity

#### 5.1.2.3. REGNAULT'S HYGROMETER (DEW POINT METHOD)

* **Principle:** Air blown through silver tube containing ether with internal thermometer
* **Process:**
	* Ether evaporates → cools silver plate via latent heat loss
	* When plate reaches dew point → condensation forms on surface
	* Indicates air is fully saturated (100% relative humidity)
	* Thermometer reads dew point temperature
* **Calculation:** Saturated vapor pressure tables determine relative humidity

#### 5.1.2.4. ELECTRONIC METHODS [PART A/B]

* **Mass Spectroscopy:**
	* Direct measurement of water vapor molecules
	* Accurate and rapid but bulky and expensive
* **Humidity Transducers:**
	* Changes in electrical conductivity or capacitance at different humidity levels
	* Modern anesthesia equipment uses this principle
	* Compact, integrated, continuous readings

## 5.2. CLINICAL IMPORTANCE OF HUMIDIFICATION

### 5.2.1. PHYSIOLOGICAL REQUIREMENTS

**Normal upper airway conditioning:**

* Nose and upper airway normally condition inspired air to 32-34°C with 100% relative humidity before reaching trachea
* **Isothermic boundary point:** Where 37°C and 100% humidity are achieved, normally a few centimeters distal to carina
* ⚠️ Tracheal/tracheostomy tubes bypass upper airway, moving this point distally

**Target values for adequate humidification:**

* **Inspired humidity:** 25-30 g/m³ absolute humidity
* **Relative humidity:** 60-70%
* **Temperature:** Gases warmed to 29-34°C
* **Minimum threshold:** 30 g/m³ water vapor at 30°C

### 5.2.2. CONSEQUENCES OF INADEQUATE HUMIDIFICATION

**Pathophysiological effects:**

* **Airway damage:** Inhaling dry gases can damage respiratory tract cells and impair ciliary function in as little as 10 minutes
* **Increased infection risk:** Susceptibility to respiratory tract infection and ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP)
* **Secretion problems:** Thickening of mucus resulting in airway plugging
* **Ciliary dysfunction:** Dry air causes ciliary disappearance, reducing airway clearance
* **Mucosal damage:** Keratinization and ulceration of airway mucosa
* **Thermal effects:** Dry gas inhalation decreases body temperature due to latent heat of vaporization loss
* **Metabolic burden:** Humidifying and warming inspired gas accounts for ~15% of body's total basal heat expenditure

**High-risk patients:**

* Prolonged anesthesia cases
* ICU patients on mechanical ventilation
* Underlying respiratory disease
* Extremes of age (neonates and elderly)
* Tracheostomies (bypassing upper airway conditioning)

### 5.2.3. OPERATING THEATER STANDARDS

**Recommended conditions:**

* **Humidity:** 50-60% relative humidity
* **Temperature:** 20-24°C (procedure-dependent)

**Rationale for humidity control:**

* **Fire safety:** Risk of static electrical shocks increases below 40% humidity, creating fire and equipment hazards
* **Tissue protection:** Moderate humidity prevents excessive drying of exposed tissues
* **Equipment function:** Affects accuracy of some monitoring equipment

**Optimal range considerations:**

* **>60% humidity:** May promote microbial growth, increase staff discomfort, create equipment condensation
* **<40% humidity:** Increases static electricity risk, accelerates tissue drying

## 5.3. HUMIDIFICATION METHODS

### 5.3.1. CLASSIFICATION SYSTEM

**Active humidification:** External energy source adds heat and moisture 
**Passive humidification:** Captures and recycles patient's own heat and moisture

### 5.3.2. HEAT AND MOISTURE EXCHANGERS (HME)

**Compact, passive, efficient humidifiers that retain expired moisture and heat and return them during inspiration**

#### 5.3.2.1. COMPONENTS AND DESIGN

* **Ports:** Two ports accepting 15mm and 22mm connections, some with gas sampling port
* **Housing/Medium:** Contains one of three media types:
	* **Hydrophobic (water repelling):** Aluminum or coated glass fibers - simple, cheap but less efficient
	* **Hygroscopic (water retaining):** Paper or foam impregnated with calcium/lithium chloride - better efficiency
	* **Combined hygroscopic-hydrophobic:** Best efficiency
* **Dead space:** Ranges from 7.8 mL (pediatric) to 100 mL

#### 5.3.2.2. MECHANISM OF ACTION

1. **Hydrophobic HME:**
	
	* Warm humidified exhaled gases condense on cooler medium
	* Condensed water evaporates during inspiration, humidifying incoming gases
	* No water addition beyond what was previously exhaled
2. **Hygroscopic HME:**
	
	* Low thermal medium preserves moisture through chemical reaction with salts
	* Chemical affinity attracts water particles, improving efficiency
3. **Performance factors:**
	
	* Temperature difference between HME sides drives heat/moisture transfer
	* Requires 5-20 minutes to reach optimal function
	* Some designs (HMEFs) include bacterial/viral filtration (pore size ~0.2 μm)

#### 5.3.2.3. PERFORMANCE CHARACTERISTICS

* **Efficiency:** Achieves ~60-70% relative humidity (~25 g/m³ compared to 34 g/m³ from nose)
* **Temperature:** Inspired gases warmed to 29-34°C
* **Contact time dependency:** Performance affected by:
	* Vapor content and temperature of gases
	* Flow rates (affecting contact time)
	* Volume and efficiency of medium

#### 5.3.2.4. ADVANTAGES

* Simple, passive device requiring no power source
* Cost-effective and easy to use
* Positioned between breathing system and catheter mount
* Can combine humidification with bacterial/viral filtration

#### 5.3.2.5. PROBLEMS AND LIMITATIONS

* ⚠️ **Resistance:** Increases flow resistance (0.1-2.0 cmH₂O depending on flow rate)
* ⚠️ **Obstruction risk:** Mucus or saturated material can cause dangerous resistance increases
* **Time dependency:** Takes 5-20 minutes to reach optimal humidification
* **Duration limits:** Recommended maximum 24 hours use on single patient
* **Volume dependency:** Decreased efficiency with large tidal/minute volumes
* **Flow requirements:** Requires two-way gas flow to function adequately
* **Positioning:** Must be close to patient for optimal function
* **Medication compatibility:** ⚠️ Generally should be removed during aerosolized medication administration

💡 **Clinical decision factors for HME use:**

* **Preferred for:** Short-medium duration anesthesia (<8 hours), transport, minimal secretions, circuit simplicity
* **Avoid in:** Thick secretions, low tidal volume (<7 ml/kg), hypothermic patients, low-flow anesthesia (<1 L/min), prolonged ventilation (>24 hours)

### 5.3.3. ACTIVE HUMIDIFICATION SYSTEMS

#### 5.3.3.1. HOT WATER BATH HUMIDIFIER

**Active humidification system that adds water vapor by passing gas over or through heated water**

**Components:**

* Disposable reservoir of sterile water with gas inlet/outlet
* Thermostatically controlled heating element with temperature sensors in reservoir and breathing system
* Feedback temperature sensor located close to patient
* Delivery tubing (should be as short as possible)
* Water trap positioned between patient and humidifier, lower than patient level

**Mechanism of Action:**

* Electrically powered heating of water to 45-60°C
* Dry cold fresh gas enters via various paths:
	* Over water surface
	* Bubbling through water
	* Contacting wicks dipped in water (increasing surface area)
* Large surface area container designed for vaporization
* System aims to fully saturate gas at water bath temperature
* Temperature regulation:
	* Higher reservoir temperature compensates for tubing cooling
	* Thermistor near patient measures delivered temperature
	* Feedback mechanism controls water temperature
* **Enhanced designs:** Heated elements in breathing circuit prevent rainout, water vapor-permeable expiratory limb

**Performance:**

* Can deliver fully saturated gas at 37°C
* Water heated to 60°C kills bacteria
* Superior to passive humidification for long-term ventilation

**Safety Features and Problems:**

* ⚠️ **Safety features:** Backup thermostat prevents scalding, overhydration, electric shock
* ⚠️ **Positioning critical:** Humidifier and water traps must be below tracheal tube level to prevent airway flooding
* **Bacterial risk:** Water colonization risk, increased temperature (60°C) helps prevent but increases burn risk
* **Practical issues:** Bulky, expensive, potentially awkward to use
* ⚠️ **Connection risk:** More connections increase disconnection/leak risk
* **Maintenance:** Requires regular cleaning and sterile water replacement

#### 5.3.3.2. CASCADE HUMIDIFIER

* **Enhanced hot water system:** Gas bubbled through hot water via perforated plate
* **Advantage:** More efficient than simple hot water bath - gas is fully saturated
* **Disadvantages:** Similar to hot water bath (bulk, electrical risks, cost)

#### 5.3.3.3. BUBBLE HUMIDIFIER

**Simple active humidifier where fresh gas flow is bubbled through sterile water**

**Components:**

* Sterile water container/bottle
* Gas inlet tube extending below water surface
* Outlet for humidified gas

**Mechanism:**

* Fresh gas bubbled through sterile water container
* Small bubbles gain humidity as they rise to surface
* Used with low-flow oxygen delivery devices (nasal cannulae)

**Limitations:**

* Not very efficient
* Water loses latent heat of vaporization, cooling and becoming less volatile
* Reduced vapor production over time

### 5.3.4. ALTERNATIVE PASSIVE METHODS

#### 5.3.4.1. SODA LIME HUMIDIFICATION

* **Principle:** CO₂ + soda lime reaction produces water as byproduct
* **Efficacy:** Can produce up to 70% relative humidity
* **Application:** Provides some humidification in circle breathing systems
* **Limitation:** Minimal contribution to overall humidification needs

#### 5.3.4.2. TRACHEAL SALINE INSTILLATION

* **Method:** Direct instillation of saline into trachea
* **Limitations:** Short-term effect, can trigger coughing, limited efficacy

#### 5.3.4.3. BOTTLE HUMIDIFIER

* **Principle:** Cold water evaporation as gas passes over water surface
* **Efficacy:** Can produce up to 40% humidity
* **Limitation:** Insufficient for long-term use in intubated patients

## 5.4. NEBULIZERS

**Devices that produce microdroplets of water or medication suspended in gas for respiratory tract delivery**

### 5.4.1. PROPERTIES AND TARGETING

**Droplet characteristics:**

* Produces mist of microdroplets suspended in gaseous medium
* Quantity not limited by gas temperature (unlike vapor)
* Smaller droplets are more stable

**Respiratory deposition by size:**

* **2-5 μm:** Deposit in tracheobronchial tree
* **0.5-1 μm:** Deposit in alveoli
* **>5 μm:** Fall back into water container

**Clinical applications:**

* Delivering water to respiratory tract
* Medication delivery to airways
* Radioactive isotope delivery for diagnostic lung ventilation imaging

### 5.4.2. TYPES OF NEBULIZERS

#### 5.4.2.1. GAS-DRIVEN (JET) NEBULIZER

**Components:**

* Capillary tube with bottom end immersed in water container
* Top end of capillary tube close to Venturi constriction

**Mechanism:**

* High-pressure gas flows through Venturi, creating negative pressure
* Water drawn up through capillary tube and broken into fine spray
* Even smaller droplets created when spray hits anvil or baffle
* Most droplets 2-4 μm, depositing primarily in pharynx and upper airway
* Larger droplets (up to 20 μm) fall back into container
* Droplets ≤4 μm float out with fresh gas flow
* Compact design allows placement close to patient

![Jet nebulizer mechanism](OTHER_EQUIPMENT_pdf_VENTHME.pdf_p61_img2_52d60a.jpeg)

#### 5.4.2.2. SPINNING DISC NEBULIZER

**Mechanism:**

* Motor-driven spinning disc throws out microdroplets by centrifugal force
* Water drawn from reservoir via tube over which disc is mounted
* Water impinges onto spinning disc

#### 5.4.2.3. ULTRASONIC NEBULIZER

**Mechanism:**

* Transducer head vibrates at ultrasonic frequency (e.g., 3 MHz)
* Transducer either immersed in water or water dropped onto it
* Produces droplets <1-2 μm, depositing in alveoli and lower airways
* Highly efficient method of humidifying and delivering drugs to airway
* ⚠️ **Risk:** Overhydration, especially in children

## 5.5. BACTERIAL AND VIRAL FILTERS

**Devices designed to minimize cross-transmission of microorganisms between patients using anesthetic breathing systems**

### 5.5.1. CLINICAL RATIONALE AND PLACEMENT

**Need for filtration:**

* British Standard: "Devices intended to reduce transmission of particulates, including microorganisms, in breathing systems"
* Bleeding after orotracheal intubation occurs in ~86% of cases, creating contamination risk
* Should be positioned as close to patient as possible (e.g., on disposable catheter mount)
* Recommended to use new filter for each patient
* Can be combined with humidification element (HMEF)

### 5.5.2. IDEAL FILTER CHARACTERISTICS

**Performance requirements:**

* Efficient against air-borne and liquid-borne microorganisms (99.99-99.999% filtration)
* Minimal dead space, especially for pediatric use
* Minimum resistance, even when wet
* Unaffected by and not affecting anesthetic agents
* Effective when wet or dry; preventing passage of contaminated body fluids

**Practical requirements:**

* User-friendly, lightweight, non-bulky, non-traumatic
* Disposable and cost-effective
* Provides some humidification if no other methods used
* Transparent housing
* Easy integration with existing equipment

![Breathing filter](OTHER_EQUIPMENT_pdf_VENTHME.pdf_p63_img1_799ba3.jpeg)

### 5.5.3. FILTRATION MECHANISMS

**Physical and electrostatic principles:**

1. **Direct interception:** Large particles (≥1 μm) physically prevented from passing through pores
	
2. **Inertial impaction:** Smaller particles (0.5-1 μm) collide with filter medium due to inertia, held by Van der Waals forces
	
3. **Diffusional interception:** Very small particles (<0.5 μm) undergo Brownian motion, increasing apparent diameter and capture likelihood
	
4. **Electrostatic attraction:** Charged particles attracted to oppositely charged fibers
	
5. **Gravitational settling:** Affects large particles (>5 μm), minimal effect in anesthesia filters

![[Pasted image 20250609144329.png]]
### 5.5.4. FILTER TYPES

#### 5.5.4.1. ELECTROSTATIC FILTERS

**Design and mechanism:**

* Felt-like material subjected to electric field producing high polarity
* Two polymer fibers (modacrylic and polypropylene) become oppositely charged
* Flat layer with lower flow resistance per unit area
* Rely on electrical charge to attract oppositely charged particles
* **Filtration efficiency:** 99.99%

**Advantages:**

* Electric charge increases filter efficiency when dry
* Can include hygroscopic layer for humidification (HMEF)

**Disadvantages:**

* ⚠️ **Moisture sensitivity:** Effectiveness deteriorates when wet
* ⚠️ **Charge decay:** Electrical charge decays over time, limiting lifespan
* Limited duration of effectiveness

![Electrostatic filter](OTHER_EQUIPMENT_pdf_VENTHME.pdf_p67_img1_e2d943.jpeg)

#### 5.5.4.2. PLEATED HYDROPHOBIC FILTERS

**Design and mechanism:**

* Very small pore size membrane with naturally occurring electrostatic interactions
* Large surface area through pleating allows low resistance with small pores
* Water-repellent - forces between water molecules stronger than between water and membrane
* **Filtration efficiency:** 99.999%

**Performance characteristics:**

* Can prevent water passage under pressures up to 60 cmH₂O
* Provide some humidification, enhanced with hygroscopic element
* Maintain effectiveness in moist conditions
* More consistent performance over time

**Viral pathogens:**

* Hepatitis virus: 0.02 μm
* Adenovirus: 0.07 μm
* HIV: 0.08 μm
* COVID-19 coronavirus: 0.05-0.1 μm
* Cytomegalovirus: 0.1 μm

**Bacterial pathogens:**

* Mycobacterium tuberculosis: 0.3 μm
* Staphylococcus aureus: 1.0 μm

#### 5.5.6.1. VAPOUR-CLEAN ACTIVATED CHARCOAL FILTERS

**Application:** Remove traces of volatile agents from anesthetic machine

**Clinical indication:** Preparing "vapour-free" machine for malignant hyperthermia susceptible patients

## 5.7. CLINICAL DECISION ALGORITHMS

### 5.7.1. HUMIDIFICATION METHOD SELECTION

**Decision factors for optimal humidification:**

1. **Duration of ventilation:**
	
	* <8 hours: HME acceptable

	* >24 hours: Active humidification preferred

2. **Patient factors:**
	
	* Thick secretions: Active humidification
	* Hypothermia: Active humidification
	* Low tidal volumes: Consider active humidification
3. **Technical factors:**
	
	* Low-flow anesthesia: May require active humidification
	* Circuit simplicity priority: HME
	* Medication nebulization: Active humidification or HME removal

### 5.7.2. FILTER SELECTION CRITERIA

**Filter type decision matrix:**

1. **High moisture environment:** Pleated hydrophobic filters
2. **Dry conditions:** Either electrostatic or hydrophobic
3. **Maximum filtration efficiency required:** Hydrophobic (99.999%)
4. **Cost considerations:** Electrostatic filters
5. **Combined humidification needed:** HMEF designs

### 5.7.3. QUALITY AND SAFETY CONSIDERATIONS

**Systems-based practice principles:**

* **Procedural standardization:** Consistent filter and humidification protocols
***Team communication:** Clear indication for humidification method changes
***Performance monitoring:** Track humidity-related complications and equipment performance
***Evidence-based practice:** Regular review of humidification protocols based on current evidence

# 6. LASERS

## 6.1. PRINCIPLES OF LASER FUNCTION

### 6.1.1. LASER DEFINITION AND COMPONENTS

* **LASER** stands for **L**ight **A**mplification by **S**timulated **E**mission of **R**adiation.
* **Three main features of laser light:**
  * **Monochromatic:** Consists of one wavelength.
  * **Coherent:** Photon wavelengths are in phase with one another.
  * **Collimated:** Light travels in parallel beams.
* **Components of a laser:**
  * **Optical resonator:** Lasing chamber with a fully reflective surface at one end and a partially reflective surface at the other end.
  * **Lasing medium:** Can be solid, liquid, or gas (e.g., Argon, CO₂, Ruby, Nd-YAG).
  * **Energy source:** Provides energy to excite the lasing medium.

### 6.1.2. MECHANISM OF OPERATION

* **Pumping:** Energy is applied to the lasing medium, such as a flash of light or voltage.
* **Electron excitation:** This elevates electrons from ground state (e1) to a higher energy shell (e2).
* **Photon emission:** When electrons fall back to ground state, they emit photons of a specific wavelength determined by the lasing medium.
* **Amplification:**
  * Emitted photons bounce back and forth between mirrors in the optical resonator.
  * This triggers other electrons to jump to higher energy shells and emit more photons.
  * When more atoms have "pumped" electrons than ground state electrons, the medium is said to "lase".
* **Output:** Laser light is produced and emitted through the partially reflective end of the resonator.
  ![Laser Stimulated Emission](physics_laser_mechanism_stimulated_3m9k2x_1765698060952.png)

💡 The unique properties of laser light (monochromatic, coherent, collimated) allow for precise targeting of tissues and minimal scatter, making lasers ideal for many surgical applications.

**Bottom Line:** Lasers produce highly focused, single-wavelength light through stimulated emission from excited atoms in a lasing medium. The specific properties of laser light enable precise cutting, coagulation, and ablation in surgical applications.

## 6.2. TYPES OF MEDICAL LASERS

### 6.2.1. RUBY

* **Wavelength:** 694 nm (red laser).
* **Medical applications:** Tattoo removal, dermatology.
* **Characteristics:** Good for targeting pigmented structures.

### 6.2.2. CO₂

* **Wavelength:** 10,600 nm (far infrared spectrum).
* **Medical applications:**
  * Superficial surgery and tissue ablation.
  * Precise cutting of soft tissues.
* **Characteristics:**
  * Very poor tissue penetration (absorbed by water).
  * Excellent for superficial procedures.
  * Good hemostasis for vessels <0.5mm.

### 6.2.3. ND-YAG (NEODYMIUM-DOPED YTTRIUM ALUMINUM GARNET)

* **Wavelength:** 1,060 nm (near infrared spectrum).
* **Medical applications:**
  * Cutting and coagulation in surgery and endoscopy.
  * Airway surgery (considered best option).
  * Vascular lesions.
* **Characteristics:**
  * Good tissue penetration (2-6mm).
  * Excellent hemostatic properties.
  * Can be conducted through flexible fibers.

### 6.2.4. ARGON

* **Wavelength:** 480-500 nm (blue-green spectrum).
* **Medical applications:**
  * Retinal coagulation and ophthalmology.
  * Dermatology (vascular lesions).
  * Best for eye surgery as it penetrates transparent tissues well.
* **Characteristics:**
  * Selectively absorbed by hemoglobin and melanin.
  * Less tissue penetration than Nd-YAG.
  * Good for precise work on vascularized tissues.

**Other Lasers**

* **Liquid (Yellow Pulsed Dye):**
  * **Wavelength:** 390-640 nm.
  * **Applications:** Dermatology, birthmark removal, cutaneous vascular lesions.
  * **Characteristics:** Very specific for vascular targets.
* **Excimer:**
  * **Wavelength:** 193-351 nm (ultraviolet).
  * **Applications:** Corneal reshaping (LASIK), dermatology.
  * **Characteristics:** Does not generate heat, breaks molecular bonds directly.

💡 Laser selection should match the procedure requirements:

* CO₂ for superficial precision cutting with minimal penetration
* Nd-YAG for deeper tissue effects and better hemostasis
* Argon for selective vascular targeting, especially in transparent tissues

**Bottom Line:** Different lasers produce specific wavelengths that interact uniquely with tissues. The choice of laser depends on the desired tissue effect, penetration depth, and hemostatic requirements of the procedure.

## 6.3. LASER SAFETY [PART B/C]

### 6.3.1. STAFF AND ENVIRONMENT SAFETY [PART B/C]

* **Laser hazard classification:**
  * Lasers are rated from Class 1 (least dangerous) to Class 4 (most dangerous).
  * Medical lasers are typically Class 4 (most dangerous).
  * Class 1 devices (e.g., CD players) stimulate the blink reflex, while Class 4 medical lasers do not.
* **Main safety concerns:**
  * Potential for burns, particularly to the retina and optic nerve.
  * Risk factors include laser power and whether they stimulate the blink reflex.
* **Staff and environment safety measures:**
  * **Protective eyewear:** Appropriate wavelength-specific goggles for all personnel.
  * **Room preparation:**
	* Block windows.
	* Lock doors.
	* Post warning signs.
	* Minimize reflective surfaces.
  * **Communication:** Clear verbal warnings before laser activation.
  * **Emergency equipment:** Fire extinguisher and saline should be immediately available.
  * **Training:** All personnel should have appropriate laser safety training    [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].

⚠️ Laser-specific eyewear must be worn by all personnel in the room. Regular glasses or generic safety goggles are insufficient protection against Class 4 medical lasers.

### 6.3.2. PATIENT SAFETY CONSIDERATIONS [PART B/C]

* **General precautions:**
  * Use low power when positioning the aiming beam.
  * Provide appropriate eye protection for the patient.
  * Use wet swabs to protect adjacent tissues.
* **Airway procedures:**
  * Fill endotracheal tube cuff with saline (not air) to prevent cuff ignition.
  * Minimize FiO₂ (<0.3) when possible to reduce fire risk.
  * Use laser-safe endotracheal tubes when available.
  * Apply wet gauze around the surgical field to prevent fire spread.
* **Other considerations:**
  * Laser plume (smoke) may contain viral particles, toxic gases, and carcinogens.
  * Effective smoke evacuation systems should be used.
  * Laser reflections from instruments can cause unintended injury.

⚠️ A dedicated "laser watchperson" should be assigned during laser procedures to monitor for safety issues, including watching for reflective surfaces and ensuring all personnel maintain appropriate precautions throughout the procedure    [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Laser safety requires a systematic approach involving proper equipment, room preparation, personnel training, and specific surgical techniques. The main risks include eye injury, fire, and tissue damage from direct or reflected laser energy.

## 6.4. AIRWAY MANAGEMENT WITH LASERS [PART B/C]

### 6.4.1. LASER-SAFE ENDOTRACHEAL TUBES

* **Features of laser-safe endotracheal tubes:**
  * Stainless steel flexible spiral construction.
  * Non-combustible material.
  * Easy to sterilize and inert in human tissue.
  * Twin distal cuffs may be available as an optional feature.

* **Alternatives when specialized tubes are unavailable:**
  * Standard PVC tubes wrapped with metallic tape (not covering the connector).
  * Fill cuff with colored saline (helps detect cuff rupture).
  * Keep tube cuff below the laser surgical field if possible.

⚠️ Never use red rubber tubes for laser airway surgery due to high flammability. If metal laser tubes are unavailable, PVC tubes with protective wrapping and saline-filled cuffs are preferred.

### 6.4.2. AIRWAY FIRE MANAGEMENT [PART B/C]

* **Immediate management of an airway fire:**
  * Turn off the laser immediately.
  * Call for help.
  * Turn down oxygen and stop ventilation if possible.
  * Remove the burnt tube.
  * Irrigate the airway with saline or water.
  * Facemask ventilate and then re-intubate using room air or lowest necessary FiO₂.
  * Perform bronchoscopy to evaluate degree of injury to airways.
  * Consider steroids, antibiotics, and ICU observation depending on extent of injury.
* **Prevention of airway fires:**
  * Minimize FiO₂ (ideally <30%) and avoid N₂O.
  * Use total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) when possible.
  * Use jet ventilation or apneic techniques for short procedures.
  * Ensure good communication between surgeon and anesthesiologist before laser activation.
  * Use saline-soaked pledgets around the tube.
  * Keep suction immediately available.

⚠️ Airway fire is a life-threatening emergency requiring immediate action. The most critical first steps are to stop ventilation/oxygen flow and remove the burning tube. Practice drills for airway fire management should be conducted regularly    [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Laser airway surgery requires specialized equipment and techniques to minimize fire risk. If an airway fire occurs, rapid response with a systematic approach is essential to minimize patient harm, focusing first on removing the ignition source, extinguishing the fire, and then assessing and managing airway injury.
