# 1. ENDOGENOUS OPIOID SYSTEM


## 1.1. OPIOID RECEPTORS AND ENDOGENOUS PEPTIDES



### 1.1.1. RECEPTOR FAMILY STRUCTURE

**G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs)** form the basis of the opioid receptor family with four main receptor types [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:

* **MOP (μ, mu)**: Mediates most clinical effects of opioids [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] . When stimulated produces analgesia, miosis, euphoria, respiratory depression, bradycardia, and inhibition of gut motility .
* **KOP (κ, kappa)**: Associated with sedation and spinal analgesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] . When stimulated produces analgesia, sedation, and miosis .
* **DOP (δ, delta)**: Contributes to analgesia and behavioral effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] . When stimulated produces analgesia and respiratory depression .
* **NOP (nociception)**: Novel receptor target for pain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]. Involved in anxiety, depression, and appetite modulation .

**Receptor Structure** :
* All opioid receptors are serpentine (span the membrane seven times) and linked to inhibitory G-proteins 
* When stimulated by an agonist, they cause :
  * Closure of voltage-sensitive Ca²⁺ channels
  * Hyperpolarization by K⁺ efflux
  * Adenylase cyclase inhibition leading to reduced cAMP
  * These processes inhibit transmitter release between nerve cells

### 1.1.2. MORE SUBTYPES AND CLINICAL EFFECTS

**More Subtypes** (identified in rodent models) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:

* **μ1**: Predominantly mediates **analgesia** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **μ2**: Primary mediator of **respiratory depression** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **μ3**: Involved in **immune suppression** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**More Location** :
* Throughout the CNS including cerebral cortex, basal ganglia, spinal cord (presynaptically on primary afferent neurons within the dorsal horn), and periaqueductal grey 
* Acts as the origin of the descending inhibitory control pathway 

**Opioid Receptor Clinical Effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:

| μ (More) | • Supraspinal analgesia (μ1)<br>• Respiratory depression (μ2)<br>• Physical dependence<br>• Muscle rigidity | **MORPHINE**, **FENTANYL**, Met-enkephalin, β-Endorphin |
| κ (KOR) | • Sedation<br>• Spinal analgesia | **MORPHINE**, **NALBUPHINE**, **BUTORPHANOL**, Dynorphin, **OXYCODONE** |

### 1.1.3. ENDOGENOUS OPIOID PEPTIDES

The body produces natural opioid peptides that act on specific receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:

* **β-Endorphins** → High affinity for More [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]. Also binds to KOP and DOP receptors 
* **Met/leu-enkephalins** → Affinity for DOP [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]. Met-enkephalins also bind to More 
* **Dynorphin A** → Affinity for KOR [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Nociceptin** → Selective for NOP receptor [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Endogenous morphine** → Acts via μ3-receptor on immune cells [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 1.1.4. GPCR SIGNALING MECHANISMS

Opioid receptors signal through several pathways [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:

* **Inhibition of adenyl cyclase** → ↓cAMP [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Activation of Ca²⁺ and K⁺ channels** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Activation of mitogen-activated protein kinase pathways** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* *Example*: **MORPHINE** produces analgesia and respiratory depression through More activation of adenylate cyclase/cAMP pathway [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Opioid receptors (primarily More, KOP, and DOP) are GPCRs activated by both endogenous peptides and exogenous drugs, with μ-receptors mediating most clinical effects of opioid analgesics including both therapeutic analgesia and adverse effects like respiratory depression.

## 1.2. MECHANISMS OF OPIOID ANALGESIA



### 1.2.1. CENTRAL OPIOID ANALGESIA

**Pain transmission pathway** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] :
* **Nociceptors** → **C-fibers** (slow, burning pain, unmyelinated, diameter <2 μm, conduct at 2 m.s⁻¹) and **Aδ fibers** (fast, sharp pain, small myelinated, diameter 2-5 μm, conduct at 40 m.s⁻¹) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Dorsal horn** (laminae I-II) → **spinothalamic tract** → **thalamic nuclei** → **cortex** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Aδ fibers synapse at Rexed laminae II and V; C fibers at Rexed lamina II 
* The substantia gelatinosa (lamina II) integrates these inputs 

**More activation** → enhanced descending pain inhibition [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
* **Periaqueductal gray (PAG)** and **rostroventral medulla (RVM)** → inhibition of dorsal horn [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Direct spinal cord action at pre/postsynaptic sites in dorsal horn [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 1.2.2. ENDOGENOUS OPIOID SYSTEM FUNCTIONS

**Electrical stimulation** of PAG/RVM → naloxone-reversible analgesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

Three clinical manifestations [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:

1. **Stress-induced analgesia**: Activated during stress (explains delayed pain in trauma) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
2. **Placebo-induced analgesia**: More activation from pain relief expectation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
3. **Conditioned Pain Modulation (CPM)**: "Pain inhibits pain" mechanism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
	* **TAPENTADOL** works partly through this pathway [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
	* 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: Diabetic neuropathy patients often have dysfunctional CPM, explaining poor response to some analgesics [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

### 1.2.3. PERIPHERAL OPIOID ANALGESIA

**Receptors located on** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
* **Sensory neurons** (Aδ and C-fibers) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Immune cells** (human leukocytes) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**Particularly important in inflammatory pain** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

**Inflammation cascade** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
1. Tissue injury → inflammatory mediators → nociceptor sensitization → pain/hyperalgesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
2. Leukocyte migration → release of opioid peptides → activation of neuronal opioid receptors → analgesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
3. Inflammation upregulates opioid receptors → enhanced analgesic effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

🏥 **Clinical Practice**: Inflammation simultaneously generates pain and initiates counteracting analgesia via endogenous opioids [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

### 1.2.4. RECEPTOR INTERACTION MECHANISMS

**Primary cellular mechanisms** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] :
* All opioids act through G-protein coupled receptors (GPCRs) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* ↓ Calcium influx presynaptically → ↓ neurotransmitter release [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* ↑ Potassium efflux postsynaptically → hyperpolarization → ↓ signal transmission [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Inhibition of GABAergic transmission in brainstem → activation of descending inhibitory circuits [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**Receptor Distribution** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
* **Ascending pain pathway** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Peripheral nociceptors (especially after inflammation) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Spinal cord dorsal horn (presynaptic and postsynaptic) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Brainstem, thalamus, and cortical regions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Descending inhibitory pathway** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Periaqueductal gray [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Nucleus raphe magnus [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Rostral ventral medulla [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 1.2.5. GATE THEORY OF PAIN

**Gate Theory** :
* Descending pathways and larger Aβ fibers (conducting 'touch') stimulate inhibitory interneurons within the substantia gelatinosa 
* These inhibit C fiber nociceptive inputs, effectively "closing the gate" to pain transmission 
* Forms a neurophysiological basis for how non-painful stimuli can modulate pain perception 

**Bottom Line:** Opioids provide analgesia through multiple mechanisms, including activation of central inhibitory pathways (PAG, RVM), direct inhibition at the spinal level, and peripheral action at inflammatory sites. This multi-level activity explains their effectiveness for different pain types. The endogenous opioid system also provides natural analgesia through stress-induced, placebo-induced, and conditioned pain modulation mechanisms.

## 1.3. MULTIFUNCTIONAL OPIOIDS



### 1.3.1. MULTIPLE RECEPTOR ACTIVITY

* Most clinical opioids act at multiple receptors with varying affinities [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **MORPHINE**: High affinity for More, lower affinities for KOR and DOR [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

### 1.3.2. DUAL-MECHANISM AGENTS

* **METHADONE**: More agonist + NMDA receptor antagonist [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] . Especially beneficial for treating certain neuropathic pain resistant to typical opioids 
* **TRAMADOL**: More agonist + inhibits reuptake of noradrenaline and 5-HT 
* **TAPENTADOL**: More agonist + noradrenaline reuptake inhibitor (NARI) with weak serotonin reuptake inhibition 
* **KETAMINE**: Primary NMDA antagonist with secondary More activity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

### 1.3.3. NOVEL MULTIFUNCTIONAL COMPOUNDS

* **Cebranopadol** (More/NOP activity) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Effective for both acute and neuropathic pain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Improved side effect profile in preclinical studies [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **DPI-125** (More/KOR/DOR activity) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Less respiratory depression (via DOR effects) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Reduced addiction potential (via KOR activation) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Many clinically useful opioids act on multiple receptors or have additional non-opioid mechanisms. This pharmacological diversity can provide advantages such as improved safety profiles, reduced respiratory depression, or effectiveness against different pain types like neuropathic pain.

## 1.4. PAIN PHYSIOLOGY AND TRANSMISSION



### 1.4.1. PAIN DEFINITION AND CLASSIFICATION

**Definition**: Pain is an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with actual or potential tissue damage. Since pain is highly subjective, it may also be described as what the patient says it is .

**Classification by Aetiology** :
* **Nociceptive pain**: Results from stimulation of nociceptors by noxious stimuli 
* **Neuropathic pain**: Results from dysfunction of the nervous system 
* **Mixed pain**: Combination of nociceptive and neuropathic components 
* **Visceral pain**: Pain associated with internal organs (e.g., gallstones) 

**Classification by Duration** :
* **Acute pain**: Short-term, associated with tissue damage 
* **Chronic pain**: Persists beyond expected healing time (approximately 12 weeks) or when pain is no longer due to the initial insult 

### 1.4.2. NOCICEPTION MECHANISMS

**Activation of Nociceptors** :
* Respond to chemical, mechanical, or thermal damage 
* **Initiating Chemical Mediators**: H+, K+, acetylcholine, histamine, serotonin (5-HT), bradykinin 
* **Sensitizing Chemical Mediators**: Prostaglandins, leukotrienes, substance P, neurokinin A, calcitonin gene-related peptide 

**Primary Afferent Fibers** :
* **Aδ fibers**: Small myelinated (2-5 μm diameter), conduct sharp pain rapidly (40 m.s⁻¹) 
* **C fibers**: Unmyelinated (<2 μm diameter), conduct dull pain slowly (2 m.s⁻¹) 

**Central Pathways** :
* Fibers enter dorsal horn of spinal cord and synapse at different sites (Aδ at Rexed laminae II and V; C at Rexed lamina II) 
* The substantia gelatinosa (lamina II) integrates these inputs 
* Second-order neurons form ascending spinothalamic and spinoreticular pathways on the contralateral side 

### 1.4.3. PAIN MODULATION

**Gate Control Theory** :
* Descending pathways and larger Aβ fibers (conducting 'touch') stimulate inhibitory interneurons 
* These inhibit C fiber nociceptive inputs at the substantia gelatinosa level 
* Forms the basis for how non-painful stimuli can reduce pain perception 

**Descending Inhibitory Pathways** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
* Originate in periaqueductal gray (PAG) and rostral ventromedial medulla (RVM) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Mediated by endogenous opioids, serotonin, and norepinephrine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Can be activated by stress or expectation of pain relief (placebo effect) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Pain is a complex subjective experience that can be classified by etiology (nociceptive, neuropathic, visceral) or duration (acute, chronic). The pain pathway involves activation of specialized nociceptors, transmission via Aδ and C fibers to the dorsal horn, and then to higher brain centers. Pain perception is modulated by both ascending and descending pathways, including the endogenous opioid system, which forms the neurobiological basis for opioid analgesia.

**Pharmacokinetic Factors Affecting Onset/Offset** :
* **Lipid solubility**: Higher lipid solubility (e.g., fentanyl) = faster onset but potentially longer offset due to redistribution 
* **Protein binding**: Higher protein binding = less free drug available to cross BBB 
* **pKa**: Lower pKa results in more unionized drug at physiological pH = faster onset 
* **Metabolism**: Site and rate of metabolism significantly impact offset (e.g., remifentanil's esterase metabolism) 

**Bottom Line:** For intraoperative use, onset/offset characteristics are critical for selecting the appropriate opioid based on procedure length, need for rapid awakening, and anticipated postoperative pain. This classification guides timing of administration and transition to post-operative analgesia.

## 2.5. DIFFERENCE BETWEEN OPIATES AND OPIOIDS



* **Opiates**: Naturally occurring compounds with properties similar to morphine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] . Refers to all naturally occurring substances with morphine-like properties derived from opium .
* **Opioids**: Any substance, natural or synthetic, with action at opioid receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] . More general term that includes synthetic substances that have an affinity for opioid receptors .
  * All opiates are therefore opioids, but the opposite is not true [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * All opioids are amine compounds and are basic in nature [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

**Bottom Line:** "Opioid" is the broader term encompassing all compounds (natural, semisynthetic, and synthetic) that act on opioid receptors, while "opiate" specifically refers to naturally occurring compounds derived from opium. In clinical practice, "opioid" is generally the preferred term.

# 3. BASIC PHARMACOLOGY


## 3.1. PHARMACOKINETICS



### 3.1.1. DISTRIBUTION & TRANSPORT

* Opioids rapidly distribute to heart and pulmonary vessels after IV injection, then disperse to various organs [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Interpatient variability**: 30-fold difference in plasma concentrations related to [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Weight parameters (lean/fat body mass) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Organ function (hepatic/renal) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Cardiac output [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration affected by** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Molecule size [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * pKa (degree of ionization depends on plasma pH) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Protein binding (to albumin and α1-acid glycoprotein) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Lipid solubility [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* *In other words*: Small, lipophilic, non-ionized drugs cross BBB more easily [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 3.1.2. CONCENTRATION-TIME PROFILE

* **Initial phase**: Rapid peak in plasma concentration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Distribution phase**: Rapid concentration drop as drug enters organs with high blood flow (brain, liver, kidney) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Redistribution phase**: Slower drop as drug enters less-perfused tissues (muscles, fat) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Volume of distribution (VD)**: Determines redistribution profile [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * High VD (fentanyl = 300L): Lipophilic drugs with low protein binding [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Low VD (remifentanil, alfentanil): High clearance and/or high protein binding [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 3.1.3. CONTEXT-SENSITIVE HALF-TIME (CST½)

* *Definition*: Time for plasma concentration to decrease by 50% after stopping infusion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Varies based on infusion duration (the "context") [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **FENTANYL**: CSt½ increases with infusion duration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **REMIFENTANIL**: CSt½ remains constant regardless of infusion duration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * 50% drop: 2 minutes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * 75% drop: 8 minutes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 3.1.5. ABSORPTION AND BIOAVAILABILITY

**Route-Dependent Absorption** :
* **Oral**: Opioids are ionized in acidic gastric environment (as they are weak bases), delaying absorption until they reach the alkaline small bowel where they become unionized 
* **Subcutaneous**: More effectively absorbed with highly lipid-soluble opioids (e.g., diamorphine) 
* **Transdermal**: Slow absorption, takes up to 12 hours to reach equilibrium (e.g., fentanyl patches) 

**Key Bioavailability Values** :
* **Morphine**: 30% oral bioavailability due to first-pass metabolism 
* **Codeine**: 50% oral bioavailability (higher than morphine due to methyl group reducing first-pass metabolism) 
* **Diamorphine**: Low oral bioavailability despite good absorption due to extensive first-pass metabolism 
* **Fentanyl**: Low oral bioavailability, good sublingual and transdermal absorption 
* **Methadone**: 75% oral bioavailability 
* **Tramadol**: 70% increasing to >90% with repeated doses 
* **Tapentadol**: 30% oral bioavailability 

**Bottom Line:** Opioid pharmacokinetics are influenced by many factors including lipid solubility, protein binding, ionization, and volume of distribution. These properties determine onset, duration of action, and context-sensitive half-time—crucial considerations when selecting agents for different clinical scenarios. Understanding concepts like context-sensitive half-time is essential for predicting recovery profiles after infusions.

## 3.2. METABOLISM AND EXCRETION



### 3.2.1. METABOLISM PATHWAYS

* Most opioids metabolized in the liver through two main pathways [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * **Phase I**: Oxidative and reductive reactions catalyzed by cytochrome P450 enzyme system [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Phase II**: Conjugation to specific substrates (glucuronidation) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Alternative metabolism sites: oral mucosa, GI tract enterocytes, kidney, brain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Excretion occurs via kidney and/or biliary tract [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Some opioids (morphine, buprenorphine) undergo enterohepatic circulation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**Clinical Significance** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
* ⚠️ Drug interactions: CYP450 inhibitors/inducers affect opioid metabolism and clinical effect [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Metabolites may be active or inactive (affects both analgesia and side effects) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Genetic CYP450 variability impacts opioid effectiveness [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 3.2.2. SPECIFIC OPIOID METABOLISM

**MORPHINE** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] :
* *Metabolism*: Rapid phase II reaction via UGT2B7 in liver [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* *Primary metabolites* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Morphine-3-glucuronide (M3G): 60% of morphine → no analgesic effect, may have effects on arousal and possibly μ-receptor antagonist properties [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Morphine-6-glucuronide (M6G): 5-10% of morphine → full More agonist, 13 times more potent than morphine with similar duration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* *Metabolite transport* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Hepatocytes → bloodstream via MRP3 transporter [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Small portion → bile via MRP2 transporter [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* *Enterohepatic cycle*: Gut deglucuronidation → partial morphine reabsorption [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* *Peak Effects*: 10 minutes after IV injection, 30 minutes after IM injection, with 3-4 hour duration of action 
* ⚠️ **Clinical Concern**: M6G accumulates in renal failure → severe sedation and respiratory depression [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

**PIPERIDINES** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:

**FENTANYL** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] :
* *Metabolism*: CYP450 in liver [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* *Clearance*: High hepatic extraction ratio (1.5 L/min, approaches liver blood flow) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* *Main metabolite*: Norfentanyl (inactive) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* *Onset*: Rapid following IV administration due to high lipid solubility (nearly 600 times more lipid-soluble than morphine) 
* *Transdermal application*: Plasma levels take 12 hours to reach equilibrium 
* *Duration*: At low doses (<3 μg/kg IV), short duration due solely to distribution; after prolonged administration or high doses, significantly prolonged as tissues become saturated 
* 🏥 **Clinical Practice**: Norfentanyl detectable in urine long after fentanyl elimination (useful for diagnosing abuse) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Gastric Trapping**: May become trapped in acidic stomach environment (>99.9% ionized), then becomes unionized and available for systemic absorption in alkaline small bowel, though rapid hepatic first-pass metabolism limits systemic effects 

**ALFENTANIL** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] :
* *Metabolism*: CYP3A4 and CYP3A5 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* *Metabolites*: Inactive noralfentanil and N-phenylpropionamide [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* *Onset*: Despite lower lipid solubility than fentanyl, has faster onset due to pKa of 6.5 (89% unionized at pH 7.4, compared to fentanyl's pKa of 8.4 with only 9% unionized) 
* *Note*: High variability in metabolism due to polymorphic CYP3A5 expression [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* *Drug Interactions*: Midazolam is metabolized by same hepatic enzymes (CYP3A3/4), so concurrent administration prolongs elimination half-lives of both drugs. Erythromycin may prolong alfentanil's activity by inhibiting hepatic CYP450 

**SUFENTANIL** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
* *Metabolism*: Hepatic via CYP450 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* *Clearance*: High hepatic extraction ratio (0.9 L/min) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* *Metabolites*: Inactive [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**REMIFENTANIL** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] :
* *Unique metabolism*: Blood and tissue nonspecific esterases (not liver) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* *Structure*: Contains methyl ester side chain → rapid hydrolysis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* *Clearance*: 3-5 L/min (exceeds liver blood flow, confirming extrahepatic clearance) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* *Context-sensitive half-life*: 2 minutes (most rapid-acting opioid available) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* *Administration*: Usually continuous infusion (50% decrease in plasma level in ~40 seconds) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* *Primary metabolite*: Essentially inactive carboxylic acid metabolite (1/4600th as potent) excreted in urine with half-life of 2 hours in healthy adults 
* *Special Properties*: Poor substrate for plasma cholinesterases, unaffected by cholinesterase deficiency; anticholinesterase drugs don't alter its metabolism 
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: Only opioid suitable for TCI (target-controlled infusion) in patients with liver failure [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 
* Impaired hepatic and renal function do not prolong its effects 

**METHADONE** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] :
* *Metabolism*: Extensive metabolism to inactive form via CYP2B6 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* *Bioavailability*: 60-95% orally [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* *Properties*: High potency, long duration, considerable variation in response [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* *Plasma half-life*: 18-36 hours 
* *Excretion*: Up to 40% excreted as unchanged drug in urine, enhanced in acidic conditions 
* ⚠️ **Warning**: Long, unpredictable duration when given orally has led to patient deaths in chronic pain management [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* 🏥 **Clinical Practice**: Safer when used IV in controlled perioperative settings [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

**NALOXONE** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] :
* *Classification*: Nonspecific More antagonist [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* *Administration*: Best given IV (low/unpredictable oral bioavailability due to >95% first-pass effect) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* *Metabolism*: Glucuronidation to inactive naloxone-3-glucuronide [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* *Duration*: Short (15-45 minutes) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: Requires redosing or continuous infusion when antagonizing long-acting opioids or overdose [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

### 3.2.3. PHARMACOGENETIC CONSIDERATIONS

**Additional Metabolism Notes** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:

**HYDROMORPHONE** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
* *Metabolism*: Conjugation → hydromorphone-3-glucuronide [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**MEPERIDINE (PETHIDINE)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] :
* *Metabolism*: N-demethylation → norpethidine (normeperidine, active metabolite with half the analgesic activity of pethidine) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* *Duration*: 120-150 minutes 
* *Excretion*: Norpethidine, pethidinic acid, and small amounts of unchanged pethidine excreted in urine 
* *Norpethidine Properties*: Longer elimination half-life (14-21 hours) than pethidine, accumulates in renal failure 
* ⚠️ **Warning**: Norpethidine can accumulate and cause hallucinations and grand mal seizures; its effects are not reversed by naloxone [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 

**CODEINE** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] :
* *Classification*: Prodrug [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* *Metabolism*: CYP2D6 → morphine (active compound) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* *Metabolic Pathways*: Three pathways :
  1. 6-hydroxyglucuronidation to codeine-6-glucuronide (predominant pathway)
  2. N-demethylation to norcodeine (10-20%, via CYP3A4)
  3. O-demethylation to morphine (up to 15%, via CYP2D6)
* *Metabolite Activity*: Only morphine has significant activity at μ-receptors 
* 🔑 **HY**: ~10% of Caucasians lack functional CYP2D6 → poor analgesia from codeine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 
* Genetic polymorphism frequency varies: ~9% in UK population but 30% in Hong Kong Chinese population 
* Fast metabolizers generate increased amounts of morphine with attendant side effects 

**DIHYDROCODEINE** :
* *Potency*: Approximately twice as potent as codeine 
* *Bioavailability*: About 20% orally 
* *Metabolism*: Analogous to codeine, but has greater efficacy for μ receptor and is less dependent on CYP2D6, making it more predictable than codeine 

**DIAMORPHINE** :
* *Chemical Nature*: Diacetylated morphine derivative with no affinity for opioid receptors; a prodrug whose active metabolites are responsible for its effects 
* *Potency*: Approximately twice as potent as morphine 
* *Bioavailability*: Low oral bioavailability despite good absorption due to extensive first-pass metabolism 
* *Metabolism*: Rapidly metabolized in liver, plasma, and CNS by ester hydrolysis to 6-monoacetylmorphine and morphine, which confer its analgesic effects 
* *Protein Binding*: 40% plasma protein-bound 
* *pKa*: 7.6 (37% unionized at pH 7.4) 
* *Half-life*: Approximately 5 minutes 
* *Clinical Note*: Produces greatest degree of euphoria among opioids, making it a drug of abuse 

**TRAMADOL** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] :
* *Classification*: Prodrug [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* *Metabolism*: CYP → O-desmethyltramadol (active metabolite) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* *Bioavailability*: 70% which increases to >90% after repeated doses 
* *Metabolic Pathways*: Hepatic CPY2D6, CYP3A4, and CYP2B6 followed by glucuronidation 
* *Volume of Distribution*: 4 l.kg⁻¹ 
* *Elimination Half-life*: 5-6 hours 

**HYDROCODONE** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
* *Metabolism*: Dual pathway [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * CYP2D6 → hydromorphone (more potent) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * CYP3A4 → norhydrocodone (less potent) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**OXYCODONE** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] :
* *Metabolism*: CYP2D6 → less potent active compounds (e.g., oxymorphone) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* *Similar Profile*: Alternative to morphine with similar profile, though individual patients may tolerate one better than the other 
* *Special Formulation*: Available combined with naloxone (Targinact) for treating severe pain with resistant constipation 

**TAPENTADOL** :
* *Bioavailability*: 30% due to extensive hepatic first-pass metabolism 
* *Metabolism*: Primarily glucuronidation, with limited oxidation by hepatic cytochromes (CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6) followed by further glucuronidation 
* *Metabolites*: None are active 
* *Protein Binding*: Only 20% bound in plasma 
* *Volume of Distribution*: Approximately 550 liters 
* *Elimination Half-life*: 4 hours 
* *Excretion*: Kidneys are main route 

**Genetic Polymorphisms** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
* **CYP2D6 Polymorphisms**: Affect codeine, tramadol, hydrocodone efficacy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **CYP3A5 Polymorphisms**: Account for variability in alfentanil metabolism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **CYP2B6 Polymorphisms**: Affect methadone metabolism and response [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 3.2.3.1. COMPREHENSIVE PHARMACOGENETICS OF OPIOID ANALGESIA [INTEGRATION]

<!-- EPA-12:K3:pharmacogenetics -->

Genetic variation accounts for 5-10 fold variability in morphine requirements between patients. Three key genes have Grade A/B evidence for clinical utility: CYP2D6 (metabolism), OPRM1 (receptor), and COMT (pain modulation). [Source: CPIC Guidelines 2020; PMC6390004]

**CYP2D6 - Drug Metabolising Enzyme** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]`

* **Function:** Hepatic CYP450 enzyme responsible for O-demethylation of prodrug opioids to active metabolites
* **Affected opioids:**
   * **Codeine** → Morphine (essential for analgesic effect)
   * **Tramadol** → O-desmethyltramadol (active metabolite)
   * **Hydrocodone** → Hydromorphone (more potent)
   * **Oxycodone** → Oxymorphone (minor pathway)

* **Metaboliser phenotypes and activity scores:** [Source: CPIC 2020]

| Phenotype | Activity Score | Prevalence | Clinical Impact |
|-----------|----------------|------------|-----------------|
| **Poor Metaboliser (PM)** | 0 | 0.4-6.5% (Caucasians ~7-10%) | No active metabolite → **ineffective analgesia** |
| **Intermediate Metaboliser (IM)** | >0 to <1.25 | 10-44% | Reduced conversion → decreased efficacy |
| **Normal Metaboliser (NM)** | 1.25-2.25 | 43-67% | Standard response |
| **Ultrarapid Metaboliser (UM)** | >2.25 | 1-28% (North African/Arab ~28%) | Excessive metabolite → **toxicity risk** |

* **CPIC/DPWG guideline recommendations:**
   * **PM:** AVOID codeine and tramadol - no analgesic effect
   * **UM:** AVOID codeine and tramadol - risk of life-threatening respiratory depression
   * **IM:** Use with monitoring, consider alternatives if ineffective
   * **NM:** Standard label dosing

⚠️ **Safety Warning:** A breastfeeding infant died from morphine toxicity when the mother (ultrarapid metaboliser) took codeine. FDA now contraindicates codeine in breastfeeding and children <12 years.

* **Drug interactions causing phenoconversion (NM → functional PM):**
   * SSRIs: Paroxetine, fluoxetine
   * Other CYP2D6 inhibitors: Duloxetine, bupropion, amiodarone, quinidine
   * ⚠️ Concomitant use reduces codeine/tramadol efficacy

* **Alternative opioids NOT metabolised by CYP2D6:**
   * Morphine, Fentanyl, Buprenorphine, Methadone, Hydromorphone, Oxymorphone
   * Use these in PM, UM, or when CYP2D6 inhibitors co-prescribed

**OPRM1 - Mu-Opioid Receptor Gene** [Source: PMC6390004]

* **Function:** Encodes the μ-opioid receptor - primary site of opioid action
* **Key polymorphism:** rs1799971 (A118G) - over 100 polymorphisms identified, this is most clinically significant
* **Evidence grade:** A (consistent, strong evidence)

| Genotype | Effect | Clinical Implication |
|----------|--------|---------------------|
| **AA** (wild-type) | Normal receptor function | Standard opioid requirements |
| **AG** (heterozygous) | Reduced receptor binding/signalling | Higher opioid doses needed |
| **GG** (variant homozygous) | Significantly reduced binding | May need **93% higher morphine dose** |

* **Clinical recommendations:**
   * GG genotype: Initiate at higher dose, implement more aggressive titration
   * G-allele carriers in labour: 1.5-2 fold higher epidural fentanyl requirements
   * Consider pharmacogenetic screening when pain persists despite appropriate treatment

**COMT - Catechol-O-Methyltransferase** [Source: PMC6390004]

* **Function:** Metabolises dopamine, adrenaline, noradrenaline; modulates pain perception and μ-opioid receptor expression in the brain
* **Key polymorphism:** rs4680 (Val158Met)
* **Evidence grade:** B (limited but patient-oriented evidence)

| Genotype | Enzyme Activity | Pain Sensitivity | Opioid Response |
|----------|-----------------|------------------|-----------------|
| **Val/Val (GG)** | High | Lower | Lower opioid requirement |
| **Val/Met (AG)** | Intermediate | Intermediate | Intermediate |
| **Met/Met (AA)** | Low | **Higher** | **Enhanced opioid sensitivity** |

* **Clinical implications:**
   * Met/Met genotype: Enhanced opioid analgesia but also more adverse effects
   * Val/Val carriers may need **63% higher morphine dose** vs Met/Met
   * COMT affects both endogenous and exogenous analgesic systems

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Low COMT activity (Met/Met) increases μ-receptor expression, enhancing opioid sensitivity. These patients may be more prone to opioid-induced side effects at standard doses.

**Gene-Gene Interactions (OPRM1 × COMT)** [Source: PMC5942486]

* Significant interactions documented affecting postoperative pain and opioid consumption
* **Combined effect on morphine requirements:**

| OPRM1 Genotype | COMT rs4680 | 24-hour Morphine Requirement |
|----------------|-------------|------------------------------|
| AA | Met/Met | **Lowest** (~87 mg) |
| AA | Val/Val | Intermediate |
| GG | Met/Met | Higher |
| GG | Val/Val | **Highest** (~147 mg) |

* Interaction accounts for ~3% of postoperative opioid consumption variance
* Patients with Met158Met + AG/GG OPRM1 combination require largest opioid amounts

**Other Relevant Pharmacogenetic Factors:**

* **UGT2B7** (morphine glucuronidation):
   * Creates M3G (neuroexcitatory) and M6G (potent analgesic)
   * Polymorphisms show variable clinical impact
* **ABCB1/P-glycoprotein** (opioid transport):
   * Affects CNS penetration of opioids
   * Influences methadone kinetics
* **CYP3A4/3A5** (fentanyl metabolism):
   * CYP3A5*3/*3 genotype: 2-fold higher fentanyl plasma concentrations
* **OCT1** (tramadol transport):
   * Reduced activity → elevated O-desmethyltramadol levels

**Perioperative Application of Pharmacogenetics:**

* **When to consider testing:** [Source: PMC6390004]
   * Persisting pain despite appropriate treatment
   * Grade 3+ adverse effects (NCI-CTCAE v4.0)
   * >4 breakthrough pain episodes/day
   * Before initiating codeine/tramadol in high-risk populations (paediatrics, breastfeeding)
   * History of opioid non-response or excessive sensitivity

* **Practical opioid selection by pharmacogenetic status:**

| Genetic Status | Avoid | Consider |
|----------------|-------|----------|
| CYP2D6 PM | Codeine, tramadol, hydrocodone | Morphine, fentanyl, hydromorphone |
| CYP2D6 UM | Codeine, tramadol | Morphine, fentanyl (standard doses) |
| OPRM1 GG | - | Higher initial doses, aggressive titration |
| COMT Met/Met | - | Lower initial doses, monitor for ADRs |

* **Current limitations:**
   * Cost of testing
   * Turnaround time (days vs acute pain needs)
   * Limited resource allocation for supportive care
   * Lack of rapid point-of-care testing

**Bottom Line:** Pharmacogenetics explains significant inter-individual opioid response variability. CYP2D6 status is most actionable - avoid codeine/tramadol in poor and ultrarapid metabolisers. OPRM1 G-allele carriers need higher opioid doses. COMT Met/Met carriers show enhanced opioid sensitivity. Gene-gene interactions compound effects. While routine preoperative testing is not yet standard, pharmacogenetic awareness guides opioid selection when standard approaches fail.

### 3.2.4. CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS

**Drug Interactions** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
* CYP inhibitors → increased opioid effect (potential toxicity) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* CYP inducers → decreased opioid effect (potential inadequate analgesia) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* 🔑 **HY**: Common CYP interactions [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Antibiotics (erythromycin, clarithromycin) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Antifungals (ketoconazole, fluconazole) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Anticonvulsants (phenytoin, carbamazepine) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Antidepressants (fluoxetine, paroxetine) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Cyclosporine enhances morphine's analgesic effect (not methadone's) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * *Mechanism*: Interference with morphine's efflux from brain via transporter proteins [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **PETHIDINE-MAOI Interaction**: ⚠️ Serious interaction if administered together, probably due to central serotoninergic hyperactivity caused by pethidine's inhibition of serotonin reuptake combined with MAOI-induced reduction in amine breakdown 
  * **Effects include**: Coma, labile circulation, convulsions, and hyperpyrexia 
  * Other opioids are safe with MAOIs 
* **TRAMADOL Interactions**: Potential interactions with drugs that inhibit central 5-HT or noradrenaline reuptake (tricyclic antidepressants and SSRIs), resulting in seizures 
* **TAPENTADOL Interactions** :
  * Can induce seizures; caution in patients with seizure disorders or epilepsy 
  * Seizure risk increased with medications that lower seizure threshold (SSRIs, tricyclics, antipsychotics) 
  * Serotonin syndrome reported when used with serotonergic antidepressants 
  * Manufacturers advise against MAOI use within 14 days due to NARI actions 

**Special Populations** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
* **Renal Impairment** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Avoid morphine (M6G accumulation) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Safer options: fentanyl, sufentanil, remifentanil [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Hepatic Impairment** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Consider dose reduction for CYP450-metabolized opioids [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Remifentanil may be safer option (non-hepatic clearance) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

**Monitoring Recommendations** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]:
* Higher vigilance needed with [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Patients on multiple medications (drug interactions) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Renal/hepatic dysfunction [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Genetic polymorphisms affecting metabolism [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Use of methadone (variable half-life) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

**Practical Selection Guide** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
* **Short procedures**: Remifentanil, alfentanil [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Renal failure**: Remifentanil, fentanyl (avoid morphine) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Hepatic failure**: Remifentanil [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Known CYP2D6 poor metabolizer**: Avoid codeine, tramadol [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Seizure history/risk**: Avoid meperidine, tramadol, tapentadol [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 

### 3.2.5. EFFECT TERMINATION AND EXCRETION

**Primary Mechanisms** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
* Unbinding from receptors and redistribution from effect sites [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **For smaller doses**: Redistribution reduces blood concentrations [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **For larger doses**: Biotransformation becomes more important [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**Renal Elimination** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
* **MORPHINE/MEPERIDINE**: Primarily renal elimination, <10% biliary excretion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**Special Considerations** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
* ⚠️ **Renal Failure Warning**: Morphine metabolites (especially morphine-6-glucuronide) can accumulate → prolonged narcosis and ventilatory depression [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Understanding opioid metabolism is crucial for safe use in diverse patient populations. Key considerations include active metabolites (especially M6G from morphine), varying metabolic pathways (CYP450 vs. esterases), genetic polymorphisms affecting metabolism, and significant drug interactions. These factors guide opioid selection in special populations, particularly those with renal or hepatic impairment.

## 3.3. PHARMACODYNAMICS



### 3.3.1. PD MODELING COMPONENTS

* *Effect compartment*: Hypothetical site at drug's target organ [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Infinitely small (doesn't influence drug's disposition) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Examples: Muscle endplate for relaxants, brain for hypnotics, CNS for opioid effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* *Plasma-effect site equilibration constant*: ke0 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Half-life: t½ke0 = ln 2/ke0 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Describes *hysteresis* (delay between peak plasma concentration and peak effect) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* For opioids, hysteresis determined by [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Blood-brain barrier passage (more lipophilic = faster transfer) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Receptor kinetics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Neuronal dynamics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 3.3.2. EFFECT SITE CONCENTRATION-EFFECT RELATIONSHIP

* Described by sigmoid Emax model [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Effect = (CE/C50)γ/[1 + (CE/C50)γ] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * CE: Drug concentration at effect site [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * C50: Measure of drug potency (concentration causing 50% of effect) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * γ: Hill/steepness parameter [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 3.3.3. TRADITIONAL PKPD MODEL ENDPOINTS

* Historically: EEG changes (slowing of frequency components) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Clinical limitation: C50 for EEG effects occurs beyond normal clinical dose range [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* More clinically useful C50 values [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Analgesic effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Respiratory depressive effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Sedative effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Opioid pharmacodynamics involves complex modeling of effect-site concentrations and drug-receptor interactions. Understanding concepts like hysteresis (delay between plasma concentration and clinical effect) and concentration-effect relationships helps predict onset, potency, and duration of clinical effects.

# 4. EFFECTS ON ORGAN SYSTEMS


## 4.1. CARDIOVASCULAR SYSTEM



* Minimal direct effects on heart [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **MEPERIDINE (PETHIDINE)**: Increases HR (structurally similar to atropine) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] . Produces anticholinergic effects including tachycardia and dry mouth 
* **MORPHINE/FENTANYL/REMIFENTANIL/ALFENTANIL**: At larger doses → vagus nerve-mediated bradycardia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* BP typically falls due to [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Opioid-induced bradycardia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Venodilation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Decreased sympathetic reflexes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Histamine release (can be minimized by slow infusion or H1/H2 antagonist pretreatment) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Anesthetic Interactions**: Hemodynamically stable when given alone, but cause decreases in CO, SV, and BP in combination with other anesthetics [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Volatile Interaction**: Reduces MAC of volatile anesthetics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

**Central Effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
* Activation of vagal nuclei [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Depression of vasomotor centers in brainstem [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**Peripheral Effects** (predominantly at high doses) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
* Direct myocardial depression [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Arterial and venous dilation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **MORPHINE**: Additional effects via histamine release [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

**Physiologic Consequences** (usually mild at clinical doses) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
* Orthostatic hypotension [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Mild bradycardia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Moderate reduction of systemic and pulmonary resistance [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

**Risk Factors for Hemodynamic Instability** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
* Combination with other drugs [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Inhalation anesthetics [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **PROPOFOL** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Benzodiazepines [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Severely ill patients (e.g., sepsis) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

**Treatment of Hemodynamic Instability** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
* **ATROPINE** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Vasopressors [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Intravascular fluid therapy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

**REMIFENTANIL** Specific Effects :
* Characterized by falls in heart rate and blood pressure, reversible with glycopyrrolate 
* Analgesic effects reversible by naloxone 

**Bottom Line:** Opioids typically cause minimal direct cardiovascular effects when used alone, but can lead to bradycardia and hypotension, especially at higher doses or when combined with other anesthetics. Effects are mediated through central mechanisms (vagal tone, vasomotor depression) and peripheral mechanisms (vasodilation, histamine release with some agents).

## 4.2. RESPIRATORY SYSTEM



**Primary Mechanisms** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
* Raises apneic threshold (greatest PaCO2 at which patient remains apneic) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Decreases hypoxic drive [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Shifts CO2 response curve downward and to the right [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**Clinical Effects** :
* More prominent reduction in respiratory rate than tidal volume 
* Anti-tussive effects (suppression of cough reflex) 
* ⚠️ **Important Note**: Respiratory depression may be potentiated if hypoxic respiratory drive is removed by supplementary oxygen 
* **MORPHINE**: May precipitate histamine release and bronchospasm 

⚠️ **Chest Wall Rigidity**: Rapid administration of larger doses of **FENTANYL**, **SUFENTANIL**, **REMIFENTANIL**, and **ALFENTANIL** can induce severe chest wall rigidity → ventilation with bag and mask nearly impossible [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] . Thought to be due to opioid receptor interaction with dopaminergic and GABA pathways in the substantia nigra and striatum 

**Risk Factors for Respiratory Depression** (🔑 HY) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
* Sleep apnea [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Obesity [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Elderly [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Pre-existing pulmonary/cardiac disease [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Concurrent CNS depressants [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Renal/hepatic insufficiency [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Opioid-induced respiratory depression is a serious, potentially life-threatening side effect mediated through altered central respiratory drive and response to CO2. Additional concerns include chest wall rigidity with rapid administration of potent synthetic opioids. Numerous patient factors increase risk, requiring vigilant monitoring and preventive strategies.

## 4.3. CENTRAL NERVOUS SYSTEM



* Minimal effects on CMRO2, CBF, cerebral blood volume, ICP (much less than other agents) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Stimulates medullary CRTZ → PONV (more common with smaller/analgesic doses than larger/anesthetic doses) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **MEPERIDINE (PETHIDINE)**: Highly effective (10-25 mg IV) for postoperative shivering [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* ⚠️ **Amnesia Warning**: Even very large doses will not reliably produce amnesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

**Special CNS Considerations** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
* **Tolerance**: Repeated dosing → progressively larger doses required for same response [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Opioid-Induced Hyperalgesia (OIH)**: Prolonged dosing → paradoxical increase in pain sensitivity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Acute Tolerance**: Infusion of large doses (particularly **REMIFENTANIL**) during GA → much larger than usual doses required for immediate postoperative analgesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] . May cause opioid induced hyperalgesia when plasma targets >5 ng.ml⁻¹ or rates >0.2 μg.kg⁻¹.min⁻¹ are used 

**Diffuse CNS effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
* Dizziness, light-headedness [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Sedation, drowsiness [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Euphoria, dysphoria [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] . **DIAMORPHINE** produces the greatest degree of euphoria among opioids 

**Cognitive dysfunction** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
* Memory loss [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Inability to concentrate or focus attention [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**Other CNS Effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
* Hallucinations [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Muscle rigidity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Miosis (pupil constriction) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] . Due to stimulation of the Edinger-Westphal nucleus, can be reversed by atropine 

**Emetic Effects** :
* Stimulates chemoreceptor trigger zone via 5-HT3 and dopamine receptors 
* Cells within the vomiting center are depressed by morphine and do not stimulate vomiting 

🏥 **Clinical Practice**: Miosis can be useful to assess depth of anesthesia in patients under GA, as pupils remain responsive to opioids even during general anesthesia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Opioids have minimal effects on cerebral hemodynamics but significant effects on consciousness, nausea/vomiting centers, pain perception, and pupillary response. Important CNS-related concepts include tolerance, opioid-induced hyperalgesia, and the lack of reliable amnesia with opioids alone.

## 4.4. GASTROINTESTINAL SYSTEM

* Peripherally acting opioid antagonists (e.g., **METHYLNALTREXONE**) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Do not cross blood-brain barrier but block GI opioid receptors [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **NALOXONE** or **GLUCAGON** for biliary spasm (both relax sphincter muscle) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 

**Clinical Impact** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
* OIC (Opioid-Induced Constipation) has greatest effect on postoperative length of stay [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

**Combination Preparations** :
* Oxycodone combined with naloxone (Targinact) treats severe pain with resistant constipation 
* Naloxone undergoes significant first-pass metabolism (no systemic effects) while preserving oxycodone's analgesic effect 
* Naloxone has local pre-hepatic effect on gut to reduce constipation 

**Biliary Effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
* Spasm of sphincter of Oddi and common bile duct [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Clinical manifestations** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Acute upper abdominal pain [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Colic-like complaints [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Misinterpretation of perioperative cholangiograms [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Treatment** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **NALOXONE** or **GLUCAGON** (both relax sphincter muscle) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **PETHIDINE**: Reportedly produces less biliary tract spasm than morphine, though clinical significance unclear 

**Bottom Line:** Opioids significantly affect GI function by decreasing motility, increasing sphincter tone, and inhibiting secretions. Clinically, this manifests as constipation, delayed gastric emptying, and potential biliary spasm. Unlike other opioid effects, tolerance rarely develops to constipation, making preventive strategies important for patient comfort and reduced length of stay.

### 4.5.1. URINARY RETENTION

* Higher incidence after neuraxial (epidural/spinal) opioid administration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

### 4.5.3. IMMUNE MODULATION

* Inhibition of cellular and humoral immune functions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

# 5. SPECIFIC OPIOID AGENTS


## 5.1. MORPHINE



### 5.1.1. CHEMISTRY AND STRUCTURE

* Naturally occurring phenanthrene derivative with complex structure 
* Reference opioid with which all others are compared 
* μ-receptor agonist with some activity at κ and δ receptors 

### 5.1.2. PRESENTATION AND FORMULATIONS

* Tablets, suspensions, suppositories, slow-release capsules and granules in various strengths 
* Parenteral preparation contains 10-30 mg.ml⁻¹ for IV or IM administration 

### 5.1.3. DOSING

* **Oral**: 5-20 mg 4-hourly 
* **Intramuscular**: 0.1-0.2 mg.kg⁻¹ 4-hourly 
* **Intravenous**: Titrated to effect, total dose similar to IM 
* **Subcutaneous**: Generally avoided due to low lipid solubility and slow absorption 
* **Intrathecal**: 0.2-1.0 mg (caution: risk of delayed respiratory depression) 
* **Epidural**: 2-4 mg (caution: risk of delayed respiratory depression) 

### 5.1.4. CLINICAL PHARMACOLOGY

**Efficacy**:
* Particularly effective for visceral pain 
* Less effective for sharp or superficial pain 
* Occasionally requires dose increases, usually due to changes in pathophysiology rather than dependence 

**Pharmacokinetics**:
* **Absorption**: When given orally, ionized in acidic stomach (as a weak base, pKa = 8.0), delaying absorption until reaching alkaline small intestine where it becomes unionized 
* **Bioavailability**: 30% orally due to hepatic first-pass metabolism 
* **Onset**: Peak effects at 10 minutes (IV) or 30 minutes (IM) 
* **Duration**: 3-4 hours 
* **Distribution**: Concentration in brain falls slowly due to low lipid solubility; plasma concentrations don't correlate well with effects 

**Metabolism**:
* Primarily hepatic with some renal metabolism 
* Up to 70% metabolized to morphine-3-glucuronide (affects arousal, possibly μ-receptor antagonist) 
* Smaller amounts to morphine-6-glucuronide (13 times more potent than morphine with similar duration) 
* Both metabolites excreted in urine and accumulate in renal failure 
* N-demethylation also occurs 

**Special Populations**:
* **Neonates**: More sensitive due to reduced hepatic conjugating capacity 
* **Elderly**: Higher peak plasma levels due to reduced volume of distribution 

**Bottom Line:** Morphine remains the prototypical opioid against which others are compared. Its relatively slow onset and prolonged duration make it suitable for management of persistent pain but less ideal for rapid titration. Key considerations include its active metabolite M6G (which accumulates in renal failure), histamine release, and respiratory depressant effects.

## 5.2. DIAMORPHINE (HEROIN)



### 5.2.1. CHEMISTRY AND STRUCTURE

* Diacetylated morphine derivative 
* Prodrug with no direct affinity for opioid receptors; active metabolites responsible for effects 
* Approximately twice as potent as morphine 

### 5.2.2. PRESENTATION AND FORMULATIONS

* 10 mg tablets 
* White powder for injection containing 5, 10, 30, 100, or 500 mg diamorphine hydrochloride 

### 5.2.3. CLINICAL USES

* Relief of severe pain 
* Dyspnea associated with pulmonary edema (2.5-10 mg) 
* Intrathecal (0.1-0.4 mg) and epidural (1-3 mg) analgesia 
  * Less likely to cause delayed respiratory depression than morphine due to higher lipid solubility 

### 5.2.4. PHARMACOKINETICS

* High lipid solubility allows effective subcutaneous administration 
* Low oral bioavailability despite good absorption due to extensive first-pass metabolism 
* 40% protein-bound in plasma 
* pKa = 7.6 (37% in unionized form at pH 7.4) 
* Rapid metabolism in liver, plasma, and CNS by ester hydrolysis to:
  * 6-monoacetylmorphine
  * Morphine (these active metabolites confer analgesic effects) 
* Plasma half-life approximately 5 minutes 
* Produces greatest degree of euphoria among opioids, contributes to abuse potential 

**Bottom Line:** Diamorphine (heroin) is a prodrug rapidly metabolized to active compounds including morphine. Its high lipid solubility facilitates administration by multiple routes including subcutaneous. For neuraxial administration, this property reduces risk of delayed respiratory depression compared to morphine. Its euphoric effects contribute to its abuse potential.

## 5.3. CODEINE AND DIHYDROCODEINE



### 5.3.1. CODEINE

**Chemistry and Potency**:
* 3-methylmorphine, 10 times less potent than morphine 
* Not suitable for severe pain 
* Acts primarily as a prodrug for morphine 

**Presentation and Dosing**:
* Oral and intramuscular adult dose: 30-60 mg 
* Intravenous route avoided due to potential for hypotension (histamine release) 
* Use restricted in children under 12 years due to reports of morphine toxicity 

**Clinical Uses**:
* Mild to moderate pain 
* Antitussive (cough suppression) 
* Antidiarrheal 
* Hypnotic and anxiolytic effects 

**Pharmacokinetics**:
* Oral bioavailability: 50% (higher than morphine due to methyl group reducing first-pass metabolism) 
* 5-15% eliminated unchanged in urine 
* Three metabolic pathways in liver :
  1. 6-hydroxyglucuronidation to codeine-6-glucuronide (predominant)
  2. N-demethylation to norcodeine (10-20% via CYP3A4)
  3. O-demethylation to morphine (up to 15% via CYP2D6)
* Only morphine has significant μ-receptor activity 

**Pharmacogenetic Considerations**:
* O-demethylation dependent on CYP2D6, which exhibits genetic polymorphism 
* Poor metabolizers experience little pain relief (9% of UK population, 30% of Hong Kong Chinese) 
* Fast metabolizers generate increased morphine with attendant side effects 
* Restriction in children under 12 years related to CYP2D6 variability 

![Metabolic pathway of codeine, dihydrocodeine and morphine](OPIOIDS_OPIOIDS_image.png_eff68f.png_f1f1b4.png)

### 5.3.2. DIHYDROCODEINE

**Chemistry and Potency**:
* Synthetic opioid structurally similar to codeine 
* Approximately twice as potent as codeine 

**Pharmacokinetics**:
* Oral bioavailability of about 20% 
* Metabolic pathways analogous to codeine 
* Greater efficacy for μ-receptor than codeine, less dependent on CYP2D6 
* More predictable analgesic effect than codeine 

**Bottom Line:** Codeine acts primarily as a prodrug, requiring CYP2D6 metabolism to morphine for analgesic effect. Significant genetic polymorphism affects efficacy and side effects. Dihydrocodeine has better inherent μ-receptor efficacy and is less dependent on metabolism for effect. Both agents are useful for mild-to-moderate pain and have additional effects including antitussive properties.

## 5.4. FENTANYL AND DERIVATIVES



### 5.4.1. FENTANYL

**Chemistry and Properties**:
* Synthetic phenylpiperidine derivative 
* μ-receptor agonist sharing morphine's effects 
* Less likely to precipitate histamine release than morphine 
* High doses (50-150 μg.kg⁻¹) can reduce or eliminate metabolic stress response to surgery but associated with bradycardia and chest wall rigidity 

**Presentation**:
* Colorless solution for injection (50 μg.ml⁻¹) 
* Transdermal patches releasing 25-100 μg per hour for 72 hours 
* Lozenges releasing 200 μg-1.6 mg over 15 minutes 

**Clinical Uses and Dosing**:
* Minor surgery pain: 1-2 μg.kg⁻¹ IV (duration ~30 minutes) 
* Laryngoscopy: Higher doses generally required 
* Opioid-based anesthetic: 50-100 μg.kg⁻¹ (requires additional hypnotic; duration ~6 hours) 
* Continuous infusion: Elimination half-life becomes apparent, significantly prolonging duration 
* Neuraxial use: 10-25 μg intrathecal, 25-100 μg epidural to augment local anesthetics 

**Pharmacokinetics**:
* **Onset**: Rapid following IV administration (high lipid solubility, nearly 600 times more than morphine) 
* **Transdermal**: 12 hours to reach equilibrium plasma levels 
* **Duration**: At low doses (<3 μg.kg⁻¹ IV), determined by distribution; with prolonged administration or high doses, significantly prolonged as tissues saturate 
* **Clearance**: Similar to morphine with longer elimination half-life reflecting higher lipid solubility and volume of distribution 
* **Metabolism**: N-demethylation to inactive norfentanyl, followed by hydroxylation 
* **Excretion**: Inactive metabolites in urine 

**Special Properties**:
* High lipid solubility allows rapid onset but also accumulation with repeated dosing 
* Intrathecal dose typically doesn't cause delayed respiratory depression (unlike morphine) due to rapid diffusion from CSF into spinal cord 
* Respiratory depression observed with continuous epidural infusion or repeated boluses 

### 5.4.2. ALFENTANIL

**Chemistry and Properties**:
* Synthetic phenylpiperidine derivative 
* μ-receptor agonist with significant differences from fentanyl 

**Presentation**:
* Colorless solution (500 μg or 0.5 mg.ml⁻¹) 

**Clinical Uses**:
* Short-term analgesia: 5-25 μg.kg⁻¹ boluses 
* Sedation by infusion (duration significantly prolonged) 

**Pharmacokinetics**:
* **pKa**: 6.5 (vs fentanyl's 8.4), resulting in 89% unionized at pH 7.4 (vs 9% for fentanyl) 
* Despite lower lipid solubility than fentanyl, has faster onset due to greater unionized fraction 
* Smaller initial volume of distribution; smaller clearance but shorter elimination half-life than fentanyl 
* **Metabolism**: N-demethylation to noralfentanil, followed by conjugation 
* **Drug Interactions**: Midazolam uses same CYP3A3/4 enzymes, prolonging both drugs' half-lives when co-administered; erythromycin inhibits metabolism 

### 5.4.3. REMIFENTANIL

**Chemistry and Properties**:
* Synthetic phenylpiperidine fentanyl derivative 
* Pure μ-receptor agonist with similar potency to fentanyl 
* Unique metabolism makes it distinct among opioids 

**Presentation**:
* Crystalline white powder in glass vials (1, 2, or 5 mg) 
* Contains glycine; not licensed for spinal or epidural use 

**Clinical Uses and Dosing**:
* Administered by IV infusion (stable in most crystalloid fluids or sterile water for 24 hours) 
* Co-induction with propofol, preferably via target-controlled infusion at 3-6 ng.ml⁻¹ for patients ≥12 years 
* Manual dosing: 0.5-1 μg.kg⁻¹ bolus over at least 45 seconds, followed by infusion up to 0.25 μg.kg⁻¹.min⁻¹ 
* ⚠️ Plasma targets >5 ng.ml⁻¹ or rates >0.2 μg.kg⁻¹.min⁻¹ may cause opioid-induced hyperalgesia 
* No contribution to post-operative analgesia due to short duration 

**Pharmacokinetics**:
* **Metabolism**: Rapid breakdown by non-specific plasma and tissue esterases 
* **Elimination Half-Life**: 3-10 minutes 
* **Duration**: Determined by metabolism, not distribution (unlike alfentanil and fentanyl) 
* **Context-Sensitive Half-Time**: Doesn't change significantly with infusion duration due to abundant esterases 
* Poor substrate for plasma cholinesterases; unaffected by cholinesterase deficiency; anticholinesterase drugs don't alter metabolism 
* **Metabolite**: Essentially inactive carboxylic acid (1/4600th as potent) excreted in urine (half-life 2 hours in healthy adults) 
* Not prolonged by impaired hepatic or renal function 

**Clinical Effects**:
* Shares morphine's effects including respiratory depression and chest wall rigidity 
* Less nausea and vomiting due to ultra-short duration 
* Characteristic decrease in heart rate and blood pressure, reversible with glycopyrrolate 
* Analgesic effects reversed by naloxone 

### 5.4.4. SUFENTANIL

**Properties**:
* Synthetic phenylpiperidine derivative [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* High potency μ-receptor agonist [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* High lipid solubility [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**Pharmacokinetics**:
* Hepatic metabolism via CYP450 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* High hepatic extraction ratio (0.9 L/min) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Metabolites are inactive [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**Clinical Uses**:
* High-potency analgesia for major procedures [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Neuraxial administration in small doses [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Synthetic piperidine opioids (fentanyl, alfentanil, sufentanil, remifentanil) offer distinct pharmacokinetic profiles for different clinical scenarios. Key distinguishing features include onset speed (alfentanil fastest), context-sensitive half-time (remifentanil uniquely consistent), and metabolism pathway (remifentanil via esterases rather than CYP450). These differences guide selection based on procedure length, need for rapid emergence, and presence of hepatic/renal dysfunction.

## 5.5. METHADONE



### 5.5.1. PHARMACOLOGY

**Mechanism of Action**:
* μ-receptor agonist [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* NMDA receptor antagonist (beneficial for certain neuropathic pain otherwise resistant to typical opioids) 
* Less sedative than morphine 

**Pharmacokinetics**:
* Relatively low first-pass metabolism resulting in high oral bioavailability (75%) 
* 90% plasma protein-bound 
* Hepatic metabolism to inactive metabolites 
* Plasma half-life: 18-36 hours 
* Up to 40% excreted as unchanged drug in urine (enhanced in acidic conditions) 

### 5.5.2. CLINICAL USES

* Treatment of opioid addiction (slow weaning programs) 
* Management of chronic pain, particularly with neuropathic component [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* ⚠️ Long, unpredictable duration when given orally has led to patient deaths in chronic pain management [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* 🏥 Safer when used IV in controlled perioperative settings [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Methadone combines opioid agonist activity with NMDA antagonism, making it useful for both addiction treatment and pain management, particularly neuropathic pain. Its long and variable half-life (18-36 hours) requires careful dose titration and monitoring to prevent accumulation. The significant disparity between analgesic duration (6-8 hours) and elimination half-life creates risks for delayed toxicity with repeated dosing.

## 5.6. TRAMADOL AND TAPENTADOL



### 5.6.1. TRAMADOL

**Chemistry and Structure**:
* Cyclohexanol derivative 
* Racemic mixture, each enantiomer producing specific actions 
* Analgesic potency one-fifth to one-tenth that of morphine 

**Presentation**:
* Tablets, capsules, sachets (50-400 mg modified release) 
* Solution for IV/IM injection (100 mg in 2 ml) 

**Mechanism of Action**:
* Agonist properties at all opioid receptors, particularly μ-receptors 
* Inhibits reuptake of noradrenaline and serotonin (5-HT) 
* Stimulates presynaptic 5-HT release 
* Provides alternative analgesia pathway via descending inhibitory pathways in spinal cord 

**Clinical Effects**:
* In equianalgesic doses to morphine, produces less respiratory depression and constipation 
* Otherwise similar to morphine 
* Respiratory depression and analgesia reversed by naloxone 

**Interactions**:
* Potential interaction with drugs inhibiting central 5-HT or noradrenaline reuptake (tricyclic antidepressants, SSRIs) resulting in seizures 
* Should not be used in patients with epilepsy 

**Pharmacokinetics**:
* Well absorbed from gut, oral bioavailability 70% increasing to >90% after repeated doses 
* Metabolized by hepatic CPY2D6, CYP3A4, and CYP2B6 followed by glucuronidation 
* Only one metabolite (O-desmethyltramadol) has analgesic activity 
* Volume of distribution: 4 l.kg⁻¹ 
* Elimination half-life: 5-6 hours 

### 5.6.2. TAPENTADOL

**Chemistry and Structure**:
* Alkylbenzene structure 
* Of four potential stereoisomers, only R,R form is presented for medical use 

**Presentation**:
* 50-75 mg tablets and 20 mg.ml⁻¹ oral solution for immediate release 
* Sustained release preparation (50-250 mg) 

**Mechanism of Action**:
* Dual mode of action: μ-receptor agonist and noradrenaline reuptake inhibitor (NARI) 
* Weak serotonin reuptake inhibitor properties 

**Clinical Uses**:
* Moderate to severe pain in both acute and chronic settings 
* Initial dose: 50 mg 4-6 hourly 

**Interactions**:
* Can induce seizures; caution in patients with seizure disorders or epilepsy 
* Seizure risk increased with medications that lower seizure threshold (SSRIs, tricyclics, antipsychotics) 
* Serotonin syndrome reported with serotonergic antidepressants 
* Manufacturers advise against MAOI use within 14 days due to NARI actions 

**Pharmacokinetics**:
* Bioavailability: 30% due to extensive hepatic first-pass metabolism 
* Primary metabolism via glucuronidation, with limited oxidation by hepatic cytochromes (CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6) followed by further glucuronidation 
* No active metabolites 
* 20% protein-bound in plasma 
* Volume of distribution: ~550 liters 
* Elimination half-life: 4 hours 
* Kidneys are main excretion route 

**Bottom Line:** Tramadol and tapentadol represent dual-mechanism analgesics combining opioid receptor activity with monoamine reuptake inhibition. Tramadol inhibits both serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake, while tapentadol primarily affects norepinephrine reuptake with minimal serotonergic effects. Both drugs offer moderate analgesia with potentially fewer classic opioid side effects, but carry unique risks including seizures, particularly in combination with other serotonergic agents.

## 5.7. OXYCODONE



### 5.7.1. PHARMACOLOGY

**General Properties**:
* Alternative to morphine with similar profile 
* Individual patients may tolerate one better than the other 
* More potent than morphine: approximately 1.5 times oral morphine potency 

**Metabolism**:
* CYP2D6 → less potent active compounds (e.g., oxymorphone) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 5.7.2. COMBINATION PREPARATIONS

**Oxycodone/Naloxone (Targinact)**:
* Fixed dose combinations: oxycodone/naloxone 5mg/2.5mg, 10mg/5mg, 20mg/10mg, 40mg/20mg 
* Presented as prolonged release tablets 

**Mechanism of Combined Action**:
* Both drugs absorbed in gut 
* Naloxone undergoes significant first-pass metabolism with no systemic effects 
* Oxycodone has oral bioavailability of 85% and exerts systemic analgesic effects 
* Naloxone has local pre-hepatic effect on gut, reducing constipation 

### 5.7.3. CLINICAL USES

* Treatment of severe pain 
* Specific indication for severe pain with resistant constipation (Targinact) 
* May have role post-operatively for patients in whom constipation would be especially troublesome (hemorrhoidectomy, pelvic floor repair) despite prolonged-release formulation 

**Bottom Line:** Oxycodone serves as an effective alternative to morphine with a similar effect profile but 1.5x higher oral potency. The oxycodone/naloxone combination (Targinact) offers an innovative approach to preventing opioid-induced constipation while maintaining analgesic efficacy, particularly valuable for patients at high risk for constipation-related complications.

## 5.8. PETHIDINE (MEPERIDINE)



### 5.8.1. PHARMACOLOGY

**Chemistry and Structure**:
* Synthetic phenylpiperidine derivative 
* Originally designed as anticholinergic agent but found to have analgesic properties 

**Presentation**:
* Tablets and solution for injection (10-50 mg.ml⁻¹) 
* Intravenous and intramuscular dose: 0.5-1.0 mg.kg⁻¹, repeatable 2-3 hourly 

**Unique Effects** (compared to morphine):
* **Anticholinergic effects**: Less marked miosis, possible mydriasis, dry mouth, sometimes tachycardia 
* **Biliary effects**: Reportedly produces less biliary tract spasm, though clinical significance unclear 
* **MAOI interaction**: ⚠️ Serious interaction with MAOIs, probably due to central serotoninergic hyperactivity from pethidine's serotonin reuptake inhibition plus MAOI-induced amine breakdown reduction 
  * **Effects**: Coma, labile circulation, convulsions, hyperpyrexia 
  * Other opioids are safe with MAOIs 

**Pharmacokinetics**:
* More lipid-soluble than morphine, resulting in faster onset 
* Oral bioavailability: 50% 
* **Metabolism**:
  * Ester hydrolysis to inactive pethidinic acid 
  * N-demethylation to norpethidine (half the analgesic activity of pethidine) 
  * Norpethidine has longer elimination half-life (14-21 hours) than pethidine 
  * Accumulates in renal failure 
  * ⚠️ Associated with hallucinations and grand mal seizures upon accumulation; effects not reversed by naloxone 
* Duration of action: 120-150 minutes 
* Excretion: Norpethidine, pethidinic acid, and small amounts of unchanged pethidine in urine 

### 5.8.2. CLINICAL USES

* Used during labor 
* Highly effective for postoperative shivering (10-25 mg IV) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 
* ⚠️ **Cautions**:
  * Due to high lipid solubility, significant amounts cross placenta to fetus 
  * Norpethidine accumulates in fetus, peaking ~4 hours after initial maternal IM dose 
  * Reduced fetal clearance prolongs half-lives of both pethidine and norpethidine by factor of three 
  * Avoid in renal failure due to norpethidine accumulation and seizure risk [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 
  * Contraindicated with MAOIs 

**Bottom Line:** Pethidine (meperidine) is distinguished by its anticholinergic properties and effectiveness for postoperative shivering. However, its active metabolite norpethidine has serious downsides: longer half-life (14-21 hours), accumulation in renal failure, and potential to cause seizures not reversible by naloxone. These limitations, plus dangerous interactions with MAOIs, have significantly reduced its clinical use despite its historical prominence.

## 5.9. OPIOID ANTAGONISTS



### 5.9.1. NALOXONE

**Pharmacology**:
* Pure opioid antagonist 
* Reverses opioid effects at μ-, κ-, and δ-receptors, with highest affinity for μ-receptors 
* At 1-4 μg.kg⁻¹ IV, drug of choice for opioid overdose 

**Pharmacokinetics**:
* Best given IV (low/unpredictable oral bioavailability due to >95% first-pass effect) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Metabolism: Glucuronidation to inactive naloxone-3-glucuronide [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Duration: Short (15-45 minutes), shorter than morphine and high-dose fentanyl [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* May require supplementary doses or continuous infusion when antagonizing long-acting opioids or overdose [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 

**Additional Effects**:
* Occasional hypertension, pulmonary edema, cardiac arrhythmias 
* Anti-analgesia in opioid-naive subjects 
* Combination with oxycodone (Targinact) for reducing constipation while preserving analgesia 

**Bottom Line:** Naloxone is the standard opioid antagonist for reversing opioid effects and treating overdose. Its key limitation is short duration (15-45 minutes), necessitating repeated dosing or continuous infusion when treating effects of longer-acting opioids. While primarily administered IV for emergencies, it's also used orally in fixed combination with oxycodone to reduce constipation without affecting analgesia.

## 5.10. PARTIAL AGONISTS



### 5.10.1. OVERVIEW

Opioid partial agonists have been used to control pain and to reverse opioid-induced respiratory depression with variable success, but are not widely used .

**Receptor Activities**:

| Drug | Agonist action at: | Antagonist action at: |
|------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Nalorphine | κ | μ |
| Pentazocine | κ (partial), δ (partial) | μ |
| Buprenorphine | μ (partial), NOP (partial, very weak) | κ, δ |
| Nalbuphine | κ (partial) | μ (partial) |

### 5.10.2. PENTAZOCINE

* Produces analgesia with little respiratory depression 
* Side effects (nausea, vomiting, hallucinations, dysphoria) have limited clinical use 

### 5.10.3. BUPRENORPHINE

**Pharmacology**:
* Structurally similar to and more potent than morphine 
* Duration up to 10 hours due to receptor binding 
* Produces analgesia at low concentrations (μ-receptor) 
* At increasing doses, NOP effects produce anti-analgesic effects 

**Presentation**:
* Sublingual tablets and transdermal patches 

**Pharmacokinetics**:
* Metabolized by CYP3A4 and subsequently conjugated 
* 70% excreted in bile, remainder in urine 

**Adverse Effects**:
* Severe and prolonged nausea and vomiting 

### 5.10.4. NALBUPHINE

* Equipotent to morphine 
* Ceiling effect for respiratory depression 
* Also has ceiling effect for analgesic actions 
* Effects may be reversed with naloxone 

**Bottom Line:** Partial opioid agonists offer theoretical advantages including ceiling effects on respiratory depression while providing analgesia. However, their clinical utility is limited by side effects, unpredictable responses, potential to precipitate withdrawal in opioid-dependent patients, and ceiling effects on analgesia. Buprenorphine has found use in addiction treatment and as a transdermal analgesic, while nalbuphine and pentazocine are rarely used in modern practice.

## 6.2. COMBINATION PREPARATIONS



### 6.2.1. OXYCODONE-NALOXONE (TARGINACT)

**Composition**:
* Fixed dose combinations: oxycodone/naloxone 5mg/2.5mg, 10mg/5mg, 20mg/10mg, 40mg/20mg 
* Presented as prolonged release tablets 

**Mechanism**:
* Both drugs absorbed in gut 
* Naloxone undergoes significant first-pass metabolism with no systemic effects 
* Oxycodone has oral bioavailability of 85% and exerts systemic analgesic effects 
* Naloxone has local pre-hepatic effect on gut, reducing constipation 

**Clinical Uses**:
* Treatment of severe pain with resistant constipation 
* May benefit post-operative patients where constipation would be particularly problematic (hemorrhoidectomy, pelvic floor repair) 

### 6.2.2. OTHER COMMON COMBINATIONS

* **Hydrocodone-paracetamol (acetaminophen)**: Common in some countries for moderate pain [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Opioid combination products may provide multimodal analgesia or address specific side effects. The oxycodone/naloxone combination (Targinact) represents an innovative approach to reducing opioid-induced constipation while maintaining analgesic efficacy. Other common combinations with acetaminophen (paracetamol) enhance analgesia through multiple mechanisms while potentially allowing lower opioid doses.

## 6.3. DRUG INTERACTIONS



### 6.3.1. CYP450 INTERACTIONS

* **CYP inhibitors** → increased opioid effect (potential toxicity) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Antibiotics (erythromycin, clarithromycin) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Antifungals (ketoconazole, fluconazole) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Antidepressants (fluoxetine, paroxetine) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **CYP inducers** → decreased opioid effect (potential inadequate analgesia) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Anticonvulsants (phenytoin, carbamazepine) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Rifampin [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * St. John's Wort [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

### 6.3.2. SPECIFIC DANGEROUS INTERACTIONS

**Pethidine-MAOI Interaction**:
* ⚠️ Serious interaction if administered together 
* Mechanism: Central serotoninergic hyperactivity caused by pethidine's inhibition of serotonin reuptake plus MAOI-induced reduction in amine breakdown 
* **Effects**: Coma, labile circulation, convulsions, hyperpyrexia 
* **Note**: Other opioids are safe with MAOIs 

**Tramadol Interactions**:
* Potential interactions with drugs inhibiting central 5-HT or noradrenaline reuptake (tricyclic antidepressants, SSRIs), resulting in seizures 
* Should not be used in patients with epilepsy 

**Tapentadol Interactions**:
* Can induce seizures; caution in patients with seizure disorders or epilepsy 
* Seizure risk increased with medications that lower seizure threshold (SSRIs, tricyclics, antipsychotics) 
* Serotonin syndrome reported with serotonergic antidepressants 
* Manufacturers advise against MAOI use within 14 days due to NARI actions 

**Alfentanil Interactions**:
* Midazolam uses same CYP3A3/4 enzymes, prolonging both drugs' half-lives when co-administered 
* Erythromycin inhibits metabolism 

### 6.3.3. CYCLOSPORINE INTERACTION

* Enhances morphine's analgesic effect (not methadone's) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Mechanism: Interference with morphine's efflux from brain via transporter proteins [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 6.3.4. ADDITIVE CNS DEPRESSION

* ⚠️ Increased respiratory depression when opioids combined with:
  * Benzodiazepines [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Alcohol [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Other sedatives/hypnotics [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Opioid drug interactions can be life-threatening and occur through multiple mechanisms including CYP450 inhibition/induction, serotonergic effects, and additive CNS depression. The most dangerous include the pethidine-MAOI interaction and combinations of opioids with other CNS depressants. Understanding specific at-risk combinations (tramadol/tapentadol with serotonergic agents, alfentanil with CYP3A4 inhibitors) is essential for safe prescribing.
