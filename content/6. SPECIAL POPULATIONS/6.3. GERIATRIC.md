## 1.1. DEMOGRAPHICS AND ECONOMICS OF AGING

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|Part A: Physiology]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B: C5]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#6. Systems-Based Practice|Part C: SBP]]

### 1.1.2. POPULATION TRENDS AND STATISTICS

**The aging population represents a growing demographic with significant implications for healthcare delivery.**

* **Current Demographics:**
  * 16.5% of the U.S. population (54 million people) is older than 65 years as of 2019 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * This represents almost triple the percentage (6.1%) when Social Security was initiated in 1935 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Currently, 10,000 people reach age 65 every day in the United States [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
* **Projected Growth:**
  * By 2050, adults over 65 will represent >20% of the population (86 million people) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * The percentage of population older than 85 is expected to nearly triple from 2010 to 2050 (from 1.8% to 4.8%) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Current 65-year-olds can expect to live approximately another 19 years [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
* **Healthcare Utilization:**
  * In 2010, patients over 65 represented 13% of the population but accounted for 45% of the 167 million total U.S. inpatient days [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * This is a rate per capita more than five times greater than for those under 65 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * 37.3% of estimated 51 million inpatient procedures were performed on patients older than 65 years [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Older patients account for one-third of annual outpatient procedures [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].

### 1.1.3. HEALTHCARE COSTS AND UTILIZATION

**Aging populations place significant burdens on healthcare systems.**

* **Medicare Expenditures:**
  * Federal spending for Medicare in 2021 was $696 billion ($875 billion gross) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * This represents approximately 13% of the federal budget [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * The amount is approximately twice what was spent in 2000 after adjusting for inflation and represents a 50% increase per enrollee [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Total Healthcare Costs:**
  * People over age 65 account for nearly half of the nation's healthcare costs [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Total U.S. healthcare costs were $4.1 trillion in 2020, approximately 19.7% of gross national product [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **COVID-19 Impact:**
  * The SARS-CoV-2 pandemic revealed increased vulnerability in older patients [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * 75% of individuals dying from COVID-19 were 65 years or older [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * For younger people, 1 in 1,400 died from COVID-19, compared to 1 in 100 for older patients [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Major elective surgery 0-4 weeks after SARS-CoV-2 infection is associated with increased risk of postoperative complications [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Increased risk of postoperative pneumonia remains present until 8 weeks after SARS-CoV-2 infection [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

### 1.1.4. 2025 ASA PRACTICE GUIDELINES FOR OLDER ADULTS [NEW EVIDENCE]

**The 2025 American Society of Anesthesiologists Practice Advisory provides evidence-based recommendations for the perioperative management of older adults undergoing inpatient surgery.**

* **Background and Purpose:**
  * Provides evidence-based recommendations for management of older adults (≥65 years) undergoing inpatient surgery `[ASA 2025:1]`.
  * Focus on minimizing complications common among older patients, particularly neurocognitive disorders `[ASA 2025:1]`.
  * Based on systematic review and meta-analysis of relevant literature using GRADE methodology `[ASA 2025:2]`.
* **Key Clinical Challenges Identified:**
  * Loss of independence is common: 19% of patients aged 80-89 years and 26% of patients ≥90 years exhibit functional decline persisting 30 days after surgery `[ASA 2025:1]`.
  * 35% of older adults with new disability after surgery have no recovery 6 months later `[ASA 2025:1]`.
  * Preserving independence is a vital goal for older adults undergoing surgery `[ASA 2025:1]`.
* **Evidence-Based Recommendations:**
  * **Expanded Preoperative Evaluation:** Consider in older adults scheduled for inpatient procedures to reduce postoperative delirium risk (Conditional recommendation, Low strength evidence) `[ASA 2025:3]`.
  * **Anesthetic Technique:** Either neuraxial or general anesthesia acceptable when clinically appropriate (Strong recommendation, Moderate strength evidence) `[ASA 2025:4]`.
  * **Maintenance Anesthesia:** Either total intravenous or inhaled anesthesia acceptable (Conditional recommendation, Low strength evidence) `[ASA 2025:5]`.
  * **Dexmedetomidine Use:** Reasonable to consider for reducing delirium risk while considering bradycardia/hypotension risks (Conditional recommendation, Moderate strength evidence) `[ASA 2025:6]`.
  * **Best Practice Statement:** Consider risks and benefits of medications with potential CNS effects as they may increase delirium risk `[ASA 2025:7]`.

### 1.1.5. MEDICARE REIMBURSEMENT AND HEALTHCARE ECONOMICS 

**Medicare reimbursement for anesthesia services presents unique challenges.**

* **Reimbursement Patterns:**
  * In 2018, private insurance payments for anesthesia services were 3.5 times more than Medicare payments [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#6. Systems-Based Practice|ⓘ]].
  * This contrasts with all other specialties, for which Medicare reimbursement is approximately 83% of commercial rates [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#6. Systems-Based Practice|ⓘ]].
* **Academic Anesthesia Billing:**
  * Medicare reimburses at the 50% level for up to four concurrent medically directed procedures [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#6. Systems-Based Practice|ⓘ]].
  * Since 2010, Medicare reimburses academic institutions at 100% for two concurrent cases [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#6. Systems-Based Practice|ⓘ]].
* **No Surprises Act (2021):**
  * Brought federal protections against surprise medical bills [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#6. Systems-Based Practice|ⓘ]].
  * Brought attention to the complexity of anesthesiology-associated costs due to the unique conversion-factor unit-of-payment structure [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#6. Systems-Based Practice|ⓘ]].
  * Despite variability in reimbursement across payers, no identified access barriers to anesthesia providers for Medicare beneficiaries exist, even in traditionally underserved areas [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#6. Systems-Based Practice|ⓘ]].

## 1.2. GENERAL CONSIDERATIONS FOR HIGHER RISK IN ELDERLY PATIENTS

**Aging involves processes not only of physical but also of psychological and social change, leading to higher surgical risk.**

* **Physiological Reserve:**
  * Aging is associated with a decline in the physiological reserve of every organ system `[A:1, Supplementary]`.
  * The mechanism of this decline is either loss of cells from an organ or reduced function of remaining cells `[A:1, Supplementary]`.
  * The decline in organ function often begins early in adult life but is not clinically evident until almost all organ reserve is lost `[A:1, Supplementary]`.
* **Functional vs. Chronological Age:**
  * While chronological age is easily measured, a patient's functional age is both more important and more difficult to measure `[A:1, Supplementary]`.
  * Organ failure occurs either when function declines to a level that can no longer support life or when a disease state requires increased organ function that cannot be met due to insufficient reserve `[A:1, Supplementary]`.
* **Chronic Disease and Polypharmacy:**
  * Older patients often have chronic diseases that may impact the development or progression of an acute illness `[A:1, Supplementary]`.
  * The polypharmacy that commonly accompanies chronic illness may influence the medical and anesthetic management of an acute illness `[A:1, Supplementary]`.

**Bottom Line:** The aging population represents a significant and growing demographic with critical implications for healthcare utilization and costs. Understanding the demographics, economics, and general considerations of aging is essential as anesthesiologists increasingly care for older patients while navigating complex reimbursement systems. The elderly have diminished physiological reserve across all organ systems, making functional age more relevant than chronological age when assessing perioperative risk.

# 2. THE PROCESS OF AGING





## 2.1. THEORIES AND MECHANISMS OF AGING

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|Part A: Physiology]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B: C5]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#6. Systems-Based Practice|Part C: SBP]]

### 2.1.2. CLASSICAL EVOLUTIONARY THEORIES

**Aging involves a gradual, cumulative process of damage and deterioration affecting cellular function and leading to decreased functional capacity.**

* **Weismann's Theory:** Older members of a species need to remove themselves to sustain the turnover essential for evolution [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
* **Mutation Accumulation Theory:** Random, detrimental mutations that don't cause early mortality accumulate and lead to deterioration later in life [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
* **Antagonistic Pleiotropy:** A single gene has multiple effects (phenotypes) - beneficial in early life but detrimental in old age [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
* **Disposable Soma Theory:** Organisms allocate energy to early survival, growth, and reproduction while compromising repair functions that would prevent age-related deterioration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].

### 2.1.3. HIERARCHICAL MECHANISMS OF AGING

* **First Tier (Primary Mechanisms)** - Clearly negative:
  * DNA damage and mitochondrial DNA mutations [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Telomere loss - chromosome ends shorten with each cell division, eventually preventing further division [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Epigenetic drift - nongenetic DNA changes (e.g., methylation) that influence gene expression [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Defective proteostasis - disrupted protein production, folding, and degradation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  
* **Second Tier (Antagonistic Mechanisms)** - Initially beneficial but produce damage after prolonged use:
  * Cellular senescence - cells remain alive and metabolically active but incapable of dividing [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Reactive oxygen species damage to DNA and proteins [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Disrupted nutrient-sensing (e.g., mTOR activity) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Mitochondrial dysfunction leading to inefficient energy use [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  
* **Third Tier (Integrative Mechanisms)** - Operate when damage cannot be compensated:
  * Declining stem cell numbers failing to replace damaged cells [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Altered intercellular communication (e.g., neurohormonal signaling) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].

### 2.1.4. MODERN UNDERSTANDING

* **Geroscience** - An interdisciplinary field studying the relationship between aging and age-related diseases [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
* Fundamental concept: numerous human diseases arise, at least in part, from aging itself [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
* Aging is the major risk factor for diseases such as Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, and numerous malignancies [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].

**Alternative Theories of Aging (Supplementary):** `[A:4-5, Supplementary]`

* **Telomere Theory:** Telomeres are non-coding structures at chromosome ends. During replication, DNA polymerase discards the three most distal base pairs, shortening the DNA sequence. With aging, the telomere is degraded and coding genetic information is lost, leading to apoptosis `[A:4-5, Supplementary]`.
* **Free Radical Theory:** Reactive oxygen species are highly reactive free radicals formed during normal metabolism. Free radicals cause oxidative stress, resulting in cellular aging `[A:5, Supplementary]`.
* **Somatic Mutation Theory:** Cellular division can result in random mutations, producing inefficient cells. These cells accumulate with age, causing progressive organ dysfunction `[A:5, Supplementary]`.

## 2.2. FUNCTIONAL RESERVE AND THE CONCEPT OF FRAILTY  

**Functional reserve represents the degree to which organ function can increase above basal levels and declines with age, ultimately leading to frailty in some individuals.**

* **Functional Reserve Patterns:**
  * Reserve peaks at approximately age 30 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Gradually declines over several decades [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Experiences more rapid decline beginning around the eighth decade [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Commonly assessed in cardiovascular systems (e.g., ability to achieve four metabolic equivalents appears to confer enough reserve for most surgical procedures) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
* **Frailty Concept:**
  * Biologic syndrome characterized by decreased reserve and limited resistance to stressors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Overlaps with, but differs from, disability and comorbidity [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] (Fig. 34-2).
  * Estimated prevalence: 22.7% of community-dwelling adults 65+ years, nearly doubling in those 85+ years [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Approximately 1 in 3 patients (29%) over age 65 presenting for major noncardiac surgery are frail [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * 43% of patients aged 70+ years presenting for cancer surgery are believed to be frail [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Frailty and Surgical Outcomes:**
  * Frail patients have higher likelihood of experiencing mortality, morbidity, complications, prolonged hospital stay, and discharge to an institution [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Frailty can be conceptualized as either:
	* A frail phenotype (weak, slowed, low energy, fall-prone, losing weight) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* A cumulative burden of deficits (comorbidities, social stressors, mental/cognitive dysfunction, physical decline, poor nutrition) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Frailty Assessment Methods:**
  * Over 70 assessments for frailty exist [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * **Fried Phenotype:** Clinical syndrome with three or more criteria: unintentional weight loss, self-reported exhaustion, weakness, slow walking speed, and low physical activity [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * **Frailty Index:** Calculates the fraction of positive deficits from the total number surveyed [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * **Clinical Frailty Scale:** Takes less than a minute, useful for mortality and nonhome discharge risk stratification [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * **Edmonton Frail Scale:** Better predictor of postoperative complications [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * **Additional validated instruments** (2025 update): Risk Analysis Index, Fried Phenotype, Frailty Index, Edmonton Frail Scale `[ASA 2025:2]`.
  * Emerging objective radiographical markers of frailty show promise [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Simple bedside procedures such as ultrasound measurements of quadriceps depth may help identify high-risk elderly patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Updated Frailty Prevalence and Demographics (2025):**
  * Overall prevalence in community-dwelling older adults averages 10.7%, with considerable variation depending on operationalization `[ASA 2025:2]`.
  * Prevalence increases with age `[ASA 2025:2]`.
  * Higher rates in African American and female patients `[ASA 2025:2]`.
  * Higher prevalence in patients with less education, lower income, and poorer health `[ASA 2025:2]`.
  * 25-40% of older surgical patients live with meaningful frailty before surgery - higher than community prevalence `[ASA 2025:2]`.
* **Frailty as Predictor:**
  * Preoperative frailty is one of the strongest predictors of postoperative delirium `[ASA 2025:2]`.
  * Preoperative identification may allow optimization of deficits in physical, cognitive, nutritional, and/or mental health domains before surgery `[ASA 2025:2]`.

### 2.2.1. PREOPERATIVE NEUROCOGNITIVE DISORDERS [NEW EVIDENCE]

**Preoperative neurocognitive disorders are increasingly common and significantly impact perioperative outcomes.**

* **Prevalence and Impact:**
  * Previously diagnosed neurocognitive disorders present in 18% of older patients scheduled for elective noncardiac surgery `[ASA 2025:1]`.
  * Additionally, 37% of patients without known neurocognitive deficits found to have significant cognitive impairment on preoperative testing `[ASA 2025:1]`.
  * Preoperative neurocognitive disorders associated with greater likelihood of developing postoperative delirium `[ASA 2025:1]`.
  * Increases risk of postoperative delirium more than fourfold when combined with frailty `[ASA 2025:2]`.
* **Clinical Implications:**
  * Patients experiencing postoperative delirium have more impaired functional recovery in the month after surgery `[ASA 2025:1]`.
  * Delirium associated with long-term cognitive decline `[ASA 2025:1]`.
  * Cognitive decline after surgery associated with loss of ability to perform independent activities of daily living `[ASA 2025:1]`.
  * Emphasizes importance of ASA Perioperative Brain Health Initiative `[ASA 2025:1]`.

## 2.3. AGING VS. DISEASE

**Defining "normal aging" is difficult as it varies between individuals and interacts with disease processes.**

* **Defining Normal Aging:**
  * Comparing young and elderly subjects may not strictly reflect aging, as elderly subjects may have experienced different diet, lifestyle, and environmental exposures [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Longitudinal studies following healthy subjects over time provide better understanding of aging effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Studies of very old may underestimate typical aging effects because individuals generally do not achieve old age unless inherently robust [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
* **Aging vs. Disease Interaction:**
  * Disease interacts with aging to further diminish functional organ reserve [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Age appears to interact with comorbid disease to increase perioperative risk [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] (Fig. 34-14).
  * For patients with zero comorbid diseases, a modest increase in complications is observed with increasing age [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * At increasing degrees of comorbid disease, the increase in complications with age becomes more pronounced [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Life Span vs. Health Span:**
  * Research focus has transitioned from extending years lived (life span) to extending the period of general health and freedom from comorbidities (health span) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Interventions like smoking cessation, weight loss, and exercise improve both life span and health span [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Aging involves hierarchical mechanisms of cellular and tissue damage leading to progressive functional decline. Theories range from evolutionary concepts to specific molecular processes like telomere shortening and free radical damage. The resulting loss of physiological reserve increases vulnerability to stressors, manifesting as frailty in some individuals. Understanding the complex interplay between aging processes and disease is crucial for perioperative risk assessment and management.

# 3. PHYSIOLOGICAL CHANGES WITH AGING





## 3.1. BODY COMPOSITION CHANGES

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|Part A: Body Composition]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B: C5]]

**Aging is associated with significant changes in body composition that affect drug distribution and metabolism.**

* **Fundamental Changes:**
  * Gradual loss of skeletal muscle [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Increase in body fat (more prominent in women) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]] (Fig. 34-4).
  * Reduction in total body water reflecting reduction in cellular water [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Basal metabolism declines, mostly due to changes in body composition `[A:15, A:4, Supplementary]`.
* **Protein Changes:**
  * Small decrease in plasma albumin levels [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Small increase in α1-acid glycoprotein [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Minimal effect on drug protein binding and delivery [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
* **Gender-Specific Changes:**
  * **Women:** Total body mass remains relatively constant as increases in body fat offset bone loss and intracellular dehydration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * **Men:** Body mass declines despite maintenance of body lipid and skeletal tissue due to accelerated loss of skeletal muscle and lean tissue, producing marked contraction of intracellular water [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].

### 3.1.2. BODY COMPARTMENTS AND VOLUME DISTRIBUTION

**Changes in body compartments and fluid distribution affect drug pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics.**

* **Blood Volume Changes:**
  * A reduced blood volume in the elderly contributes to higher initial drug concentrations `[A:4, Supplementary]`.
  * This can lead to changes in the volume of distribution for many drugs `[A:4, Supplementary]`.
* **Protein Binding:**
  * Plasma protein concentration is reduced, resulting in decreased drug protein binding `[A:4, Supplementary]`.
  * This leads to an increase in the free drug fraction and a higher free drug concentration, which can enhance drug effects `[A:4, Supplementary]`.
* **Body Fat Proportion:**
  * Higher proportion of body fat changes distribution patterns of lipophilic drugs `[A:4, Supplementary]`.
  * This may affect duration of action for fat-soluble medications `[A:4, Supplementary]`.

## 3.2. CENTRAL NERVOUS SYSTEM AGING 

**The aging brain undergoes structural and functional changes affecting cognitive function and drug sensitivity.**

* **Structural Changes:**
  * Brain mass begins to decrease at approximately age 50 and declines more rapidly later [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * An 80-year-old brain has typically lost 10% of its weight [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Cerebral atrophy occurs, though the aged brain can still form new neurons and dendritic connections [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * A generalized loss of cells leads to reduced brain weight and the appearance of cerebral atrophy on computed tomography scan `[A:3, Supplementary]`.
* **Neurotransmitter Changes:**
  * Decreased function of multiple neurotransmitter systems: dopamine, serotonin, γ-aminobutyric acid, and especially acetylcholine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Acetylcholine system reduction is particularly important due to its connection to Alzheimer's disease [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Loss of dopaminergic neurons results in Parkinson's disease, while loss of cholinergic neurons in the hypothalamus is implicated in dementia development `[A:3, Supplementary]`.
* **Functional Changes:**
  * Increased response times and more difficult learning [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Better preservation of vocabulary, "wisdom," and past knowledge [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Nearly 50% of individuals aged 85+ have significant cognitive impairment [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Some degree of atherosclerosis appears inevitable [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Reduction in cell number results in lower cerebral metabolic O₂ demand with cerebral blood flow reduced by 10-20% due to the lower metabolic demand `[A:3, Supplementary]`.
* **Anesthetic Implications:**
  * Increased sensitivity to many anesthetic agents [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Approximately 6% decrease in minimum alveolar concentration (MAC) per decade after age 40 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]] (Fig. 34-6).
  * Enhanced response to many intravenous agents [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Increased risk of interaction between anesthesia, surgical stress, and limited brain reserve [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Age is a major risk factor for postoperative delirium and cognitive decline [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].

### 3.2.1. SENSORY CHANGES

* **Sensory Impairment:**
  * Deafness is very common in older people `[A:3, Supplementary]`.
  * Visual impairment affects around one-third of the elderly population `[A:3, Supplementary]`.
  * Both can significantly impact perioperative communication and management `[A:3, Supplementary]`.
* **Cognitive Function:**
  * Cognitive impairment becomes more common with advancing age, affecting 20% of patients aged over 80 `[A:3, Supplementary]`.
  * This has important implications for informed consent and perioperative care `[A:3, Supplementary]`.

## 3.3. CARDIOVASCULAR AGING 

**Multiple components of the cardiovascular system are affected by aging, altering hemodynamics and stress responses.**

* **Major Cardiovascular Changes:**
  * **Decreased β-receptor response:** Reduced heart rate and contractility responses to both endogenous catecholamine release and exogenous administration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * **Tissue stiffening:** Affects myocardium, arteries, and veins [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * **Autonomic changes:** Increased sympathetic activity and decreased parasympathetic activity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * **Conduction system changes:** Fibrosis and loss of sinoatrial node cells [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * **Impaired ischemic preconditioning:** Reduced protective effects of brief ischemic periods [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].

### 3.3.1. VASCULAR CHANGES

* **Arterial Stiffening:**
  * Arteries stiffen with age, leading to systolic hypertension through two mechanisms `[A:3, Supplementary]`:
	* The walls of the aorta normally distend to accommodate blood ejected from the left ventricle (LV). With aging, the LV must generate greater pressure to eject stroke volume into a stiff aorta `[A:3, Supplementary]`.
	* Normally, ejection of blood into the aorta causes a pressure wave that passes along the aorta and is reflected back. In the elderly, stiffened arteries increase the speed of this pressure wave. The reflected wave reaches the heart in late systole, further increasing the pressure against which the ventricle must pump `[A:3, Supplementary]`.
  * Increased pressure needed to stretch a stiffened aorta [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * More rapid pressure wave transmission causing earlier return of reflected waves, increasing late-systolic pressure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
* **Venous System Changes:**
  * Veins stiffen with age. Normally, veins act as a reservoir of blood to buffer changes in venous pressure, maintaining constant right ventricular preload `[A:3, Supplementary]`.
  * With increasing age, the veins stiffen, reducing their compliance and impairing this buffering mechanism `[A:3, Supplementary]`.
  * This is why the elderly are more likely to become hypotensive with mild hypovolaemia `[A:3, Supplementary]`.
  * Venous system stiffening impairs blood volume buffering capacity, making hemodynamics more unstable with small volume changes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Pulse pressure widening (difference between systolic and diastolic pressure ≥80 mmHg) associates with increased mortality and morbidity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].

### 3.3.2. CARDIAC STRUCTURE AND FUNCTION

* **Left Ventricular Changes:**
  * Left ventricular hypertrophy due to increased afterload [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * As a consequence of increased afterload, the LV undergoes hypertrophy `[A:3, Supplementary]`.
  * Ventricular hypertrophy increases the stiffness of the LV, which impairs diastolic relaxation `[A:3, Supplementary]`.
  * This diastolic dysfunction worsens with age, making the heart progressively more dependent on atrial contraction for ventricular filling `[A:3, Supplementary]`.
  * This explains why development of atrial fibrillation can so easily result in LV failure in the elderly `[A:3, Supplementary]`.
* **Cardiac Function:**
  * Diastolic dysfunction: Slowed ventricular relaxation impairing early filling [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Greater dependence on atrial contraction for ventricular filling [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Increased left atrial pressure to maintain diastolic filling [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Narrow optimal filling pressure range, with low pressures causing inadequate filling and high pressures causing pulmonary edema [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Most congestive heart failure in very old persons is due to diastolic dysfunction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Cardiac output (CO) falls with age at a rate of approximately 1% per year due to reduced contractility of the LV as a result of both the reduced response to β-adrenergic stimuli and reduced myocyte function with advancing age `[A:3, Supplementary]`.

### 3.3.3. AUTONOMIC AND BARORECEPTOR FUNCTION

* **Sympathetic Response:**
  * Increased resting sympathetic activity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Exaggerated response to sympathetic stimuli [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Decreased α-receptor responsiveness with age [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Decreased β-adrenergic response with aging causes:
	* Reduced responsiveness of heart rate to catecholamines during exercise, resulting in a reduced maximum heart rate, though resting HR is unchanged `[A:3, Supplementary]`.
	* The baroreceptors are less able to rapidly reflexively modify HR in response to changes in blood pressure, predisposing the elderly to postural hypotension `[A:3, Supplementary]`.
* **Baroreceptor Function:**
  * Decreased baroreflex control of blood pressure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Reduced heart rate response to blood pressure changes, primarily due to lower vagal tone and reduced β-receptor response [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Decreased maximal heart rate (often quoted as 220 minus age) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].

### 3.3.4. RHYTHM DISTURBANCES

* **Conduction System Abnormalities:**
  * Fibrosis of the conduction system may lead to conduction blocks [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Sick sinus syndrome becomes more common [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Atrial fibrillation prevalence increases exponentially with age [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Increased tendency to supraventricular arrhythmias, particularly atrial and ventricular ectopic beats, and atrial fibrillation (AF) `[A:3, Supplementary]`.
  * This is thought to be due to fibrosis of the atria and sinoatrial node, as well as a large reduction in the number of pacemaker cells `[A:3, Supplementary]`.
* **Valve Problems:**
  * Cardiac valve degeneration becomes more common `[A:3, Supplementary]`.
  * Calcification of the aortic valve is more common in older patients, leading to aortic sclerosis and aortic stenosis `[A:3, Supplementary]`.

## 3.4. PULMONARY AGING 

**The aging respiratory system experiences changes in mechanics, gas exchange, and control of breathing.**

### 3.4.1. AIRWAY AND THORACIC CAGE CHANGES

* **Airway Considerations:**
  * The elderly are often edentulous, making bag-valve-mask ventilation more difficult, but intubation easier `[A:1, Supplementary]`.
  * Upper airway becomes more prone to collapse, particularly at night: partial obstruction (snoring) and arterial hypoxaemia are common `[A:1, Supplementary]`.
  * Decreased upper airway tone can be problematic during recovery from anaesthesia, with airway obstruction being more common `[A:1, Supplementary]`.
  * Decreased tone in hypopharyngeal and genioglossal muscles predisposes to upper airway obstruction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
* **Thoracic Cage Changes:**
  * Increased chest wall stiffness [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * The thoracic cage becomes more rigid due to calcification of the coastal cartilages, leading to reduced thoracic wall compliance `[A:1, Supplementary]`.
  * Vertebral column deformity leads to kyphosis, which adversely affects lung mechanics `[A:1, Supplementary]`.
  * The diaphragm and intercostal muscles atrophy. At times of high respiratory workload, this makes the elderly more susceptible to respiratory muscle fatigue `[A:1, Supplementary]`.
  * More barrel-shaped thorax with flattened diaphragm [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Mechanical disadvantage for generating negative intrapleural pressure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Increased work of breathing [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Greater susceptibility to respiratory fatigue when minute ventilation increases [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
* **Lung Tissue Changes:**
  * Decreased lung parenchymal stiffness due to elastin loss [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Degeneration of elastic fibers of the alveolar septae leads to:
	* Loss of support for alveoli and small airways, resulting in airway collapse in expiration (i.e., increased closing capacity, CC). This is a major cause of V̇/Q̇ mismatch and hypoxaemia in the elderly `[A:1-2, Supplementary]`.
	* Increased lung compliance, which partially offsets the reduced thoracic wall compliance. Overall, the combined respiratory compliance is lower in older patients; the gradient of the pressure-volume curve is reduced (Figure 85.1) `[A:2, Supplementary]`.

### 3.4.2. SMALL AIRWAY FUNCTION

* **Closing Capacity:**
  * Decreased lung tissue elasticity reduces airway tethering [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Increased closing capacity with age [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]] (Fig. 34-10).
  * Closing capacity typically exceeds functional residual capacity in mid-60s [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * CC exceeds FRC when supine at age 45 `[A:2, Supplementary]` (Figure 85.2).
  * Early airway closure during forced exhalation due to reduced tethering [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Less uniform ventilation contributing to V/Q mismatch [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
* **Lung Volumes:**
  * An increase in residual volume and functional residual capacity (FRC) (Figure 85.2) `[A:2, Supplementary]`.
  * FRC occurs at the point where the inward elastic forces match the outward spring of the thoracic cage. Reduced lung elastic recoil means that FRC occurs at increased lung volume `[A:2, Supplementary]`.
  * The anterior-posterior diameter of the lung increases as a consequence of the higher resting lung volume. This results in flattening of the hemi-diaphragms, putting them at a mechanical disadvantage; the energy expended during inspiration increases `[A:2-3, Supplementary]`.

### 3.4.3. GAS EXCHANGE AND CONTROL OF BREATHING

* **Oxygenation Changes:**
  * Modest decline in resting PaO₂ with age due to V/Q mismatch and reduced alveolar surface area [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Alveolar-arterial (A-a) O₂ gradient increases with age due to:
	* V̇/Q̇ mismatch associated with increased CC
	* Reduced diffusing capacity of the alveoli due to both reduced alveolar surface area and increased alveolar-capillary membrane thickness
	* Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction being less active, which further exacerbates V̇/Q̇ mismatch `[A:3, Supplementary]`.
* **Ventilatory Control:**
  * Approximately 50% decrease in ventilatory response to hypercapnia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Even greater decrease in response to hypoxia, especially at night [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * High prevalence (possibly 75%) of sleep-disordered breathing in people over age 65 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].

### 3.4.4. UPPER AIRWAY AND PROTECTIVE MECHANISMS

* **Cough and Swallowing:**
  * Less effective coughing and impaired swallowing [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Increased risk of aspiration, contributing to community-acquired pneumonia and potentially postoperative pneumonia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].

### 3.4.5. SPIROMETRY CHANGES

* **Lung Volume Changes:**
  * Virtually no change in total lung capacity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Decreased expiratory reserve volume and increased residual volume [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Functional residual capacity remains largely unchanged [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].

## 3.5. RENAL AND HEPATIC AGING 

**Age-related changes in kidney and liver function significantly impact fluid homeostasis and drug elimination.**

### 3.5.1. RENAL CHANGES

* **Anatomical Changes:**
  * Renal cortical mass decreases by 20-25% with age [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Loss of up to half of glomeruli by age 80 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Decreased glomerular filtration rate of approximately 1 mL/min/yr after age 40 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]] (Fig. 34-5).
  * Decrease in glomerular filtration rate. Young adults have a significant reserve in renal function `[A:3, Supplementary]`.
  * From the age of 30 years, there is a progressive loss of glomeruli and reduced renal plasma flow, resulting in a lower creatinine clearance `[A:3, Supplementary]`.
* **Functional Changes:**
  * Drug dosage adjustment becomes progressively important beginning at approximately age 60 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Impaired sodium elimination and retention [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Reduced aldosterone secretion contributing to sodium conservation problems [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Decreased ability to retain or eliminate free water when challenged [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Reduced sensation of thirst [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * More vulnerable fluid and electrolyte homeostasis, especially during acute injury or disease [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Obstructive nephropathy is common in elderly men due to prostatism `[A:4, Supplementary]`.

### 3.5.2. HEPATIC CHANGES

* **Anatomical Changes:**
  * Liver mass decreases with age [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Reduced liver size and hepatic blood flow with advancing age `[A:4, Supplementary]`.
* **Functional Changes:**
  * 20-40% decrease in liver blood flow, partly due to decreased mass [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Modest reduction in phase I drug metabolism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Decreased bile secretion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Adequate liver reserve in the absence of disease, even in very old [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Decreased synthesis of plasma proteins with advancing age. Of note, albumin concentration decreases and plasma cholinesterase levels are reduced `[A:4, Supplementary]`.

## 3.6. THERMOREGULATION CHANGES 

**Aging impairs temperature regulation, increasing susceptibility to perioperative hypothermia.**

* **Thermoregulatory Mechanisms:**
  * Initial response to cold is vasoconstriction; if insufficient, shivering occurs [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Both mechanisms are triggered by decreases in core and/or skin temperature [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Decrease in skin temperature of 1°C is equivalent to a decrease in core temperature of approximately 0.2°C [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
* **Age-Related Changes:**
  * Impaired vasoconstriction response to cold environments [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Decreased heat production due to lower metabolism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Further impairment of thermal regulation thresholds by approximately 1°C (2°F) during general and spinal anesthesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * The elderly are at risk of hypothermia due to a reduction in heat production: basal metabolic rate decreases by 1% per year, and there is a reduction in the peripheral vasoconstrictor response to cold exposure `[A:4, Supplementary]`.
* **Anesthetic Effects on Thermoregulation:**
  * Inhalational and some intravenous agents (e.g., propofol, alfentanil) alter regulatory thresholds [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Body temperature must fall by as much as 4°C (7°F) before vasoconstriction or shivering initiation during anesthesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
* **Consequences of Hypothermia:**
  * Increased frequency of hypothermia in older patients [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Associated risks: myocardial ischemia, surgical wound infection, coagulopathy, increased blood loss, and impaired drug metabolism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Shivering places significant metabolic stress on patients, poorly tolerated by those with borderline cardiac or pulmonary reserve [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Older patients are especially at risk of intraoperative hypothermia. Post-operative shivering is the normal mechanism of returning to normothermia. The elderly may lack the muscle bulk to shiver effectively or may have insufficient cardiopulmonary reserve to meet the increased O₂ demand of shivering `[A:4, Supplementary]`.
* **Hyperthermia Risk:**
  * The elderly are also at risk of hyperthermia due to impairment of sweating `[A:4, Supplementary]`.

## 3.7. PHARMACOLOGY AND AGING  

**Older patients typically experience enhanced drug effects due to both pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic changes.**

### 3.7.1. GENERAL PRINCIPLES

* **Enhanced Drug Effects:**
  * Drugs typically have more pronounced effects in older patients [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * This can result from either pharmacodynamic (target organ more sensitive) or pharmacokinetic (higher blood levels) changes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * The physiological changes that occur with age have significant pharmacological implications for the anesthetist `[A:5, Supplementary]`.

### 3.7.2. DRUG DISTRIBUTION CHANGES

* **Initial Concentration:**
  * Higher initial drug concentrations due to:
	* Mildly contracted blood volume [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
	* Reduced muscle mass limiting drug removal [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Higher and more sustained blood levels drive more drug into highly perfused organs (brain, heart) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Drugs often take longer to achieve their (greater) effect, possibly due to slower brain-plasma equilibration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Increased Vdss (volume of distribution at steady state) with age, likely due to increased body fat [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]] (Fig. 34-7).
* **Altered Pharmacokinetics:**
  * Higher peak plasma concentration of drug due to the lower volume of distribution `[A:5, Supplementary]`.
  * Prolonged terminal half-life of some drugs due to sequestration of lipophilic drugs in the increased body fat and the reduced hepatic and renal clearance `[A:5, Supplementary]`.
  * Of particular note is that the effects of benzodiazepines given at induction of anesthesia may persist into the post-operative period `[A:5, Supplementary]`.

### 3.7.3. DRUG ELIMINATION CHANGES

* **Metabolism and Clearance:**
  * Decreased drug metabolism is the most prominent pharmacokinetic effect of aging [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Decreased clearance due to:
	* Reduced liver mass and blood flow affecting both high and low extraction drugs [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
	* Reduced renal function affecting renally excreted drugs [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
	* Drug interactions from polypharmacy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Typically doubles the elimination half-life between old and young adults [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Some drugs show dramatic effects (e.g., diazepam half-life in hours roughly equals patient's age in years) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]] (Fig. 34-8).
* **Context-Sensitive Half-Time:**
  * Time for 50% decrease in plasma concentration after termination of an infusion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * More significantly increased by aging when a large percentage decrease in plasma level is necessary to terminate drug effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].

### 3.7.4. DRUG-SPECIFIC CHANGES

* **Vasopressors:**
  * The reduced cardiac response to β-agonists means that mixed α- and β-agonists (e.g., ephedrine) become less effective with advancing age `[A:5, Supplementary]`.
  * Pure α-agonists (e.g., phenylephrine) are as effective in the elderly as in young people `[A:5, Supplementary]`.
* **Intravenous Induction Agents:**
  * The altered pharmacokinetics in the elderly leads to higher free drug plasma concentrations of the intravenous induction agents `[A:5, Supplementary]`.
  * The dose of induction agent therefore needs to be reduced `[A:5, Supplementary]`.
  * The reduced CO prolongs the arm-brain circulation time, so intravenous induction drugs must be injected slowly to avoid inadvertent overdose and the associated hypotension `[A:5, Supplementary]`.
* **Inhalational Agents:**
  * Minimum alveolar concentration of inhalational anesthetics is reduced by up to 30% in the elderly `[A:5, Supplementary]`.
  * The duration of inhalational induction may be prolonged due to the reduced alveolar diffusion and increased V̇/Q̇ mismatch that occur with advancing age `[A:5, Supplementary]`.
* **Neuromuscular Blocking Agents:**
  * The dose of non-depolarizing muscle relaxants is unchanged in the elderly `[A:6, Supplementary]`.
  * The time taken to reach intubating conditions is increased due to reduced CO `[A:6, Supplementary]`.
  * The duration of neuromuscular blockade may be prolonged in the elderly depending on the mechanism of drug metabolism:
	* **Atracurium** has the same duration of action. Its metabolism is independent of renal and liver function as atracurium is metabolized by plasma esterases and through Hofmann elimination `[A:6, Supplementary]`.
	* **Mivacurium and suxamethonium** have prolonged effects; these drugs are metabolized through the action of plasma cholinesterases, which are reduced in the elderly. The prolonged action of suxamethonium is clinically insignificant `[A:6, Supplementary]`.
	* **Aminosteroid muscle relaxants** may have prolonged action in the elderly as their metabolism relies on both hepatic metabolism and renal excretion `[A:6, Supplementary]`.
* **Sedative-Hypnotics:** Both pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic changes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
* **Opioids:** Primarily pharmacodynamic (increased brain sensitivity) with less effect on pharmacokinetics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
* **Muscle Relaxants:** Not more potent in older patients, but often have modestly slowed metabolism (especially vecuronium and rocuronium), leading to increased duration with repetitive dosing [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
* **Specific drug dosing adjustments:** See Table 34-2 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]] for comprehensive age-based dosing recommendations.

## 3.8. OTHER SYSTEM CHANGES

**Various other organ systems undergo significant age-related changes affecting perioperative management.**

### 3.8.1. ENDOCRINE SYSTEM

* **Glucose Metabolism:**
  * Decreased insulin secretion in response to glucose load [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Increased insulin resistance, particularly in skeletal muscle [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Healthy elderly patients may require perioperative insulin therapy more often than young adults, but at lower doses [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Diabetes mellitus is common in the elderly with an increase in peripheral insulin resistance and impaired insulin secretion in response to hyperglycemia `[A:4, Supplementary]`.
* **Hormone Changes:**
  * Decreases in testosterone, estrogen, and growth hormone production [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].

### 3.8.2. GASTROINTESTINAL SYSTEM

* **Anatomical Changes:**
  * Increased prevalence of hiatus hernia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Delayed gastric emptying [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].

### 3.8.3. MUSCULOSKELETAL AND SKIN

* **Musculoskeletal Changes:**
  * Loss of muscle mass (sarcopenia) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Stiffening of joints and connective tissue [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Changes may affect positioning, airway management, and functional recovery [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * There is a generalized loss of muscle mass with advancing age, known as sarcopenia; 6 kg of muscle is lost by 80 years of age. This is thought to be due to loss of motor neurons, resulting in decreased levels of Ca²⁺ within the sarcoplasm `[A:4, Supplementary]`.
* **Joint and Bone Issues:**
  * Arthritis and bony deformities are very common in older people `[A:4, Supplementary]`.
  * This may lead to difficulty with mobility and positioning, potentially making regional anesthesia more difficult `[A:4, Supplementary]`.
  * Larger-calibre spinal needles may be required to pass through calcified ligaments `[A:4, Supplementary]`.
  * Fortunately, post-dural puncture headache is far less common in the elderly `[A:4, Supplementary]`.
* **Skin and Tissue Changes:**
  * The elderly tend to have reduced subcutaneous tissue, thin skin, and fragile veins; achieving venous access can be difficult `[A:4, Supplementary]`.
  * Infusions of fluids or drugs can extravasate, especially if introduced under pressure `[A:4, Supplementary]`.
  * Pressure sores are more common, and additional care must be taken with patient positioning and padding of pressure points `[A:4, Supplementary]`.

### 3.8.4. SENSORY SYSTEMS

* **Visual and Hearing:**
  * Decreased visual acuity `[A:1, Supplementary]`.
  * Hearing impairment `[A:1, Supplementary]`.
  * Sensory impairment is common. Deafness is very common in older people, and visual impairment affects around a third of the elderly population `[A:3, Supplementary]`.
  * Sensory impairments can affect communication and increase risk of perioperative confusion `[A:1, Supplementary]`.
* **Airway Concerns:**
  * Dental issues - may be edentulous or have expensive bridges and dentures affecting intubation, supraglottic airway function, and facemask ventilation `[A:1, Supplementary]`.
  * TMJ dysfunction `[A:1, Supplementary]`.
  * Cervical spondylosis or arthritis `[A:1, Supplementary]`.

**Bottom Line:** Age-related physiological changes affect virtually every organ system, with important implications for perioperative management. The elderly experience reduced functional reserve, altered drug responses, impaired homeostatic mechanisms, and increased vulnerability to stressors. Significant changes include stiffened cardiovascular systems, altered respiratory mechanics with V/Q mismatch, decreased drug clearance, impaired thermoregulation, and multiple neurological effects. Understanding these comprehensive changes is fundamental to developing appropriate anesthetic plans and risk mitigation strategies for geriatric patients.

# 4. PERIOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT OF OLDER PATIENTS





## 4.1. PREOPERATIVE ASSESSMENT AND OPTIMIZATION

> **Syllabus**: [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B: C5 Perioperative Medicine]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#6. Systems-Based Practice|Part C: SBP]]

**Preoperative patient-centered care and multi-system evaluation focused on goals and risk-mitigation are critical for enhancing recovery of function after surgery.**

* **Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment:**
  * The American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (ACS NSQIP) and American Geriatrics Society (AGS) provide best practice guidelines [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * The Strong for Surgery Program by ACS highlights important components of preoperative risk stratification [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * About 25% of older surgical patients remain significantly compromised in some way postoperatively [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

### 4.1.2. GOALS OF CARE AND ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS

* **Advance Care Planning:**
  * Older patients more likely to have living wills, healthcare proxies, and directives [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Almost one-third of elderly Medicare beneficiaries undergo surgery in their last year of life, with over 18% undergoing surgery in the last month of their lives [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Expectations for surgery may differ from younger patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Important to discuss and document "Limited Attempt at Resuscitation" agreements if appropriate [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Functional Recovery:**
  * Prospect of functional impairment may be more worrisome than death [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Most older patients require at least 3 months for ADLs and independent ADLs to return to baseline after major abdominal surgery [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Persistent disability at 6 months varies: 9% incidence of persistent ADL deficits, 19% for independent ADLs, 52% for diminished grip strength [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

### 4.1.3. COGNITIVE ASSESSMENT

* **Preoperative Cognitive Dysfunction:**
  * Common (estimated 25% of older patients presenting for orthopedic surgery) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Important risk factor for postoperative complications, including delirium [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Has implications for informed consent [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Despite importance, preoperative cognitive evaluation occurs "rarely" or "never" for >80% of elderly patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Postoperative Cognitive Risks:**
  * Discussion of postoperative cognitive impairment is an important disclosure during informed consent [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Despite 83% of patients being aware of postoperative delirium, concern about its effects is low [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Only 25% of patients report prior counseling on delirium [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

### 4.1.4. EXPANDED PREOPERATIVE EVALUATION [2025 ASA GUIDELINES]

**Evidence-based expanded preoperative evaluation can reduce postoperative complications in older adults.**

* **Recommendation and Evidence:**
  * Consider expanded preoperative evaluation in older adults scheduled for inpatient procedures to reduce postoperative delirium risk `[ASA 2025:3]`.
  * Conditional recommendation with low strength evidence based on pooled results from six randomized trials `[ASA 2025:3]`.
  * Risk ratio 0.77 (95% CI, 0.60 to 0.99) for postoperative delirium with expanded vs. standard evaluation `[ASA 2025:3]`.
* **Components of Expanded Evaluation:**
  * **Systematic Assessment Areas:** Frailty, cognitive impairment, physical function, nutritional status, psychosocial issues `[ASA 2025:3]`.
  * **Validated Cognitive Tools:** Mini-Mental State Examination, Montreal Cognitive Assessment, Trail Making Test, Digit Symbol Test `[ASA 2025:Appendix]`.
  * **Validated Frailty Tools:** Clinical Frailty Scale, Edmonton Frail Scale, FRAIL questionnaire (Fatigue, Resistance, Ambulation, Illnesses, Loss of weight) `[ASA 2025:Appendix]`.
  * **Psychosocial Assessment:** Geriatric Depression Scale, SF-36 Mental Health, State-Trait Operation Anxiety Inventory `[ASA 2025:Appendix]`.
  * **Physical Function Assessment:** Groningen Activity Restriction Scale, Short Physical Performance Battery, SF-36 Physical Functioning `[ASA 2025:Appendix]`.
* **Interventions Following Assessment:**
  * **Multidisciplinary Team Involvement:** Implemented in 84% of studies (26 of 31) `[ASA 2025:3]`.
  * **Medication Management:** De-prescribing in 42% of studies (13 of 31) `[ASA 2025:3]`.
  * **Nutritional Support:** Supplementation in 29% of studies (9 of 31) `[ASA 2025:3]`.
  * **Geriatric Specialist Visits:** Implemented in 35% of studies (11 of 31) `[ASA 2025:3]`.
  * **Active Delirium Screening:** Reported in 13% of studies (4 of 31) `[ASA 2025:3]`.
  * **Optimized Chronic Disease Management:** In inpatient, outpatient, and prehospital settings `[ASA 2025:3]`.
* **Clinical Implementation:**
  * If patients identified with cognitive impairment and/or frailty, changes in patient care should be initiated `[ASA 2025:3]`.
  * Multidisciplinary care may include hospitalists, geriatric nurse champions, psychiatry, pharmacy, physical/occupational therapy, nutritionists, chaplaincy, and volunteer services `[ASA 2025:3]`.
  * Treatment plans for at-risk patients involve geriatric care throughout hospitalization `[ASA 2025:3]`.

### 4.1.5. KEY ASSESSMENT AREAS

**Cognitive Screening:**
  * Recommended for all older surgical patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Brief Mini-Cog assessment linked to recovery speed, mortality risk, and delirium risk [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Depression Screening:**
  * Tools such as Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Frailty Assessment:**
  * Using standardized tools (see Table 34-1) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Alert surgeons and anesthesiologists of frailty status for risk stratification [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Nutritional Status:**
  * Often underappreciated risk factor [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * May include "anorexia of aging" with early satiety and diminished taste sense [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Albumin shown to be a sensitive index for mortality or morbidity [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Medication Review:**
  * Compare medications to Beers Criteria (Table 34-4) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Consider appropriate perioperative adjustments [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Monitor for polypharmacy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

### 4.1.6. PREHABILITATION

* **Concept and Components:**
  * Interventions before surgery targeting aging-specific care concerns [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Appears superior to similar interventions provided after surgery [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Multifaceted approach to protect cognitive and physical functions [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] (Fig. 34-11).
  * Components include:
	* Exercise-based interventions (aerobic, resistance, flexibility, balance) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* Lifestyle modifications (reduced alcohol, smoking cessation) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* Nutritional interventions (protein/multivitamin supplementation) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* Behavioral health stress-coping mechanisms [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* Cognitive exercises for protection of cognitive reserve [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Potential cost savings reported for major abdominal surgery [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

### 4.1.7. PREHABILITATION - 2025 ASA GUIDELINES UPDATE

**The 2025 ASA guidelines provide updated perspective on prehabilitation for older adults, highlighting both promise and limitations.**

* **Definition and Scope:**
  * Process of enhancing capacity and reserve before an acute stressor (surgery) to improve tolerance of upcoming injury `[ASA 2025:Prehabilitation]`.
  * Includes physical exercise, nutritional supplementation, and/or cognitive training interventions `[ASA 2025:Prehabilitation]`.
  * Topic not included as key question in systematic review due to lack of studies focusing specifically on older adults `[ASA 2025:Prehabilitation]`.
* **Current Evidence Base:**
  * **General Adult Population:** Moderate-certainty evidence that prehabilitation improves functional recovery; low-certainty evidence for complications and length of stay `[ASA 2025:Prehabilitation]`.
  * **Older Adult-Specific Limitations:** Minimal data currently available specific to older adults, especially vulnerable populations with frailty or sarcopenia `[ASA 2025:Prehabilitation]`.
  * **Evidence Quality:** Low certainty evidence for most outcomes limits ability to make specific recommendations for older adults requiring anesthesia and surgery `[ASA 2025:Prehabilitation]`.
* **Major Research Gaps:**
  * **Component Optimization:** Lack of understanding which components (physical exercise vs. nutrition vs. cognitive training) are most effective for older patients `[ASA 2025:Prehabilitation]`.
  * **Program Design:** Unknown optimal intervention intensity, duration, program location (home vs. facility-based), and supervisory approaches `[ASA 2025:Prehabilitation]`.
  * **Vulnerable Populations:** Need for randomized trials targeting older patients, particularly those with frailty or sarcopenia `[ASA 2025:Prehabilitation]`.
  * **Patient-Centered Outcomes:** Research should address outcomes prioritized by older patients, including maintenance of independence and returning to preoperative living situation `[ASA 2025:Prehabilitation]`.
* **Implementation Challenges:**
  * **Baseline Complexity:** Medical complexity and disease-related symptom burden are recognized barriers to participation in frailty and sarcopenia populations `[ASA 2025:Prehabilitation]`.
  * **Adherence Issues:** Strategies needed to enhance adherence and support efficacy for vulnerable populations before routine use recommendation `[ASA 2025:Prehabilitation]`.
  * **Program Structure:** Questions remain about what structure and support programs are required to achieve safety, adequate adherence, and efficacy `[ASA 2025:Prehabilitation]`.
* **Cognitive Prehabilitation:**
  * **Early Promise:** Early evidence promising for delirium reduction, but primary results remain inconclusive `[ASA 2025:Prehabilitation]`.
  * **Research Needs:** Additional studies needed to evaluate efficacy of different interventions (product interface, target pathways, timing, intensity) `[ASA 2025:Prehabilitation]`.
  * **Realistic Effect Sizes:** Future research should be powered for more realistic effect sizes `[ASA 2025:Prehabilitation]`.
* **Future Trials:**
  * **PREPARE Trial:** Upcoming multicenter trial specifically powered to detect meaningful differences in patient-reported disability and complication rates in older surgical patients with frailty should provide important insights `[ASA 2025:Prehabilitation]`.

### 4.1.8. KEY ASSESSMENT AREAS

**Intraoperative management must consider the increased sensitivity to drugs in the elderly patient, as well as an increased likelihood of hemodynamic, pulmonary, and thermoregulatory instability.**

### 4.1.9. ANESTHETIC PLAN CONSIDERATIONS

* **Evidence for Anesthetic Approach:**
  * No single "best" anesthetic plan for older adults is supported by current evidence [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Important to confirm goals and treatment plan, especially if changed since initial assessment [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Clarify advance directives and legal decision makers [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Implement customized pain management plan with multimodal approaches and regional techniques [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

### 4.1.10. INDUCTION CONSIDERATIONS

* **Intravenous Induction:**
  * Effects of bolus induction doses are highly variable [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Smaller doses needed compared to young adults [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Allow more time for drug to achieve peak target organ effect [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Propofol causes greater decrease in brain activity and blood pressure in older patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Strategies to minimize blood pressure decrease:
	* Reduce propofol dose with adjuncts (opioids, small doses of etomidate) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* Use propofol infusion of 400 μg/kg/min until BIS reaches 60 instead of bolus [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Etomidate produces less hypotension than propofol in older patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

### 4.1.11. GENERAL VS. REGIONAL ANESTHESIA

* **2025 ASA Guideline Evidence:**
  * **Strong Recommendation:** Choose either neuraxial or general anesthesia for older adults when either is clinically appropriate, based on shared decision-making `[ASA 2025:4]`.
  * **Moderate Strength Evidence:** No superiority with either technique in reducing postoperative delirium `[ASA 2025:4]`.
  * Evidence synthesis found neither technique associated with lower delirium risk (overall analysis and subgroup analyses) `[ASA 2025:4]`.
  * **Hip Fracture Subgroup:** Risk ratio 1.05 (95% CI, 0.76 to 1.43) for delirium `[ASA 2025:4]`.
  * **Non-Hip Fracture Procedures:** Risk ratio 0.74 (95% CI, 0.35 to 1.60) for delirium `[ASA 2025:4]`.
  * **Other Outcomes:** Neuraxial anesthesia may reduce risk of acute kidney injury and pneumonia, but evidence strength low to very low `[ASA 2025:4]`.
* **Clinical Decision-Making:**
  * Choice likely involves tradeoffs for most patients, making it suitable for shared decision-making `[ASA 2025:4]`.
  * Consider individual patient preferences and characteristics when choosing optimal technique `[ASA 2025:4]`.
  * This is likely a "preference-sensitive" decision in many cases `[ASA 2025:4]`.
* **Comparison of Outcomes:**
  * A recent study of 1,600 older hip fracture patients found:
	* No difference in primary composite outcome (postoperative death by day 60 and inability to walk independently) between general and spinal anesthesia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* No differences in delirium and hospital length of stay [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* Neither anesthetic approach clearly superior in this population [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * THRIVE trial currently studying quality of recovery and safety between propofol TIVA and volatile anesthesia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Growing evidence suggests surgery-associated inflammation may be more important than anesthetic regimen differences [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

### 4.1.12. MAINTENANCE PHASE CONSIDERATIONS

* **Hemodynamic Management:**
  * Significant blood pressure decrease common, more than in younger patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Decrease in vascular resistance is likely the largest contributor to blood pressure decrease [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] (Fig. 34-12).
  * MAP <55 mm Hg associated with increased odds of postoperative delirium, risk growing with hypotension duration [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * α-Agonists may be appropriate therapy and potentially more effective than volume alone [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Need for careful balance between vasoconstriction and volume administration [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

### 4.1.13. AIRWAY MANAGEMENT

* **Device Selection:**
  * Choice between endotracheal tube and laryngeal mask airway involves multiple considerations [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Endotracheal tube advantage: guarantees control of ventilation, preventing hypercarbia and intraoperative atelectasis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Disadvantage: adverse effects on mucociliary clearance and possibly swallowing [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

### 4.1.14. VENTILATION STRATEGY

* **Respiratory Approach:**
  * Goal: maintain lung volume exceeding closing capacity to prevent atelectasis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Achieved by either high tidal volumes or modest tidal volumes with PEEP [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Lower tidal volume with PEEP strategy associated with lower inflammatory markers and decreased perioperative pulmonary complications [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

### 4.1.15. NEUROMUSCULAR BLOCKADE MANAGEMENT

* **Relaxant Considerations:**
  * Older patients at almost double the risk of residual neuromuscular blockade and adverse respiratory events [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Common relaxants (vecuronium, rocuronium) have modestly slowed metabolism with aging [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Sugammadex (2 mg/kg) effectively reverses deep neuromuscular blockade in older adults, though time to TOF ratio >0.9 is about 40 seconds longer than in younger adults [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Additional 1 mg/kg can shorten reversal time to <2 minutes in patients >70 years old in emergency situations [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Sugammadex (2 mg/kg) significantly reduces residual postoperative neuromuscular blockade compared to neostigmine [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

### 4.1.16. TOTAL INTRAVENOUS ANESTHESIA VS. INHALED ANESTHESIA [2025 EVIDENCE]

**The 2025 ASA guidelines provide new evidence on anesthetic maintenance techniques for older adults.**

* **2025 ASA Guideline Recommendation:**
  * **Conditional Recommendation:** Either total intravenous or inhaled anesthesia is acceptable for general anesthesia in the older population `[ASA 2025:5]`.
  * **Low Strength Evidence:** Inconclusive with respect to comparative risk of postoperative delirium `[ASA 2025:5]`.
  * Pooled estimate from eight randomized clinical trials did not favor either technique for delirium risk `[ASA 2025:5]`.
* **Neurocognitive Outcomes:**
  * **Short-term effects:** Pooled estimate from five trials suggests lower risk of neurocognitive disorder up to 30 days with TIVA `[ASA 2025:5]`.
  * **Longer-term effects:** Single trial and three nonrandomized studies found no difference at 30 days to 1 year `[ASA 2025:5]`.
  * Evidence limited by variability in measurement methods and timing `[ASA 2025:5]`.
* **Other Outcomes:**
  * **Patient Satisfaction:** Higher satisfaction with TIVA in ophthalmologic and gastrointestinal/abdominal surgery `[ASA 2025:5]`.
  * **Complications:** Low-grade evidence favoring decreased pulmonary embolism and respiratory failure with TIVA `[ASA 2025:5]`.
  * **Mortality and Length of Stay:** No difference between techniques `[ASA 2025:5]`.
* **Clinical Considerations:**
  * Complexity of surgical procedures across studies complicates direct outcome comparisons `[ASA 2025:5]`.
  * Drawing definitive conclusions about specific impact of surgery type on outcomes proves challenging `[ASA 2025:5]`.
  * Findings specific to certain surgical procedures and patient populations `[ASA 2025:5]`.

### 4.1.17. ### 4.2.9. EEG MONITORING CONSIDERATIONS

**Brain Monitoring Evidence:**
  * Utility remains controversial [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * ENGAGES trial (1,232 patients aged 60+ years): BIS-guided group had lower 30-day mortality (0.65% vs. 3.07%) but no other outcome differences [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * BALANCED trial: no benefit in either BIS target group (35 vs. 50) for 1-year mortality or serious adverse events [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Cumulative time of burst suppression correlates with postoperative delirium risk [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Current evidence does not support routine use of processed EEG specifically for preventing postoperative delirium [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * MAC dosages should be adjusted down with increasing age [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **2025 ASA Guidelines Perspective on EEG Monitoring:**
  * **Conflicting Evidence:** Topic not addressed in advisory due to limited and conflicting nature of available evidence `[ASA 2025:Introduction]`.
  * **Regional Variation in Results:**
	* **Positive Evidence:** Meta-analyses and randomized trials from East Asia and Spain suggested processed EEG monitoring may reduce delirium incidence and hospital stay `[ASA 2025:Introduction]`.
	* **Negative Evidence:** Large North American trials (ENGAGES, SHARP) failed to demonstrate clear benefit of EEG-guided anesthetic depth reduction on postoperative delirium `[ASA 2025:Introduction]`.
	* **Mortality:** No reduction in 1-year mortality observed `[ASA 2025:Introduction]`.
  * **Ongoing Debate:** Specific link between deep anesthesia and delirium remains debated; baseline patient vulnerabilities might be more influential `[ASA 2025:Introduction]`.
  * **Clinical Implication:** While adequate postoperative pain control widely recognized as crucial, scarcity of high-quality research exists to definitively determine its impact on delirium in older adults `[ASA 2025:Introduction]`.

### 4.1.18. PHARMACOLOGIC DELIRIUM PREVENTION [2025 EVIDENCE]

**The 2025 ASA guidelines provide comprehensive evidence on dexmedetomidine for delirium prevention.**

* **Dexmedetomidine Recommendation:**
  * **Conditional Recommendation:** Reasonable to consider dexmedetomidine to lower risk of postoperative delirium while considering effects on bradycardia and/or hypotension `[ASA 2025:6]`.
  * **Moderate Strength Evidence:** Based on pooled results of 31 randomized clinical trials `[ASA 2025:6]`.
  * **Delirium Reduction:** Risk ratio 0.58 (95% CI, 0.49 to 0.67) compared with placebo or no intervention `[ASA 2025:6]`.
* **Additional Cognitive Benefits:**
  * **Short-term Neurocognitive Function:** Pooled results of nine trials showed lower incidence of neurocognitive disorder <30 days `[ASA 2025:6]`.
  * **Longer-term Effects:** Two small trials showed reduction in neurocognitive disorder at 30 days to 1 year `[ASA 2025:6]`.
* **Adverse Effects:**
  * **Bradycardia:** Pooled analysis of 17 trials showed increased risk `[ASA 2025:6]`.
  * **Hypotension:** Pooled analysis of 20 trials showed increased risk `[ASA 2025:6]`.
  * These findings must be considered when weighing risk-benefit profile `[ASA 2025:6]`.
* **Important Considerations for Clinical Use:**
  * **Geographic Variation:** Stronger effects reported from trials conducted in China; generalizability to all populations unclear `[ASA 2025:6]`.
  * **Baseline Risk Effect:** Relative effect appeared to diminish with decreasing baseline delirium risk `[ASA 2025:6]`.
  * **Timing:** Administration timing (preoperative, intraoperative, or postoperative) did not clearly modify results `[ASA 2025:6]`.
  * **Potential Publication Bias:** Small-study effects apparent; pooled result may overstate true effect `[ASA 2025:6]`.
  * **Surgical Variation:** Effect magnitudes generally consistent across surgery types, but heterogeneity varied considerably `[ASA 2025:6]`.
  * **Optimal Care Context:** Extent to which similar effects would be observed with optimal nonpharmacologic care should be considered `[ASA 2025:6]`.
* **Dosing and Administration:**
  * **Heterogeneity:** Wide variation in dosing and timing across trials `[ASA 2025:6]`.
  * **Loading Doses:** Ranged from 0.2 to 4.0 mcg/kg `[ASA 2025:Appendix]`.
  * **Maintenance Doses:** Ranged from 0.1 to 1.5 mcg·kg⁻¹·h⁻¹ `[ASA 2025:Appendix]`.
  * **Administration Times:** Preoperatively, at induction, intraoperatively, postoperatively, or combinations `[ASA 2025:Appendix]`.

### 4.1.19. MEDICATION CONSIDERATIONS

**Drug Selection in Elderly:**
  * Meperidine should be avoided except perhaps in small doses for shivering [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * No superior choice among fentanyl, hydromorphone, and morphine [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Subanesthetic intraoperative ketamine does not significantly decrease delirium risk [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Dexmedetomidine decreases delirium risk in mechanically ventilated ICU patients and cardiac surgery patients, but utility in other surgical populations needs further study [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Gabapentinoids require dose adjustment for diminished renal function, and carry serious concerns about adverse effects when combined with opiates in older patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * No volatile anesthetic (desflurane, sevoflurane, isoflurane, nitrous oxide) shows superiority for postoperative cognitive function [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Medications with Central Nervous System Effects [2025 EVIDENCE]:**
  * **Best Practice Statement:** Consider risks and benefits of medications with potential CNS effects in older adults, as these drugs may increase postoperative delirium risk `[ASA 2025:7]`.
  * **Benzodiazepines:** Four randomized trials and four nonrandomized studies did not detect difference in delirium with short-acting benzodiazepines vs. placebo; however, two large database studies found lower delirium with short-acting but higher with long-acting benzodiazepines `[ASA 2025:7]`.
  * **Antipsychotics:** Five randomized trials reported lower delirium incidence vs. placebo, but three trials were inconclusive `[ASA 2025:7]`.
  * **Ketamine:** Pooled analysis of four randomized trials comparing ketamine with placebo did not detect difference in delirium `[ASA 2025:7]`.
  * **Corticosteroids:** Four trials inconclusive concerning delirium incidence vs. placebo, while two trials reported lower delirium incidence vs. no drug `[ASA 2025:7]`.
  * **NSAIDs:** Two large retrospective studies reported lower delirium incidence compared to no drug `[ASA 2025:7]`.
  * **Gabapentin:** One randomized trial found no difference vs. placebo, but one large retrospective study found increased delirium incidence `[ASA 2025:7]`.
  * **Clinical Decision Framework:** Weigh risks and benefits based on patient's condition, chronic medications, comorbidities (especially pre-existing neurocognitive disorders), and planned procedure `[ASA 2025:7]`.
  * **Polypharmacy Considerations:** Consider polypharmacy as known delirium risk factor and potential drug-drug interactions `[ASA 2025:7]`.
  * **Alignment with Guidelines:** Best practice statement aligns with American Geriatrics Society 2023 Beers Criteria `[ASA 2025:7]`.

## 4.2. POSTOPERATIVE CARE  

**As older patients progress through the days following surgery, vigilance for neurologic and physiologic perturbations that can derail recovery is critical.**

### 4.2.1. DELIRIUM MANAGEMENT

* **Recognition and Importance:**
  * High priority for all surgical patients >65 years or with risk factors [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Often hypoactive and undiagnosed in elderly patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Associated with increased hospitalization duration, costs, poorer long-term functional recovery, and increased mortality [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Confusion Assessment Method is the gold standard for diagnosis, though easier instruments exist (e.g., 3D-CAM) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Nonpharmacologic Risk-Reduction Strategies:**
  * Daily physical activity and early mobility [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Cognitive reorientation with assistive devices and aids [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Family member presence whenever possible [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Sleep enhancement [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Appropriate pain management [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Nutrition and fluid repletion [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Avoiding antipsychotics except when safety concerns exist [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Prevention of constipation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Minimizing patient tethers (lines and drains) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

### 4.2.2. PAIN MANAGEMENT

* **Challenges and Approaches:**
  * Effective, multimodal opiate-sparing analgesia is paramount [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Both opiate use and inadequate pain control are associated with delirium [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Elderly patients sometimes underreport pain and may be more tolerant of acute pain [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Older patients have more difficulty with visual analog scoring systems than verbal or numeric systems [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Cognitive impairment complicates pain assessment and use of patient-controlled analgesia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Inadequate analgesia associated with numerous adverse outcomes: sleep deprivation, respiratory impairment, ileus, suboptimal mobilization, insulin resistance, tachycardia, and hypertension [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Opioids can produce many of those same adverse outcomes [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Adjunctive medications like NSAIDs reduce opioid requirements but carry their own risks (renal damage, GI toxicity) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Epidural analgesia provides superior analgesia to intravenous therapy, with more rapid return of bowel function, earlier mobilization, and better nutritional status [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

### 4.2.3. INTEGRATED CARE MODELS

* **Specialized Programs:**
  * Show positive impact on postoperative recovery and outcomes [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * **Hospital Elder Life Program (HELP):** Prevents delirium and other cognitive and functional decline [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * **Acute Care for Elders (ACE):** Improves independent functioning, shortens length of stay, and decreases costs [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * **Nurses Improving Care for Healthsystem Elderly (NICHE):** Improves institutional protocol compliance and clinical outcomes [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

### 4.2.4. OTHER POSTOPERATIVE CONSIDERATIONS

* **System-Specific Monitoring:**
  * Monitor for signs of fluid overload (rales, dyspnea, tachypnea, orthopnea) especially around postoperative day 2 when third-space fluid mobilizes [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Check for atrial fibrillation, which is often paroxysmal in elderly patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Implement aspiration precautions and aggressive pulmonary hygiene to lower pulmonary complication risk [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Use universal fall precautions without unnecessarily restricting mobilization [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Evaluate ability to consume adequate nutrition daily [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Regularly evaluate indwelling bladder catheters for potential removal [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Include family in care, provide early physical/occupational therapy consultation, geriatric consultation, and comprehensive discharge planning [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

## 4.3. ENHANCED RECOVERY MODELS FOR GERIATRIC PATIENTS 

**Enhanced recovery and perioperative surgical home models of care are well-suited for older surgical patients.**

* **Major Initiatives:**
  * **The Age-Friendly Health System:** Framework by Institute for Healthcare Improvement (IHI) focusing on the "4-M's" - What Matters, Medication, Mentation, Mobility [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * **Geriatric Surgical Verification (GSV) Program:** Sponsored by ACS with over 30 components focused on screening vulnerable patients and creating individualized management plans [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * **Enhanced Recovery Protocols:** Effective in helping patients tolerate surgical stress [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * **Perioperative Surgical Home (PSH):** ASA-supported comprehensive patient-centered care model [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Implementation Challenges:**
  * Lag in execution of recommended practices despite growth of geriatric-focused clinical expertise [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Balance between evidence-based care and innovation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Necessity for anesthesiologists to be effective coordinators of care programs [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Resource Gaps and Future Considerations:**
  * Expanding "human resource" gaps in healthcare, particularly for direct care workers for aging patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Need for skilled geriatric clinicians for complex elderly patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Potential for artificial intelligence to address these challenges through machine-learning and advanced health monitoring [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Healthcare disparities affecting quality of care for elderly patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Need for multidisciplinary approach to complete care of geriatric surgical patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Perioperative management of older patients requires comprehensive assessment, careful drug selection and dosing, vigilant monitoring, and integrated care models. The 2025 ASA Practice Guidelines provide evidence-based recommendations emphasizing expanded preoperative evaluation for cognitive and frailty screening, consideration of dexmedetomidine for delirium prevention, and careful assessment of medications with CNS effects. Early identification of frailty and cognitive impairment allows for targeted interventions. Either neuraxial or general anesthesia and either TIVA or inhaled anesthesia are acceptable choices based on patient factors and shared decision-making. Multimodal analgesia, delirium prevention, and early mobilization remain key elements of postoperative care. Enhanced recovery protocols and perioperative surgical home models provide frameworks for optimizing outcomes in this vulnerable population.

# 5. PERIOPERATIVE COMPLICATIONS IN OLDER PATIENTS





## 5.1. DELIRIUM AND PERIOPERATIVE NEUROCOGNITIVE DISORDERS

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System|Part A: CNS]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B: C5]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#6. Systems-Based Practice|Part C: SBP]]

**Central nervous system complications are a major source of morbidity and mortality, with new terminology and classification for perioperative cognitive disorders.**

### 5.1.2. TERMINOLOGY CHANGES AND UPDATED CLASSIFICATION [2025 UPDATE]

* **Evolution of Classification:**
  * "Perioperative neurocognitive disorders" (PNDs) is now the primary term for cognitive impairment identified in the pre or postoperative periods [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Replaces older term "postoperative cognitive dysfunction" (POCD) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Subclassified based on DSM-V criteria and temporal association with surgery [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **2025 ASA Guidelines Terminology:**
  * **Critical Outcomes Defined:** Postoperative delirium, neurocognitive disorder less than 30 days, and neurocognitive disorder 30 days or more to 1 year `[ASA 2025:2]`.
  * **Assessment Tools for Delirium:** Confusion Assessment Method, Confusion Assessment Method–Intensive Care Unit, Delirium Rating Scale, Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Intensive Care Delirium Screening Checklist `[ASA 2025:2]`.
  * **Assessment Tools for Neurocognitive Disorder:** Mini-Mental State Examination, Montreal Cognitive Assessment, Digit Span Test `[ASA 2025:2]`.

### 5.1.3. CLASSIFICATION OF PERIOPERATIVE NEUROCOGNITIVE DISORDERS

* **Categories of Impairment:**
  * **Postoperative Delirium:** Acute onset, waxing and waning alterations in attention and cognition in first 7 postoperative days [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * **Delayed Neurocognitive Recovery (dNCR):** Cognitive impairments present 7-30 days postsurgery [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * **Postoperative Neurocognitive Disorder (NCD):** New diagnosis made after expected surgical recovery time (30 days) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* **Mild NCD:** 1-2 standard deviations below norms or controls [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* **Major NCD:** ≥2 standard deviations below norms or controls [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

### 5.1.4. POSTOPERATIVE DELIRIUM

* **Incidence and Impact:**
  * Incidence can exceed 50% for high-risk surgeries [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Costs exceed $150 billion per year in the United States [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * At least one in three cases likely preventable [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * **2025 Update:** Associated with adverse in-hospital and patient-reported outcomes `[ASA 2025:1]`.
  * Patients experiencing delirium have more impaired functional recovery in month after surgery than counterparts without delirium `[ASA 2025:1]`.
* **Risk Factors:**
  * Age, baseline low cognitive function, depression, frailty, general debility, dehydration, sensory impairment [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * More risk factors present, fewer perioperative stressors required to cause delirium [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Drug contributors: narcotics (especially meperidine), benzodiazepines (especially lorazepam), anticholinergics [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Other contributors: sleep deprivation, unfamiliar environment, perioperative blood loss [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Associated with hypotension (MAP < 55 mmHg) and blood pressure fluctuations [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Prevention Strategies [2025 EVIDENCE]:**
  * **Expanded Preoperative Evaluation:** Reduces delirium risk (risk ratio 0.77; 95% CI, 0.60 to 0.99) `[ASA 2025:3]`.
  * **Dexmedetomidine:** May reduce delirium risk (risk ratio 0.58; 95% CI, 0.49 to 0.67) while considering bradycardia/hypotension risks `[ASA 2025:6]`.
  * **Medication Considerations:** Weigh risks and benefits of medications with potential CNS effects `[ASA 2025:7]`.

### 5.1.5. POSTOPERATIVE COGNITIVE DECLINE

* **POCD/NCD Characteristics:**
  * Harder to diagnose than delirium, requiring neuropsychologic testing [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * A seminal study showed 10% incidence of POCD at 3 months in surgical patients vs. 3% in non-surgical controls [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * **Risk Factors for POCD at 3 months:** Age, lower education levels, prior stroke (even without residual deficit), POCD at hospital discharge [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Increased 1-year mortality associated with cognitive decline at both hospital discharge and 3 months postoperatively [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Short-term cognitive decline occurs in all age groups, with more impairment after general versus regional anesthesia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * By 3 months, POCD almost exclusively a problem in older patients, with no differences between anesthetic techniques [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * By 1 year, little or no demonstrable cognitive decline in most studies [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

### 5.1.6. MECHANISMS OF CNS COMPLICATIONS

* **Pathophysiological Theories:**
  * Animal studies show volatile anesthesia increases brain production of β-amyloid and abnormal τ-protein, associated with Alzheimer's disease and cell apoptosis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Other studies found memory impairment and CNS changes only with anesthesia plus surgery, not anesthesia alone [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Neuroinflammatory stress response to surgery and/or anesthesia may contribute [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Relationship between delirium and POCD not fully elucidated [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Predictors of delirium (e.g., hypoxemia) not found to be risk factors for POCD [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Postoperative delirium not found to be a risk factor for cognitive dysfunction at 3 months [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

## 5.2. CARDIOVASCULAR COMPLICATIONS 

**Cardiovascular complications are associated with high perioperative mortality in older patients.**

* **Risk and Incidence:**
  * Perioperative complications of myocardial infarction or cardiac arrest carry high associated mortality rates [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] (Table 34-5).
  * Incidence of these complications increases with age [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Anesthetic Sensitivity Indicators:**
  * "Triple low" state (low concentration of volatile anesthetic accompanied by low blood pressure and deep hypnotic level on BIS monitor) is associated with longer hospital stay and increased 1-month mortality [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * May be a marker of low physiologic reserve rather than causative [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Hypotension and Outcomes:**
  * Intraoperative hypotension associated with 1-month adverse outcomes including cardiac events and kidney dysfunction [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Age above 68 was an independent risk factor in one study [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Older subjects had higher 1-year mortality corresponding to duration of intraoperative hypotension [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Whether hypotension is causal or just a marker of vulnerability is unclear [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Cardiac Risk Management:**
  * Careful preoperative cardiac evaluation according to ACC/AHA algorithm [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Estimation of functional capacity in terms of metabolic equivalents (METs) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Balance between vasoconstriction and volume administration particularly important in elderly [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

## 5.3. PULMONARY COMPLICATIONS 

**Pulmonary complications contribute significantly to perioperative mortality in older patients.**

* **Incidence and Mortality:**
  * Higher incidences of pneumonia, prolonged intubation, and reintubation than cardiac complications [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Higher overall mortality resulting from pulmonary than cardiac complications despite lower mortality rate per event [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Highlights need for better understanding of postoperative pneumonia mechanisms, particularly silent aspiration [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Risk Factors:**
  * Age-related changes in pulmonary mechanics and control of breathing [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Higher risk for aspiration, atelectasis, and other complications even without pulmonary disease [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Residual neuromuscular blockade associated with adverse respiratory events; older patients at almost double the risk [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Prevention Strategies:**
  * Preoperative identification of pulmonary risk factors [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Appropriate intraoperative ventilation strategies (e.g., modest tidal volumes with PEEP) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Aspiration precautions and aggressive pulmonary hygiene postoperatively [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Appropriate neuromuscular blockade management and reversal [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

## 5.4. PAIN MANAGEMENT CONSIDERATIONS

**Analgesia is an important component of postoperative care but is made more difficult by the increased likelihood of adverse consequences in older patients.**

* **Pain Assessment Challenges:**
  * Elderly patients sometimes underreport pain levels [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * May be more tolerant of acute pain, perhaps due to existing chronic pain [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * More difficulty with visual analog scoring systems than verbal or numeric systems [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Cognitive impairment further complicates pain assessment and use of patient-controlled analgesia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Consequences of Inadequate Analgesia:**
  * Sleep deprivation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Respiratory impairment [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Ileus [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Suboptimal mobilization [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Insulin resistance [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Tachycardia and hypertension [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Opioid Considerations:**
  * Can produce many of the same adverse outcomes as inadequate analgesia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Meperidine should be avoided except perhaps in small doses for shivering [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * No superior choice among fentanyl, hydromorphone, and morphine [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Use requires careful judgment, caution, and frequent monitoring [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Multimodal Approaches:**
  * NSAIDs reduce opioid requirements but carry risks of renal damage and GI toxicity [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Gabapentinoids reduce opiate use and pain but increase adverse events and require dose adjustment for renal function [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Epidural analgesia provides superior analgesia to IV therapy in elderly [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Benefits include more rapid return of bowel function, earlier mobilization, and better nutritional status [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Customized pain management plan with multimodal approaches and regional techniques can lower mortality risk, reduce complications, and improve recovery [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

## 5.5. OTHER SYSTEM-SPECIFIC COMPLICATIONS 

**The older patient is at increased risk for various other complications in the perioperative period.**

* **General Risk Factors:**
  * Decreased organ system reserve due to aging process [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Interaction between aging and comorbid disease increases risk [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] (Fig. 34-14).
  * Greater risk for almost every perioperative complication [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] (Fig. 34-13).
* **Stroke:**
  * Incidence approximately 0.5% in older general surgical population [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Risk factors: age, atrial fibrillation (OR 2.0), prior stroke/TIA (OR ≥1.6), recent MI, COPD/smoking, renal dysfunction, certain surgeries, and comorbidities [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Elevated arterial pulse pressure is a risk factor, particularly for cardiac surgery patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Typically occurs well after surgery (average 7 days later), though up to half occur within 24 hours [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Most are ischemic, presumably from thromboembolism originating in heart or arteries [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Mortality after perioperative stroke approximately twice that of stroke in general population (at least 20%) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Renal Complications:**
  * Age-related decline in renal function increases vulnerability to acute kidney injury [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Cautious fluid management and avoidance of nephrotoxic drugs important [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Thermoregulatory Complications:**
  * Higher risk of hypothermia with associated risks of myocardial ischemia, wound infection, coagulopathy, increased blood loss, and impaired drug metabolism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
  * Active patient warming strategies required [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Multiorgan Complications:**
  * Compounding effects of individual organ system complications [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Increased likelihood of cascade effects where one complication leads to others [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Older patients face increased risk of nearly all perioperative complications. Perioperative neurocognitive disorders, cardiovascular and pulmonary complications, and pain management challenges require particular attention. A comprehensive, multidisciplinary approach to prevention and early intervention is essential to optimize outcomes in this vulnerable population.

# 6. SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS





## 6.1. ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS

> **Syllabus**: [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B: C5]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#3. Professionalism|Part C: Professionalism]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#6. Systems-Based Practice|Part C: SBP]]

**Planning for geriatric surgery has unique ethical considerations that should be addressed preoperatively.**

* **Informed Consent Issues:**
  * Older patients often recognize that end of life is no longer theoretical, so are more likely to have advance directives in place [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#6. Systems-Based Practice|ⓘ]].
  * Patient's expectations for surgery may differ from younger counterparts [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#6. Systems-Based Practice|ⓘ]].
  * Medical practitioners must avoid judging patient's decision-making based on practitioner's values or expectations [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#6. Systems-Based Practice|ⓘ]].
  * Risk-benefit discussions need to include probable degree and speed of functional recovery [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#6. Systems-Based Practice|ⓘ]].
  * Discussion of postoperative cognitive impairment is an important disclosure of risk [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#6. Systems-Based Practice|ⓘ]].
* **End-of-Life Planning:**
  * Almost one-third of elderly Medicare beneficiaries undergo surgery in their last year of life [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#6. Systems-Based Practice|ⓘ]].
  * Over 18% undergo surgery in the last month of their lives [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#6. Systems-Based Practice|ⓘ]].
  * If healthcare directives prohibit various life-sustaining or resuscitative procedures, mutual understanding must be reached about what will or will not be performed in the perioperative period [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#6. Systems-Based Practice|ⓘ]].
  * Discussion of goals/values can result in a "Limited Attempt at Resuscitation" agreement [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#6. Systems-Based Practice|ⓘ]].
* **Functional Outcome Concerns:**
  * Prospect of functional impairment may be more worrisome than death [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#6. Systems-Based Practice|ⓘ]].
  * Need for realistic expectations about recovery timeline and potential for incomplete recovery [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#6. Systems-Based Practice|ⓘ]].
  * Focus on whether interventions will improve quality of life rather than just extend it [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#6. Systems-Based Practice|ⓘ]].

## 6.2. FRAILTY ASSESSMENT TOOLS  

**Multiple tools exist to evaluate frailty, each with specific advantages for perioperative assessment.**

* **Common Frailty Assessment Tools (Table 34-1):**
  * **Clinical Frailty Scale:**
	* Takes <2 minutes to administer [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* Observational [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* Associated with discharge destination and mortality [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* Does not require extra space or equipment [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  
  * **Edmonton Frailty Scale:**
	* Takes <5 minutes to administer [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* Observational/patient-reported [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* Requires space, includes clock draw and timed get-up-and-go tests [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* Software application available [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* Can predict postoperative complications [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  
  * **ACS NSQIP Frailty Score:**
	* Takes 5-10 minutes to administer [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* Medical chart review [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* Limited to 30-day outcomes [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  
  * **Fried Phenotype:**
	* Takes 5-20 minutes to administer [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* Observational [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* Most widely studied [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* Requires patient activity and special equipment [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* Associated with postoperative complications, including delirium [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  
  * **Other Tools:**
	* FRAIL Scale (Fatigue, Resistance, Ambulation, Illness, and Loss) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* Risk Analysis Index [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* VESPA (short form) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* Frailty Index [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Implementation and Utilization:**
  * Raising awareness of positive frailty screening can improve outcomes [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * When identified, frail patients can be further assessed to guide targeted intervention [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Objective radiological methods for frailty identification (e.g., quadriceps depth ultrasound) show promise [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

## 6.3. POLYPHARMACY MANAGEMENT  

**Medication management is a critical component of geriatric perioperative care.**

* **Beers Criteria Medications:**
  * American Geriatrics Society Beers Criteria lists medications to avoid or use with caution in older adults due to unfavorable benefit-harm balance [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Common perianesthesia medications to avoid (Table 34-4) include:
	* **CNS medications:** certain antiemetics, antidepressants, first-generation antihistamines, antipsychotics, barbiturates, benzodiazepines, Z-drugs [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* **Pain medications:** meperidine, high-dose NSAIDs, especially indomethacin [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
	* **Skeletal muscle relaxants:** due to anticholinergic effects, sedation, and increased fracture risk [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Perioperative Medication Management:**
  * Take accurate and detailed medication history [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Consider appropriate perioperative adjustments [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Monitor for polypharmacy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Consider pharmacist referral for complex medication reviews [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Discontinue high-risk, infrequently used medications when appropriate [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Arrange primary care follow-up for more complex medication reviews [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Anesthetic Drug Considerations:**
  * Use of Beers Criteria medications for PONV prophylaxis (steroids, transdermal scopolamine, promethazine, prochlorperazine) presents potential cognitive impairment risks [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Meperidine should be avoided except perhaps in small doses for shivering [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Careful consideration needed for gabapentinoids due to serious concerns about adverse effects when combined with opiates in older patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].

## 6.4. PREHABILITATION AND REHABILITATION 

**Interventions before surgery can potentially improve outcomes for older surgical patients.**

* **Prehabilitation Concept:**
  * Multifaceted approach for optimization before surgery to protect functions and promote resiliency [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] (Fig. 34-11).
  * Appears superior to similar interventions provided after surgery, though patient adherence can be low [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Can have varying degrees of intensity based on baseline functionality, risk areas, and resources [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Most studies report enhancement of cardiopulmonary functional status, associated with lower severity and rate of complications [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Prehabilitation Components:**
  * **Exercise-based interventions:** Aerobic, resistance, flexibility, and balance training [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * **Lifestyle modifications:** Reduced alcohol consumption, smoking cessation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * **Nutritional interventions:** Protein/multivitamin supplementation, immunonutrition [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * **Behavioral health interventions:** Stress-coping mechanisms, relaxation, breathing exercises [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * **Cognitive exercises:** Video-game training showing benefits in multi-tasking, working memory, and sustained attention [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Outcomes and Cost-Effectiveness:**
  * Potential cost savings reported for patients having major abdominal surgery [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * A randomized pilot study combining preoperative cognitive training and physical exercise demonstrated feasibility of comprehensive program [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * May be particularly beneficial for frail patients, though this is an area of active investigation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] (Fig. 34-11).

**Bottom Line:** Caring for older surgical patients requires attention to unique ethical considerations, comprehensive frailty assessment, meticulous medication management, and consideration of prehabilitation interventions. These special considerations help optimize outcomes in this vulnerable population, with emphasis on functional preservation and alignment with patient goals of care.
