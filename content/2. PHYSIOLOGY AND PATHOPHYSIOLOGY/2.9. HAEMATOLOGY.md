# 1. PHYSIOLOGY OF HEMOSTASIS AND CLOTTING

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|Part A: Haemostasis & Coagulation]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|Part B: C4 Trauma & Resuscitation]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C: Medical Knowledge]]

## 1.1. OVERVIEW OF HEMOSTASIS

**Hemostasis** is a collective term for the mechanisms that stop blood loss, converting liquid blood to a solid gel through coagulation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].

* **Five main components to hemostasis:**
  * **Coagulation factors** – Produced primarily by the liver in an inactive form and are activated when coagulation is initiated (usually by tissue factor (TF), which is released by vessel injury) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
    * **Factor nomenclature**: Clotting factors are designated by Roman numerals (I-XIII), with factor VI being obsolete
    * **Vitamin K-dependent factors**: Factors II (prothrombin), VII, IX, and X require vitamin K for proper synthesis
    * **Contact factors**: Factors XI, XII, and XIII are involved in the contact activation pathway
    * **Labile factors**: Factors V and VIII have shorter half-lives and are heat-labile
  * **Coagulation inhibitors** – These either circulate in plasma or are bound to endothelium and are necessary to prevent widespread coagulation throughout the body once coagulation has been initiated [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
    * **Natural anticoagulants**: Include antithrombin III, protein C, protein S, and tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI)
    * **Endothelial-derived inhibitors**: Heparan sulfate, thrombomodulin, and endothelial protein C receptor (EPCR)
  * **Fibrinolysis** – This process limits fibrin deposition at the site of injury due to activity of the key enzyme plasmin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
    * **Plasminogen activators**: tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) and urokinase-type plasminogen activator (uPA)
    * **Fibrinolytic inhibitors**: Plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), α2-antiplasmin, and thrombin-activatable fibrinolysis inhibitor (TAFI)
  * **Platelets** – Vital for hemostasis as they aggregate at sites of vessel injury to form the primary hemostatic plug, which is then stabilized by fibrin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
    * **Normal platelet count**: 150,000-400,000/μL (150-400 × 10⁹/L)
    * **Platelet lifespan**: 7-10 days in circulation
    * **Platelet function**: Adhesion, activation, aggregation, and procoagulant activity
  * **Blood vessels** – Both initiate and limit coagulation. Intact vascular endothelium secretes prostaglandin I2 and nitric oxide (which promote vasodilation and inhibit platelet aggregation). Damaged endothelium releases TF and procoagulants (e.g., collagen and von Willebrand factor, vWF) and there are inhibitors of coagulation on the endothelial surface (thrombomodulin, antithrombin and protein S) to modulate coagulation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
    * **Endothelial function**: Maintains vascular tone, regulates per
* **Importance of balance**:
  * Hemostasis can be life-saving to prevent excessive blood loss when vessels are damaged [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * However, it must be controlled and localized to the area of vessel damage [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Widespread coagulation could prevent blood flow completely, damage red blood cells through microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, or lead to paradoxical bleeding through disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Endothelial role in hemostasis**:
  * Normally functions to prevent hemostasis and promote blood flow [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * When damaged, it rapidly initiates hemostatic response [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * It is intrinsically thromboresistant (procoagulant factors are not exposed)
  * Prevents hemostasis through:
	* Inhibition of platelet adhesion via nitric oxide (NO) and prostacyclin (PGI2) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	* Anticoagulant effects via membrane-bound proteins (heparan sulphate, thrombomodulin) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	* Fibrinolytic effects through secretion of tissue plasminogen activator (t-PA) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].

![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_Untitled.png_f8bf96.png_4fee99.png_37d96e.png)

**Bottom Line:** Hemostasis involves a complex balance between five key components: coagulation factors, inhibitors, fibrinolysis, platelets, and blood vessels, all working together to form clots at injury sites while preventing inappropriate widespread coagulation. The endothelium plays a crucial regulatory role, promoting blood flow in normal conditions but rapidly initiating hemostasis when damaged.

## 1.2. PRIMARY HEMOSTASIS

**Primary hemostasis** is the first phase of clot formation and involves the formation of a platelet plug to initially stop bleeding [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].

![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_Untitled_1.png_cf842e.png_8bef39.png_d92c80.png)

Primary hemostasis involves four key stages [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:

### 1.2.2. ADHERENCE
* **Disruption of endothelial lining** exposes underlying matrix (collagen) → Platelets attach to collagen via surface integrin receptors (i.e., GP Ia/IIa/VI) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Collagen adherence** is favored in low-shear conditions, i.e., venous circulation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* In **high shear arterial flow**, von Willebrand factor (vWF) from endothelial cells and from pre-existing clots bind to integrin Ib/IX → forms the other major adherence anchor [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* When a vessel is damaged, plasma becomes exposed to several substances [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * **von Willebrand factor (vWF)** – Endothelial cells are the main site of synthesis and storage of vWF. A damaged vessel releases a large amount of vWF which then binds platelets to subendothelial collagen fibers [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * **Collagen fibers** – Vessel damage exposes subendothelial collagen fibers. Platelets bind to collagen (through a bridging vWF molecule) and become activated [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * **Tissue factor (TF)** – Expressed by subendothelial cells but not normally by endothelial cells unless damaged [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].

### 1.2.3. ACTIVATION
* Numerous signaling pathways from the platelet surface mediate platelet activation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * **"Outside-in" signaling** (i.e., adherence integrins, GPCR activated by various agonists like ADP, TxA2, etc.) → activation of phospholipase C (PLC) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Activated PLC → via inositol-1,4,5-triphosphate (IP3) → Ca2+ released from storage tubules [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	* → Release of dense granules and α granules at platelet surface containing more agonists → activates more platelets [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	  * α granules contain numerous proteins including **platelet factor 4 (PF4)** - promotes clotting binding and neutralizing heparin-like compounds and heparin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	  * Heparin-PF4 complex - target antigen for antibodies causing heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	* → Rearrangement of platelet microskeleton → platelet shape change from round and discoid to flat and spiky [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	* → Activates phospholipase A2 (PLA2) → releases arachidonic acid (AA) from platelet membrane → catalyzed by COX1 and modified into TxA2 (agonist for more platelet activation) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* Platelets also have surface P-selectin, CD40 ligand (surface-bound and released), release of microparticles to attract and activate leukocytes - play a role in inflammation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* When platelets are activated, they change shape from disc-like to stellate and release a number of molecules from storage granules [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Serotonin (5-HT) and thromboxane A2 – potent vasoconstrictors that reduce blood flow at the site of injury [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Adenosine diphosphate (ADP) – causes more platelets to change shape and release chemicals from storage granules [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Additional factors – platelet activating factor (PAF), vWF, fibrinogen, thrombin, Ca2+ ions, and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].

### 1.2.4. STABILIZATION
* Activated PLC initiates "inside-out" signaling of GP IIb/IIIa via diacylglycerol (DAG) and protein kinase C (PKC) → changes shape of GP IIb/IIIa to better bind fibrin and vWF [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* Fibrin binding enmeshes the platelets → formation of platelet plug [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* Activated platelets exhibit glycoprotein IIb/IIIa receptors on their surface. Fibrinogen and vWF 'glue' platelets together through this receptor [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* More platelets become activated and bind to the site of injury, forming a soft platelet plug [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* The soft platelet plug is reinforced by fibrin (converted from fibrinogen by thrombin) to make a strong clot [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].

### 1.2.5. INHIBITION
* Platelets are naturally inhibited in the endothelial environment [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* Endothelial cells secrete:
  * **Prostaglandin I2 (PGI2)** which binds to surface receptor → increases cAMP (cyclic adenosine monophosphate) → activates protein kinase A (PKA) - multisite inhibitor of vWF adherence, TxA2 activation and PLC internal signaling [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	* cAMP is metabolized by PDE [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * **Nitric oxide (NO)** - high levels inhibit TxA2 receptor [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].

### 1.2.6. MECHANISMS OF ANTIPLATELET MEDICATIONS
* **Aspirin and triflusal** inhibit COX1 → reduced conversion of AA to TxA2 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * Aspirin is a non-specific, irreversible inhibitor of COX that prevents platelets from producing thromboxane A2 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * As platelets have no nucleus, COX remains inhibited for their entire lifespan (about 7 days) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Endothelial cells can produce new COX within hours, resulting in a net increase in platelet inhibition [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Clopidogrel, ticagrelor** (P2Y12 receptor antagonists) block ADP (an agonist) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * These drugs specifically block the platelet ADP receptor, preventing further platelet activation and inhibiting the expression of the glycoprotein IIb/IIIa complex [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Vorapaxar** blocks protease-activated receptor-1 (PAR 1) for thrombin activation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* **Abciximab, eptifibatide, tirofiban** block GP IIb/IIIa - preventing formation and stabilization of platelet plug [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * Inhibit platelet aggregation by preventing platelets from binding to fibrinogen [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Dipyridamole and cilostazol** upregulate endothelial PGI2 - increase inhibitory signals [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * Dipyridamole inhibits the platelet phosphodiesterase enzyme, increasing platelet cAMP and inhibiting ADP release and thromboxane A2 synthesis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].

![Thin arrows: signaling pathways. Thick arrows: ligand binding. Curved arrows: catalysis. Clear arrows: secretion. Slashed circles: inhibitory signaling pathways. Round circles: antiplatelet drug targets.](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_image.png_d8bbbf.png_ce3c7a.png_44cad7.png)

**Bottom Line:** Primary hemostasis consists of four phases: platelet adherence to exposed collagen, activation via multiple signaling pathways, stabilization through fibrin cross-linking, and regulation by endothelial inhibitors. Antiplatelet medications target specific pathways in this process to prevent inappropriate clotting. Understanding this process is essential for managing patients with bleeding disorders or those requiring antiplatelet therapy.

## 1.3. SECONDARY HEMOSTASIS

**Secondary hemostasis** involves the activation of clotting factors to reinforce the initial platelet plug with a fibrin mesh [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:

* **Clotting factors** activate at sites of endothelial injury → assemble into enzymatic complexes → activate thrombin → amplify production of itself → propagate a thrombin burst [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Thrombin** converts fibrinogen to fibrin → cross-links with platelets to form platelet plug [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* Each of the three enzymatic complexes in the clotting process consists of four parts [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * An enzyme in the serine protease family
  * A cofactor
  * A plasma membrane phospholipid surface such as the platelet
  * Calcium ion (Ca2+)
* All clotting factors are primarily produced in the liver, except for **Factor VIII**, which is also released by endothelial cells and is well maintained in liver disease [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* Plasma half-life is 1.5-3 days except **Factor VII** (6h) and cofactors V and VIII (8-12h) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* Factors **VII** (extrinsic tenase), **IX** (intrinsic tenase), **X** (prothrombinase) and **II** (prothrombin) must be carboxylated at multiple glutamic acid residues after translation to interact with phospholipid and Ca2+ [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Vitamin K in its reduced form is the cofactor for the glutamyl-carboxylase enzymes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Thus, factors VII, IX, X, II are vitamin K-dependent [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].

### 1.3.2. COAGULATION CASCADE
![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_Untitled_1.png_b57476.png_a0f22b.png_eb89e5.png)

![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_Untitled_2.png_0f72fd.png_bb3e4e.png_85c6cc.png)

![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_Coag.001.png_afb83c.png_6a3e08.png_4fd173.png)

* The coagulation cascade is a complex biochemical pathway involving plasma proteins that culminates in thrombin formation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* It is a biological amplification system where sequential activation of circulating coagulation proteins creates a magnified response [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* The extrinsic pathway acts as a 'trigger' in response to tissue damage and release of tissue factor [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* This produces enough factor VIIa, Xa and thrombin to then trigger the intrinsic pathway, which acts as an amplifier [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* This then produces the massive amounts of fibrin needed to maintain a stable clot [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].

### 1.3.3. EXTRINSIC PATHWAY
* **Endothelial disruption** exposes tissue factor (extrinsic to circulation) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Tissue Factor (TF)** binds Factor VII and VIIa (circulates at low levels, cofactor for activation of VII) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **VIIa enzyme + TF cofactor + cell membrane phospholipid + Ca2+** → the first complex, a low-efficiency extrinsic-pathway "tenase" that activates Factor X and Factor IX [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* Then **Xa enzyme + cofactor Va** (derived in large part from Factor V released from activated platelet α-granules) + phospholipid + Ca2+ → second complex, a "prothrombinase," which converts prothrombin **(II)** to thrombin (IIa) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* The extrinsic pathway is activated when blood vessel disruption exposes circulating clotting factors to subendothelial tissue factor [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* Any factor VII passing the site of injury is activated to factor VIIa, which then activates factor X, starting the final common pathway [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].

### 1.3.4. INTRINSIC PATHWAY
* **Thrombin** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Cleaves XI to XIa → XIa cleaves more IX to IXa
  * Activates VIII to VIIIa
	* Factor VIII is carried and stabilized in plasma by vWF until needed, so vWF deficiency results in low plasma VIII levels
  * Activates FXIIIa
* **FIXa + VIIIa cofactor + phospholipid + Ca2+** → high efficiency intrinsic-pathway "tenase," which provides many times more FXa for more prothrombinase complex [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* The intrinsic pathway is activated by contact with negatively charged substances like subendothelial collagen in vivo or glass in vitro [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* While the intrinsic pathway has only a minor haemostatic role in vivo, understanding it is important for interpreting laboratory coagulation tests and diagnosing specific clotting factor deficiencies [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].

### 1.3.5. COMMON PATHWAY
* **Thrombin** cleaves fibrinogen → fibrin monomers → polymerize and cross-linked by FXIIIa → stable fibrin clot [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* The final common pathway involves factor Xa (produced by either intrinsic or extrinsic pathway), which converts prothrombin (factor II) to thrombin (factor IIa) with cofactors (factor V, Ca2+, and platelet factor 3) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* Thrombin then hydrolyzes fibrinogen to produce insoluble fibrin strands [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* Thrombin also has other key roles [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Activation of factor XIII, forming covalent cross-bridges between fibrin strands
  * Creating a positive feedback loop by activating factors V and VIII
  * Activating protein C when complexed with thrombomodulin

### 1.3.6. INHIBITION OF CLOTTING FACTORS
![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_image_1.png_dbbdf8.png_6b6eaa.png_b40b89.png)

There are 3 main regulatory pathways [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:

1. **TF pathway inhibitor (TFPI)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
   * TFPI binds to VIIa protease and Xa product → inhibits external tenase complex
   * Produced by endothelial cells, release stimulated by heparin
   * Binds to and raises the inhibitory efficiency of TFPI

2. **Antithrombin III (AT-III)** - serine protease inhibitor / serpin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
   * Serpins disrupt the active sites → increases clearance of target proteases
   * Inhibits all proteases - VIIa (extrinsic tenase), Xa (prothrombinase), XIa and IXa (intrinsic tenase pathway) and thrombin
   * AT-III inhibitory function is greatly increased when bound to heparin

3. **Protein C-ase** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
   * Enzymatic complex with same 4-part structure as the coagulation complexes - enzyme, thrombin, its cofactor thrombomodulin, phospholipid, and Ca2+
   * Thrombomodulin on endothelial cell membranes
   * Thrombin cleaves and activates protein C → Activated protein C (APC)
   * APC cleaves VIIIa and Va (cofactors for external tenase and prothrombinase complexes)
   * Protein S is a cofactor for Protein C - both are vitamin K dependent
   * Protein C has short half-life of 6h

### 1.3.7. CELL-BASED MODEL OF COAGULATION
* The cell-based model of coagulation has been developed more recently to better represent in vivo haemostasis mechanisms, addressing limitations of the classical model [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* This model emphasizes the interaction between specific cell surfaces and clotting factors, with haemostasis occurring on cell surfaces [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* The cell-based model proposes three processes occurring on different cell surfaces [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * **Initiation phase** - Occurs on tissue factor-bearing cells exposed to circulation after vascular injury, generating a small amount of thrombin
  * **Amplification phase** - Further activation of clotting factors and platelets, preparing for large-scale thrombin generation
  * **Propagation phase** - Occurs on activated platelet surfaces loaded with activated clotting factors, catalyzing formation of large amounts of thrombin
* This model places thrombin at the center, involved in its own generation and regulation through feedback loops, as well as converting fibrinogen to fibrin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].

### 1.3.8. CLASSICAL AND CELL-BASED MODELS OF COAGULATION
**Classical Model** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]

* The classical model describes three elements: platelets, coagulation cascade, and fibrinolysis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* The first two elements prevent hemorrhage through thrombus formation, while fibrinolysis limits the process [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* The coagulation cascade consists of intrinsic and extrinsic pathways converging on the final common pathway [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* The intrinsic pathway is triggered by collagen exposure activating factor XII, while the extrinsic pathway is triggered by tissue factor release activating factor VII [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* Venous thrombi consist mainly of fibrin web with entrapped platelets and red cells, while arterial thrombi have a greater platelet component [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].

**Cell-Based Model** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]

* Developed to address limitations of the classical model in explaining in vivo hemostatic mechanisms [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* The cell-based model emphasizes interactions between specific cell surfaces and clotting factors [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* It proposes that hemostasis occurs on cell surfaces in three phases [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * **Initiation** - Tissue factor-bearing cells exposed to circulation generate thrombin [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
  * **Amplification** - Thrombin amplifies the procoagulant signal and activates platelets [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
  * **Propagation** - Large amounts of thrombin are formed on activated platelet surfaces, leading to fibrin formation and stable clot development [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
* This model explains clinical observations that the classical model couldn't account for, such as why factor XII deficiency doesn't increase bleeding tendency despite abnormal in vitro tests [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Secondary hemostasis involves intrinsic and extrinsic pathways converging on a common pathway to generate thrombin and ultimately a cross-linked fibrin clot. Three main regulatory systems (TFPI, antithrombin III, and protein C) provide critical control to prevent excessive coagulation. The modern cell-based model better explains in vivo coagulation by emphasizing the role of cell surfaces in the initiation, amplification, and propagation of the coagulation process.

## 1.4. FIBRINOLYSIS

### 1.4.2. FIBRINOLYTIC PATHWAY
![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_image_2.png_3444e2.png_9001fe.png_f616a9.png)

![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_Fibrinolysis.001.png_49eda8.png_c68cff.png_5acb00.png)

* **Fibrinolysis** is a physiological mechanism where fibrin within blood clots is slowly dissolved, a normal part of wound healing and important for keeping small vessels patent [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Plasminogen** is activated to plasmin → breaks down fibrin polymers by one of two main serine proteases [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Major activator of plasminogen**: tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) secreted by endothelial cells and platelets [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * tPA and plasminogen bind to lysine sites on fibrin → tPA becomes much more efficient when associated with cross-linked fibrin → plasmin forms → plasmin then cleaves tPA to a more active form [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * tPA directly cleaves fibrin polymers as well [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* In tissues, **urokinase** is the main plasminogen activator [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Urokinase-type plasminogen activator secreted by endothelium, monocytes, macrophages, urinary epithelium [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Urokinase cleaves plasminogen → plasmin also activates urokinase to more active form [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* Urokinase and tPA are used as medications to lyse thrombi [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* Key components of the fibrinolytic pathway [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * **Plasminogen** - a β-globulin proenzyme synthesized by the liver.
  * **Plasminogen** becomes interwoven into the fibrin clot as it forms.
  * **t-PA** - a physiological activator of plasminogen expressed by endothelial cells to keep their surface free of fibrin deposits.
  * **Fibrin degradation products (FDPs)** - produced when plasmin cleaves fibrin, include D-dimer, used to diagnose venous thromboembolic disease.
  * Fibrinolysis is much slower than coagulation, allowing healing to take place before clot dissolution.

### 1.4.3. INHIBITION OF FIBRINOLYSIS
* **Plasminogen activation inhibitor-1 (PAI-1)** - serpin binding to tPA and urokinase, accelerating their clearance from plasma [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Released in α-granules by activated platelets [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **PAI-2** is secreted by placenta [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Thrombin activated fibrinolysis inhibitor (TAFI)** is secreted by endothelial cells and activated by the thrombin-thrombomodulin complex [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * TAFI cleaves fibrin and fibrin polymers → inhibits action of tPA and blocks action of plasmin on fibrin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **α2-Antiplasmin** binds to plasmin and blocks its action, although this also slows the metabolism of plasmin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Antifibrinolytic medications** include epsilon-aminocaproic acid (EACA) and tranexamic acid (TXA), which stabilize clots [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * These drugs are lysine analogs that block the lysine-binding sites of plasminogen, preventing it from acting on fibrin [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].

### 1.4.4. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS
* **Manipulation of the fibrinolytic pathway** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * **Thrombolysis** - In life-threatening thrombosis (ST-elevation myocardial infarction, acute ischemic stroke, massive pulmonary embolism), thrombolytic drugs (alteplase, streptokinase) promote conversion of plasminogen to plasmin [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * **Inhibition of fibrinolysis** - Antifibrinolytic drugs (tranexamic acid) inhibit plasminogen activation and are used following hemorrhage, in trauma, orthopedic, spinal and cardiac surgery, and for menorrhagia treatment [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Fibrinolysis involves the conversion of plasminogen to plasmin, which breaks down fibrin clots. This process is regulated by several inhibitors including PAI-1, TAFI, and α2-antiplasmin. Antifibrinolytic medications like TXA are used clinically to inhibit this process and stabilize clots in bleeding patients, while thrombolytic agents promote clot dissolution in conditions like stroke and myocardial infarction.

# 2. ANTICOAGULATION PHARMACOLOGY

## 2.1. ANTIPLATELET AGENTS

### 2.1.2. CYCLOOXYGENASE INHIBITORS
* **COX1** - involved in gastric lining maintenance, renal blood flow, TxA2 formation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Inhibition → increased risk of bleeding [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **COX2** - synthesizes prostaglandin mediators of pain and inflammation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Aspirin** - noncompetitive and irreversible inhibitor of both COX enzymes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Effect lasts the lifetime of affected platelets and can only be reversed with platelet transfusion + DDAVP [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Commonly prescribed for arterial thrombosis risk, including myocardial infarction and stroke [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Reduces unstable angina progression to MI and mortality following acute MI; reduces stroke risk in transient ischemic attacks [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **NSAIDs** - competitive antagonists, duration of action depends on drug elimination time [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Non-selective NSAIDs - i.e., naprosyn, ibuprofen [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * COX2 selective - i.e., celecoxib - long-term intended outcomes of decreased GI bleeding were not sustained; there was increased cardiovascular risk (impaired vascular endothelial function) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
	* Unchecked inhibition of PGI2 without antagonizing the synthesis of TxA2 from COX1 → favors prothrombotic states [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	* American Heart Association recommends a stepwise approach to prescribing NSAIDs in patients with cardiovascular disease, emphasizing the use of nonselective or partially selective COX inhibitors first [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].
	* If selective COX-2 inhibitors (e.g., celecoxib) are needed, AHA recommends the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration of treatment in conjunction with a proton pump inhibitor and low-dose aspirin [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].

### 2.1.3. PHOSPHODIESTERASE INHIBITORS
* **PDEIs** - increase production of cAMP - an active inhibitor of platelet aggregation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Dipyridamole** - reversible ADP reuptake inhibitor [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Inhibits platelet adhesion to damaged vessel walls (more than platelet aggregation) by inhibiting adenosine uptake, potentiating prostacyclin effects, and at high doses inhibiting platelet phosphodiesterase activity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Used with limited success with warfarin to prevent thrombus formation on prosthetic valves; some evidence suggests combining with aspirin may further reduce stroke risk compared to aspirin alone [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Also a potent coronary artery vasodilator used in myocardial imaging with thallium-201 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* Others: caffeine, aminophylline, theophylline [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].

### 2.1.4. ADP RECEPTOR ANTAGONISTS
* **P2Y12 ADP receptor antagonists** - i.e., clopidogrel, prasugrel, ticagrelor - prevent expression of GP IIb/IIIa on surface of activated platelets → inhibiting platelet adhesion and aggregation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* Indicated in coronary artery disease (CAD), cerebrovascular disease (CVD) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Clopidogrel** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Class of thienopyridines - noncompetitive and irreversible antagonist [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Inactive prodrug requiring oxidation to active metabolite [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Genetic polymorphism discovered in some patients that results in inability to metabolize clopidogrel → FDA black box warning [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Direct measurement of P2Y12 ADP receptor now exists [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * If resistant to clopidogrel, convert to prasugrel or non-thienopyridines (i.e., ticagrelor) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Requires hepatic CYP450 for conversion to active form; many genetic variants of CYP2C19 lead to variable antiplatelet effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Omeprazole (often prescribed alongside clopidogrel) has CYP2C19 inhibitory effect, reducing conversion to active form [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Used to prevent atherothrombotic events in peripheral vascular disease; with aspirin for STEMI and NSTEMI; crucial part of post-coronary stent therapy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * 300 mg loading dose followed by 75 mg maintenance; effects seen within 2 hours, lasting 7 days due to irreversible binding [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Prasugrel** - irreversible [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Like clopidogrel, is a prodrug, but less susceptible to genetic CYP450 polymorphism and unaffected by CYP450 inhibitors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Licensed only for PCI in acute coronary syndrome; faster onset than clopidogrel, more effective but with more serious bleeding episodes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Ticagrelor** - reversible [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Binds to P2Y12 receptor → conformational change [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * No bioactivation required [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Metabolized by CYP3A4 with active metabolites [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].

### 2.1.5. GLYCOPROTEIN IIB/IIIA RECEPTOR ANTAGONISTS
* **Inhibit cross-linkage of fibrinogen** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Abciximab** - monoclonal antibody [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Tirofiban, Eptifibatide** - simulate binding site of fibrinogen [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* Monitor action with ACTs [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* Renally excreted, T1/2 20-40min except abciximab (t1/2 24-48h due to protein binding) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* ⚠️ All can cause thrombocytopenia - most prevalent with abciximab [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].

### 2.1.6. PROTEASE-ACTIVATED RECEPTOR (PAR)-1 ANTAGONIST
* **Vorapaxar** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Inhibits PAR-1 → antagonizes thrombin-induced platelet activation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Limited clinical utility; consider only in severe CAD/peripheral arterial disease (PAD) unresponsive to standard regimens of dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) and those with chronic kidney disease (CKD) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Antiplatelet agents work through various mechanisms to inhibit platelet function, including COX inhibition (aspirin, NSAIDs), phosphodiesterase inhibition (dipyridamole), ADP receptor antagonism (clopidogrel, prasugrel, ticagrelor), GP IIb/IIIa inhibition (abciximab, tirofiban), and PAR-1 antagonism (vorapaxar). Each agent has unique pharmacokinetics and safety considerations that must be balanced against bleeding risk in perioperative settings.

## 2.2. ANTICOAGULANTS

### 2.2.2. VITAMIN K ANTAGONISTS
* **Warfarin** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Declining use scenarios in view of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs). Currently used in specific circumstances including stroke prevention in patients with mechanical heart valves, mitral valve stenosis or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and for those with a history of antiphospholipid antibody syndrome [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * May be preferred in those with BMI >40 and CKD [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * **Mechanism of Action**: Competes with vitamin K for carboxylation-binding sites, inhibits synthesis of vitamin K-dependent clotting factors II, VII, IX, and X, as well as proteins C and S [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	* During initial treatment, patients may be hypercoagulable as protein C and S have shorter half-lives → hence require bridging if high risk for venous thromboembolism (VTE) until target INR is achieved [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * **Common uses** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
	* Atrial fibrillation to prevent systemic embolization (e.g., ischemic stroke)
	* Deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolus, including following lower limb arthroplasty
	* Metallic heart valves to prevent systemic embolization
  * **Reversal** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
	* **Non-urgent**:
		* INR > 5 with no signs/symptoms of bleeding: Hold warfarin for 1-2 doses [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
		* INR > 8 with no signs/symptoms of bleeding: PO vitamin K [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
		* High INR + nonmajor bleed: IV vitamin K 1-3mg - correction expected within 6-8h [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
	* **For surgery within 24-48h**:
		* INR 1.5-1.9: Hold warfarin, PO Vit K 1mg
		* INR 1.9-5: Hold warfarin, PO Vit K 1-2.5mg (PO 1-2mg if INR still elevated after 24h)
		* INR 5-9: PO Vit K 2.5-5mg (PO 1-2mg if INR still elevated after 24h)
	* ⚠️ **Emergency reversal**: 4-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) (containing factors II, VII, IX, X) 30-50u/kg + IV vitamin K 2-5mg(4F-PCC half-life is short) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
		* INR 1.5-1.9: PCC (30-50u/kg)
		* INR 1.9-5: PCC or FFP + 1-3mg IV Vit K
		* INR 5-9: PCC or FFP + 2-5mg IV Vit K
	  * Reversal with plasma (10-30ml/kg) is unreliable and requires large amounts [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
	  * Consequently, the British Committee for Standards in Haematology (BCSH), Society of Critical Care Medicine (SCCM), and Neurocritical Care Society recommend the use of plasma only when PCCs are not available, especially for patients with warfarin-related intracranial hemorrhage [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * Metabolized by liver CYP2 and P450 enzymes, and thus interacts with other medications including antibiotics, barbiturates, phenytoin, proton-pump inhibitors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * 99% protein bound, easily displaced by other highly protein-bound drugs leading to increased anticoagulant effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Genetic variations in P450 activity and foods/drugs co-metabolized by P450 cause variations in anticoagulant effect, requiring close monitoring through PT/INR testing [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Advantages: easily reversible in emergency (intracranial hemorrhage), safer in severe renal impairment, only oral anticoagulant licensed for metallic heart valves [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].

### 2.2.3. DIRECT ORAL ANTICOAGULANTS (DOACS)
* **Types** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * **Direct thrombin inhibitor (DTI)**: Dabigatran
  * **Factor Xa inhibitors**: Rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban
* **Characteristics** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Short half-life and rapid onset of action
  * No need for bridging
  * Reliable bioavailability and little interindividual variability
  * Dabigatran and rivaroxaban are renally excreted
  * Coagulation monitoring is unnecessary, although efficacy can be measured with diluted thrombin times or agent-specific anti-Xa assays
  * Few drug or dietary interactions
  * Wide therapeutic window
* **Evidence base**: The Re-Ly investigators, the ARISTOTLE group, and the EINSTEIN, ENGAGE-AF, and ROCKET-AF trials, all compared dabigatran, apixaban, rivaroxaban, and edoxaban to warfarin, in large, multicenter, randomized controlled trials [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* **Perioperative management**: Planning for discontinuing DOACs should be based on holding the specific agent 2 to 4 days prior to surgery depending on the bleeding risks and renal function [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* **Reversal** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * **Dabigatran** - Idarucizumab (proven in the RE-VERSE AD study) / Dialysis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * **Factor Xa inhibitors** have recently developed reversal agents. New recommendations from expert panels suggest the use of andexanet alfa for patients with major bleeding or to reverse the effects of DOACs before emergency invasive procedures [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
	* **Andexanet alfa**: Synthetic variant of Factor Xa which binds to Factor Xa inhibitors with high affinity [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
	* Some specific situations where andexanet alfa is not preferred: cardiac surgery patients who are expected to require heparinization therapy for cardiopulmonary bypass [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* **Perioperative timing**: Discontinue 48-72h before major surgery; half-life ~12h (>4 half-lives) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* Widely used for shortand long-term anticoagulation due to favorable pharmacokinetics with fixed dosing, decreased drug-drug interactions, and no monitoring requirements [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* Associated with less intracranial bleeding than warfarin, though may have higher gastrointestinal bleeding incidence [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* Not licensed for mechanical heart valves due to higher thrombotic event incidence compared to warfarin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].

### 2.2.4. HEPARIN AND DERIVATIVES
#### 2.2.4.1. UNFRACTIONATED HEPARIN (UFH)
* **Mechanism**: Indirect thrombin and FXa inhibition by binding and causing a conformational change to AT-III (increasing its activity) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Monitoring**: Clinical effects of heparin therapy are monitored with an anti-factor Xa assay, aPTT, or ACT [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Resistance**: Resistance to heparin is conferred by: hereditary insufficiency of AT-III, acquired deficiency from prolonged heparin administration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * AT-III can be replenished with plasma transfusion or administration of AT-III concentrate [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Pharmacokinetics** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Size: 3,000-30,000 daltons
  * 50% protein bound
  * Rapid onset
  * Half-life of 90 minutes
  * 40% bioavailability
  * Rapid breakdown
* **Monitoring** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]: APTT
* **Reversal** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]: Protamine sulphate:
  * 1mg of protamine inactivates 100 units of heparin, max dose is 50mg in 10 minutes
  * Side effects:
	* Anaphylaxis (fish allergy)
	* Myocardial depression
	* Bradycardia
	* Hypotension (caused by histamine release)
	* Pulmonary hypertension (caused by thromboxane release)
* **Uses** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * Thromboprophylaxis
  * Used in:
	* Cardiopulmonary bypass
	* ECMO
	* Cell salvage
	* Dialysis
  * Doesn't cross the placenta so can be used in pregnancy
* **Neuraxial blockade** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * Stop 4 hours before
  * Restart 4 hours after

#### 2.2.4.2. LOW MOLECULAR WEIGHT HEPARIN (LMWH)
* **Mechanism**: Fractionated form of heparin with similar mechanism of action, but more specific inhibition of FXa [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Examples**: Enoxaparin, dalteparin, reviparin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Advantages**: Laboratory monitoring is unnecessary unless obese or in patients with CKD (use FXa levels), and has longer half-life allowing once-daily/twice-daily dosing - preferred to UFH [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Reversal**: Reversal with protamine is unpredictable and not likely to completely resolve bleeding tendencies [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* **Pharmacokinetics** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Size: 4,000-6,500 daltons
  * Also binds antithrombin III but small molecule so only inhibits factor 10a
  * 10% protein bound
  * Half-life of 3-5 hours
  * Not rapidly degraded
* **Route** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]: Subcutaneous
* **Monitoring** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]: Not monitored routinely but can use anti-Xa activity
* **Uses** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]: Does not cross the placenta so can be used in pregnancy
* **Neuraxial blockade** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * Stop 12 hours before if prophylactic dose, 24 hours if treatment dose
* **Side effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]: Far less likely to cause HIT

#### 2.2.4.3. HEPARIN-INDUCED THROMBOCYTOPENIA (HIT)
* Associated with significant organ failure, vascular compromise, mortality, and thromboembolic complications [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Types** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * **HIT type 1** - mild thrombocytopenia, benign, no involvement of immune complexes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * **HIT type 2** - immune mediated, significant risk of thrombosis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
	* IgG antibodies bind to heparin-PF4 complexes on surface of platelets → primary hemostasis and thrombin generation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	* Usually 5-10 days after heparin therapy but can present immediately [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	* UFH more likely than LMWH (fractionated form less antigenic with weaker bond to heparin-PF4) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Diagnosis**: No specific diagnostic criteria. Guidelines from the American College of Chest Physicians and the national European guidelines recommend a diagnostic scoring algorithm, the 4T-score which determines the pretest probability of clinical HIT [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * Variables to consider include the degree of thrombocytopenia (defined as a fall in platelet count of 30% to 50%), the timing of platelet decrease (typically day 5 to 10 of heparin therapy), any thromboembolic complications, and the likelihood of alternative diagnoses [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * If immediate-high suspicion → ELISA for heparin-PF4 IgG antibodies is sensitive but not specific [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * Serotonin release functional assay is gold standard [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* **Treatment**: Therapeutic anticoagulation with IV DTIs (bivalirudin and argatroban) + discontinuation of all heparin therapy including catheters [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * ⚠️ Vitamin K antagonists are contraindicated as decreased protein C and S synthesis enhances patient's prothrombotic state [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * ⚠️ Warfarin also causes gangrenous thrombosis of limbs in patients with HIT [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * Hold platelet transfusion unless <20,000/µL with signs/symptoms of bleeding [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].

### 2.2.5. INDIRECT FACTOR XA ANTAGONISTS
* **Fondaparinux** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Highly specific for free FXa [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Acts by binding with AT-III [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Renal elimination - require renal dose adjustment [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * ⚠️ No antidote available [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * Given the expanding use of DOACs for anticoagulation and secondary prevention in VTE, the indications for fondaparinux are limited [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * It is considered appropriate for use in pregnancy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].

### 2.2.6. DIRECT THROMBIN INHIBITORS
* **Examples**: Argatroban and Bivalirudin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Characteristics** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Synthetic agents inhibiting thrombin in free and fibrin-bound states [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Not immunogenic, no risk of HIT [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * No known antidotes; reversal dependent on elimination [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
	* Argatroban - liver metabolism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	* Bivalirudin - renal clearance but can be used in renal dysfunction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Hence, bivalirudin is versatile and a good agent for use during cardiopulmonary bypass in patients with HIT [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * Monitored with ACT or aPTT [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	* Argatroban will prolong INR and aPTT [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Indicated for treatment of HIT [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Anticoagulants include vitamin K antagonists, DOACs, heparins, indirect Factor Xa antagonists, and direct thrombin inhibitors, each with distinct mechanisms of action and reversal strategies. Understanding their pharmacokinetics and perioperative management is essential for balancing bleeding risk against thrombotic risk. Specific reversal agents exist for some agents but not all, influencing clinical decision-making in emergent situations.

## 2.3. HEMOSTATIC AGENTS

### 2.3.2. RECOMBINANT ACTIVATED FACTOR VII (RFVIIA)
* **Indications** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * Hemophilia A or B (and their acquired forms) and Factor VII deficiency [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * Off-label use - prevention and treatment of coagulopathy and major blood loss in patients with postpartum hemorrhage, trauma, reversal of various anticoagulants, and high-risk cardiac surgery [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * Lack of consistent dosing protocols and definitive evidence leaves one without clear indications for the use of rFVIIa for critical bleeding [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* ⚠️ **Contraindications** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)
  * High thromboembolic risk
* **Mechanism** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * rFVIIa will only act with tissue factor (TF) exposed from the vascular endothelial lining [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * However, DIC/polytrauma can cause systemic release of TF + rFVIIa direct activation of FX and platelets → procoagulant activity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].

### 2.3.3. PROTHROMBIN COMPLEX CONCENTRATES (PCCS)
* **Indications** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * Hemophilia
  * Reversal of vitamin K antagonists
  * Reduce therapy of trauma-induced coagulopathy
  * Control of critical bleeding and reversal of DOAC (evidence limited and off-label use) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * Preferred to plasma + rFVIIa for reversal of warfarin as [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
	* Correct coagulopathy faster (than time to thaw and administer plasma) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
	* But their action still depends on adequate concentrations of platelets and fibrinogen [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
	* PCCs are treated with at least one viral reduction process, whereas plasma (in large volumes) carries significant risk of infectious and noninfectious transfusion reactions [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* **Formulations** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Available formulations of PCC may have varying number (3-4) coagulation factors - Vitamin K dependent factors (II, VII, IX and X) but not all products contain significant concentrations of FVII [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Some may be 3-factor (lacking FVII), most are 5-factor [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Natural anticoagulant may be inside - heparin or AT to decrease thrombogenic risks [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Specialized Formulations** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Specific activated PCCs - i.e., Factor Eight Inhibitor Bypass Agent (FEIBA) contains activated FVIIa and FXa [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * FEIBA indications: bypass therapy in hemophilia patients with inhibitors, reversal of direct antifactor Xa antagonists [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* ⚠️ **Thrombotic Risk** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * Has risk for thrombotic complications [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * Thrombosis pathogenesis is unclear - possibly due to accumulation of prothrombin, inactive FII and FX [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * Minimize risk by avoiding repeat dosing + coadministration of vitamin K [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
	* Factors within PCC have varied half-life - prothrombin 60h, FX 30h in plasma; FVII half-life 6h [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
	* Repeat dosing will cause accumulation of FII and FX [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
	* Vitamin K increases synthesis of coagulation factors [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * PCC dosing to be guided by appropriate coagulation test - thromboelastography (TEG) preferred (consider before redosing) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
	* PT/INR - procoagulant half of hemostatic mechanism (ignores presence of anticoagulant activity i.e., heparin, antithrombin, proteins C and S) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
	* In patients with severe liver disease or dilutional coagulopathy, the PT and INR times may be prolonged, but the concentrations of anticoagulants are also decreased, leaving hemostasis balanced [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* ⚠️ **Contraindications** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * DIC
  * Active HIT

### 2.3.4. DESMOPRESSIN
* **Characteristics** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Synthetic analog of antidiuretic hormone (ADH), vasopressin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Acts on V2 receptors in nephron and endothelial cells - has effect on hemostasis and platelet function [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Indications** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * Diabetes insipidus
  * Mild bleeding in von Willebrand disease and mild hemophilia A
  * Reduced bleeding times in select patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * 💡 The only consistent clinical benefit of DDAVP is improved bleeding times for patients with congenital or acquired platelet dysfunction from cardiopulmonary bypass, chronic renal failure, or aspirin therapy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* **Mechanism of Action**: Releases FVIII and vWF from vascular endothelial cells → improves platelet function [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Dose for hemostasis**: 0.3 μg/kg intravenously over 20 to 30 minutes, with therapeutic effects lasting for approximately 6 to 8 hours [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* **Side effects** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * Hypotension - arterial vasodilation from release of NO - slow infusion rate [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * Hyponatremia and water retention are rare [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* ⚠️ **Contraindications**: Avoid in high thromboembolic risk patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].

### 2.3.5. ANTIFIBRINOLYTIC THERAPY
* **Types**:
  * **Lysine analogs** - epsilon-aminocaproic acid (EACA), tranexamic acid (TXA).
  * **Serine protease inhibitor** - aprotinin - superior efficacy but removed from markets for relative risk of renal failure, myocardial infarction and death (BART trial).
	* Aprotinin prevents actions of several proteins - trypsin, plasmin and kallikrein.

#### 2.3.5.1. LYSINE ANALOGS
* **Characteristics**:
  * EACA and TXA - synthetic derivatives of lysine.
  * Lysine analogs are currently used as part of multimodal approach to perioperative blood conservation strategies [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* **Mechanism of Action**: Competitive inhibition of binding site on plasminogen → prevents cleavage to plasmin and fibrinolysis.
* **Evidence**: EACA and TXA have equivalent efficacy (cardiac surgery, liver transplant, orthopedic, spine fusions, trauma - CRASH II, traumatic brain injury - CRASH III, postpartum hemorrhage - WOMAN) but TXA has more evidence [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* **Side Effects**: Only documented adverse effect is seizure risk associated with TXA at high doses [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* Tranexamic acid is commonly used in trauma, orthopedic, spinal, and cardiac surgery and for treatment of menorrhagia.

**Bottom Line:** Hemostatic agents such as rFVIIa, PCCs, desmopressin, and antifibrinolytics each play specific roles in managing bleeding. Each has a unique mechanism of action, indications, and contraindications. Understanding when to use these agents, their potential side effects, and proper dosing is crucial for effective management of bleeding in various clinical scenarios.

  * Signs and symptoms of heart failure, anemia, psychosocial impact [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]

### 3.1.4. LABORATORY INVESTIGATIONS
* **Initial Tests** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Full blood count (FBC), peripheral blood film (PBF) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
  * Prothrombin time (PT) - Factors 2, 5, 7, 10 - extrinsic pathway - ~12-15s [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
  * Activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) - Factors 2, 5, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 - intrinsic pathway - ~25-35s [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
  * Plasma mixing studies - to determine if an inhibitor or anticoagulant is present (no correction with mixing) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
  * Quantitative fibrinogen assay - normal 150-400mg/dL [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
  * Thrombin time - tests for deficiency or dysfunction of fibrinogen [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
  * D-dimers [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
  * Biochemical screen - renal and liver function tests [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
* **Specialized Tests** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * von Willebrand disease screen (antigen and activity), qualitative platelet function (platelet aggregation testing) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
	* Platelet aggregation testing using platelet-rich plasma is the gold standard for platelet function - platelets are tested with multiple isolated agonists including ADP, arachidonic acid, collagen and epinephrine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
  * 💡 'Bleeding time' is no longer used to investigate platelet disorders, as it is unreliable [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	* Replaced by in vitro tests of platelet function on a platelet function analyzer, which can be performed on a peripheral blood sample [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
* **Special Considerations** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * **Neonates**: In the neonate, the levels of all clotting factors **except factor VIII (FVIII) and fibrinogen** are lower; preterm infants have even lower levels. Therefore the results have to be compared with normal values in infants of a similar gestational and postnatal age [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Anticoagulant Monitoring** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * **Warfarin testing** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
	* Monitored with INR (PT) - for deficiencies in Factors II, VII, IX, X [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
	* When warfarin is started or stopped, the factors with the fastest plasma turnover (i.e., the shortest half-lives) decline or rise the fastest, respectively [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	  * Thus, the inhibitor protein C, with a 6-hour half-life, declines faster than most clotting factors as warfarin takes effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	  * → Imbalance toward clotting during the initiation of warfarin therapy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
	  * → Warfarin-induced skin necrosis is a thrombotic complication often occurring when previously unrecognized congenital protein C deficiency accentuates this imbalance [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	* Affected by genetic variations in metabolism (P450, CYP2C9) and vitamin K (vitamin K epoxide reductase complex subunit 1 - VKORC1) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
  * **Heparin testing** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
	* aPTT - dependent on therapeutic target range [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
	* Functional enzyme test for heparin - antifactor Xa activity (not FX activity level) - Using the aFXa assay, therapeutic target heparin levels of 0.3 to 0.7 aFXa units/mL are correlated with aPTT results for that range [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	* Low–molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) drugs and their analog, synthetic pentasaccharide (e.g., fondaparinux), do not affect the aPTT assay, and coagulation testing is usually not needed. However, if necessary, the drugs' plasma activity levels can be assessed by aFXa assays calibrated for each drug [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	* **HIT testing** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
	  * Clinical suspicion for HIT should arise with thrombocytopenia or thrombosis after the initiation of heparin therapy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	  * Diagnosis should be based on the clinical criteria established with the "4T-score system" which includes thrombocytopenia, timing of platelet count decline, evidence of thrombosis, and influence of other factors that may contribute to thrombocytopenia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	  * If suspected, the ELISA immunoassay and/or functional assays such as the serotonin release assay, should be ordered to identify heparin-PF4 antibodies [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
  * **Direct-acting anticoagulant testing (DOAC)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
	* E.g., dabigatran - direct thrombin inhibitor; "xaban" drugs - direct Factor Xa inhibitors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
	* Monitoring typically not required; PT is not reliable indicator of drug effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
	* **Dabigatran** - dTT or Etc coagulation assays [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
	  * Ecarin (in Etc) is an enzyme found in snake venom that cleaves prothrombin to a metabolic intermediate - this is inhibited by hirudin and its analogs [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
	  * But can be confounded by other molecules - including hirudin from leeches, its recombinant "-rudin" mimicking molecules (intravenous bivalirudin, desirudin), and small synthetic molecules acting at the same site as hirudin on thrombin (intravenous argatroban, oral dabigatran) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	* **Factor Xa inhibitors** - aFXa assays calibrated for each specific drug may be used or quantitative plasma drug concentrations [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** A comprehensive approach to suspected bleeding disorders includes detailed history focusing on bleeding patterns, thorough physical examination, and targeted laboratory investigations. Initial tests include FBC, PT, APTT, and fibrinogen, with specialized tests based on clinical suspicion. Specific testing methods exist for monitoring anticoagulant therapy, with different approaches required depending on the agent.

### 3.2.2. VESSEL WALL ABNORMALITIES
#### 3.2.2.1. HEREDITARY HEMORRHAGIC TELANGIECTASIA (HHT)
* **Definition** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Autosomal dominant mutations in genes encoding endoglin and activin receptor-like kinase - endothelial cell receptors for TGF-beta - potent angiogenic cytokine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Clinical Features** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * Presents with [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
	* Recurrent bleeds - epistaxis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
	* Iron deficiency anemia due to occult gastrointestinal bleeding [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
	* Telangiectasia and small aneurysms are found on the fingertips, face and tongue, and in the nasal passages, lung and gastrointestinal tract [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
	* Significantly, larger pulmonary arteriovenous malformations (PAVMs) → arterial hypoxemia due to a right-to-left shunt → predisposition to paradoxical embolism → stroke or cerebral abscess [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
* **Investigations** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * Screened for PAVMs → ablation by percutaneous embolization should be considered [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* **Management** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * Treatment can be difficult because of the multiple bleeding points [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
	* Regular iron therapy often allows the marrow to compensate for blood loss [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
	* Local cautery or laser therapy may prevent single lesions from bleeding [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
	* A variety of medical therapies have been tried but none has been found to be universally effective [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]

#### 3.2.2.2. EHLERS-DANLOS SYNDROME TYPE 4 (EDS)
* **Definition** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Vascular EDS (type 4) - rare autosomal dominant disorder - defect in type 3 collagen → fragile blood vessels and organ membranes → bleeding and organ rupture [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Clinical Features** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * Classical joint hypermobility is often limited in this form of the disease but skin changes and facial appearance are typical [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * The diagnosis should be considered when there is a history of bleeding with normal laboratory tests [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].

#### 3.2.2.3. SCURVY
* **Definition** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Vitamin C deficiency affects the normal synthesis of collagen and results in a bleeding disorder characterized by perifollicular and petechial hemorrhage, bruising and subperiosteal bleeding [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Ask for dietary history [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].

### 3.2.3. PLATELET FUNCTION DISORDERS
#### 3.2.3.1. ACQUIRED PLATELET DISORDERS
* **Causes**:
  * **Iatrogenic**: Aspirin, clopidogrel, ticagrelor, dipyridamole, glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors.
  * **Chronic liver disease**:
	* → Quantitative defect via hypersplenism
	* → Qualitative defect too
  * **Cardiopulmonary bypass**:
	* **Qualitative effect**: Numerous factors, including:
	  * Interaction of platelets with the non-physiologic surface components of the bypass machine.
		* Platelets are activated, their granular contents are secreted, and their adhesive properties are compromised due to alteration in surface glycoprotein expression.
	  * Hypothermia during bypass, complement activation, release of cytokines, and thrombin generation may also contribute.
	* **Quantitative effect**:
	  * Hemodilution from the priming of the extracorporeal circuit prior to bypass.
	  * Adherence of platelets to the artificial surfaces of the bypass machine.
  * **Uremia from chronic renal failure**.
  * **Dysproteinemia** - multiple myeloma or Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia:
	* Can affect all stages of platelet function including adherence, activation, aggregation, and procoagulant activity.
	* However, most bleeding complications are due to the effects of hyperviscosity or acquired von Willebrand disease.
  * **Myeloproliferative disorders**.
  * **Diabetes mellitus** (increased platelet reactivity):
	* Characterized by dysregulation of several signaling pathways, both receptor (e.g., increased expression) and intracellular downstream signaling abnormalities, leading to **increased** platelet reactivity, with intensified adhesion, activation, and aggregation.
	* → May play a role in the higher risk of developing acute coronary syndrome, as well as in the larger proportion of diabetic patients with inadequate response to antiplatelet agents (e.g., clopidogrel, aspirin) compared with non-diabetic patients.
  * **Trauma**.
  * **Acquired Glanzmann thrombasthenia**:
	* Usually associated with alloor auto-antibodies to the platelet integrin αIIbβ3 (integrin alphaIIbbeta3; previously known as GPIIb/IIIa).
	* Associated with pregnancy, autoimmune conditions (e.g., systemic lupus erythematosus, immune thrombocytopenia), and the use of therapeutic antagonists of integrin αIIbβ3 (e.g., abciximab, eptifibatide).

#### 3.2.3.2. INHERITED PLATELET DISORDERS (RARE)
* **Giant platelet disorders**:
  * Glycoprotein abnormality - Bernard-Soulier syndrome
  * Deficiency of platelet alpha granule - Gray platelet syndrome
  * May-Hegglin anomaly
  * Montreal platelet syndrome (type 2B von Willebrand disease)
* **Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome** (microthrombocytes)
* **Storage pool disorders** - dense granule deficiencies:
  * Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome
  * Chediak-Higashi syndrome
  * Hermansky-Pudlak syndrome
  * Thrombocytopenia-absent radius syndrome
* **Glanzmann thrombasthenia**:
  * Autosomal recessive bleeding disorder characterized by a defect in the platelet integrin αIIbβ3 (integrin alphaIIbbeta3; previously known as glycoprotein [GP]IIb/IIIa)
  * May occur together with leukocyte adhesion deficiency III
* **Platelet release disorders** - too specialized for general labs
* **Glycoprotein VI defects**
* **Sticky platelet syndrome**:
  * Autosomal dominant platelet disorder associated with arterial and venous thromboembolic events
* **Others**:
  * Deficiency in ADP receptor P2Y12
  * Scott syndrome
  * Various other rare disorders

* Most are mild - except Glanzmann's thrombasthenia

#### 3.2.3.3. MANAGEMENT OF PLATELET FUNCTION DISORDERS
* **Local mechanical measures** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
* **Antifibrinolytics**: Tranexamic acid may be useful [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]

### 3.2.4. VON WILLEBRAND DISEASE
* Most common hereditary bleeding disorder

#### 3.2.4.1. FUNCTION
* vWF is produced in endothelial cells and megakaryocytes, and functions in primary platelet adhesion and aggregation at the site of vascular injury through interaction with the GP Ib receptor on the platelet surface.
* Once exposed, vWF facilitates the interaction of platelets with collagen within the subendothelium as well as platelet–platelet interactions. Both of these result in the initial platelet plug and subsequent platelet activation.
* vWF circulates as a complex with FVIII, providing stability to the otherwise labile clotting factor until vWF binds to activated platelets via the GP IIb/IIIa receptor. This localizes FVIII to the site of injury.

#### 3.2.4.2. CLASSIFICATION
* **Quantitative** (Types 1 and 3):
  * Type 1 - most common (60-80%)
* **Qualitative** (Type 2)

![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_Untitled_4.png_dc724a.png_b9e533.png_5a853e.png)

* Types 2A and 2M result in deficiencies in platelet adhesion and decreased activity of vWF relative to the factor levels within the plasma.
* Type 2B involves increased affinity of vWF for the GP Ib receptor on the platelet surface → spontaneous binding → increasing cleavage and clearance of vWF (may be associated with thrombocytopenia).
* Type 2N is characterized by decreased affinity for FVIII and deficiencies in secondary hemostasis.

#### 3.2.4.3. PATHOLOGY
* Synthesized by endothelial cells and megakaryocytes - involved in both platelet function and coagulation → normal multimeric structure:
  * Interacts with subendothelial collagen and platelets - for platelet adhesion
  * Also carrier protein for Factor VIII - non-covalently bound - deficiency in von Willebrand disease → Factor VIII deficiency
* Gene on chromosome 12 - autosomal dominant usually, except type 2N and 3 - autosomal recessive:
  * Variable penetrance
* Blood group antigens (A and B) are expressed on vWF, reducing its susceptibility to proteolysis; as a result, people with blood group O have lower circulating vWF levels than individuals with non-O groups. This needs to be borne in mind when making a diagnosis of von Willebrand disease.

#### 3.2.4.4. CLINICAL FEATURES
* Hemorrhagic manifestations similar to individuals with reduced platelet function [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* Superficial bruising, epistaxis, menorrhagia, gastrointestinal hemorrhage common [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* Less frequent bleeding episodes than severe hemophilia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* Excessive bleeding only after trauma or surgery [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].

#### 3.2.4.5. INVESTIGATIONS
* Traditional coagulation profile tests such as PT and aPTT are often normal in patients with vWD.
* Reduced activity of vWF and Factor VIII.
* Classified according to combination of assays: functional and antigenic measures of vWF, multimeric analysis of protein, and specific tests of function (determine binding to platelet glycoprotein Ib - ristocetin-induced platelet aggregation [RIPA] and factor VIII).

#### 3.2.4.6. DIAGNOSTIC CRITERIA
* History of mucosal bleeding, prolonged bleeding after dental, surgical procedures or postpartum hemorrhage [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* Family history of bleeding disorders [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* Reduced activity of vWF - various assays designed to test platelet adhesion, aggregation, and levels of vWF or FVIII complexes (e.g., vWF:factor antigen, vWF:ristocetin cofactor activity, vWF:collagen-binding activity, and vWF:GP Ib binding activity) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].

#### 3.2.4.7. MANAGEMENT
* Avoid intramuscular injections, aspirin, NSAIDs [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* Most (Type 1) - local means or with desmopressin [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * DDAVP promotes the cleavage of vWF from FVIII and increases the availability of both [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * ⚠️ DDAVP should be used with caution in children less than 1 year of age as it can cause hyponatremia due to water retention and may cause seizures, particularly after repeated doses, and if fluid intake is not strictly regulated [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * ⚠️ **Young children and patients with severe arterial disease** should not receive desmopressin, and patients with **type 2B** disease develop **thrombocytopenia** that may be troublesome following desmopressin [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* If mucosal bleeding - tranexamic acid [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* If serious - DDAVP may not be therapeutic by itself for patients with type 3 and severely depressed levels of vWF [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * Often these patients require treatment with additional hemostatic medications such as antifibrinolytics and/or factor replacement with plasma-derived vWF/FVIII concentrates (Heamate P/Humate P) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * Cryoprecipitate is no longer used to treat vWD as it has not undergone viral inactivation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].

### 3.2.5. THROMBOCYTOPENIA
#### 3.2.5.1. DEFINITION
* Thrombocytopenia is a platelet count less than 150 × 10^9/L. The risk of bleeding depends on the level of the platelet count:
  * **Severe thrombocytopenia** (platelets <20 × 10^9/L) – risk of spontaneous bleeding.
  * **Moderate thrombocytopenia** (platelets 20–50 × 10^9/L) – at risk of excess bleeding during operations or trauma but low risk of spontaneous bleeding.
  * **Mild thrombocytopenia** (platelets 50–150 × 10^9/L) – low risk of bleeding unless there is a major operation or severe trauma.

#### 3.2.5.2. CAUSES
* First exclude spurious causes - send for **peripheral blood film (PBF)**:
  * Spurious (look for fibrin strands in PBF): platelet clumping, inadequate anticoagulation, difficult venepuncture.

![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_Untitled_6.png_ac44c9.png_e14cf0.png_e586e0.png)

![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_Untitled_7.png_6fb944.png_e25a16.png_a4ab67.png)

#### 3.2.5.3. IDIOPATHIC THROMBOCYTOPENIC PURPURA (ITP)
* **Pathophysiology**:
  * Immune-mediated with involvement of autoantibodies - most often directed against glycoprotein IIb/IIIa (sensitizes the platelet → premature removal from circulation by cells of the reticuloendothelial system).
  * Not a single disorder - may occur with underlying immune dysregulation in connective tissue disorders, HIV, B-cell malignancies, pregnancy, drug therapies.
* **Epidemiology**:
  * 4 per 100,000 children/year
  * Presenting between age 2-10 years with onset 1-2 weeks after viral infection
* **Disease onset**: Can be sudden.
* **Triggers**: Post-viral, trauma, idiopathic.

##### 3.2.5.3.1. CLINICAL FEATURES
* Depends on platelet count [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * <20 × 10^9/L - spontaneous bleeding [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
	* ⚠️ Intracranial bleeding is a serious but rare complication, occurring in 0.1% to 0.5%, mainly in those with a long period of severe thrombocytopenia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * Otherwise, easy bruising, epistaxis, menorrhagia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
  * Otherwise, incidental low platelet count [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
* Women more common than men, insidious onset, unusual to have preceding viral infection in adults unlike children [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* May have symptoms or signs of connective tissue disease [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].

##### 3.2.5.3.2. SECONDARY ITP
* These inciting events can be categorized broadly into two main mechanisms: infections (typically viral) and systemic conditions that disrupt immune homeostasis (e.g., autoimmune disease, lymphoid malignancy) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].

![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_Untitled_8.png_6fda9e.png_acb6bb.png_c7a9da.png)

##### 3.2.5.3.3. INVESTIGATIONS
* FBC with differential count and reticulocytes
* PT/APTT
* PBF - normal apart from greatly reduced platelet number
  * In the younger child, a congenital cause (such as Wiskott–Aldrich or Bernard–Soulier Syndromes) should be considered.
* HIV testing
* If >65 years old:
  * Do bone marrow examination - look for B-cell malignancy
  * Do appropriate autoantibody testing if connective tissue disease likely
* Bone marrow examination shows obvious increase in megakaryocytes:
  * Any atypical clinical features, such as the presence of anemia, neutropenia, hepatosplenomegaly, or marked lymphadenopathy, should prompt a bone marrow examination to exclude acute leukemia or aplastic anemia.
  * Should also be performed if child is going to be treated with steroids - masks diagnosis of acute lymphoblastic lymphoma.
  * ⚠️ Inadvertent steroid therapy in undiagnosed acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) mimicking ITP is likely to delay effective treatment of the leukemia and reduce the chance of cure of such patients.
  * If the clinical features are characteristic, with no abnormality in the blood other than a low platelet count and no intention to treat, there is no need to examine the bone marrow.

##### 3.2.5.3.6. MANAGEMENT
![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_F5B8D6CE-7587-4843-9588-F0628E89685F.jpeg_0d6e0b.jpeg_03b30b.jpeg_9d2bcd.jpeg)

![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_Untitled_11.png_387620.png_17bf5e.png_736617.png)

* **General measures** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * Advise to avoid physical activities, NSAIDs, intramuscular injections, complete rest in bed [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* **Mild/Stable cases** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * Stable compensated ITP and platelet >30 × 10^9/L - no treatment required unless undergoing surgery or biopsy (increased bleeding risk); reassurance if no significant bleeding [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* **Treatment for significant bleeding** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * **Spontaneous bleeders**: High-dose glucocorticoids - prednisolone (1-2mg/kg OD for 2 weeks then rapidly taper dose over 1 week) or dexamethasone (40mg OD for 4 days) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
	* ⚠️ If there is reason to suspect hematological malignancy (e.g., leukemia), do NOT start steroids unless a bone marrow aspiration has been done [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * **Severe hemostatic failure** (especially if there is significant mucosal bleeding or slow response to glucocorticoids alone) - intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) + glucocorticoids [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
	* **IVIG**: Small risk of allergic reactions and transmission of blood products [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
	* **Anti-D** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
	  * Suitable alternative to IVIG in patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
	  * Carries lower risk of infusion-related side effects [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
	  * ⚠️ **Contraindicated in patients with** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
		* Rh-negative blood type [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
		* Splenectomy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
		* Renal abnormalities [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
		* Liver cirrhosis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
		* Acute febrile illness [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
	* **Adjuncts** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
	  * Tranexamic acid stabilizes bleeding [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
		* ⚠️ Do NOT use in hematuria [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
* ⚠️ **Platelet transfusion is ineffective** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * Only reserved for acute life-threatening hemorrhages (e.g., intracranial hemorrhage) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
  * Platelet administration may potentially worsen thrombocytopenia beyond short-term as it aggravates ongoing immune destruction [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
* **Chronic ITP management** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * Condition can become chronic - relapses and remissions [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
  * Chronic ITP in 10% of children, the platelet count remains low **6 months** after diagnosis; this is known as chronic ITP [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
	* 90% recovery completely [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
	* ⚠️ **Risks of dangerous/life-threatening bleeding is <1%** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
	* In the majority of children, treatment is mainly supportive; drug treatment is only offered to children with chronic persistent bleeding that affects daily activities or impairs quality of life [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
	* Children with significant bleeding are rare and require specialist care. A variety of treatment modalities are available, including **rituximab**, a monoclonal antibody directed against B lymphocytes [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
	  * Typical dose: IV 375 mg/m^2 weekly for 4 weeks [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
	  * Mild, transient side effects, especially with first transfusion: urticarial rash, headache, fever [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
	  * Can administer glucocorticoid at the same time to minimize adverse reactions [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
	* Newer agents (second-line therapies) such as thrombopoietic growth factors **(TPO-RA eltrombopag and romiplostim)** have shown clinical response in adults and may be used in children with severe nonresponsive disease [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
	* **Splenectomy** can be effective for this group but is **mainly reserved for children who fail drug therapy** as it significantly increases the risk of infection and patients require lifelong antibiotic prophylaxis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
	  * Reduces thrombocytopenia in 70% [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
	  * Because of **hypersplenism** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
	* Regular screening for systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) should be performed, as the thrombocytopenia may predate the development of autoantibodies [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].

##### 3.2.5.3.7. ADVERSE EFFECTS OF IVIG
* **Common side effects** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * Fever [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
  * Rash [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
  * Headache [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
  * Nausea and vomiting [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
  * Tiredness [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
  * Swelling or pain and redness of the legs [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
* **Serious side effects** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * Breathlessness [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
  * Dark urine, yellow eyes [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
  * Reduced urination or sudden weight gain [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
* **Risks** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * Small risk of blood-borne infections [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
  * In the past, a small number of people who received IVIG became ill with hepatitis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
	* There have been no documented cases of the transmission of the virus that causes AIDS (HIV) through IVIG [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * There is also a small chance of a severe allergic reaction to IVIG [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
	* If that happens, IVIG will be stopped [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
	* Medications to treat allergic reactions will be given immediately [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * There is also a small risk of meningitis (inflammation of the brain lining) and you should inform your doctor if you have a severe headache or weakness [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * In some individuals, IVIG can also result in kidney problems, strokes, heart attacks, or the formation of abnormal blood clots in the body [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Disorders of primary hemostasis include vessel wall abnormalities (HHT, Ehlers-Danlos), platelet function disorders (both acquired and inherited), von Willebrand disease (most common inherited bleeding disorder), and thrombocytopenia (including ITP). Management varies by condition but may include local measures, desmopressin, antifibrinolytics, factor replacement, and in some cases, immunomodulatory therapy. Understanding these conditions is essential for perioperative planning and managing bleeding risk.

## 3.3. DISORDERS OF SECONDARY HEMOSTASIS

### 3.3.2. HEMOPHILIA A
#### 3.3.2.1. DEFINITION
* Factor VIII deficiency [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
* Primary synthesized by the liver and endothelial cells - half-life 12 hours [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
* Protected from proteolysis in the circulation by binding to von Willebrand factor [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]

#### 3.3.2.2. EPIDEMIOLOGY
* 1 in 5,000 male births [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
* Sidenote: Acquired hemophilia is a rare disease that usually develops in association with connective tissue disorders, pregnancy, or malignancy. It rarely occurs in young patients and stems from the development of antibodies to FVIII [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].

#### 3.3.2.3. PATHOLOGY
* Factor VIII gene located on X chromosome - X-linked recessive [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
* Associated with range of mutations in Factor VIII gene - major inversions, large deletions, missense, nonsense, splice site [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
* Antenatal diagnosis possible by chorionic villous sampling [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
* Female carriers of hemophilia may have reduced factor VIII levels because of random inactivation of their normal X chromosome in the developing fetus [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
  * This can result in a mild bleeding disorder; thus all known or suspected carriers of hemophilia A should have their factor VIII level measured [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].

#### 3.3.2.4. CLINICAL FEATURES
* Extent and patterns of bleeding closely related to residual Factor VIII levels - International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) criteria [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]

* ⚠️ Babies with severe hemophilia have increased risk of intracranial hemorrhage - avoid head trauma and perform imaging of newborn in first 24 hours [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
* Almost 40% of cases present in the neonatal period, particularly with intracranial hemorrhage, bleeding post-circumcision or prolonged oozing from heel stick and venepuncture sites. The severity usually remains constant within a family [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* Major morbidity of recurrent bleeding in severe hemophilia is musculoskeletal [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * Bleeds into large joints - knees, elbows, ankles, hips → synovial hypertrophy, destruction of cartilage, chronic hemophilic arthropathy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
  * Muscle hematomas - calf and psoas → dependent on location → femoral nerve compression, compartment syndrome with ischemia, necrosis, fibrosis, subsequent contraction and shortening of Achilles tendon [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]

#### 3.3.2.5. MANAGEMENT
* ⚠️ Intramuscular injections, aspirin, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs should be avoided in all patients with hemophilia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].

![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_Untitled_9.png_3fa3da.png_3f4a60.png_522691.png)

* **Prophylactic coagulation factor replacement** - maintain trough levels of Factor VIII (or IX) above 0.02 U/mL (2%) 2 to 3 times a week [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * Primary prophylaxis usually begins at age 2-3 [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
  * Prophylaxis results in better joint function in adult life [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
  * May be changing due to recombinant factor concentrates having manipulated half-life - i.e., new products produced by Fc fusion and pegylation/glycopegylation that extend the half-life of factor VIII to the degree that it can be used to alter dosing schedules for prophylaxis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * Emicizumab is a relatively new therapeutic option approved for routine prophylaxis use in hemophilia A. It is a synthetic antibody that binds to factors IX and X to mimic the role of FVIII in the tenase complex. Of note, this agent is not indicated for treatment of bleeding, and only used as prophylaxis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* **Alternative: treat on demand (episodic)** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * In general, raising the circulating level to 30% of normal is sufficient to treat minor bleeds and simple joint bleeds [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].

  ![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_Untitled_10.png_c28578.png_dec3a7.png_bfd980.png)

  * Major surgery or life-threatening bleeds require the level to be raised to 100% and then maintained at 30% to 50% for up to 2 weeks to prevent secondary hemorrhage [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
	* This can only be achieved by regular infusion of factor concentrate (usually 8–12-hourly for FVIII, 12–24-hourly for FIX, or by continuous infusion) and by closely monitoring plasma levels [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * Antifibrinolytic agents such as tranexamic acid and epsilon aminocaproic acid are good adjuncts to use during minor procedures and perioperatively [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * **Severe hemophilia**: IV infusion of Factor VIII concentrate [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
	* * Bed rest or splint the bleeding site [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
	* * Mobilization and physical therapy after bleeding episode [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
	* Offer hepatitis A and B immunizations if not immune [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
  * **Mild or moderate hemophilia**: Desmopressin: raises vWF and Factor VIII (stimulates endogenous release) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
	* Higher dose than used in diabetes insipidus via IV or subcutaneous or intranasal 300μg [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
	* Look for evidence of water retention or hyponatremia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
	* ⚠️ Contraindicated in: history of severe arterial disease - provokes thrombotic events, and young children - hyponatremia → seizures [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
* Parents are usually taught to give replacement therapy at home when the child is 2-3 years of age, and many children are able to administer their own treatment from 7-8 years of age [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].

### 3.3.3. HEMOPHILIA B
#### 3.3.3.1. DEFINITION
* Aberrations of Factor IX gene, X-linked [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]

#### 3.3.3.2. EPIDEMIOLOGY
* 1 in 30,000 male births [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]

#### 3.3.3.3. CLINICAL FEATURES
* Clinically indistinguishable from Hemophilia A but less common [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]

![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_Untitled_13.png_bd5ad7.png_73d854.png_8eb270.png)

#### 3.3.3.4. TREATMENT
* Treatment is with a factor IX concentrate, used in much the same way as factor VIII for hemophilia A [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * See hemophilia A management [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
  * But requires less frequent dosing regimens than FVIII concentrates due to a longer half-life (18 hours as opposed to 12 hours) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
  * The new extended half-life recombinant factor IX products made by Fc fusion, albumin fusion and pegylation offer the possibility of prophylaxis on a once-weekly or even two-weekly schedule [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* Although factor IX concentrates shared the problems of virus transmission seen with factor VIII, they do not commonly induce inhibitor antibodies (< 1% patients); when this does occur, however, it may be heralded by the development of a severe allergic-type reaction [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].

### 3.3.4. RARE INHERITED BLEEDING DISORDERS
* Autosomal recessive - Factors VII, X, XIII [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
* Rare, severe bleeding [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
* Hemorrhage from umbilical stump, intracranial hemorrhage [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
* Factor XIII in women - recurrent fetal loss [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
* Factor XI deficiency can occur in heterozygous or homozygous individuals [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
* Bleeding variable, not accurately predictable by coagulation factor level [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
* Severe bleeding if <15% of normal [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]

### 3.3.5. ACQUIRED BLEEDING DISORDERS
#### 3.3.5.1. LIVER DISEASE
* Both excess of bleeding and risk of venous thrombosis
* Although there is reduced hepatic synthesis of procoagulant factors, this is balanced to a degree by the reduced production of natural anticoagulant proteins and reduced fibrinolytic activity in patients with advanced liver disease.
* **Primary hemostasis issues**:
  * Primary hemostasis was typically thought to be inefficient in chronic liver disease because of thrombocytopenia secondary to decreased production of thrombopoietin in the liver. However, the low platelet count is balanced with increased circulating levels of vWF that result from a reduced presence of ADAMTS-13, the protease that regulates plasma concentrations of vWF.
	* This balance remains fragile, given that patients with severe liver disease and acute illness are also prone to endothelial and platelet dysfunction.
* **Hemostatic equilibrium**:
  * Is not stable and can be tipped toward either hemorrhage or thrombosis by acute illness, malnutrition, renal injury, infection, or medications.
* **Hemorrhage risk**:
  * There is reduced hepatic synthesis, for example, of factors V, VII, VIII, IX, X, XI, prothrombin and fibrinogen.
  * Thrombocytopenia may occur secondary to hypersplenism in portal hypertension. In cholestatic jaundice, there is reduced vitamin K absorption, leading to deficiency of factors II, VII, IX and X, but also of proteins C and S.
	* Without vitamin K, these proteins do not undergo carboxylation and therefore cannot actively bind to the phospholipid membrane of platelets during secondary hemostasis.
	* Two sources of vitamin K—phylloquinone (K1) is available in a number of foods such as leafy greens, whereas menaquinone (K2) is synthesized in the GI tract by intestinal bacteria and accounts for the bulk of vitamin K stored in the liver.
	* Hence sterile gut in newborns and oral antibiotic treatment may cause vitamin K deficiency.
* **Thrombosis risk**:
  * Fibrinolysis is maintained in chronic liver disease.
  * Although these patients are plasminogen deficient, they have higher than normal levels of tPA secondary to lower levels of thrombin-activatable fibrinolysis inhibitor (TAFI).
  * This maintains the normal ratio of plasminogen to plasmin.
  * However, the balance of fibrinolysis and antifibrinolysis can be disrupted by infection, trauma, surgery, and medications common to chronic liver patients.
* **Investigations**: Use rotational thromboelastometry (ROTEM); patients with normal coagulation profiles can present with catastrophic gastrointestinal bleeding or prolonged PTs, yet develop venous, arterial, and portal thrombosis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* **Treatment**: Treatment with plasma products or platelet transfusion should be reserved for acute bleeds or to cover interventional procedures such as liver biopsy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].
* **Vitamin K deficiency**: Can be readily corrected with parenteral administration of vitamin K [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].

#### 3.3.5.2. RENAL FAILURE
* Severity of hemorrhagic state in renal failure - proportional to plasma urea concentration
* **Bleeding manifestations** - platelet dysfunction:
  * Gastrointestinal tract most common
* **Multifactorial**: Anemia, mild thrombocytopenia, accumulation of low molecular weight waste products inhibiting platelet function
* **Treatment**: Dialysis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]:
  * Rarely: Platelet concentrate infusions, red cell transfusions indicated [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
  * Increasing the concentration of vWF, either by cryoprecipitate or by desmopressin, may promote hemostasis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]

#### 3.3.5.3. VITAMIN K DEFICIENCY IN CHILDREN
* Hemorrhagic disease of the newborn due to vitamin K deficiency
	* Vitamin K does not cross placenta well.
	* Fetal colon is sterile thus there is no bacterial production of vitamin K.
	* Breast milk also has low Vitamin K content.
* Liver disease
* Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP)
* Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)

**Bottom Line:** Secondary hemostasis disorders include hemophilia A (Factor VIII deficiency) and B (Factor IX deficiency), rare factor deficiencies, and acquired disorders due to liver/renal disease. Management varies by severity, with options including prophylactic factor replacement, on-demand treatment, desmopressin (for mild cases), and antifibrinolytics. Complications include development of inhibitor antibodies and potential infection transmission from plasma-derived products. Understanding the specific factor deficiency and its severity is critical for appropriate perioperative planning and bleeding management.

# 4. DIAGNOSTIC APPROACHES

## 4.1. STANDARD LABORATORY TESTS

* **PT/INR** - Factors 2, 5, 7, 10 - extrinsic pathway - ~12-15s [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Warfarin testing is monitored with INR (PT) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * When warfarin is started or stopped, the factors with the fastest plasma turnover (i.e., the shortest half-lives) decline or rise the fastest, respectively [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	* Thus, the inhibitor protein C, with a 6-hour half-life, declines faster than most clotting factors as warfarin takes effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	* → Imbalance toward clotting during the initiation of warfarin therapy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	* → Warfarin-induced skin necrosis is a thrombotic complication often occurring when previously unrecognized congenital protein C deficiency accentuates this imbalance [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Affected by genetic variations in metabolism (P450, CYP2C9) and vitamin K (vitamin K epoxide reductase complex subunit 1 - VKORC1) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * PT mainly assesses clotting factor VII of the extrinsic pathway, along with factors II, X and fibrinogen of the final common pathway [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * PT is prolonged by [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
	* Decreased hepatic synthesis of vitamin K-dependent clotting factors (II, VII, IX, X) due to warfarin therapy, vitamin K deficiency, or liver disease
	* DIC (consumption of clotting factors)
* **APTT** - Factors 2, 5, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 - intrinsic pathway - ~25-35s [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Heparin testing is monitored with aPTT - dependent on therapeutic target range [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * APTT mainly assesses clotting factors VIII, IX, XI and XII of the intrinsic pathway and factors II, X and fibrinogen of the common pathway [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * APTT is prolonged by [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
	* Unfractionated heparin therapy (through activation of antithrombin III)
	* Hemophilia (factor VIII deficiency), Christmas disease (factor IX deficiency), von Willebrand disease
	* DIC (consumption of clotting factors)
* **FBC, Blood Film (PBF)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
* **Plasma Mixing Studies** - to determine if an inhibitor or anticoagulant is present (no correction with mixing) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
* **Quantitative Fibrinogen Assay** - normal 150-400mg/dL [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
* **Thrombin Time** - tests for deficiency or dysfunction of fibrinogen [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Thrombin is added to plasma and the clotting time is measured; tests the interaction between thrombin and fibrinogen [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Prolonged in fibrinogen deficiency (e.g., due to DIC) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **D-dimers** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * D-dimer is a cleavage product of cross-linked fibrin, used to aid diagnosis of venous thromboembolic disease like pulmonary embolism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Biochemical Screen** - renal and liver function tests [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
* **Other Tests**: vWD screen (antigen and activity), qualitative platelet function (platelet aggregation testing) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Platelet aggregation testing using platelet-rich plasma is the gold standard for platelet function - platelets are tested with multiple isolated agonists including ADP, arachidonic acid, collagen and epinephrine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * 💡 'Bleeding time' is no longer used to investigate platelet disorders, as it is unreliable [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	* Replaced by in vitro tests of platelet function on a platelet function analyzer, which can be performed on a peripheral blood sample [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Special Considerations** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * In the neonate, the levels of all clotting factors **except factor VIII (FVIII) and fibrinogen** are lower; preterm infants have even lower levels. Therefore the results have to be compared with normal values in infants of a similar gestational and postnatal age [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Anticoagulant Monitoring** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * **Heparin testing** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
	* Functional enzyme test for heparin - antifactor Xa activity (not FX activity level) - Using the aFXa assay, therapeutic target heparin levels of 0.3 to 0.7 aFXa units/mL are correlated with aPTT results for that range [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	* Low–molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) drugs and their analog, synthetic pentasaccharide (e.g., fondaparinux), do not affect the aPTT assay, and coagulation testing is usually not needed. However, if necessary, the drugs' plasma activity levels can be assessed by aFXa assays calibrated for each drug [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	* **HIT testing** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
	  * Clinical suspicion for HIT should arise with thrombocytopenia or thrombosis after the initiation of heparin therapy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	  * Diagnosis should be based on the clinical criteria established with the "4T-score system" which includes thrombocytopenia, timing of platelet count decline, evidence of thrombosis, and influence of other factors that may contribute to thrombocytopenia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	  * If suspected, the ELISA immunoassay and/or functional assays such as the serotonin release assay, should be ordered to identify heparin-PF4 antibodies [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
  * **Direct-acting anticoagulant testing (DOAC)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
	* E.g., dabigatran - direct thrombin inhibitor; "xaban" drugs - direct Factor Xa inhibitors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
	* Monitoring typically not required; PT is not reliable indicator of drug effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
	* **Dabigatran** - dTT or Etc coagulation assays [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
	  * Ecarin (in Etc) is an enzyme found in snake venom that cleaves prothrombin to a metabolic intermediate - this is inhibited by hirudin and its analogs [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
	  * But can be confounded by other molecules - including hirudin from leeches, its recombinant "-rudin" mimicking molecules (intravenous bivalirudin, desirudin), and small synthetic molecules acting at the same site as hirudin on thrombin (intravenous argatroban, oral dabigatran) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	* **Factor Xa inhibitors** - aFXa assays calibrated for each specific drug may be used or quantitative plasma drug concentrations [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Standard laboratory tests for hemostasis include PT/INR, APTT, fibrinogen levels, thrombin time, and D-dimers. Specialized tests for specific conditions include mixing studies (for inhibitors), platelet function assays, and specific anticoagulant assays. Interpretation must consider patient age (especially in neonates) and the specific anticoagulant being used. These tests help diagnose bleeding disorders and monitor anticoagulant therapy but have limitations in predicting clinical bleeding risk.

### 4.2.2. THROMBOELASTOGRAPHY (TEG)

* **The R time** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Time from stimulation until beginning of clot formation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
	* Normal is 5-10 minutes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]

* **The K time** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * The time from initial clot formation until width of deviation of 20mm on trace [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
	* Normal is 3-5 minutes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
	  * Determined by fibrinogen [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]

* **The alpha angle** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * The rate of clot formation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
	* Determined by fibrinogen [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
	  * 50-70 degrees is normal [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
		* If lower, give FFP [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
* **The maximum amplitude (MA)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * The maximum extent of clot formation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
	* Determined by platelets [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
	  * Normal is 50-70mm [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
		* Give platelets if low [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]]
* **LY30 (lysis at 30 minutes)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * A measure of clot breakdown after 30 minutes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]

### 4.2.3. ROTATIONAL THROMBOELASTOMETRY (ROTEM)
#### 4.2.3.1. PRINCIPLES AND METHODOLOGY
* ROTEM and TEG (thromboelastography) are increasingly used as point-of-care tests (POCTs) of coagulation. Traditionally, this is investigated with PT/aPTT/INR. One of the major arguments for the use of point-of-care instruments like TEG and ROTEM is their ability to detect fibrinolysis as the cause of bleeding, which no other convenient test can actually do [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **iTACTIC trial:** A study that compared the effectiveness of using viscoelastic haemostatic assays, like ROTEM, versus standard coagulation tests to guide transfusions in trauma patients. The study did not find a difference in overall mortality, though viscoelastic assays showed better guidance for transfusions in certain cases.

![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_Untitled.png_413422.png_555995.png_cf7677.png)

* In ROTEM, whole blood is heated to 37°C in a cuvette. A pin is suspended into the blood with an optical detector in the case. The pin oscillates in a limited arc of 4°75' every 6 seconds. As the blood clots, fibrin-platelet strands form between the cup and the pin and as the viscoelastic strength of the clot increases, it impedes rotation of the pin. This is detected and a trace is generated. With the onset of fibrinolysis, the fibrin-platelet structure begins to dissolve affecting movement of the pin and this is again reflected in the trace [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].

![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_Untitled_1.png_2759a8.png_3af190.png_3b16d0.png)

![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_Untitled_2.png_93131c.png_69774a.png_ea30f5.png)

#### 4.2.3.2. ROTEM PARAMETERS
* A standard curve looks like that (first picture below). A few variables of note [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:

![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_Untitled_3.png_b23fa4.png_31786b.png_93dc62.png)

1. **Clotting time (CT)** - is the time (min) from start of test to when waveform reaches 2mm above baseline - represents initiation of coagulation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
2. **Clot formation time (CFT)** - is the time [min] from 2mm above baseline to 20mm above baselinerepresents amplification process of coagulation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
3. **Alpha angle (degrees)** - angle between end of CT and CFT time - represents thrombin burst [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
4. **Maximal clot firmness (MCF)** - represents overall strength of clot; dependent upon formation of fibrin, interaction with platelets, stabilization of fibrin clot by F13 and hematocrit [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
   * MCF-t - time taken to reach MCF [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
5. **Maximum lysis (ML)** - measure of percent decrease in amplitude at end of test - A ML value of <15% at 60 minutes indicates a stable clot whereas an ML value of >15% is indicative of fibrinolysis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
6. **Clot lysis at 30 and 60min (LY30, LY60)** - measure of percent decrease in amplitude at 30 and 60min after waveform has reached max amplitude - represents clot stability and fibrinolysis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]

#### 4.2.3.3. ROTEM TEST TYPES
A few curves will be generated in a standard ROTEM - usually the INTEM, EXTEM, APTEM, FIBTEM. Extra tests include HEPTEM and ECATEM [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].

![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_Untitled_4.png_6308bd.png_d0a29b.png_0144d6.png)

![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_Untitled_5.png_1ae45b.png_27a3f3.png_aa9a7d.png)

![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_Untitled_6.png_b020cc.png_0910b7.png_07126d.png)

![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_Untitled_7.png_e9f6a9.png_d4cf97.png_4487a4.png)

![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_Untitled_8.png_8f7965.png_b0bcf2.png_dbbb33.png)

1. **The INTEM curve** contains phospholipid and ellagic acid as activators and provides information similar to that of the APTT [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
2. **The EXTEM curve** contains tissue factor as an activator and provides information similar to that of the PT [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
3. **The APTEM** contains aprotinin for inhibiting fibrinolysis in addition to tissue factor (like EXTEM). Coagulation is thus initiated like EXTEM but in the presence of fibrinolysis inhibitor, will allow rapid detection of hyperfibrinolysis (represented by shortened CT, higher MCF - clot forming faster and stronger) and in addition can determine if antifibrinolytic therapy alone (such as tranexamic acid) will normalize coagulation or if additional treatments such as fibrinogen supplementation, is necessary [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
4. **The FIBTEM** is like INTEM but has an addition of cytochalasin D, a platelet inhibitor. By blocking platelet contribution to clot formation (leaving only coagulation factors), it allows for qualitative analysis of functional fibrinogen component. Without platelets the clot is totally useless from a hemostatic standpoint, and this is well demonstrated by the FIBTEM trace which rarely even reaches an amplitude of 20mm at its maximum. But a FIBTEM MCF of less than 9mm suggests that there is insufficient fibrinogen level, and a MCF in excess of 25mm suggests that there is an excess of fibrinogen and perhaps some sort of procoagulable state [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
5. **The HEPTEM** is like INTEM but contains heparinase for neutralizing heparin. The use of HEPTEM is ideal for situations where the patient is heavily heparinized (e.g., on bypass) and the anesthetist is interested in the degree of residual coagulopathy one might expect after the heparin is reversed with protamine. When comparing INTEM and HEPTEM, a significantly shortened HEPTEM CT would suggest a heparin effect (i.e., the CT is shortened by adding heparinase) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
6. **The ECATEM** contains ecarin (a prothrombin activator, derived from the venom of saw-scaled viper Echis carinatus). Useful in detecting presence of direct thrombin inhibitors - Etc will be prolonged; however with heparin or warfarin Etc will be normal [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].

#### 4.2.3.4. ROTEM PATTERN INTERPRETATION
After looking at the standard graph, different shapes can help identify specific coagulation issues [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:

1. **Thrombocytopenia / Poor Platelet Function** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
   * CT intervals are normal (coagulation factors functioning normal), but CFT increased (as clot propagation is impaired - it could be due to low or non-functioning platelets or low fibrinogen). The MCF is reduced suggesting that clot stability is poor. A decreased MCF can be attributed to low or non-functioning platelets, low fibrinogen or hyperfibrinolysis. It is unlikely hyperfibrinolysis as the amplitudes do not narrow with time. Looking at FIBTEM, it is normal (it is only worrying if too low <9mm), suggestive that it is not an issue of fibrinogen quantity or function. Therefore the problem is either low platelet number or poor platelet function [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].

   ![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_Untitled_9.png_42a6b4.png_7d42ee.png_ec8b67.png)

2. **Low Fibrinogen** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
   * CT intervals are prolonged (coagulation factors are not functioning normally and initiation of clotting is impaired), CFT prolonged (problem with clot propagation - possibilities as previously described above). MCF is low (poor clot stability - possibilities as previously described above). FIBTEM has a very low amplitude suggestive of a lack of fibrinogen [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].

   ![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_Untitled_10.png_61f309.png_51b6b0.png_0f711d.png)

   ![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_Untitled_11.png_3fb5c4.png_0f0fa2.png_220716.png)

3. **Heparin Effect** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
   * The INTEM graph is highly abnormal (compared to the EXTEM) suggestive of a pathology primarily of the intrinsic pathway. The HEPTEM (essentially INTEM test with added heparinase) shows normalization of CT, CFT, alpha angle and MCF. Thus this patient has no other coagulation issues other than heparin. If there is no normalization, despite an abnormal INTEM with prolonged CT - consider factor deficiencies/inhibitors (other than heparin) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].

   ![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_Untitled_12.png_e5dbbe.png_7e20b6.png_0d9d57.png)

4. **Hyperfibrinolysis** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
   * There is rapid decrease in amplitude in all tests except APTEM - to zero. Aprotinin in APTEM is added thus inhibiting fibrinolytic enzymes, restoring clot stability. In this case, antifibrinolytics may be useful [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c4-trauma-and-resuscitation|ⓘ]].

   ![](HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_HAEMATOLOGY_Untitled_13.png_217d08.png_51eea7.png_586d66.png)

5. **Clotting Factor Deficiency** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
   * CT intervals are prolonged. If EXTEM CT prolonged, but MCF normal, this is suggestive of deficiency of vitamin K-dependent factors or inhibitors. If INTEM CT prolonged but MCF normal, a HEPTEM should be performed to rule out heparin effect. If pathology still persists, this is suggestive of intrinsic pathway factor deficiencies or inhibitors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* Thromboelastography is a near-patient method testing the whole hemostatic process (platelet function, coagulation, fibrinolysis) in a single test [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* It's quicker than laboratory tests and thought to better represent in vivo hemostasis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* The test involves adding whole blood to a rotating cuvette with a suspended pin; as the blood clots, fibrin strands form between the cuvette and pin, changing the torsion of a wire [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* The resulting cigar-shaped graph can quickly identify abnormalities in hemostasis or provide detailed analysis of various parameters [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Viscoelastic testing (TEG and ROTEM) provides real-time assessment of whole blood coagulation, including clot formation, strength, and breakdown. These tests offer advantages over conventional coagulation tests by evaluating the entire hemostatic process and can guide targeted component therapy, particularly useful in trauma, major surgery, and critical bleeding scenarios. Different ROTEM tests (INTEM, EXTEM, FIBTEM, etc.) allow specific aspects of coagulation to be assessed.

# 5. SPECIAL HEMATOLOGICAL CONDITIONS

## 5.1. SICKLE CELL DISEASE

#### 5.1.1.1. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY
* **Hemoglobin structure** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Hemoglobin has four polypeptide chains, each with a covalently bound heme moiety [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
	* This moiety has a porphyrin ring with an iron atom in the middle, usually in its ferrous state (Fe2+) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
	* 96% of adult hemoglobin is HbA1 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
	  * This has 2 alpha and 2 beta chains [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
* **Genetic basis** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Sickle cell is a genetic condition caused by a switch of adenine for thymine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
	* This causes an amino acid substitution from glutamic acid to valine at position 6 on the beta chain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
	  * This is called HbS [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
		* Homozygous is HbSS and causes sickle cell disease [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
		* Heterozygous is HbAS and causes sickle cell trait [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
	* HbS polymerizes at low oxygen tensions, changing the shape of red blood cells making them stiffer and more fragile [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
	  * This leads to increased blood viscosity, and microclots in the circulation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
	  * It also shortens red blood cell life to between 5 and 15 days rather than 120 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
		* This leads to hemolytic anemia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]

#### 5.1.1.2. FACTORS INDUCING SICKLING
* **Hypoxia** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * HbSS will sickle at 85% oxygen saturation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
  * HbAS will sickle at 40% oxygen saturation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
* **Hypothermia** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
* **Acidosis** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
* **Dehydration** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
* **Pooling of blood or stasis** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Low cardiac output [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
  * Hypovolemia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]

#### 5.1.1.3. ANESTHETIC CONSIDERATIONS
* **Avoid Hypoxia** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#s7-special-populations|ⓘ]]:
  * Preoxygenate and supplement oxygen for at least 24 hours after surgery [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#s7-special-populations|ⓘ]]
  * Try and shift oxygen dissociation curve to the left [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#s7-special-populations|ⓘ]]
	* Slight hyperventilation can help [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#s7-special-populations|ⓘ]]
* **Avoid Dehydration** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#s7-special-populations|ⓘ]]:
  * Keep hydrated [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#s7-special-populations|ⓘ]]
	* Aim for hematocrit <0.35 [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#s7-special-populations|ⓘ]]
  * Consider transfusion to aim Hb >100 g/L [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#s7-special-populations|ⓘ]]
	* ⚠️ Crossmatching may take longer so perform this well in advance [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#s7-special-populations|ⓘ]]
* **Avoid Hypothermia** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#s7-special-populations|ⓘ]]:
  * Active warming [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#s7-special-populations|ⓘ]]
* **Avoid Infection** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#s7-special-populations|ⓘ]]:
  * Sickle cell patients have functional hyposplenism [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#s7-special-populations|ⓘ]]
	* Therefore more at risk from encapsulated bacteria [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#s7-special-populations|ⓘ]]
	  * Discuss with microbiology and hematology but likely to need prophylaxis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#s7-special-populations|ⓘ]]
		* They should already be on penicillin V long term [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#s7-special-populations|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ If patient has had previous transfusions in other countries, be aware of risk of blood-borne virus infections [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#s7-special-populations|ⓘ]]

#### 5.1.1.4. TESTING FOR SICKLE CELL DISEASE
* **Sickledex test** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Exposes red cells to sodium metabisulphite [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
	* This will cause sickling in HbS but won't distinguish between HbSS and HbAS [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
* If Sickledex is positive then HPLC test needed, or electrophoresis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Sickle cell disease results from a point mutation causing hemoglobin S, which polymerizes at low oxygen tensions, leading to erythrocyte sickling. Key anesthetic considerations include avoiding hypoxia, dehydration, hypothermia, and infection, as these trigger sickling crises. Preoperative optimization with hydration and possible transfusion, along with appropriate antibiotic prophylaxis, are important management strategies.

## 5.2. HEMATOLOGICAL ASPECTS OF SYSTEMIC DISEASE

### 5.2.2. LIVER DISEASE
* **Hemorrhagic and thrombotic risk** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Both excess of bleeding and risk of venous thrombosis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]
  * Although there is reduced hepatic synthesis of procoagulant factors, this is balanced to a degree by the reduced production of natural anticoagulant proteins and reduced fibrinolytic activity in patients with advanced liver disease [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Primary hemostasis issues** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Primary hemostasis was typically thought to be inefficient in chronic liver disease because of thrombocytopenia secondary to decreased production of thrombopoietin in the liver. However, the low platelet count is balanced with increased circulating levels of vWF that result from a reduced presence of ADAMTS-13, the protease that regulates plasma concentrations of vWF [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	* This balance remains fragile, given that patients with severe liver disease and acute illness are also prone to endothelial and platelet dysfunction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Hemostatic equilibrium** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Is not stable and can be tipped toward either hemorrhage or thrombosis by acute illness, malnutrition, renal injury, infection, or medications [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Hemorrhage risk** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * There is reduced hepatic synthesis, for example, of factors V, VII, VIII, IX, X, XI, prothrombin and fibrinogen [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Thrombocytopenia may occur secondary to hypersplenism in portal hypertension. In cholestatic jaundice, there is reduced vitamin K absorption, leading to deficiency of factors II, VII, IX and X, but also of proteins C and S [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	* Without vitamin K, these proteins do not undergo carboxylation and therefore cannot actively bind to the phospholipid membrane of platelets during secondary hemostasis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	* Two sources of vitamin K—phylloquinone (K1) is available in a number of foods such as leafy greens, whereas menaquinone (K2) is synthesized in the GI tract by intestinal bacteria and accounts for the bulk of vitamin K stored in the liver [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
	* Hence sterile gut in newborns and oral antibiotic treatment may cause vitamin K deficiency [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Thrombosis risk** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]]:
  * Fibrinolysis is maintained in chronic liver disease [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * Although these patients are plasminogen deficient, they have higher than normal levels of tPA secondary to lower levels of thrombin-activatable fibrinolysis inhibitor (TAFI) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * This maintains the normal ratio of plasminogen to plasmin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
  * However, the balance of fibrinolysis and antifibrinolysis can be disrupted by infection, trauma, surgery, and medications common to chronic liver patients [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.8 Haematology, Immunology|ⓘ]].
* **Investigations**: Use rotational thromboelastometry (ROTEM); patients with normal coagulation profiles can present with catastrophic gastrointestinal bleeding or prolonged PTs, yet develop venous, arterial, and portal thrombosis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#s7-special-populations|ⓘ]].
* **Treatment**: Treatment with plasma products or platelet transfusion should be reserved for acute bleeds or to cover interventional procedures such as liver biopsy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#s7-special-populations|ⓘ]].
* **Vitamin K deficiency**: Can be readily corrected with parenteral administration of vitamin K [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#s7-special-populations|ⓘ]].

### 5.2.3. RENAL FAILURE
* **Severity**: Hemorrhagic state in renal failure - proportional to plasma urea concentration
* **Bleeding manifestations** - platelet dysfunction:
  * Gastrointestinal tract most common
* **Multifactorial**: Anemia, mild thrombocytopenia, accumulation of low molecular weight waste products inhibiting platelet function
* **Treatment**: Dialysis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#s7-special-populations|ⓘ]]:
  * Rarely: Platelet concentrate infusions, red cell transfusions indicated [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#s7-special-populations|ⓘ]]
  * Increasing the concentration of vWF, either by cryoprecipitate or by desmopressin, may promote hemostasis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#s7-special-populations|ⓘ]]

### 5.2.4. DISSEMINATED INTRAVASCULAR COAGULATION
* **Causes**:
  * Infection
  * Bleeding
  * Trauma
  * Shock
  * Adenocarcinoma
  * Transfusion

**Bottom Line:** Systemic diseases like liver and renal failure have significant hematological impacts. Liver disease creates a complex, unstable balance between bleeding and thrombosis risks due to reduced production of both proand anticoagulant factors. Renal failure causes platelet dysfunction proportional to uremia. Both conditions require careful assessment of bleeding risk, with judicious use of blood products only when clinically indicated rather than based solely on laboratory values.

## 5.3. ANAEMIA AND POLYCYTHAEMIA

### 5.3.2. RED BLOOD CELL PRODUCTION
* **Erythropoiesis** involves several cell types during red blood cell (RBC) development [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * Starting with the common pluripotent haemopoietic stem cell, which differentiates into myeloid progenitor stem cells dedicated to erythropoiesis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * First called burst-forming unit erythroid (BFU-E) cells, before becoming colony-forming unit erythroid (CFU-E) cells [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * CFU-E cells then pass through a series of erythroblast phases in the bone marrow [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * Erythroblasts require both vitamin B12 and folate for their DNA synthesis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * Erythroblasts synthesise haemoglobin (Hb) from the early stages of their maturation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * Globin chains are produced in the cytoplasm, whilst the haem moiety, which requires iron, is synthesised in the mitochondria [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * The penultimate cell type is the reticulocyte, which has lost its nucleus but continues to synthesize Hb from residual ribosomal RNA [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * At 1–2 days following their release into the circulation, reticulocytes lose their RNA and become mature erythrocytes [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
* **Erythropoietin (EPO)** controls erythropoiesis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * Glycoprotein secreted mainly by the kidney in response to hypoxaemia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * Around 10% originates from the liver, especially important in end-stage renal failure [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * EPO increases erythropoiesis by stimulating the differentiation of BFU-E and CFU-E progenitor cells [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * Forms a negative-feedback loop: hypoxaemia stimulates EPO production, which increases RBC production, increasing O2-carrying capacity and counteracting the initial hypoxaemia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 

### 5.3.3. CAUSES OF ANAEMIA
* **Definition**: Haemoglobin concentration below the expected value when gender, pregnancy and altitude have been taken into account; World Health Organization defines anaemia as Hb < 130 g/L in men and Hb < 120 g/L in non-pregnant women [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
* **Prevalence**: As high as 60% in the surgical population [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
* **Causes** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * **Insufficient RBC production** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
	* **Iron-deficiency anaemia**: Deficient haem synthesis results in a reduced number of microcytic and hypochromic RBCs [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
	* **Folic acid and vitamin B12 deficiency**: RBCs produced are megaloblastic [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
	* **End-stage renal disease**: Insufficient EPO is produced by the kidneys; RBCs are normocytic [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
	* **Anaemia of chronic disease**: Now thought to result from cytokine-mediated (especially interleukin-6) hepatic synthesis of hepcidin, which blunts the response of erythropoietic progenitor cells to EPO and reduces gastrointestinal iron absorption [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * **RBC haemolysis** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
	* **Inherited abnormalities of RBCs** (e.g., hereditary spherocytosis): Abnormally shaped RBCs are taken out of circulation by the spleen, reducing their lifespan [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
	* **Inherited abnormalities of Hb** (e.g., sickle cell disease): The spleen haemolyses sickle-shaped cells, significantly reducing the average RBC lifespan [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
	* **RBC enzyme deficiencies** (e.g., glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency): Some Hb in the ferrous state (Fe2+) is oxidised to the ferric state (Fe3+), forming MetHb; RBCs containing MetHb are haemolysed by the spleen [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
	* **Transfusion reactions**: Transfusion of ABO-incompatible blood leads to an antibody-mediated intravascular haemolysis reaction [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
	* **Autoimmune haemolytic anaemia**: RBCs are haemolysed by autoimmune attack [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
	* **Mechanical trauma to RBCs** (e.g., cardiopulmonary bypass and valvular dysfunction): May result in traumatic haemolysis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * **Bleeding**: May be acute (e.g., postpartum haemorrhage) or chronic (e.g., colonic carcinoma, menstruation); in critical care patients, repeated arterial blood sampling is a major contributor to anaemia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 

### 5.3.4. CONSEQUENCES OF ANAEMIA
* **Mild anaemia**: Has few consequences in itself, but the underlying disease may be significant (e.g., colonic carcinoma) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
* **Acute blood loss**: Patients exhibit signs of hypovolaemia: tachycardia, hypotension, and oliguria [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
* **Chronic anaemia**: Develops signs and symptoms dependent on severity [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * **Mild anaemia**: General malaise and dyspnoea on exertion [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * **Severe anaemia**: Reduced O2-carrying capacity necessitates increased cardiac output; patients may experience palpitations [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * **Pre-existing comorbidities**: Those with coronary artery disease or peripheral arterial disease may experience angina or claudication, respectively; high-output heart failure may develop [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 

### 5.3.5. PATIENT BLOOD MANAGEMENT
* **Concept**: Multidisciplinary approach aimed at reducing unnecessary perioperative blood transfusions [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
* **Components** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * **Preoperative optimisation of red cell mass**: For example, through use of intravenous iron therapy in iron-deficiency anaemia (the most common cause of preoperative anaemia) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * **Minimisation of intraoperative blood loss**: May include minimally invasive surgical techniques, topical haemostatic agents, antifibrinolytic drugs (tranexamic acid), and cell salvage; blood products should be guided using thromboelastography when needed [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * **Management of post-operative anaemia**: The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) recommends a transfusion trigger of 70 g/L, or 80 g/L in patients with cardiovascular disease; a 'single-unit' transfusion strategy is recommended in stable patients without active bleeding [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 

### 5.3.6. IRON HANDLING AND HOMEOSTASIS
* **Importance of iron** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * Essential element involved primarily in O2 transport and storage (Hb and myoglobin), as well as catalysis of biological reactions (cytochrome enzymes, catalase, peroxidase) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * Total body iron is typically 3–5 g, with the majority found within Hb (60–70%), 5–10% in myoglobin and iron-containing enzymes, and 20–30% stored in the liver as ferritin and haemosiderin [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
* **Iron absorption** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * A typical Western diet contains 15 mg of iron per day, but usually only 1–2 mg is absorbed; absorption can double in pregnancy and iron deficiency [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
	  * Adult male: 0.6-1mg/day
	  * Adult female: 2.1mg/day due to menstrual blood loss
	  * Pregnancy: 6-7mg/day (in 3rd trimester, fetus takes 4mg/day from mother)
  * Two forms of dietary iron, both absorbed in the duodenum [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]]   
	  ![[Pasted image 20250609190759.png]]
	* **Haem groups**: Directly absorbed by enterocytes through a haem transport protein; once inside, it is broken down by haem oxygenase to release Fe2+ [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
		* Haem is soluble in alkaline duodenum (insoluble at pH<6)
		* Absorbed by haem transporter
	* **Dietary iron salts**: Found in both ferrous (Fe2+) and ferric (Fe3+) states; enterocytes can only absorb iron in the ferrous state, which is why iron supplements are ferrous rather than ferric (e.g., ferrous fumarate) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
		* Fe3+ salts precipitate when the environmental pH is greater than pH 3 (i.e., the duodenum) and cannot be absorbed [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
		* The low-pH environment of acidic chyme entering the duodenum facilitates the enzymatic reduction of iron from Fe3+ to Fe2+, allowing duodenal absorption [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
			* Conversion of Fe3+ to Fe 2+ is augmented by dietary ascorbic acid
			* Apical membrane ferrireductase also converts Fe3+ to Fe2+
		* Fe2+ is transported across membrane by divalent metal transporter 1 (DMT1)
		* Drugs that reduce stomach acidity (e.g., proton-pump inhibitors) significantly decrease duodenal ferrous iron, leading to iron deficiency [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
	* Fe2+ at the basolaterla membrane is transported by ferroportin
		* Coupled to a copper-containing ferrioxidase (hephaestin) converting Fe2+ to Fe3+
		* Fe3+ binds to blood transport transferrin
			* Each transferrin has 2 Fe3+ binding site (normally about 1/3 saturated)
		* Fe3+ can also bind to apoferritin within cytoplasm to form ferritin
* **Iron homeostasis** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * **Tightly regulated, but the body has no mechanism for iron excretion, so control is solely through regulation of absorption [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] **
  * Hepcidin, a liver protein hormone, is the key regulator [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
	* When hepcidin levels are high, Fe2+ cannot be transported out of the enterocyte (down regulation of ferroportin); iron is stored within the enterocyte's cytoplasmic ferritin before being excreted from the body as the mucosal cell sloughs [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
	* When hepcidin levels are low, Fe2+ passes into the circulation through the enterocyte basolateral ferroportin channels [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
* **Iron storage**:
	* 3 major pools
		* Functional pool:
			* Hb 65-70% (2500mg)
			* Myoglobin 3-5% (130mg)
			* Other proteins 0.2% (8mg)
		* Transport pool:
			* Transferrin 0.1% (4mg)
		* Storage pool: 
			* Ferritin (mostly in liver): 25-30% (1000mg)
	  * Once in plasma, Fe2+ is oxidised to Fe3+ and bound to transferrin for transport to where it's needed, such as the bone marrow [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
	  * Excess iron is stored by the intracellular protein ferritin [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * Conservation of iron is extremely important: absorption is only 1 mg/day, yet erythropoiesis requires 20 mg of iron per day; at the end of the erythrocyte lifespan, iron is liberated from Hb and carried by transferrin to the bone marrow for recycling [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * Hepcidin deficiency is implicated in hereditary haemochromatosis, a genetic condition of iron overload: hepcidin levels are inappropriately low, resulting in an excess of total body iron [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 

### 5.3.7. POLYCYTHAEMIA
* **Definition**: A persistently increased ratio of RBCs to plasma (i.e., haematocrit) – above 0.51 in men and 0.48 in women [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
* **Classification** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * **Primary polycythaemia**: An abnormality in the bone marrow results in the inappropriate production of an increased number of RBCs; for example, polycythaemia rubra vera (PRV) is a myeloproliferative condition in which the bone marrow undergoes excessive erythropoiesis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * **Secondary polycythaemia**: Increased erythropoiesis due to increased EPO secretion; EPO is usually secreted in response to chronic hypoxaemia (e.g., high altitude, smoking, chronic respiratory disease, congenital heart disease), but is occasionally due to an EPO-secreting tumour [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
* **Clinical implications** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * **Primary polycythaemia** (PRV): Patients are at greatly increased risk of both arterial and venous thrombosis due to [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
	* **Hyperviscosity**: Increased haematocrit results in increased blood viscosity, which increases resistance to blood flow per the Hagen–Poiseuille equation; the resultant reduction in venous blood flow predisposes to venous thrombosis, and cardiac work is increased [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
	* **Hypercoagulability**: The coagulation cascade is abnormal in PRV patients, putting them at greater risk of thrombotic disease even when their haematocrit is normalised by venesection [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
	* **Thrombocytosis**: Some PRV patients also have excessive platelet production, increasing their risk of arterial thrombosis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * **Secondary polycythaemia**: Patients have increased blood viscosity but do not seem to have an increased risk of thrombosis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
* **Perioperative management of PRV patients** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * Increased risk of perioperative arterial and venous thromboses, including stroke, pulmonary embolus, myocardial infarction, and hepatic and portal venous thrombosis; paradoxically, also at greater risk of haemorrhage due to abnormal platelet function [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * **Elective surgery**: Hb concentration should be reduced to 'normal' levels by phlebotomy or drugs such as hydroxyurea [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * **Urgent surgery**: Hb concentration can be normalised by venesection, replacing whole blood with the same volume of crystalloid [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * Neuraxial blockade is relatively contraindicated due to potential for abnormal coagulation and platelet function [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#c2-perioperative-medicine|ⓘ]] 

**Bottom Line:** Understanding the physiology of red blood cell production and disorders of red cell count is crucial for perioperative management. Anaemia may result from decreased production, increased destruction, or blood loss, with consequences ranging from mild symptoms to severe cardiovascular compromise. Iron homeostasis is tightly regulated through hepcidin, with no mechanism for excretion. Polycythaemia increases blood viscosity and potentially thrombotic risk, especially in primary conditions like PRV. Patient blood management strategies aim to optimize red cell mass, minimize blood loss, and manage anemia with restrictive transfusion approaches.

# 6. IMMUNE SYSTEM FUNDAMENTALS

**Body's defense mechanisms against foreign antigens and pathogens**

## 6.1. INNATE IMMUNITY

**Non-specific defense mechanisms that do not require prior exposure to foreign antigens**

### 6.1.1. KEY CHARACTERISTICS
* **Immediate response** without need for prior antigen exposure
* **Non-specific** - responds similarly to various threats
* **No memory component** - response remains consistent upon re-exposure
* **First line of defense** against pathogens

### 6.1.2. MAJOR COMPONENTS
#### 6.1.2.1. BARRIER FUNCTIONS

* **Physical barriers:**
	* Intact skin provides mechanical barrier to pathogen entry
	* Mucous membranes in respiratory and gastrointestinal tracts
	* Ciliary action in respiratory tract for pathogen clearance
* **Chemical barriers:**
	* Low gastric pH (pH 1.5-2.0) destroys many ingested pathogens
	* Lysozyme in lacrimal secretions breaks down bacterial cell walls
	* Antimicrobial peptides in various body secretions

#### 6.1.2.2. CELLULAR COMPONENTS

* **Macrophages (reticuloendothelial system):**
	* Function as antigen-presenting cells in lymph nodes
	* Phagocytosis of foreign materials and cellular debris
	* Release of inflammatory mediators and cytokines
* **Natural killer (NK) cells:**
	* Specialized lymphocytes that destroy infected or abnormal cells
	* Do not require antigen presentation for activation
	* Important in early viral infection control and tumor surveillance

#### 6.1.2.3. COMPLEMENT SYSTEM

* **Proteolytic cascade system** similar to coagulation pathway
* **Two activation pathways** (classical and alternative)
* **Final common pathway** leading to membrane attack complex formation
* **Multiple effects:** cell lysis, inflammation, opsonization

### 6.1.3. CLINICAL RELEVANCE
* ⚠️ **Compromised barrier function** increases infection risk during:
	* Invasive procedures and surgery
	* Prolonged mechanical ventilation
	* Immunosuppressive therapy
* **Anesthesia considerations** for patients with innate immune defects

---

## 6.2. ACQUIRED/ADAPTIVE IMMUNITY

**Specific, powerful immune response that develops memory and improves with repeated exposure**

### 6.2.1. KEY CHARACTERISTICS
* **Depends on lymphocytes** for primary function
* **Powerful and specific** responses to particular antigens
* **Delayed onset** especially on initial contact with new antigens
* **Immunologic memory** greatly reduces response delay on repeat exposure to same antigen
* **Two major divisions:** humoral and cellular immunity

### 6.2.2. HUMORAL IMMUNITY
* **Primary cells:** B-lymphocytes, plasma cells, memory B-cells
* **Function:** Production of specific antibodies against antigens
* **Mechanism:**
	* B-lymphocyte encounters matching antigen → activation
	* Differentiation into plasma cells and memory B-cells
	* Plasma cells produce large quantities of specific antibodies
* **Memory component:** Memory B-cells provide rapid response upon re-exposure

### 6.2.3. CELLULAR IMMUNITY
* **Primary cells:** T-lymphocytes, memory T-cells
* **Function:** Direct cellular attack and immune system coordination
* **Types:**
	* Cytotoxic T-cells (CD8+) for direct cellular destruction
	* Helper T-cells (CD4+) for immune system coordination
	* Suppressor T-cells for immune regulation
	* Memory T-cells for rapid future responses

### 6.2.4. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS
* **Vaccination principles:** Small antigen exposure → robust memory response
* **Anesthesia implications:**
	* Patients on immunosuppressive therapy
	* Risk of opportunistic infections in immunocompromised patients
	* ⚠️ **Anaphylaxis risk** from previous drug/allergen exposure

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The "8-8 rule" for MHC matching: CD8 × MHC I = 8, CD4 × MHC II = 8

---

## 6.3. IMMUNE SYSTEM INTEGRATION
**Coordinated interaction between innate and adaptive immunity for optimal host defense**

### 6.3.1. ANTIGEN PRESENTATION PROCESS

* **Antigen processing:** Macrophages/dendritic cells engulf and process antigens
* **MHC presentation:** Antigen fragments displayed on MHC molecules
* **T-cell activation:** Specific T-cell receptors bind to antigen-MHC complexes
* **Cascade activation:** Activated T-cells coordinate broader immune response

### 6.3.2. MEMORY DEVELOPMENT

* **Primary response:** Initial slow response to new antigen
* **Memory formation:** Generation of memory B and T cells
* **Secondary response:** Rapid, robust response upon re-exposure
* **Clinical relevance:** Basis for vaccination and allergy development

---

# 7. HUMORAL IMMUNITY

**B-lymphocyte mediated antibody production for specific antigen neutralization**

## 7.1. B-LYMPHOCYTES AND PLASMA CELLS

**Specialized lymphocytes responsible for antibody production and humoral immune memory**

### 7.1.1. B-LYMPHOCYTE CHARACTERISTICS
* **Each B-lymphocyte differentiates into one specific type of plasma cell**
* **Produces only one type of antibody** with unique antigen specificity
* **Antigen recognition:** B-cell surface receptors bind to specific matching antigens
* **Clonal expansion:** Upon activation, single B-cell produces multiple identical plasma cells

### 7.1.2. PLASMA CELL DEVELOPMENT AND FUNCTION
* **Differentiation trigger:** B-lymphocyte encounters its matching antigen
* **Primary function:** High-volume production of specific antibodies
* **Antibody secretion:** Plasma cells release large quantities of immunoglobulins into circulation
* **Limited lifespan:** Plasma cells have finite productive period

### 7.1.3. MEMORY B-CELL FORMATION
* **Dual differentiation:** Some stimulated B-cells become memory cells rather than plasma cells
* **Enhanced response characteristics:**
	* Faster activation upon subsequent antigen contact
	* Larger antibody response to subsequent contact with same antigen
	* Long-term immunologic memory maintenance
* **Clinical significance:** Basis for vaccination effectiveness and allergic sensitization

---

## 7.2. ANTIBODY CLASSES AND FUNCTIONS

**Five distinct immunoglobulin classes with specific structural and functional characteristics**

### 7.2.1. IMMUNOGLOBULIN CLASSIFICATION
* **Classification basis:** Type of heavy chain present in antibody structure
* **Five classes:** IgM, IgG, IgA, IgD, IgE
* **Heavy and light chain structure:** Each antibody composed of heavy and light protein chains

### 7.2.2. SPECIFIC IMMUNOGLOBULIN FUNCTIONS
* **IgM:**
	* Early antibody response in primary immune reactions
	* Large molecular size limits tissue penetration
* **IgG:**
	* Major circulating antibody in secondary immune responses
	* Crosses placental barrier for maternal-fetal antibody transfer
* **IgA:**
	* Mucosal immunity in respiratory and gastrointestinal tracts
	* Secretory component allows function in body secretions
* **IgD:**
	* Surface receptor function on naive B-lymphocytes
	* Role in B-cell activation and differentiation
* **IgE:**
	* ⚠️ **Primary mediator of anaphylactic reactions**
	* Binds to mast cells and basophils for immediate hypersensitivity

### 7.2.3. CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS
* **Anaphylaxis mechanism:** IgE-mediated mast cell degranulation
* **Blood transfusion reactions:** IgG and IgM antibodies against blood group antigens
* **Mucosal protection:** IgA deficiency increases respiratory infection risk

---

## 7.3. ANTIGEN-ANTIBODY INTERACTIONS

**Detailed mechanism of humoral immune response from antigen encounter to antibody production**

### 7.3.1. INITIAL ANTIGEN PROCESSING
* **B-cell activation:** B-cell encounters and binds to its matching antigen
* **Antigen engulfment:** B-cell engulfs the bound antigen through endocytosis
* **Antigen digestion:** Intracellular processing breaks antigen into smaller fragments
* **MHC display:** Processed antigen fragments displayed on unique MHC molecules

### 7.3.2. T-CELL INTERACTION AND COORDINATION
* **MHC-T-cell binding:** Antigen-MHC complex attracts and binds to matching T-cell
* **T-cell activation:** Helper T-cell (CD4+) recognizes antigen presentation
* **Cytokine secretion:** Activated T-cell releases lymphokines including:
	* IL-2 (Interleukin-2)
	* IL-6 (Interleukin-6)
	* IFN-γ (Interferon-gamma)
	* Granulocyte-monocyte colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF)

### 7.3.3. IMMUNE SYSTEM AMPLIFICATION
**T-cell lymphokines stimulate multiple immune components:**

* **Phagocytic activity enhancement:** Increased macrophage function and efficiency
* **Cytotoxic T-cell proliferation:** Expansion of CD8+ T-cell populations
* **B-lymphocyte effects:**
	* Proliferation of antigen-specific B-lymphocytes
	* Differentiation into antibody-producing plasma cells
* **Positive feedback:** Further activation of helper T-cells creating amplification loop

### 7.3.4. ANTIBODY-ANTIGEN COMPLEX FORMATION AND CLEARANCE
* **Antibody release:** Plasma cells secrete antibodies into blood circulation
* **Antigen binding:** Antibodies lock onto matching antigens forming immune complexes
* **Complex clearance mechanisms:**
	* Complement cascade activation
	* Liver and spleen processing
	* Immune complex removal from circulation

### 7.3.5. CLINICAL CORRELATIONS
* **Vaccination response:** This entire mechanism underlies vaccine-induced immunity
* **Drug allergies:** Previous drug exposure can sensitize through this pathway
* **⚠️ Anaphylaxis prevention:** Understanding prior exposure history critical for anesthetic planning

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The humoral immune response explains why first-time drug exposures rarely cause anaphylaxis, but subsequent exposures can trigger severe reactions

---

# 8. CELLULAR IMMUNITY

**T-lymphocyte mediated immune responses providing direct cellular defense and immune system coordination**

## 8.1. T-LYMPHOCYTE TYPES AND FUNCTIONS

**Specialized lymphocytes providing cellular immune responses and immune system regulation**

### 8.1.1. MAJOR T-LYMPHOCYTE CATEGORIES
* **Cytotoxic T-cells (CD8+ cells):** Direct cellular destruction mechanisms
* **Helper T-cells (CD4+ cells):** Immune system coordination and regulation
* **Suppressor T-cells:** Negative feedback and immune response modulation
* **Memory T-cells:** Rapid response capability for previously encountered antigens

### 8.1.2. SUPPRESSOR T-CELL FUNCTION
* **Negative feedback mechanism:** Provides regulatory control of immune responses
* **Target cells:** Suppresses activity of both cytotoxic T-cells and helper T-cells
* **Immune balance:** Prevents excessive immune responses and autoimmune reactions
* **Clinical relevance:** Dysfunction can lead to autoimmune disorders or immunodeficiency

### 8.1.3. MEMORY T-CELL FUNCTION
* **Immunologic memory:** Maintains long-term recognition of previously encountered antigens
* **Rapid response:** Accelerated activation upon re-exposure to familiar antigens
* **Enhanced efficiency:** Faster and more robust cellular immune response
* **Clinical application:** Basis for cellular immunity component of vaccination

---

## 8.2. CYTOTOXIC T-CELLS (CD8+ CELLS)

**Specialized T-lymphocytes that directly destroy infected or abnormal cells**

### 8.2.1. ACTIVATION MECHANISM
* **T-cell receptor (TCR) binding:** Cytotoxic T-cell binds to specific short peptide antigen bound to MHC Class I protein
* **Antigen source:** Peptide derived from degraded foreign protein of infecting agent
* **Self vs non-self recognition:** MHC Class I presentation allows recognition of intracellular infections
* **Dual activation:** Also activates specific B-lymphocytes for coordinated immune response

### 8.2.2. MHC CLASS I INTERACTION
* **Memory aid:** CD8 × MHC Class I = 8 (mathematical relationship for recall)
* **Antigen presentation:** All nucleated cells present intracellular peptides on MHC Class I
* **Foreign antigen display:** Infected cells present foreign peptides from intracellular pathogens
* **Recognition specificity:** CD8+ T-cells specifically recognize foreign peptides on MHC Class I

### 8.2.3. CYTOTOXIC MECHANISMS
* **Perforin insertion:** Cytotoxic substances create pores in target cell membrane
* **Membrane perforation:** Perforins form channels allowing cytotoxic substances to enter
* **Enzymatic injection:** Cytotoxic enzymes injected through perforin-created pores
* **Cellular destruction:** Target cell swells due to electrolyte influx and subsequently dies

### 8.2.4. TARGET CELL DEATH PROCESS
* **Membrane compromise:** Perforin-mediated membrane damage
* **Osmotic effects:** Loss of membrane integrity causes cellular swelling
* **Cellular lysis:** Progressive cell membrane failure leads to cell death
* **Pathogen elimination:** Destruction of infected cells eliminates intracellular pathogens

---

## 8.3. HELPER T-CELLS (CD4+ CELLS)

**Central coordinators of immune system responses through lymphokine secretion**

### 8.3.1. PRIMARY FUNCTIONS
* **Immune system regulation:** Central role in coordinating overall immune responses
* **Lymphokine secretion:** Release of multiple signaling molecules to activate other immune cells
* **Response amplification:** Potentiate entire immune reaction through multiple pathways

### 8.3.2. ACTIVATION MECHANISM
* **MHC Class II interaction:** Activated by binding of TCR to specific peptide antigen bound to MHC Class II proteins
* **Antigen presenting cells:** Interaction occurs with antigen presenting cells (APCs)
* **Primary APCs:** Mostly macrophages or dendritic cells serve as antigen presenters
* **Memory aid:** CD4 × MHC Class II = 8 (mathematical relationship for recall)

### 8.3.3. LYMPHOKINE PRODUCTION AND EFFECTS
**Helper T-cells secrete multiple lymphokines with diverse immune effects:**

#### 8.3.3.1. SPECIFIC LYMPHOKINES RELEASED

* **IL-2 (Interleukin-2):** T-cell proliferation and activation
* **IL-6 (Interleukin-6):** B-cell differentiation and acute phase response
* **IFN-γ (Interferon-gamma):** Macrophage activation and antiviral effects
* **Granulocyte-monocyte colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF):** White blood cell production

#### 8.3.3.2. TARGET CELL STIMULATION EFFECTS

* **Phagocytic activity enhancement:**
	* Increased macrophage function and efficiency
	* Enhanced pathogen clearance capabilities
* **Cytotoxic T-cell proliferation:**
	* Expansion of CD8+ T-cell populations
	* Increased cellular immune capacity
* **B-lymphocyte effects:**
	* Proliferation of antigen-specific B-lymphocytes
	* Differentiation into antibody-producing plasma cells
* **Positive feedback mechanism:**
	* Further activation of helper T-cells
	* Creates amplification loop for immune response

---

## 8.4. MHC COMPLEX SYSTEM

**Major histocompatibility complex proteins essential for antigen presentation and T-cell activation**

### 8.4.1. MHC CLASS I SYSTEM
* **CD8+ T-cell interaction:** Specific binding site for cytotoxic T-lymphocytes
* **Cellular distribution:** Present on all nucleated cells in the body
* **Antigen presentation:** Displays intracellular peptides for immune surveillance
* **Function:** Allows detection of intracellular infections or abnormalities

### 8.4.2. MHC CLASS II SYSTEM
* **CD4+ T-cell interaction:** Specific binding site for helper T-lymphocytes
* **Cellular distribution:** Present on antigen presenting cells (macrophages, dendritic cells)
* **Antigen presentation:** Displays processed external antigens
* **Function:** Initiates coordinated immune responses through helper T-cell activation

### 8.4.3. CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE [PART B/C]

* **Tissue transplantation:** MHC matching critical for transplant compatibility
* **Autoimmune diseases:** MHC dysfunction can lead to inappropriate immune responses
* **Drug reactions:** Some drug allergies involve MHC-mediated antigen presentation
* **⚠️ Immunosuppression risks:** Patients on immunosuppressive therapy have compromised MHC function

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The "8-8 rule" (CD8×MHC I = 8, CD4×MHC II = 8) helps remember which T-cell type interacts with which MHC class

---

# 9. COMPLEMENT SYSTEM

**Proteolytic cascade system providing innate immunity through cell lysis and inflammatory responses**

## 9.1. COMPLEMENT SYSTEM OVERVIEW

**Innate immunity component with proteolytic cascade similar to coagulation pathway**

### 9.1.1. BASIC CHARACTERISTICS
* **Part of innate immunity:** No memory component, consistent response upon activation
* **Proteolytic system:** Sequential enzyme activation similar in morphology to coagulation cascade
* **Two activation pathways:** Classical and alternative pathways with different triggers
* **Final common pathway:** Both pathways converge at breakdown of C3 component

### 9.1.2. COMPLEMENT COMPONENT NOMENCLATURE
* **Primary components:** C1 through C9 designation system
* **Activation fragments:** Components split into "a" and "b" fragments (e.g., C3a, C3b)
* **Enzyme complexes:** Multiple components combine to form convertase enzymes
* **Membrane attack complex:** Final C5b-9 complex formation

---

## 9.2. CLASSICAL PATHWAY

**Antibody-dependent complement activation leading to membrane attack complex formation**

### 9.2.1. ACTIVATION MECHANISM
* **Trigger:** Activated by certain antigen-antibody complexes (IgM or IgG)
* **Clinical significance:** Major mechanism in blood transfusion reactions
* **Initial component:** C1 complex activation starts the cascade

### 9.2.2. SEQUENTIAL ACTIVATION PROCESS
#### 9.2.2.1. C1 COMPLEX ACTIVATION

* **C1 components:** Consists of C1q, C1r, C1s (three separate complexes forming C1)
* **Antigen-antibody binding:** C1 complex binds to antigen-antibody complexes
* **Conformational change:** Binding triggers C1 activation and proteolytic activity

#### 9.2.2.2. C4 AND C2 CLEAVAGE

* **C4 breakdown:** C1 cleaves C4 into C4a and C4b fragments
* **C2 breakdown:** C1 cleaves C2 into C2a and C2b fragments
* **Fragment functions:** Different fragments have distinct biological activities

#### 9.2.2.3. C3 CONVERTASE FORMATION

* **Complex assembly:** C4a and C2b combine to form C3 convertase (C4a-C2b)
* **C3 cleavage:** C3 convertase splits C3 into C3a and C3b fragments
* **C3b function:** C3b forms part of C5 convertase complex

#### 9.2.2.4. C5 CONVERTASE AND FINAL PATHWAY

* **C5 convertase formation:** C3b combines with C3 convertase to form C5 convertase
* **C5 cleavage:** C5 convertase cleaves C5 into C5a and C5b fragments
* **Membrane attack complex initiation:** C5b begins assembly of final cytolytic complex

#### 9.2.2.5. MEMBRANE ATTACK COMPLEX ASSEMBLY

* **Component assembly:** C5b combines with C6, C7, C8, and C9
* **C5b-9 complex:** Forms cylindrical membrane attack complex
* **Membrane insertion:** Complex inserts into target cell membrane creating pores
* **Cell lysis:** Membrane perforation leads to osmotic cell swelling and death

---

## 9.3. ALTERNATIVE PATHWAY

**Antibody-independent complement activation providing immediate innate immune response**

### 9.3.1. ACTIVATION MECHANISM
* **C3 spontaneous conversion:** C3 undergoes spontaneous conversion to hydrolyzed C3
* **No antibody requirement:** Can be activated in the absence of antibodies
* **Factor B and D interaction:** Hydrolyzed C3 interacts with factors B and D
* **Alternative C3 convertase:** Forms complex which can split C3 to C3a and C3b

### 9.3.2. C3B AMPLIFICATION PROCESS
* **Foreign surface binding:** C3b interacts with certain membranes including:
	* Bacterial cell membranes
	* Parasite surfaces
	* Other foreign cellular structures
* **Factor B and D recruitment:** C3b bound to foreign surfaces recruits factors B and D
* **C3 convertase formation:** Different C3 convertase formation compared to classical pathway
* **Amplification loop:** Generated C3b can bind more factor B and D, creating positive feedback

### 9.3.3. FINAL COMMON PATHWAY CONVERGENCE
* **C3 breakdown:** Both pathways converge at the stage of C3 component breakdown
* **C5 convertase formation:** Alternative pathway C3b also participates in C5 convertase assembly
* **Membrane attack complex:** Same C5b-9 complex formation as classical pathway
* **Identical final effects:** Cell lysis and inflammatory mediator release

---

## 9.4. EFFECTS OF COMPLEMENT ACTIVATION

**Multiple biological effects resulting from complement cascade activation**

### 9.4.1. DIRECT CYTOLYTIC EFFECTS
* **Cell lysis of bacteria:** Membrane attack complex creates pores in bacterial cell walls
* **Osmotic cell death:** Membrane perforation allows uncontrolled ion flux
* **Target cell destruction:** Effective elimination of complement-coated pathogens

### 9.4.2. INFLAMMATORY MEDIATOR RELEASE
* **C3a and C5a effects:** Minor complement fragments with major inflammatory activity
* **Mast cell degranulation:** C3a and C5a trigger histamine release from:
	* Mast cells
	* Basophils
	* Neutrophils
* **Inflammatory cascade:** Released mediators initiate broader inflammatory response

### 9.4.3. VASCULAR AND CELLULAR EFFECTS
* **Local vasodilation:** Histamine and other mediators cause localized blood vessel dilation
* **Neutrophil aggregation:** Complement fragments attract and activate neutrophils
* **Chemotaxis:** C5a provides chemotactic signal to attract phagocytes
* **Enhanced immune response:** Multiple mechanisms amplify local immune activity

### 9.4.4. OPSONIZATION PROCESS
* **Bacterial coating:** C3b opsonization coats target cells (e.g., bacteria)
* **Phagocyte binding sites:** Opsonization provides specific binding sites for phagocytes
* **Enhanced phagocytosis:** C3b receptors on phagocytes facilitate bacterial uptake
* **Improved clearance:** Opsonized bacteria more efficiently removed by immune system

### 9.4.5. CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS
* **⚠️ Transfusion reactions:** Classical pathway activation causes major hemolytic reactions
* **Anaphylactoid reactions:** Complement activation can mimic anaphylaxis
* **Sepsis pathophysiology:** Alternative pathway contributes to septic shock syndrome
* **Bacterial clearance:** Important mechanism for preventing and controlling infections

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Complement activation can cause anaphylactoid reactions that clinically resemble true anaphylaxis but do not require prior sensitization

# 10. CLINICAL IMMUNE DISORDERS

**Inherited and acquired immune system dysfunction with anesthetic implications**

## 10.1. C1 ESTERASE INHIBITOR DEFICIENCY

**Inherited deficiency leading to uncontrolled complement activation and severe angioedema**

### 10.1.1. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY
* **C1 esterase inhibitor function:** Involved in regulation of several plasma protease systems including complement
* **Regulatory role:** Prevents excessive activation and degradation of complement pathway
* **Deficiency effects:** Loss of inhibitory control leads to spontaneous complement activation
* **Cascade dysfunction:** Uncontrolled complement pathway generates excessive inflammatory mediators

### 10.1.2. GENETIC PATTERN
* **Inheritance pattern:** Autosomal dominant inheritance
* **Clinical designation:** Also known as hereditary angioedema
* **Penetrance:** High penetrance with variable expression severity
* **Family history:** Often positive family history of similar episodes

### 10.1.3. CLINICAL MANIFESTATIONS
* **Episodic angioedema:** Recurrent episodes of severe tissue swelling
* **Precipitating factors:** Minor trauma may precipitate severe angioedema episodes
* **Unpredictable onset:** Episodes can occur spontaneously without obvious triggers
* **Variable severity:** Range from mild localized swelling to life-threatening airway compromise

### 10.1.4. AIRWAY INVOLVEMENT AND EMERGENCY MANAGEMENT
* **⚠️ Life-threatening complication:** Angioedema of airway structures
* **Anatomical involvement:**
	* Laryngeal edema causing stridor and airway obstruction
	* Tongue and pharyngeal swelling compromising upper airway
	* Progressive nature can lead to complete airway occlusion
* **Emergency interventions required:**
	* Emergency cricothyroidotomy for airway access
	* Emergency tracheostomy as definitive airway management
* **Anesthetic considerations:** High-risk patients for airway management

### 10.1.5. PHARMACOLOGICAL MANAGEMENT
* **Danazol therapy:** Long-term use effective in preventing attacks
* **Prophylactic treatment:** Reduces frequency and severity of angioedema episodes
* **Mechanism:** Danazol increases C1 esterase inhibitor production
* **Maintenance therapy:** Ongoing treatment required for attack prevention

### 10.1.6. PERIOPERATIVE IMPLICATIONS [PART B/C]

* **Preoperative assessment:** Identify patients with history of hereditary angioedema
* **Anesthetic planning:** Prepare for potential difficult or emergency airway management
* **⚠️ Trigger avoidance:** Minimize trauma and stress that could precipitate episodes
* **Emergency preparedness:** Immediate access to surgical airway equipment

# 11. HEMATOPOIETIC REGULATION

**Hormonal control of red blood cell production and hematopoietic function** Tags: #pA_endocrine #pA_hematology #pB_periop #pC_complications Part A Reference: Endocrine (Page 6) - Kidney endocrine functions Part A Reference: Hematology (Page 6-7) - Blood constituent production Part B Reference: C5 Perioperative Medicine (Page 10) - Hematological disorders Part C Reference: Complications of therapy

## 11.1. ERYTHROPOIETIN SYSTEM

**Kidney-derived hormone regulating red blood cell production in response to tissue oxygenation**

### 11.1.1. HORMONE PRODUCTION AND SOURCE
* **Primary production site:** Kidney in adults
* **Alternative production:** Liver in fetus during development
* **Quantitative distribution:** 10-15% of synthesis occurs in liver in adults
* **Cellular origin:** Interstitial cells of peritubular capillaries in kidney

### 11.1.2. PHYSIOLOGICAL STIMULUS AND REGULATION
* **Primary stimulus:** Hypoxia (reduced oxygen delivery to tissues)
* **Oxygen delivery concept:** Related to DO₂ (oxygen delivery = cardiac output × oxygen content)
* **Hypoxic conditions triggering EPO release:**
	* Hypoxic hypoxia (reduced inspired oxygen)
	* Anemic hypoxia (reduced hemoglobin concentration)
	* Circulatory hypoxia (reduced cardiac output)
	* Histotoxic hypoxia (cellular oxygen utilization problems)

### 11.1.3. MECHANISM OF ACTION
* **Target organ:** Bone marrow immature erythroid cells
* **Cellular effects:**
	* Differentiation of immature erythroid cells into mature red blood cells
	* Proliferation of erythroid cell populations
	* Enhanced red blood cell production rate
* **Receptor interaction:** Binds to and activates cell-membrane erythropoietin receptors
* **Dose-response relationship:** Higher EPO levels produce greater erythropoietic response

### 11.1.4. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS AND COMPLICATIONS
* **Recombinant erythropoietin:** Synthetic EPO available as recormon
* **Clinical indications:**
	* End-stage renal failure with associated anemia
	* Chronic anemia from various causes
* **⚠️ Complications of therapy:**
	* Polycythemia (excessive red blood cell production)
	* Increased stroke risk due to hyperviscosity
	* Thrombotic complications from elevated hematocrit

### 11.1.5. PERIOPERATIVE CONSIDERATIONS [PART B/C]

* **Anemia assessment:** Understanding EPO response in perioperative anemia
* **Renal failure patients:** Often have blunted EPO response requiring supplementation
* **⚠️ Polycythemia risks:** Patients on EPO therapy may have increased bleeding/clotting risks
* **Transfusion decisions:** EPO therapy may influence perioperative transfusion thresholds

## 11.2. HORMONE VS VITAMIN DISTINCTIONS

**Fundamental differences between endogenous hormones and exogenous vitamins**

### 11.2.1. HORMONE CHARACTERISTICS
* **Source:** Endogenous chemical messenger produced within the body
* **Production sites:** Secreted into blood by various ductless glands including:
	* Pituitary gland
	* Thyroid gland
	* Adrenal glands
	* Pancreatic islet cells
* **Receptor interaction:** Combines with specific receptors on distant target cells
* **Metabolic effects:** Produces changes in metabolism of target cells
* **Concentration:** Required in relatively larger physiological amounts

### 11.2.2. VITAMIN CHARACTERISTICS
* **Source:** Organic substance that cannot be produced within the body
* **Acquisition:** Obtained from exogenous sources, usually dietary intake
* **Quantity required:** Required only in small amounts for normal physiological function
* **Essential nature:** Essential for survival of organism
* **Biochemical function:** Affects specific biochemical reactions in the body
* **Energy source:** Not used as caloric source for cellular metabolism

### 11.2.3. FAT-SOLUBLE VITAMINS
* **Classification:** Vitamins A, D, E, K are fat-soluble
* **Absorption:** Require dietary fat for proper absorption
* **Storage:** Can be stored in body fat tissue
* **Deficiency pattern:** Deficiencies develop slowly due to body stores

### 11.2.4. CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS [PART B/C]

* **Perioperative nutrition:** Understanding vitamin requirements in prolonged fasting
* **Fat-soluble vitamin absorption:** Malabsorption syndromes affect vitamins A, D, E, K
* **⚠️ Vitamin K deficiency:** Can cause coagulopathy affecting perioperative bleeding risk
* **Hormone replacement:** Some conditions require hormone rather than vitamin supplementation

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Erythropoietin exemplifies a hormone (endogenously produced, larger quantities, specific target organs) while vitamins require external sources and work in small amounts on biochemical pathways
