## 1. MONITORING STANDARDS AND INTEGRATION
<!-- EPA-6:K3:equipment_monitoring EPA-6:K6:cardiovascular_monitoring -->

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Cardiovascular Physiology|Part A]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3: Perioperative Management and Monitoring|Part B]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Systems-Based Practice|Part C]]

**Monitoring is essential for patient safety during anesthesia, requiring both clinical observation and technological support.**

#### 1.1.1. ASA STANDARDS FOR BASIC ANESTHETIC MONITORING


* **Standard 1:** Qualified anaesthesia personnel shall be present in the room throughout the conduct of all general anesthesia, regional anesthesia and monitored anesthesia care.
* **Standard 2:** During all anaesthetics, the patients **oxygenation, ventilation, circulation** and **temperature** shall be **continually** evaluated.

  * **Oxygenation**:

    * Quantitative method - pulse oximetry with variable pitch tone
    * Inspired gas FiO2 analyzer + Low O2 concentration alarm
  * **Circulation**:

    * ECG - minimum 3 lead; else 5 lead with cardiac concern
    * BP minimum cycle Q5 min
    * At least one additional continual circulatory assessment: pulse oximetry, arterial line tracing, palpable pulse, auscultation, or doppler
  * **Ventilation**:

    * Capnography
    * Disconnect alarm
  * **Temperature**:

    * Probe required if clinically significant changes in body temperature anticipated
#### 1.1.2. INTEGRATED MONITORING SYSTEMS


* **Evolution of monitoring technology:** From discrete, bulky monitoring devices adorning the anesthetic machine to integrated systems with a single visual display .
* **Modern integrated systems** feature:

  * Plug-in monitoring modules feeding a single visual display
 
  * Color-coded values and waveforms that can be arranged according to user preference
 
  * Improved ergonomics and workflow flexibility
 
* **Clinical observation remains vital** despite technological advances. Key observations include
 
  * Skin perfusion and capillary refill
 
  * Presence of cyanosis or pallor
 
  * Skin temperature and turgor
 
  * Chest movement and heart auscultation
 
* **Emerging trends** in monitoring technology
 
  * Attempts to use wire-free devices with Bluetooth or wireless internet connectivity
 
  * Currently limited acceptance in clinical practice 

**Bottom Line:** ASA monitoring standards establish minimum requirements for patient safety, requiring qualified personnel presence and continuous monitoring of oxygenation, ventilation, circulation, and temperature. Modern integrated monitoring systems facilitate comprehensive patient monitoring with improved ergonomics, but clinical observation remains essential for effective patient assessment.

## 2. ELECTROCARDIOGRAPHY (ECG)

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Cardiovascular Physiology|Part A]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3: Perioperative Management and Monitoring|Part B]]

**ECG monitoring provides continuous assessment of cardiac electrical activity, heart rate, and rhythm.**

### 2.1. PRINCIPLES AND COMPONENTS


* **Definition:** The ECG monitors the electrical activity of the heart with electrical potentials measuring 0.5–2 mV at the skin surface .
* **Clinical value:** Provides information about heart rate, ischemia, arrhythmias, and conduction defects but **does not** assess cardiac output .
* **Lead types:**
  * **Bipolar leads** (I, II, III, AVR, AVL and AVF) measure voltage difference between two electrodes .

  * **Unipolar leads** (V1–V6) measure voltage at different electrodes relative to a zero point .

#### 2.1.1. KEY COMPONENTS OF ECG MONITORING SYSTEM

1. **Skin electrodes:** 
   * Use silver and silver chloride as a stable electrode combination
 
   * Electrodes held in a cup and separated from skin by a foam pad soaked in conducting gel
 
   * Polarization of silver/silver chloride electrode occurs with changes in skin potential difference 

1. **Connecting cables:** 
   * Color-coded cables transmit the polarization signal from electrodes to monitor
 
   * Available in 3and 5-lead versions as snap or grabber design
 
   * All cables in a particular set should have the same length to minimize electromagnetic interference 

1. **Signal amplification:** 
   * ECG signal boosted using an amplifier with frequency range of 0.05–150 Hz
 
   * Filters used to remove noise/artifacts while preserving the diagnostic signal 

1. **Display system:** 
   * High-resolution monochrome or color monitor used to display the amplified ECG signal 

### 2.2. TYPES OF ECG MONITORING SYSTEMS

#### 2.2.1. 3-ELECTRODE SYSTEM


* Monitors leads I, II, III - only one lead (electrode pair) can be examined while the 3rd electrode serves as ground* Lead II - best to see sinus rhythm and p waves, also better at seeing inferior wall ischaemia* Limited by poor sensitivity for ST changes, but good for atrial arrhythmia detection and R wave changes
#### 2.2.2. MODIFIED 3-ELECTRODE SYSTEM


* If concerned for anterior wall ischemia, can move L arm lead to V5 position and monitor lead I
#### 2.2.3. 5-ELECTRODE SYSTEM


* 4 limb leads + V5 (L anterior axillary line, 5th ICS) - can monitor 7 leads* V5 is 75% sensitive for detecting ischemic events - as it lies over 5th ICS - good for detection of anterior and lateral wall leads* A modified V5 can be monitored by rearranging the standard three-limb lead placement
![Untitled](CVS_MONITORING_CVS_MONITORING_Untitled.png_62bf9a.png_3b8990.png)


* II + V5 - 80% sensitive
  * Ideally, because each lead provides unique information, leads II and V5 should be monitored simultaneously
  * If only a single-channel machine is available, the preferred lead for monitoring depends on the location of any prior infarction or ischemia and whether arrhythmia or ischemia appears to be the greater concern* II + V4 + V5 - 98% sensitive
### 2.3. ELECTRODE PLACEMENT AND CONFIGURATION


* **Proper attachment technique:** 
  * Clean the skin and gently abrade the stratum corneum
 
  * Ensure adequate contact using conductive gel
 
  * Position on bony prominences to reduce respiratory artifacts
 
  * Note that skin impedance varies at different sites and may be higher in females
 
* **Placement mnemonic:** "Smoke over fire, clouds over grass, white on the right" (Black lead over red, White over green, white lead on right)* **Standard 3-electrode setup:** RA, LA, LL
![Untitled](CVS_MONITORING_CVS_MONITORING_Untitled_1.png_afd139.png_b769ea.png)


* **CM5 configuration:** Used for detecting left ventricular ischemia with 66%-75% sensitivity
 
  * Right-arm electrode positioned on the manubrium (chest lead from manubrium)
 
  * Left-arm electrode on V5 position (fifth interspace in left anterior axillary line)
 
  * Indifferent lead on left shoulder or any convenient position
 
* **CB5 configuration:** Useful during thoracic anesthesia
 
  * Right-arm electrode positioned over center of right scapula
 
  * Left-arm electrode over V5 position 

### 2.4. ECG PRINCIPLES AND SIGNAL PROCESSING


* **Cardiac electrical activity:** During myocardial contraction, a wave of depolarisation passes from the SA node to the AV node, and down the conducting pathways to ventricles* **Signal attenuation:** From an original depolarisation of approximately 90-100 mV, the attenuation of the signal in the body tissues means that only 1-2 mV is detectable on the skin* **Signal amplitude variation:** Thinner myocardium generates a smaller signal, so the atrial ***P*** wave is much smaller on an ECG than the ventricular ***QRS*** complex* **Lead detection principle:** A lead will detect a positive deflection if the ***net*** depolarisation is in the same ***vector plane*** as the lead, and a negative deflection if in the opposite direction
#### 2.4.1. SIGNAL PROCESSING AND FILTERING
 

* **Filter types used for ECG signals:** 
  * **High-pass filters:** Attenuate frequency components below a certain frequency
 
    * Remove lower frequency noise from the signal
 
    * Example: A 1-Hz high-pass filter can remove respiratory component of an ECG
 
    * Center the signal around the zero isoline
 
  
  * **Low-pass filters:** Attenuate frequency components above a certain frequency
 
    * Block higher frequencies and allow lower frequency content to pass
 
    * Example: A 35-Hz low-pass filter will remove/attenuate signals above 35 Hz
 
    * Help to reduce interference from diathermy, muscle shivering, spasms and other movement artifacts
 
* **ECG monitor modes:** 
  * **Monitoring mode:** Limited frequency response of 0.5–40 Hz
 
    * Filters narrow the bandwidth to reduce environmental artifacts
 
    * High-frequency filters reduce distortions from muscle movement, mains current and electromagnetic interference
 
    * Low-frequency filters provide stable baseline by reducing respiratory and body movement artifacts
 
  
  * **Diagnostic mode:** Wider frequency response of 0.05–150 Hz
 
    * High-frequency limit allows assessment of ST segment, QRS morphology and tachyarrhythmias
 
    * Low-frequency limit allows representation of Pand T-wave morphology and ST-segment analysis 

### 2.5. LEAD POSITIONING AND FUNCTIONS


* **EINTHOVEN'S LAW:** If two points of a triangle are known, the third can be calculated; for ECG, only two leads are required to calculate the vector of the third* **CM5 Position**:

  * Red - sternum
  * Yellow - left shoulder
  * Green - left leg or hip
  * Lead I - red and yellow
  * Lead II - red and green
  * Lead III - yellow and green* **Augmented Leads (AVR calculation)**:

  * Calculated from leads I and II
  * When three electrodes are connected via the standard limb leads to a common central terminal, a zero potential can be obtained producing a 'common electrode'
  * Comparing this to the limb leads means the potential differences can be measured
  * These potential differences are very small, so are 'augmented' artificially* **Chest Lead Placement**:

  * V1 - 4th intercostal space right of the sternum
  * V2 - 4th intercostal space left of the sternum
  * V3 - Halfway between V2 and V4
  * V4 - 5th intercostal space in the midclavicular line
  * V5 - Halfway between V4 and V6
  * V6 - 5th intercostal space, mid axillary line* **Lead Relationships to Cardiac Areas**:

  * Anterior: V3 and V4
  * Septal: V1 and V2
  * Lateral: I, aVL, V5 and V6
  * Inferior: II, III and aVF* **Right Lower Limb Lead Function**:

  * Differential filter to reduce background noise* **ECG CALIBRATION:** Standard: 25mm/s and 10mm/mV
### 2.6. TECHNICAL CONSIDERATIONS AND TROUBLESHOOTING


* **INTERFERENCE SOURCES**  * Diathermy
  * Muscle movement
  * MRI* **Common ECG signal interference types:** 
  * **Electromagnetic induction:**
    * Sources: Electrical cables, lights
 
    * Prevention: Use long and twisted ECG leads to reject the induced signal as common mode
 
    * Solution: Use selective filters in amplifiers
 
  
  * **Electrostatic induction and capacitance coupling:**
    * Sources: Stray capacitances between table, lights, monitors, patients and electrical cables
 
    * Prevention: ECG leads surrounded by copper screens
 
  
  * **Radiofrequency interference (>150 Hz):**
    * Sources: Diathermy entering system via mains supply, direct probe application, radio transmission
 
    * Prevention:

      * High-frequency filters to clean up signal before entering input
 
      * Filtering power supply of amplifiers
 
      * Double screen electronic components of amplifiers and earth outer screen
 
      * Newer machines operate at higher frequencies to avoid interference
 
* **Electrical interference reduction techniques:** 
  * **Shielding** of cables and leads
 
  * **Differential amplifiers** measure difference between two input terminals
 
    * If interference is common to both input terminals (e.g., mains frequency), it can be eliminated
 
    * This is called **common mode rejection ratio (CMRR)** 
    * High-quality ECG amplifiers have CMRR of 100,000:1 to 1,000,000:1
 
    * Should have high input impedance (about 10 MΩ) to minimize current drawn from electrodes
 
  
  * **Electronic filters** to remove specific frequency noise
 
* **Muscle activity artifacts:**
  * Positioning electrodes over bony prominences reduces these artifacts
 
  * Use of low-pass filters can help reduce these artifacts
 
* **Safety features:**
  * High and low ventricular rate alarms and audible indicator of ventricular rate
 
  * ST-segment monitoring capability in advanced models for early diagnosis of ischemic changes
 
* ⚠️ **Diathermy-related hazards:**
  * Absence or improper positioning of patient diathermy plate can cause burns at ECG electrode sites
 
  * Burns result from passage of diathermy current via electrodes causing high current density

### 2.7. INVASIVE ELECTROCARDIOGRAM METHODS
 

* **Three invasive approaches to ECG monitoring:** 
  * **Oesophageal ECG:** 
    * Utilizes oesophageal electrodes incorporated into oesophageal stethoscope and temperature probe
 
    * Useful for detecting atrial arrhythmias
 
    * Positioned near posterior aspect of left ventricle, helpful for detecting posterior wall ischemia
 
  * **Intracardiac ECG:** 
    * Electrodes inserted using multipurpose PA flotation catheter
 
    * Typically includes three atrial and two ventricular electrodes
 
    * In addition to ECG recording, electrodes can be used for atrial or atrioventricular pacing
 
    * Great diagnostic capabilities and may be part of implantable defibrillator
 
    * Allows assessment of loci that cannot be monitored by surface electrodes (e.g., bundle of His, ventricular septal activity)
 
  * **Tracheal ECG:** 
    * Uses two electrodes embedded into a tracheal tube
 
    * Especially useful for diagnosing atrial arrhythmias in children 

**Bottom Line:** ECG is essential for monitoring cardiac rhythm and ischemia. Lead II is preferred for rhythm assessment, while V5 (or V4+V5) provides the best sensitivity for ischemia detection. Modern monitoring systems employ various filtering techniques to produce clean signals despite environmental interference. Proper electrode placement is crucial for accurate monitoring, and lead selection should be based on the specific monitoring goals (arrhythmia vs. ischemia detection). When needed, invasive ECG methods can provide specialized monitoring capabilities not available with surface electrodes.

## 3. NON-INVASIVE BLOOD PRESSURE MONITORING

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Cardiovascular Physiology|Part A]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3: Perioperative Management and Monitoring|Part B]]

**Non-invasive blood pressure monitoring provides intermittent measurements of systolic, diastolic, and mean arterial pressure.**

### 3.1. PRINCIPLES AND METHODS


* **Definition:** Oscillometry is the most common method used to measure arterial blood pressure non-invasively during anesthesia .
* **Measured parameters:** Systolic, diastolic, mean arterial pressures, and pulse rate .

    * Intraoperative hypotension associated with postoperative myocardial injury, acute kidney injury, and mortality

    * Harm threshold: ~MAP <65 mm Hg / SBP <90 mm Hg for ~15 minutes

    * Expert opinion is to maintain intraoperative BP targets above MAP ≥60 to 65 or SBP >90 mm Hg

    * Currently insufficient trial evidence to support higher BP targets.
* **Clinical utility:** Provides reliable trend information about blood pressure but less reliable with sudden changes or when minimal changes are clinically relevant .
* **Technical name:** Device for indirect noninvasive automated mean arterial pressure .
* **Basic Methods of Measurement:**  * **Palpation Method**: Estimates systolic BP by feeling arterial pulse return during cuff deflation
  * **Modified Techniques**:

    * Doppler sound return
    * Transduced arterial pressure trace
    * Photoplethysmographic pulse wave (pulse oximeter)* **Korotkoff Sound Method**:

  * Measures both **systolic (SP)** and **diastolic (DP)** pressures
  * Based on turbulent flow sounds from cuff compression
  * **First sound** = Systolic pressure
  * **Sound disappearance/muffling** = Diastolic pressure
  * **Manual auscultation technique** allows identification of Korotkoff sounds:

    * 1 - Tapping sound indicates systolic pressure
    * 2 - Reduction or loss of this tapping sound is the start of the auscultatory gap
    * 3 - Reappearance of tapping sound
    * 4 - Reduction of sound - diastolic pressure
    * 5 - Disappearance of sound
  * **MAP calculation**: MAP = DP + (SP − DP)/3* **Historical devices:** Von Recklinghausen oscillotonometer was widely used in the pre-microprocessor era
 
  * Used two cuffs: upper occluding cuff (5-cm wide) overlapping a lower sensing cuff (10-cm wide)
 
  * Mechanical system using bellows and amplification to detect and display oscillations
 
  * More reliable at low pressures but less accurate for diastolic pressure measurements 

### 3.2. OSCILLOMETRIC TECHNIQUE


* **Definition**: Standard method in modern practice using occluding cuff to detect arterial pulsations* **Measurement Process**:

  * *Deflation Method*:

    * Rapid cuff inflation above expected systolic pressure
    * Sensor verification of no pressure fluctuations
    * Gradual deflation in small decrements
    * Measurement of oscillation magnitude at each interval
    * Rapid deflation once below detection threshold
  * *Inflation Method*:

    * Incremental cuff inflation until oscillations cease
    * Rapid deflation once above systolic pressure
![Oscillometric Measurement](CVS_MONITORING_CVS_MONITORING_image.png_8fc3d4.png_0118f6.png)


* **Working principle:** 
  * Microprocessor controls sequence of inflation and deflation
 
  * Cuff inflation to pressure above previous systolic reading
 
  * Return of blood flow causes oscillation in cuff pressure
 
  * Transducer senses pressure changes, which are interpreted by microprocessor
 
  * Output from transducer passes through filter to amplifier that amplifies oscillations
 
  * Output then processed through analog digital converter and microprocessor
 
* **Pressure determination:** 
  * **Systolic pressure** corresponds to onset of rapidly increasing oscillations
 
  * **Mean arterial pressure** corresponds to maximum oscillation at lowest cuff pressure
 
  * **Diastolic pressure** corresponds to onset of rapidly decreasing oscillations
 
  * Diastolic also mathematically computed from systolic and mean pressure values
 
  * Formula: Mean blood pressure = diastolic blood pressure + 1/3 pulse pressure
 
* **Pressure Measurements**:

  * **Systolic**: Maximum cuff pressure with detectable oscillations
  * **MAP**: Cuff pressure at maximum oscillation amplitude
  * **Diastolic**: Minimum cuff pressure with detectable oscillations
  * ⚠️ **Important Note**: Measurement algorithms vary between devices due to proprietary technology
### 3.3. EQUIPMENT AND SETUP


* **Key components:** 
  1. **Cuff with tubing:** Used for inflation and deflation; some designs have extra tube for transmitting pressure fluctuations 
  2. **Monitoring unit housing:**
     * Microprocessor
 
     * Pressure transducer (accuracy of ±2%)
 
     * Solenoid valve for deflation control
 
     * Display and timing mechanism
 
     * High/low alarm limit settings
 
* **Cuff Size and Positioning**
  * **AHA Guidelines**:

    * Bladder width: ~40% of extremity circumference
    * Bladder length: ≥80% of extremity circumference
  * Cuff must be of correct size (standard guide):
 
    * 3 cm: Neonate
 
    * 5 cm: Infant
 
    * 6 cm: Child
 
    * 9 cm: Small adult
 
    * 12 cm: Standard adult
 
    * 15 cm: Large adult
 
  * Should cover at least two-thirds of upper arm
 
  * Middle of bladder should be positioned over brachial artery
 
* **Venous stasis feature:** Some designs can apply venous stasis to facilitate intravenous cannulation 
- There is also *Finapres* - finger arterial pressure that utilizes the principles of oscillometry while using infrared plethysmograph to detect changes in volume of blood in finger

### 3.4. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS AND LIMITATIONS


* **Indications**  * **ASA Standard 4.2.2 Requirements**
    * Every patient receiving anesthesia must have BP and HR monitored Q5min
    * Oscillometric NIBP cuff typically sufficient for standard cases without comorbidities requiring continuous monitoring* **Contraindications**  * **Anatomical/Pathological Contraindications**
    * Local bone fracture (e.g., humeral)
    * Open injuries to extremity
    * Arteriovenous dialysis fistula
    * Indwelling PICC line
    * Post axillary lymph node dissection
      * Risk of limb edema from impaired lymphatic drainage
      * Susceptibility to vascular occlusion complications* **Potential Complications**
  * Iatrogenic injuries in prolonged cases:

    * Local skin abrasion
    * Contusion
    * Radial nerve neurapraxia (due to nerve's spiral path around humerus)
  * ⚠️ Frequently repeated cuff inflations can cause ulnar nerve palsy and petechial hemorrhage under the cuff
 
* **Risk Mitigation**
  * Apply light dressing under cuff to prevent skin injury
  * Relocate BP cuff Q few hours during prolonged cases* **Common Problems and Limitations**  * **False Readings**:

    * Falsely **high**: small cuffs, loose application, extremity below heart
    * Falsely **low**: large cuffs, extremity above heart, quick deflations
  * **Height Effect on Readings**:

    * Blood pressure changes with vertical height difference between measurement site and heart level
    ![Height Effect on BP](CVS_MONITORING_CVS_MONITORING_Untitled_2.png_f94e71.png_f4e657.png)

    ![Height Effect Diagram](CVS_MONITORING_CVS_MONITORING_Untitled_3.png_147c20.png_d4dc5e.png)


    * **Rule**: For every 10cm height difference, BP changes by 7.4 mmHg
    * **Direction**:

      * Above heart level: BP reading is falsely low
      * Below heart level: BP reading is falsely high
  * **Technical Specifications for Accuracy:** 
    * Fast cuff inflation (to avoid venous congestion)
 
    * Slow cuff deflation at 3 mmHg/s or 2 mmHg/beat (to provide time to detect arterial pulsation)
 
  * **Cuff Size Effects:** 
    * Too small: Blood pressure over-read
 
    * Too large: Blood pressure under-read
 
    * Error is greater with too small than too large a cuff
 
  * **Pressure Range Limitations:** 
    * Systolic pressure over-read at low pressures (systolic < 60 mmHg)
 
    * Systolic pressure under-read at high pressures
 
  * **Oscillometric Monitoring Considerations**
    * Correlates well with direct arterial MAP and DP
    * Accuracy affected by:

      * Cuff movement
      * Erratic pulse transmission
      * Arrhythmias
      * External force on cuff
      * Tubing occlusion
    * Measurement frequency:

      * Standard: Q5min
      * Evidence suggests decline in predictive ability >3min intervals
  * **Alternative Placement Sites**
    * Options: forearm, wrist, ankle (if upper arm inaccessible)
    * Peripheral measurements:

      * SP tends to increase
      * DP tends to decrease
    * **Special Populations**:

      * Parturients: poor correlation between calf and upper arm BP
      * Pediatric patients:

    * Upper extremity preferred (better cerebral perfusion correlation)
    * Premature infants: right arm placement (preductal) for accurate cerebral perfusion
    * Greatest BP disparity in neonates <1,000g
### 3.5. ALTERNATIVE TECHNOLOGIES


* **Finapres (Finger Arterial Pressure) Device:** 
  * Uses combination of oscillometry and servo control unit
 
  * Small cuff around finger keeps blood volume constant
 
  * Infrared photo-plethysmograph detects blood volume changes with each cardiac cycle
 
  * Controller system alters cuff pressure to maintain constant blood volume
 
  * Applied pressure waveform correlates with arterial blood pressure
 
  * Displays continuous real-time arterial blood pressure waveform
 
* **Arterial Tonometry**:

  * Non-invasive beat-to-beat ABP measurement
  * Mechanism: Measures pressure needed to flatten superficial artery against bone
  * Limitations:

    * Movement artifact sensitivity
    * Requires frequent calibration* **ClearSight** (Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, CA)
  * Uses noninvasive finger blood-pressure cuff
  * *Methodology*: Dynamic inflation/deflation to track brachial artery pressure
  * Based on Peñáz method:
    1. Fingertip compressed by pressure cuff    2. Simultaneous transillumination by infrared light    3. Arterial pulsations → increased blood volume → increased infrared absorption    4. System rapidly adjusts cuff pressure to maintain constant infrared absorption    5. Constant absorption → constant blood volume → cuff pressure equals arterial pressure
  * System refinements include:

    * Compensation for pressure reflection in vascular tree
    * Adjustments for vertical fluid column between fingertip and heart
**Bottom Line:** NIBP monitoring provides reliable, non-invasive blood pressure measurements suitable for most patients. Oscillometric devices measure MAP directly and calculate systolic and diastolic values using proprietary algorithms. Accuracy depends on proper cuff size, positioning at heart level, and understanding the limitations in patients with arrhythmias or poor peripheral perfusion. Alternative technologies like finger arterial pressure monitoring can provide continuous measurements but have their own limitations.

## 4. INVASIVE ARTERIAL BLOOD PRESSURE MONITORING

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Cardiovascular Physiology|Part A]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3: Perioperative Management and Monitoring|Part B]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Patient Care|Part C]]

**Invasive arterial blood pressure monitoring provides continuous, beat-to-beat pressure measurements and arterial blood sampling access.**

### 4.1. BASIC PRINCIPLES


* **Definition**: Indwelling arterial cannulation allows:

  * Continuous BP monitoring

  * Arterial blood sampling access
* **Primary benefits:** 
  * Provides beat-to-beat real-time information with sustained accuracy
 
  * Information gained includes heart rate, pulse pressure, respiratory swing, LV contractility, vascular tone, and stroke volume 

### 4.2. COMPONENTS AND SETUP

![[Pasted image 20250609172323.png]]


* **System components**:

  * Arterial cannula (indwelling)

  * Fluid-filled tubing

  * Pressure transducer (silicon crystal)

  * Signal amplification and display system
* **Detailed component breakdown:** 
  1. **Indwelling Teflon arterial cannula (20-G or 22-G)** 
     * Design with parallel walls to minimize effect on distal blood flow
 
     * Cannulation options: direct threading (insertion or transfixation technique) or modified Seldinger technique with guidewire assistance 
  
  2. **Bubble-free 0.9% normal saline column** 
     * Maintained at pressure of 300 mmHg
 
     * Incorporates flushing device 
  
  3. **Pressure transducer system** 
     * Connected to cannula via fluid column
 
     * Uses strain gauge variable resistor technology
 
     * Thin diaphragm acts as interface between transducer and fluid column
 
     * Converts pressure changes to electrical signals 
    ![[Pasted image 20250609172442.png]]

  4. **Flushing device** 
     * Allows 3-4 mL/h of saline to prevent clotting and backflow
 
     * Manual flushing capability when needed 

### 4.3. PRINCIPLES OF OPERATION


* **System characterized by two key properties**:

  * **Damping (ζ)**: Describes fluid's tendency to extinguish motion

  * **Natural frequency (fn)**: Frequency at which system is most prone to resonance
* **System Fidelity**:

  * Depends on optimizing both ζ and fn

  * Must respond to pressure wave frequencies (1-30 Hz)

  * "Fast flush" test used at bedside to assess both parameters
* **Pressure transduction mechanism:** 
  * Saline column moves with arterial pulsation, causing diaphragm movement
 
  * Movement changes resistance and current flow through transducer wires
 
  * Changes measured electronically and converted to pressure display 

#### 4.3.1. STRAIN GAUGES AND WHEATSTONE BRIDGE


* **Strain Gauge Definition**: A resistor wire whose resistance increases with stretch
* **Pressure Transduction Mechanism**: Pressure from fluid moves the diaphragm and stretches the strain gauge, causing a change in resistance
* **Wheatstone Bridge Circuit**:

  * Purpose: Amplifies small changes in resistance

  * Structure: DC supply to two parallel circuits each with two known resistors

  * Function: One circuit has a variable resistor, the other has the unknown resistance (strain gauge)

  * Operation: Voltmeter between circuits detects resistance differences with high sensitivity

  * Result: As arterial pressure moves the diaphragm, it changes the strain gauge resistance and voltage across the voltmeter

  * Applications: Ideal for measuring small resistance changes found in strain gauges
 
  * Configuration: Most pressure transducers contain four strain gauges forming the four resistors of the Wheatstone bridge 

![Wheatstone Bridge Circuit](CVS_MONITORING_CVS_MONITORING_nse-5395933198855280650-114595.jpg_ec3c16.jpg_9d9619.jpg)

### 4.4. THE ARTERIAL PRESSURE WAVEFORM


* **Waveform characterization:** 
  * Complex sine wave formed by summation of simpler sine waves with different amplitudes and frequencies
 
  * Fundamental frequency (first harmonic) equals heart rate
 
    * Example: 60 beats/min = 1 beat/s = 1 Hz
 
  * First 10 harmonics of fundamental frequency contribute to waveform
 
  * System measuring arterial BP should respond to frequency range of 0.5-40 Hz for accurate display
 
* **Waveform components:** 
  * **Dicrotic notch:** Represents pressure changes due to aortic valve closure vibrations
 
  * **Upstroke rate (dP/dt):** Reflects myocardial contractility
 
    * Slow rise might indicate need for inotropic support
 
    * Maximum upward slope relates to ventricular ejection speed
 
  * **Dicrotic notch position:** Reflects peripheral vascular resistance
 
    * Lower on curve in vasodilated patients (after epidural block or in sepsis)
 
    * Higher in vasoconstricted patients
 
  * **Downstroke slope:** Indicates resistance to outflow
 
    * Slow fall seen in vasoconstriction
 
  * **Area measurement:** Stroke volume can be estimated by measuring area from beginning of upstroke to dicrotic notch
 
    * Multiplying by heart rate gives estimated cardiac output
 
  * **Timing components:** 
    * Systolic time indicates myocardial oxygen demand
 
    * Diastolic time indicates myocardial oxygen supply
 
* **Mean arterial pressure calculation:** 
  * Average pressure throughout cardiac cycle
 
  * Slightly less than halfway between systolic and diastolic (since systole shorter than diastole)
 
  * Estimate: Diastolic pressure + (1/3 × pulse pressure)
 
  * Alternate calculation: Integrate pressure signal over one cycle, divided by time
 
* **Key Principles of Arterial Line Waveforms**:

  * **Systolic Amplification**
    * Peak systolic pressure increases with distance from proximal aorta

    * Caused by reflected waves

    * Offset by narrowing of systolic pressure wave

    * *Result*: Mean arterial pressure (MAP) remains unchanged

  * **Peripheral Waveform Changes**
    * Wave reflection causes pressure waveform distortion

    * Leads to exaggeration of systolic and pulse pressures

    * Radial vs. Aortic pressure differences:

      * Radial systolic pressure > aortic systolic pressure (more distal)

      * Post-hypothermic CPB: Radial pressures may underestimate central pressures

  * **Distance Effects from Aorta**
    * Later dicrotic notch appearance

    * Higher systolic pressure → wider pulse pressure

    * MAP remains constant

![Arterial Waveform](CVS_MONITORING_CVS_MONITORING_Untitled_5.png_afa698.png_e189d5.png)


* **Clinical Implications**
  * Organ perfusion monitoring limitations:

    * Direct monitoring devices are complex and often unreliable

    * Adequate BP used as surrogate for organ perfusion

    * *Important note*: Flow depends on both pressure AND vascular resistance

    * ⚠️ High pressure with high resistance may still result in low flow
* **Arterial Waveform Components and Analysis**
  * The arterial waveform is a **complex** waveform consisting of multiple sine waves of differing frequencies

  * The **fundamental** is the slowest of these sine waves

  * The smaller, faster waves are known as **harmonics**, and these are multiples of the fundamental frequency

  * Analyzed using **Fourier analysis** which breaks complex patterns down into basic sine wave components

  * Invasive blood pressure analysis is done up to the **tenth** harmonic

  * Specific features of the waveform:

    * The **area under the curve** of systolic pressure over time allows measurement of myocardial work and oxygen consumption

    * The **diastolic decay** gradient gives an idea of outflow resistance and compliance

    * The **area under the curve** of **diastolic** pressure over time gives an idea of myocardial perfusion

    * The **area under the curve** of the whole waveform gives an impression of the stroke volume and cardiac output

    * The gradient of the **upstroke** gives information about contractility of the heart

### 4.5. TECHNICAL CONSIDERATIONS AND NATURAL FREQUENCY


* **Common Sources of Error**
  * Physical properties of fluid motion

  * Catheter-transducer-amplification system performance
* **Transducer System Optimization**
  * Requirements for optimal fidelity:

    * Stiff catheters and tubing

    * Minimal fluid mass

    * Limited number of stopcocks

    * Minimal connecting tubing length

  * **System Damping Effects**:

    * **Underdamped systems**: Overestimate systolic pressure by 15-30 mmHg and amplify artifacts

    * **Excessive damping (ζ)**: Reduces fidelity and underestimates systolic pressure

    ![Damping Effects](CVS_MONITORING_CVS_MONITORING_image_1.png_0c235c.png_b7addc.png)


* **Understanding the Natural Frequency:** 
  * Definition: Frequency at which monitoring system resonates after disturbance
 
  * Determines frequency response of monitoring system
 
  * System should have frequency significantly different from patient's pulse rate
 
  * Should be at least 10 times the fundamental frequency (patient's pulse rate)
 
  * Monitoring system typically needs natural frequency of at least 40 Hz
 
  * Factors affecting natural frequency:

    * Directly related to catheter diameter
 
    * Inversely related to square root of system compliance
 
    * Inversely related to square root of tubing length
 
    * Inversely related to square root of fluid density
 
* **Optimizing damping and resonance:** 
  * **Damping** caused by energy dissipation in system
 
    * Increased damping lowers systolic and elevates diastolic pressures
 
    * Can be caused by air bubbles, clots, narrow tubing, long tubing, compliant tubing, three-way taps, or vasospasm
 
  
  * **Resonance** occurs when driving force frequency matches system's resonant frequency
 
    * Elevated systolic and lowered diastolic pressures (mean unchanged)
 
    * Due to stiff, noncompliant components
 
    * Worsens with tachycardia
 
* **Square Wave Test (Fast Flush Test):** 
  * System flushed by applying 300 mmHg pressure using flush button/lever
 
  * Creates square waveform followed by oscillations
 
  * **Optimal damping:** 2-3 oscillations before settling to zero
 
  * **Overdamped:** Settles to zero without oscillations
 
  * **Underdamped:** Oscillates for >3-4 cycles before settling
 
  * Performed by pulling on the flush toggle and suddenly releasing it

  * Generates a square wave on the monitor

  * As the line returns to baseline, check for oscillations around zero to indicate the level of damping

  * There should be a single overshoot oscillation before returning to zero

  * 2 or more oscillations = under damped

  * No oscillations = over damped
* **Monitoring system requirements:**
  * Optimal damping value of 0.64
 
  * Regular calibration and zeroing
 
    * Zero calibration eliminates atmospheric pressure effects
 
    * Calibration at higher pressures (100 and 200 mmHg) corrects gradient drift [Supplementary
    ![[Pasted image 20250609173608.png]]

    * Offset drift: zero art-line to recalibrate to atm pressure

    * Gradient drift: calibrate at higher pressures
* **Safety Considerations**
  * ⚠️ **Blood sampling precautions**: Return blood only intravenously due to thromboembolism risk

  * ⚠️ **Line security**: Ensure tight tubing connections to prevent hemorrhage
* **Continuous Flush Systems**
  * Standard infusion rate: 3-6 mL/hr

  * Considerations:

    * Minimal effect on BP measurement

    * Risk of air embolism from pressurized systems

    * Neonates: Monitor for fluid overload

### 4.6. CANNULATION SITES AND TECHNIQUES


* **Common Sites for Direct BP Measurement**:

  * **Radial Artery** (Most Preferred)

    * Advantages:

      * Excellent accessibility

      * Protected by collateral ulnar circulation

      * Optimal monitoring site

    * Technical note: Use non-tapered catheters

  * **Ulnar Artery**
    * Primary hand blood supply source

    * Similar complication profile to radial

  * **Brachial Artery**
    * Insertion: Medial to biceps tendon

    * Accommodates 18G cannula

    * ⚠️ **Caution**: Risk of median nerve injury

  * **Axillary Artery**
    * Insertion point: Pectoralis-deltoid junction

    * Requires specialized equipment

  * **Femoral Artery**
    * Beneficial in low-flow states

    * Requires longer catheters

    * ⚠️ **Risks**: Local/retroperitoneal hemorrhage

  * **Dorsalis Pedis Artery**
    * Backed by posterior tibial collateral flow

    * Note: Tends to overestimate systolic pressure
* **Site Selection Considerations**:

  * **Radial Artery** (First Choice)

    * Advantages: Superficial location, good collateral flow

    * Note: 5% have incomplete palmar arches

    * Allen's Test

      * Assesses collateral flow (thumb flush within 5s)

      * Current status: Limited clinical utility, often omitted

  * **Alternative Sites - Key Points**
    * Ulnar: Technically challenging due to depth and tortuosity

    * Brachial: Large caliber but prone to kinking

    * Femoral: Higher risk of complications (infection, thrombosis, pseudoaneurysm)

    * Distal vessels (dorsalis pedis/posterior tibial): Most distorted waveforms

    * Axillary: Special concerns

      * Risk of plexus injury from hematoma

      * Danger of cerebral embolism during flushing
* **Three common cannulation approaches**:

  * Direct arterial puncture

  * Guidewire-assisted (Seldinger technique)

  * Transfixion-withdrawal method
* **Key requirements**:

  * Must identify arterial pulse

  * 💡 Ultrasound with Doppler useful for difficult/small vessels

### 4.7. COMPLICATIONS AND TROUBLESHOOTING


* **Major Complications**
  * **Ischemic Events**
    * Causes: thrombosis, proximal emboli, prolonged shock

    * Risk Factors:

      * Severe atherosclerosis

      * Diabetes

      * Low cardiac output states

      * Severe vasoconstriction

  * **Vascular Complications**
    * Hemorrhage

    * Thrombosis

    * Embolism (including **cerebral air embolism** from retrograde flow)

    * Aneurysm formation

    * Arteriovenous fistula

  * **Local Complications**
    * Skin necrosis

    * Infection
* **High-Risk Patients**
  * Compromised collateral circulation:

    * **Raynaud phenomenon**
    * **Thromboangiitis obliterans** (Buerger disease)
* **Procedure-Related Complications**
  * **Immediate**
    * Hematoma

    * Thrombosis

    * Nerve injury

  * **Vascular**
    * Vasospasm

    * Arterial thrombosis

    * Pseudoaneurysm

    * Embolization (air/thrombi)

  * **Tissue**
    * Catheter site skin necrosis

    * Extremity/digit necrosis

    * Infection

  * **Iatrogenic**
    * ⚠️ Accidental intraarterial drug injection
* **Detailed complications data:** 
  * **Ischemia** (rare): Should be monitored for
 
    * Risk increased by multiple insertion attempts and hematoma formation
 
  
  * **Arterial thrombosis:** Occurs in 20-25% of cases
 
    * Rarely causes adverse effects like ischemia/necrosis
 
    * Uncommon with cannulae in place <24 hours
 
  
  * **Waveform distortion:** 
    * Arterial wave narrows and increases amplitude in peripheral vessels
 
    * Makes systolic pressure higher in distal arteries (e.g., dorsalis pedis vs. radial)
 
    * Due to peripheral arteries containing fewer elastic fibers (stiffer, less compliant)
 
    * Arterial bifurcations cause impedance of forward flow, leading to backward pressure wave reflection
 
  
  * **Bleeding:** Risk from disconnection
 
  
  * **Medication errors:** 
    * Clear labeling needed to prevent fatal errors
 
    * Inadvertent air injection → air embolus
 
    * Inadvertent drug injection → distal vascular occlusion and necrosis
 
  
  * **Infection:** 
    * Local infection <20%
 
    * Systemic infection <5%
 
    * Higher risk with arterial cannula in place >4 days with traumatic insertion
 
* **Key setup and monitoring elements:** 
  * **Level reference point:** Transducer should be at right atrium level (midaxillary line)
 
    * Moving transducer 10 cm above/below heart level causes 7.5 mmHg error
 
  
  * **Contraindications:** 
    * Sites with evidence of infection or trauma
 
    * Through vascular prosthesis
 
  
  * **Calibration requirements:** 
    * Periodic checks, calibrations, and rezeroing needed to prevent baseline drift
 
* **ARTERIAL LINE SETUP AND MONITORING** 
* **Key Components of Setup**:

  * **Transducer Zeroing**:

    * Position at right atrium level

    * Open stopcock to atmosphere

    * Select "Zero Sensor" on monitor
* **Understanding Zero & Level Concepts**:

  * **Zeroing Process**:

    * Creates air-fluid interface

    * Establishes atmospheric pressure (Patm) as zero reference

    * *Note*: Transducer height irrelevant during zeroing (minimal Patm variation floor to ceiling)

  * **Leveling Process**:

    * Assigns zero reference to specific anatomical point

    * Standard: Level at right atrium

    * Special cases: Circle of Willis level for neurosurgical beach-chair position
* **MONITORING CONSIDERATIONS** 
* **Requires constant vigilance**
  * Verify correlation with clinical condition

  * Common errors:

    * Sudden increases: Check transducer height/table position

    * Sudden decreases: Check for kinked catheter/tubing

  * ⚠️ Always verify system before initiating therapy
* **Post-Removal Management**
  * **Flow Abnormalities**
    * Typical normalization period: 3-70 days

  * **Thrombosis Prevention**
    * Catheter selection:

      * Prefer smaller caliber catheters

      * Avoid polypropylene-tapered types

    * Technical considerations:

      * Minimize duration of cannulation

      * Use flexible guidewires for tortuous vessels
* **Monitoring Protocol**
  * **Regular Assessment**
    * Frequent examination of perfused tissues

    * Monitor for thromboembolism/ischemia signs

  * **Removal Technique**
    * Compress both proximal and distal segments

    * Perform aspiration during withdrawal

**Bottom Line:** Invasive arterial monitoring provides continuous BP measurement and arterial sampling access. Proper setup, maintenance, and understanding of waveform characteristics are essential for accurate pressure measurements. The radial artery is the most common site due to collateral circulation and accessibility, but alternatives exist for specific clinical scenarios. Meticulous attention to damping, natural frequency, and transducer positioning is critical for accurate readings. Major complications include ischemia, thrombosis, and hemorrhage, with highest risk in patients with impaired collateral circulation.

## 5. DYNAMIC HEMODYNAMIC MONITORING

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Cardiovascular Physiology|Part A]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3: Perioperative Management and Monitoring|Part B]]

**Dynamic parameters like pulse pressure variation provide insight into fluid responsiveness by analyzing cardiopulmonary interactions.**

### 5.1. PULSE PRESSURE VARIATION (PPV)


* **Key Concepts**:

  * Low absolute pulse pressure indicates early underfilling

  * Visible on invasive arterial line tracings

  * Used to guide volume resuscitation
* **Clinical Application**:

  * PPV/SVV >10-15% suggests:

    * Potential hypovolemia

    * Likely positive response to fluid challenge
* **Validity Requirements**:

  * **Ventilation Parameters**
    * Full mechanical ventilation (no spontaneous breaths)

    * Tidal volume > 7-8 mL/kg

    * HR:RR ratio > 4

  * **Patient Factors**
    * Regular cardiac rhythm (no arrhythmias)

    * PEEP effects considered (↑PEEP = ↑variation)

  * **Anatomical Considerations**
    * Open abdomen: Reduces SVV/PPV by 40-50%

    * Altered by changes in:

      * Lung/chest compliance

      * Patient positioning

      * LV or RV dysfunction

      * Pneumoperitoneum

![PPV Waveform Example](CVS_MONITORING_CVS_MONITORING_Untitled_6.png_c1ee06.png_3c1757.png)

### 5.2. STROKE VOLUME VARIATION (SVV)


* **Definition**: Variation in stroke volume during respiratory cycle
* **Measurement**:

  * Calculated as (SVmax - SVmin)/SVmean × 100%

  * Requires continuous cardiac output monitoring device
* **Threshold values**:

  * SVV >13% predictive of fluid responsiveness

  * Similar limitations as PPV

### 5.3. LIMITATIONS AND CLINICAL CONSIDERATIONS


* **Confounding scenarios**:

  * Right ventricular dysfunction

  * Pulmonary hypertension

  * Severe mitral or aortic regurgitation

  * Intra-abdominal hypertension
* **Clinical Interpretation**:

  * PPV/SVV values must be interpreted in clinical context

  * Trends may be more valuable than absolute values

  * ⚠️ Elevated values don't necessarily mean fluid should be given - consider overall clinical picture
* **False negatives**:

  * Low tidal volume ventilation may result in insufficient cardiopulmonary interaction to generate measurable PPV/SVV despite fluid responsiveness
* **Surrogates**: Passive leg raise (1-2min) looking at change in Stroke Volume (18% by echo, >5% change in ETCO2), IVC distensibility

**Bottom Line:** Pulse pressure variation and stroke volume variation are valuable dynamic parameters for assessing fluid responsiveness, but require specific conditions to be valid, including regular heart rhythm, mechanical ventilation with adequate tidal volumes, and absence of factors that might alter cardiopulmonary interactions. When properly applied, they can guide fluid therapy more effectively than static measures of preload.

## 6. PULMONARY ARTERY CATHETERIZATION
<!-- EPA-10:K3:pac EPA-10:K3:hemodynamic_monitoring -->

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Cardiovascular Physiology|Part A]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3: Perioperative Management and Monitoring|Part B]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Patient Care|Part C]]

**Pulmonary artery catheterization provides advanced hemodynamic monitoring including PA pressures, cardiac output, and mixed venous oxygen saturation in selected high-risk patients.**

### 6.1. INTRODUCTION AND EVIDENCE BASE


* **Historical perspective:** The balloon-flotation pulmonary artery (PA) catheter was introduced in 1970 and expanded rapidly in clinical practice since the late 1970s `[Guidelines:2003]`.
* **Utilization:** As of 1996, an estimated 2 million catheters were sold annually worldwide, with an estimated 2 billion dollars spent in the United States alone `[Guidelines:2003]`.
* **ASA Guidelines:** The American Society of Anesthesiologists published comprehensive practice guidelines in 1993, updated in 2003 following systematic review of evidence and expert opinion `[Guidelines:2003]`.
* **Evidence challenges:** Multiple studies, most notably Connors et al. (1996), raised doubts about effectiveness and safety, leading to additional policy statements from professional organizations `[Guidelines:2003]`.

### 6.2. ASA PRACTICE GUIDELINES RECOMMENDATIONS

#### 6.2.1. EVIDENCE MODEL AND METHODOLOGY


* **Task Force composition:** Nine-member Task Force including university-based and community-practice anesthesiologists with median experience of 21 years in PA catheter use `[Guidelines:2003]`.
* **Evidence review:** Systematic review of 860 clinical trials, controlled observational studies, and case reports from original 1991-1992 search, updated with 90 additional studies meeting inclusion criteria through 2002 `[Guidelines:2003]`.
* **Expert consensus:** Task Force used confidential voting scheme to assess appropriateness and necessity of PAC for 27 clinical scenarios combining patient risk, surgical risk, and practice setting variables `[Guidelines:2003]`.

#### 6.2.2. CLINICAL EFFECTIVENESS ASSESSMENT


* **Benefits identified:**
  * **Treatment decisions:** Survey studies demonstrate PA catheter data provide new information or change therapy in 30-62% of cases, though clinical significance uncertain `[Guidelines:2003]`.

  * **Goal-directed therapy:** Some evidence for improved outcomes when PA catheter data used to achieve supranormal metabolic goals in selected high-risk surgical patients `[Guidelines:2003]`.

  * **Hemodynamic optimization:** Studies show PA catheter data more accurate than clinical assessment in evaluating hemodynamic status of complicated patients `[Guidelines:2003]`.


* **Limitations in evidence:**
  * Most studies retrospective with inadequate control for confounding variables `[Guidelines:2003]`.

  * Propensity score analysis by Connors et al. suggested 24-27% increased mortality risk, with 58% increased risk in postoperative patients `[Guidelines:2003]`.

  * No high-quality evidence exists to support routine or even selective preoperative catheterization `[Guidelines:2003]`.

#### 6.2.3. COMPLICATIONS AND ADVERSE EFFECTS


* **Major complications include:**
  * **Insertion-related:** Arterial injury, pneumothorax, arrhythmias `[Guidelines:2003]`.

  * **Catheter residence:** Potentially fatal PA hemorrhage, thromboembolism, sepsis, endocardial damage `[Guidelines:2003]`.

  * **Specific risks:** Right bundle branch block, complete heart block (in patients with pre-existing left bundle branch block), tricuspid regurgitation `[Guidelines:2003]`.


* **Reported incidence rates:**
  * **Dysrhythmias:** Primary complication during insertion procedure `[Guidelines:2003]`.

  * **Catheter-related sepsis:** Risk increased with duration of catheter residence and insertion technique `[Guidelines:2003]`.

  * **PA rupture:** Rare but potentially fatal complication `[Guidelines:2003]`.

### 6.3. CLINICAL INDICATIONS AND RISK STRATIFICATION

#### 6.3.1. THREE-FACTOR RISK MODEL

The ASA guidelines base recommendations on three interdependent variables:


* **Patient factors:** Underlying medical conditions, severity of illness, comorbidities `[Guidelines:2003]`.
* **Surgical factors:** Type and complexity of procedure, anticipated hemodynamic changes `[Guidelines:2003]`.
* **Practice setting factors:** Catheter-use skills, technical support, nursing experience, specialist availability `[Guidelines:2003]`.

#### 6.3.2. APPROPRIATENESS RATINGS

Based on Task Force voting using 1-9 scale:
* **Scores 7-9:** Appropriate/necessary
* **Scores 4-6:** Uncertain/possibly appropriate
* **Scores 1-3:** Inappropriate/unnecessary

#### 6.3.3. SPECIFIC RECOMMENDATIONS


* **Routine use NOT supported:** Evidence clearly does not support routine use of PA catheters when there is low risk of hemodynamic complications `[Guidelines:2003]`.


* **High-risk scenarios where PAC may be appropriate:**
  * High-risk patients undergoing high-risk surgery in high-risk settings `[Guidelines:2003]`.

  * Complex cardiac surgery cases `[Guidelines:2003]`.

  * Selected critically ill patients requiring precise hemodynamic management `[Guidelines:2003]`.


* **Preoperative catheterization:** Insufficient evidence to support preoperative PA catheter monitoring in hemodynamically stable patients `[Guidelines:2003]`.

### 6.4. COMPETENCY AND TRAINING REQUIREMENTS

#### 6.4.1. COGNITIVE AND TECHNICAL SKILLS


* **Training requirements:** All persons using PA catheters should undergo high-quality, supervised training to establish competency `[Guidelines:2003]`.


* **Minimum procedures for competency:**
  * American College of Physicians/Cardiology/Heart Association: At least 25 procedures for technical and cognitive skills `[Guidelines:2003]`.

  * National survey of critical care physicians: 73% believed 20-50 supervised catheterizations required `[Guidelines:2003]`.

  * Learning curve research shows variable experience requirements depending on procedure complexity `[Guidelines:2003]`.

#### 6.4.2. KNOWLEDGE DEFICITS


* **Physician competency studies:**
  * 1990 Iberti study: Only 67% of answers correct on 31-item PA catheter examination among 496 North American physicians `[Guidelines:2003]`.

  * 1996 survey: Although 83% of questions answered correctly, one-third could not correctly identify PA occlusion pressure or oxygen transport components `[Guidelines:2003]`.


* **Nursing competency:**
  * California study of critical care nurses: Only 57% of responses correct on 31-item examination `[Guidelines:2003]`.

  * Only 39% correctly identified PA wedge measurement from waveform recording `[Guidelines:2003]`.

#### 6.4.3. MAINTENANCE OF SKILLS


* **Regular use required:** Competence related to experience and regular performance after training `[Guidelines:2003]`.
* **Quality measures:** Best measure is clinical outcome, but surrogate measures like frequency of use or proficiency examinations may be necessary `[Guidelines:2003]`.

### 6.5. TECHNOLOGY AND ALTERNATIVES

#### 6.5.1. TRANSESOPHAGEAL ECHOCARDIOGRAPHY


* **Alternative monitoring:** TEE can provide similar and additional information, may supplant need for PAC when available and appropriate `[Guidelines:2003]`.
* **Limitations:** May be unavailable or impractical in some settings `[Guidelines:2003]`.

#### 6.5.2. CENTRAL VENOUS CATHETER MONITORING


* **Standard alternative:** CVC monitoring often adequate for lower-risk scenarios `[Guidelines:2003]`.
* **Limitations:** Task Force believes reliance on clinical assessment or CVC monitoring inadequate for high-risk patients requiring precise hemodynamic management `[Guidelines:2003]`.

### 6.6. SPECIAL POPULATIONS

#### 6.6.1. CARDIAC SURGERY


* **Mixed evidence:** Studies show conflicting results regarding benefit in cardiac surgery patients `[Guidelines:2003]`.
* **Observational data:** Some retrospective studies suggest adverse association between PAC and outcomes, but may be due to confounding variables `[Guidelines:2003]`.

#### 6.6.2. OBSTETRIC AND GYNECOLOGIC PROCEDURES


* **Limited evidence:** No controlled clinical outcome studies reported for severe preeclampsia or other obstetric conditions `[Guidelines:2003]`.
* **Case reports:** Support value in selected cases, but systematic study lacking `[Guidelines:2003]`.

#### 6.6.3. PEDIATRIC CATHETERIZATION


* **Poor evidence base:** PA catheterization performed in critically ill newborns, infants, and children, but effect on clinical outcomes poorly studied `[Guidelines:2003]`.
* **Diagnostic utility:** Uncontrolled case series show usefulness in clarifying diagnoses `[Guidelines:2003]`.

### 6.7. IMPLEMENTATION CONSIDERATIONS

#### 6.7.1. PATIENT CONCERNS


* **Access disparities:** Disadvantaged patients may lack access to hospitals with qualified personnel `[Guidelines:2003]`.
* **Informed consent:** Patients undergoing elective procedures may benefit from discussion of indications and risks `[Guidelines:2003]`.
* **Comfort considerations:** Insertion in operating room may be more comfortable and less anxiety-provoking than preoperative insertion `[Guidelines:2003]`.

#### 6.7.2. PROVIDER CONSIDERATIONS


* **Emergency insertion risks:** Delay in catheter placement after complications develop may endanger patients and increase insertion risks `[Guidelines:2003]`.
* **Sterile technique:** Prospective studies show 2.1 relative risk for catheter-related sepsis when inserted in operating room with less stringent sterile technique `[Guidelines:2003]`.

#### 6.7.3. HEALTHCARE SYSTEM FACTORS


* **Reimbursement:** Should not be bundled with surgical procedures given selective indications `[Guidelines:2003]`.
* **Utilization review:** Guidelines should not be used as standards of care to define unnecessary catheterization `[Guidelines:2003]`.

### 6.8. FUTURE RESEARCH PRIORITIES

#### 6.8.1. RANDOMIZED CONTROLLED TRIALS


* **Clinical equipoise:** Future trials should be undertaken in settings where profession has split opinions about appropriateness `[Guidelines:2003]`.
* **Identified scenarios for equipoise:**
  * Low-risk patients undergoing high-risk surgery `[Guidelines:2003]`.

  * Moderate-risk patients undergoing moderate-risk surgery `[Guidelines:2003]`.

  * High-risk patients undergoing low-risk surgery `[Guidelines:2003]`.

  * Moderate-risk patients undergoing high-risk surgery in moderate-risk settings `[Guidelines:2003]`.

  * High-risk patients undergoing high-risk surgery in high-risk settings `[Guidelines:2003]`.

#### 6.8.2. STUDY DESIGN REQUIREMENTS


* **Essential elements:**
  * Adequate sample size with proper power calculations `[Guidelines:2003]`.

  * Concealment of allocation to reduce bias `[Guidelines:2003]`.

  * Intention-to-treat analysis `[Guidelines:2003]`.

  * Clear specification of treatment endpoints and hemodynamic targets `[Guidelines:2003]`.

  * Detailed documentation of practitioner skills and data interpretation `[Guidelines:2003]`.

#### 6.8.3. OBSERVATIONAL STUDIES


* **Large-scale multicenter studies recommended:**
  * Adequate numbers for each indication `[Guidelines:2003]`.

  * Comprehensive database of medical history, comorbidity, severity of illness `[Guidelines:2003]`.

  * Details about insertion techniques, skills, and data interpretation `[Guidelines:2003]`.

### 6.9. KEY CLINICAL PEARLS

💡 **Clinical Decision-Making:**
* PAC should be limited to cases where anticipated benefits outweigh potential risks `[Guidelines:2003]`.
* Consider patient factors, surgical complexity, and practice setting capabilities `[Guidelines:2003]`.
* Emergency catheterization under hastily prepared conditions increases complication risk `[Guidelines:2003]`.

💡 **Quality Improvement:**
* Regular assessment of technical and cognitive performance essential `[Guidelines:2003]`.
* Experienced users achieve better outcomes with fewer complications `[Guidelines:2003]`.
* Nursing skill and attentiveness to PA catheter tracings significantly influence outcomes `[Guidelines:2003]`.

💡 **Evidence-Based Practice:**
* Guidelines based on expert opinion should not be used as absolute standards of care `[Guidelines:2003]`.
* Individual clinical judgment remains paramount in determining appropriateness `[Guidelines:2003]`.
* Consider alternatives like TEE when available and appropriate `[Guidelines:2003]`.

⚠️ **Safety Considerations:**
* Complications can be serious and potentially fatal `[Guidelines:2003]`.
* Risk increases with duration of catheter residence `[Guidelines:2003]`.
* Proper sterile technique during insertion is crucial `[Guidelines:2003]`.
* Regular monitoring for complications required throughout catheter residence `[Guidelines:2003]`.

### 6.10. TECHNICAL SPECIFICATIONS AND CATHETER DESIGN

#### 6.10.1. CATHETER STRUCTURE AND CHARACTERISTICS


* **Standard design**: 7.5 FR catheter, 110-cm long, polyvinylchloride body
* **Size range:** 5-8G, usually 110 cm in length
* **Five integrated lumens**:

  * **Thermistor wiring lumen** - connects tip thermistor to CO computer

  * **Air channel** - for balloon inflation

  * **Proximal port** (30 cm from tip) - for infusions, CO injections, right atrial pressure measurements

  * **Ventricular port** (20 cm from tip) - for drug infusions

  * **Distal port** - for mixed venous blood sampling and PA pressure measurements


* **Detailed lumen configuration:**
  1. **Distal lumen:** Opens in pulmonary artery

     * Measures PA and pulmonary capillary wedge pressures

     * Samples mixed venous blood
  2. **Proximal lumen:** Positioned ~30 cm from tip, ideally opens in right atrium

     * Continuously monitors CVP

     * Administers injectate for cardiac output measurement

     * Used for fluid infusions

     * Some designs have second proximal lumen dedicated to drug infusions
  3. **Thermistor lumen:** Contains insulated wires

     * Leads to thermistor ~3.7 cm from catheter tip

     * Connects proximally to cardiac output computer
  4. **Balloon inflation lumen:** Controls tip balloon

     * Requires up to 1.5 mL of air for inflation

     * When inflated, allows catheter to float with blood flow into PA branch

### 6.11. INSERTION TECHNIQUE AND PROCEDURE

#### 6.11.1. INSERTION SEQUENCE


* **Pre-insertion preparation:**
  * Flush all lines and test balloon with 1–1.5 mL of air

  * Connect distal lumen to pressure transducer system for continuous monitoring during advancement


* **Step-by-step insertion**:
  1. Place sheath introducer in central circulation
  2. Float PA catheter through introducer
  3. Monitor ECG for arrhythmias (transient RV ectopy is common)
  4. Use pressure tracings to confirm position:

     * Right atrium: central venous pressure tracing

     * Right ventricle: sudden increase in systolic pressure

     * Pulmonary artery: sudden increase in diastolic pressure (at 35-45 cm depth)

     * Wedge position: PAOP trace (equilibrates with left atrial pressure)

#### 6.11.2. DETAILED CATHETER ADVANCEMENT


* **Superior vena cava to right atrium passage:**
  * Low pressure waves displayed (mean 3–8 mmHg normally)

  * Distance from internal jugular/subclavian to right atrium approximately 15–20 cm


* **Right atrium to right ventricle passage:**
  * Inflate balloon to let blood flow carry catheter tip through tricuspid valve

  * Tall RV pressure waves displayed (15–25 mmHg systolic, 0–10 mmHg diastolic)


* **Right ventricle to pulmonary artery passage:**
  * Balloon carries tip through pulmonary valve into PA

  * Pressure waveform shows higher diastolic pressure (10–20 mmHg) but similar systolic pressures

  * Dicrotic notch visible (from pulmonary valve closure)

  * Distance from right ventricle to PA typically <10 cm (more in cardiomegaly)


* **Wedge position achievement:**
  * Fully inflate balloon to carry tip into PA branch where it wedges

  * Damped pressure waveform (PCWP) displayed (mean 4–12 mmHg)

  * Reflects left atrial filling pressure

  * Deflate balloon after reading (allowing catheter to float back to PA)

  * Keep balloon deflated until another PCWP reading needed

![PAC Pressure Tracings](CVS_MONITORING_CVS_MONITORING_image_5.png_516c63.png_8e34ac.png)

![PAC Insertion](CVS_MONITORING_CVS_MONITORING_Untitled_10.png_2db4c0.png_2e33c9.png)

#### 6.11.3. TECHNICAL SAFETY POINTS


* ⚠️ **Wedging before maximal balloon inflation** indicates overwedged position → slightly withdraw catheter (with balloon deflated)
* ⚠️ **PA rupture from balloon overinflation** may cause fatal hemorrhage
* **Obtain wedge readings infrequently** to minimize risk
* **Monitor ECG continuously** during insertion for arrhythmias

### 6.12. CLINICAL INDICATIONS AND CONTRAINDICATIONS

#### 6.12.1. SPECIFIC CLINICAL INDICATIONS


* **Measurement of pulmonary artery occlusion pressure (PAOP)/wedge pressure**
  * Estimates left ventricular end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP)

  * Approximates LV end-diastolic volume (preload) depending on ventricular compliance

  * More precise than CVP or physical exam (though less precise than TEE)


* **Hemodynamic monitoring for circulatory instability**
  * Helps differentiate causes of hemodynamic instability

  * Guides targeted therapy based on underlying problem:

    * ↓SV + low PAOP → hypovolemia → fluid administration needed

    * ↓SV + high PAOP ("full heart") → cardiac dysfunction → inotropic support needed

    * Normal/↑SV + hypotension → vasodilatory shock → vasoconstrictors needed


* **Sampling of mixed venous blood**
  * Allows assessment of tissue oxygen extraction

  * Normal mixed venous oxygen saturation (SvO₂) is 75%

  * Calculation of oxygen consumption: ψ⋅O₂ = 13.8 × [Hgb] × CO × (SaO₂ − SvO₂)


* **Direct cardiac output (CO) measurement**
  * Allows derivation of stroke volume (SV)

  * Enables calculation of oxygen delivery to tissues


* **Expanded list of indications:**
  * **Specific disease states monitoring:**
    * Ischemic and valvular heart disease

    * Cardiogenic shock

    * Left and/or right ventricle failure

    * Sepsis and septic shock

    * Adult respiratory distress syndrome

    * Oliguria and unexplained hypotension

  * **Perioperative monitoring:**
    * Coronary artery bypass graft (CABG)

    * Major vascular surgery

#### 6.12.2. CONTRAINDICATIONS


* **Absolute:**
  * Right-sided endocarditis - risk of dislodging vegetations

  * Tumors or masses - risk of embolization

  * Left bundle branch block (LBBB) - risk of complete heart block

    * Use catheter with pacing capability if needed


* **Relative:**
  * Bacteremic patients - catheter may become nidus of infection

  * Hypercoagulable states - increased risk of thrombus formation

  * Severely coagulopathic/thrombocytopenic patients - bleeding risk

    * Requires risk-benefit analysis before placement

**Bottom Line:** Pulmonary artery catheterization is a specialized invasive monitoring technique that should be reserved for selected high-risk patients where the anticipated benefits clearly outweigh the potential risks. The 2003 ASA practice guidelines emphasize that routine use is not supported by evidence, and that decisions should be based on a careful assessment of patient factors, surgical complexity, and practice setting capabilities. Proper training, competency maintenance, and adherence to strict safety protocols are essential for appropriate use. Alternative monitoring modalities like TEE should be considered when available and appropriate.

## 7. CENTRAL VENOUS CATHETERIZATION
<!-- EPA-10:EQ1:cvc EPA-10:S2:vascular_access -->

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Cardiovascular Physiology|Part A]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3: Perioperative Management and Monitoring|Part B]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Patient Care|Part C]]

**Central venous catheterization provides access to central circulation for monitoring, fluid/medication administration, and sampling.**

### 7.1. PRINCIPLES AND INDICATIONS


* **Central venous cannulas** serve as critical portals for:

  * Intraoperative vascular access

  * Assessment of vascular volume changes

  * Rapid fluid administration

  * Insertion of pulmonary artery catheters (PACs)

  * Insertion of central venous oximetry catheters

  * Insertion of transvenous electrodes

  * Central venous pressure (CVP) monitoring

  * Site for observation and treatment of venous air embolism
* **Hemodynamic monitoring value**: Main benefit of central venous and right-heart pressure monitoring is their ability to:

  * Approximate or trend in conjunction with left ventricular end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP)

  * LVEDP predicts left-ventricular filling (left ventricular end-diastolic volume, LVEDV) through the Frank–Starling mechanism
* **Pressure progression in cardiopulmonary system**:

  * CVP → Right atrial pressure → Right ventricular pressure → Pulmonary artery pressure → Pulmonary capillary wedge pressure → Left atrial pressure → LVEDP

  * Ideally, all proximal pressures reflect changes in LVEDP

  ![Pressure Progression](CVS_MONITORING_CVS_MONITORING_image_3.png_02414a.png_686cc9.png)


* **Central Venous Pressure (CVP)**:

  * Easiest to measure (no need for catheter placement within heart)

  * Essentially equivalent to right atrial pressure

  * Serves as reflection of right ventricular preload
* **Cardiac Output Relationship**:

  * Right ventricular output and left ventricular output must be approximately equal in a structurally normal cardiopulmonary system

  * Small amount of physiologic shunt is normal
* **Clinical Limitations**:

  * ⚠️ Right-sided heart pressures often are poor indicators of left ventricular filling

  * Limitations apply to both absolute numbers and direction of change in response to therapy

  * Correlation of these pressures as estimates of LVEDP (and LVEDV) depends on:
    1. Proximity to the left ventricle
    2. Status of ventricular compliance


* **Indications for Central Venous Access**
* **Central venous access provides route for administration of:**
  * Vasoactive drugs

  * Parenteral nutrition (TPN)

  * Higher-concentration electrolyte solutions

  * Prolonged vascular access

  * Temporary pacemaker placement
* **Central venous pressure (CVP) monitoring indicated for:**
  * Quantitative, direct assessment of right ventricular preload

  * Cases with expected rapid or large shifts in intravascular volume

  * Assessment of volumetric status when other clinical signs (e.g., urine output) may be inaccurate or unavailable
* **Additional indications:**
  * Aspiration of air emboli

  * Insertion of transcutaneous pacing leads

  * Gaining venous access in patients with poor peripheral veins

  * Continuous monitoring of central venous oxygen saturation (ScvO₂)

    * *Normal ScvO₂*: >65%

    * *Decreased ScvO₂*: Suggests possibility of inadequate oxygen delivery to tissues

    * *Increased ScvO₂ (>80%)*: Suggests arterial/venous shunting or impaired cellular O₂ utilization (e.g., cyanide poisoning)

### 7.2. CATHETER TYPES AND SELECTION
 

* **Different types based on:** 
  * Lumen size
 
  * Length
 
  * Number of lumens
 
  * Presence/absence of subcutaneous cuff
 
  * Material composition
 
  * Most designed for Seldinger technique insertion
 
  * Some designed as 'long' intravenous cannulae (cannula over needle)
 
* **Antimicrobial-coated catheters:** 
  * Designed to reduce catheter-related bloodstream infection
 
  * Types:

    * **Antiseptic-coated:** chlorhexidine/silver sulfadiazine, benzalkonium chloride, platinum/silver
 
    * **Antibiotic-coated:** minocycline/rifampin
 
    * Can be coated on internal surface, external surface, or both
 
  * Antibiotic-coated lines considered more effective in reducing infection
 
  * Chlorhexidine-impregnated foam discs can surround skin entry site as part of line dressing
 
* **Multilumen catheter types:** 
  * Multiple lumens of different sizes (e.g., 16G and 18G)
 
  * Single and double lumen versions available
 
  * Allows simultaneous drug administration and CVP monitoring
 
  * Does not allow insertion of pulmonary artery catheter
 
  * Typically made of polyurethane for good tensile strength
 
* **Long central catheters/peripherally inserted central catheters (PICC):** 
  * Approximately 60 cm long
 
  * Inserted through peel-away sheath via upper arm vein (usually basilic)
 
  * Used when central access required but internal jugular/subclavian undesirable
 
  * Common applications: long-term cancer chemotherapy, antibiotic treatment
 
  * Made of soft flexible polyurethane or silicone
 
  * Available in single (4 Fr), double (5 Fr), and triple (5 or 6 Fr) versions
 
  * Some designs suitable for power injection for contrast during CT scans
 
* **Hickman catheters:** 
  * Made of polyurethane or silicone
 
  * Usually inserted into internal jugular or subclavian vein
 
  * Available in one, two, or three lumens
 
  * Proximal end tunneled under skin for approximately 10 cm
 
  * Dacron cuff positioned 3-4 cm from skin entry site
 
    * Induces fibroblastic reaction to anchor catheter
 
    * Reduces infection risk by blocking spread from entry site
 
    * Some catheters have silver-impregnated cuff as antimicrobial barrier
 
  * Used for long-term chemotherapy, parenteral nutrition, frequent blood sampling, or readily available venous access
 
  * Designed to remain in place for months unless infected
 
  * Require daily maintenance
 
* **Dialysis catheters:** 
  * Large-caliber catheters for high flow rates (≥300 mL/min)
 
  * Made of silicone or polyurethane
 
  * Mostly dual lumen with staggered end/side holes
 
  * Design prevents admixture at inflow/outflow portions to reduce recirculation 

### 7.3. INSERTION TECHNIQUES


* **Three Main Approaches**:
  1. **Catheter over needle**
     * Similar to peripheral catheterization
  2. **Catheter through needle**
     * Requires large-bore needle stick
  3. **Catheter over guidewire (Seldinger technique)**
     * Most commonly utilized technique

![Central Line Anatomy](CVS_MONITORING_CVS_MONITORING_Untitled_7.png_7a4112.png_57aefc.png)

![Central Line Placement](CVS_MONITORING_CVS_MONITORING_Untitled_8.png_89489d.png_c7643f.png)


* **Seldinger technique in detail:** 
  * Standard method regardless of catheter type
 
  * Requires sterile conditions 
  1. Introduce cannula/needle into vein using landmarks or ultrasound guidance 
  2. Insert J-shaped soft-tip guidewire through needle into vein
 
     * J-shape minimizes endothelial trauma 
  3. Remove needle 
  4. Make small skin incision 
  5. Introduce dilator over guidewire to create track through skin/tissues 
  6. Withdraw dilator 
  7. Railroad catheter over guidewire into final position 
  8. Withdraw guidewire 
  9. Aspirate blood from all ports to confirm positioning 
  10. Flush all ports with saline 
  11. Seal unused ports (never leave open to air due to air embolism risk) 
  12. Secure catheter to skin and apply sterile dressing 
  13. Obtain chest X-ray to confirm position and check for pneumothorax/hemothorax
 

* **Ultrasound guidance:** 
  * Should be routinely considered for central venous catheter insertion
 
  * Evidence shows reduced mechanical complications during internal jugular vein catheterization
 
  * Reduces catheter placement failures
 
  * Decreases time required for insertion 

### 7.4. CENTRAL VENOUS PRESSURE MONITORING
 

* **Definition:** Measures filling pressure of right atrium
 
* **Measurement methods:**

1. **Pressure Transducer Method:** 
   * Similar to arterial pressure monitoring system
 
   * Components: catheter, saline column, transducer, diaphragm, flushing device, oscilloscope
 
   * Transducer positioned at right atrium level
 
   * Limited frequency range adequate (simpler waveform shape) 

1. **Fluid Manometer Method:** 
   * Giving set with saline/dextrose connected to vertical manometer via three-way tap
 
   * Also connected to central venous catheter
 
   * Spirit-level side arm positioned at right atrium level (zero reference point)
 
   * Upper end of column open to air via filter (must stay dry)
 
   * Manometer filled to ~20-cm mark
 
   * Opening three-way tap to patient should show respiratory swing
 
   * CVP read in cm H2O when fluid level stabilizes
 
   * Works by balancing downward fluid pressure against central venous system pressure
 
* **Zero reference point:** Both methods require zeroing at right atrium level (midaxillary line) to eliminate hydrostatic pressure effects 

### 7.5. WAVEFORM COMPONENTS AND INTERPRETATION

![CVP Waveform](CVS_MONITORING_CVS_MONITORING_image_4.png_4bab03.png_387c3d.png)


* **CVP Waveform Components**: The normal CVP waveform consists of three peaks (a, c, and v waves) and two descents (x, y)

  * **a waves**: Result from atrial contraction

    * Absent in atrial fibrillation

    * Exaggerated in junctional rhythms ("cannon" a waves)

    * Large a waves observed with:

      * Tricuspid stenosis

      * Right ventricular hypertrophy (from pulmonic stenosis)

      * Pulmonary hypertension (from acute or chronic lung disease)

      * Impaired right ventricular compliance

  * **c waves**: Due to tricuspid valve elevation during early ventricular contraction

  * **v waves**: Reflect venous return against a closed tricuspid valve

    * Giant v waves typically seen in tricuspid regurgitation (begin immediately after QRS complex)

    * Large v waves observed with:

      * Right ventricular ischemia or failure

      * Impaired ventricular compliance (constrictive pericarditis or cardiac tamponade)

      * ⚠️ Prominent v wave may suggest right ventricular papillary muscle ischemia

  * **x descent**: Caused by downward displacement of tricuspid valve during systole

  * **y descent**: Due to tricuspid valve opening during diastole

### 7.6. TECHNICAL CONSIDERATIONS


* **Proper Setup**:

  * Careful leveling and zeroing of pressure transducers is essential

  * Same technique as invasive arterial pressure monitoring
* **Waveform Factors**: CVP trace character depends on multiple factors

  * Heart rate

  * Conduction disturbances

  * Tricuspid valve function

  * Intrathoracic pressure changes (normal or abnormal)

  * Right ventricular compliance changes

### 7.7. PATHOLOGICAL PATTERNS


* **Decreased RV Compliance**: When right ventricular compliance decreases

  * CVP often increases

  * Prominent a and v waves fuse to form an m or w configuration
* **Tricuspid Regurgitation**:

  * Typically produces giant v waves

  * Begin immediately after QRS complex

### 7.8. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS


* **Hemodynamic Assessment**:

  * Provides understanding of right-sided hemodynamic events

  * Indicates status of vascular volume

  * Less invasive and costly than pulmonary artery monitoring

  * ⚠️ Can be unreliable for estimating left ventricular filling pressures, especially with cardiac or pulmonary parenchymal disease

### 7.9. CANNULATION SITES COMPARISON

1. **Internal Jugular Vein (Right)**
   * **Preferred site for anesthesiologists** due to:

     * Accessibility from head of operating table

     * Predictable anatomy

     * High success rate in both adults and children

   * *Considerations*:

     * ⚠️ Potential for carotid artery puncture

     * **Ultrasound guidance strongly recommended** by professional societies to:

       * Reduce complications

       * Improve first-attempt success rates

     * In pediatrics: Highest placement success rate but may limit neck mobility causing patient discomfort
2. **Internal Jugular Vein (Left)**
   * *Less desirable due to*:

     * Risk of thoracic duct damage

     * Difficulty maneuvering catheters through jugular-subclavian junction

     * Increased risk of pleural effusion and chylothorax
3. **Subclavian Vein**
   * **CDC preferred site** to potentially reduce bloodstream infections

   * *Advantages*:

     * Lower risk of infection compared to femoral approach

     * In pediatrics: Lower risk of dislodgement and less restriction of motion

   * *Disadvantages*:

     * Greater risk of pneumothorax during insertion

     * May be difficult to stop bleeding in coagulopathic patients

     * In pediatrics: Lower success rates, increased catheter malposition, higher rates of inadvertent arterial puncture
4. **External Jugular Vein**
   * *Limitation*: Acute angle joining the great veins → increased likelihood of failure to access central circulation compared to IJ
5. **Femoral Vein**
   * *Considerations*:

     * Higher infection risk compared to subclavian approach

     * In pediatrics: May be preferred site for critically ill patients

     * No increased infection risk in children (compared to subclavian/jugular)

     * Higher risk of catheter thrombosis and accidental dislodgement

💡 **Clinical Pearl**: Physician experience and comfort with a particular site is the primary determinant of insertion success and complication rates.


* **Catheter Tip Placement** Optimal location: Just superior to or at the junction of the superior vena cava and right atrium
* **Special Considerations for Pediatric Patients**
* **Site Selection Factors**:

  * **Internal Jugular**: Highest success rate but may restrict neck mobility

  * **Subclavian**: Lower dislodgement risk but higher risk of malposition and pneumothorax

  * **Femoral**: Preferred in critically ill pediatrics; higher thrombosis risk and accidental dislodgement
* 📋 **Mnemonic: "RILES-F" for central line site selection considerations**
  * **R**ight internal jugular (first choice for anesthesiologists)

  * **I**nfection risk (lowest with subclavian)

  * **L**eft internal jugular (thoracic duct risk)

  * **E**xternal jugular (difficult central access)

  * **S**ubclavian (pneumothorax risk)

  * **F**emoral (thrombosis risk)

💡 **Clinical Pearl**: Routine use of ultrasound for central venous catheter placement decreases risk of iatrogenic vascular injury in both adults and children.

### 7.10. COMPLICATIONS AND MANAGEMENT


* **Absolute contraindications:**
  * Tumors, clots, or tricuspid valve vegetations that can be dislodged/embolized during cannulation
* **Site-specific contraindications:**
  * **Subclavian approach**: Inability to provide direct compression in event of accidental arterial puncture

    * ⚠️ Contraindicated in patients receiving anticoagulants
* **Access-related complications:**
  * Unintentional puncture of nearby arteries

  * Bleeding

  * Neuropathy

  * Pneumothorax from needle insertion into adjacent structures

  * Air embolism (if cannula open to atmosphere during/after placement)
* **Procedure-related complications:**
  * **Dysrhythmias**: Common (4.7-68.9% incidence)

    * Ventricular tachycardia or fibrillation during catheter advancement

    * Right bundle-branch block

    * Complete heart block (in patients with pre-existing left bundle-branch block)
* **Additional complications:** 
  * **Inaccurate readings:** Catheter blockage, excessive insertion, wrong zero level
 
  * **Pneumo/hemothorax:** 2-10% with subclavian approach, 1-2% with internal jugular
 
  * **Arterial puncture:** Potentially leading to arteriovenous fistula formation
 
  * **Air embolism** 
  * **Hematoma formation** 
  * **Tracheal puncture** 
  * **Sepsis/infection:** 2.8-20% incidence (Staphylococcus aureus and Enterococcus most common)
 
  * **False passage:** If guidewire/dilator advanced against resistance
 
  * **Cardiac complications:**
    * Self-limiting arrhythmias from guidewire/catheter irritation
 
    * Rare but serious: Venous or cardiac perforation
 
  * **Catheter-related venous thrombosis:** Up to 40% depending on site, duration, technique, patient condition
 
  * **Microshock:** Direct pathway to heart for minute electrical currents that can cause ventricular fibrillation
 
* **Guidelines for reducing sepsis and infection:** 
  * Education and training of staff for insertion and maintenance
 
  * Maximum sterile barrier precautions during insertion
 
  * > 0.5% chlorhexidine with alcohol for skin antisepsis
 
  * Use subclavian site instead of jugular or femoral in adults to minimize infection
 
  * Use ultrasound guidance if available (trained personnel only)
 
  * Use minimum number of ports/lumens needed for management
 
  * Promptly remove unnecessary catheters
 
  * Replace catheters inserted during emergency within 48 hours
 
  * Use sterile gauze or transparent dressing
 
  * Consider antimicrobial-impregnated catheters for expected placement >5 days 

**Bottom Line:** Central venous catheterization provides essential vascular access and monitoring capabilities but carries significant risks. Site selection should be based on clinical indication, patient factors, and physician experience. The right internal jugular vein is generally preferred for anesthesiologists due to its consistent anatomy and accessibility. Ultrasound guidance reduces complications and improves success rates. Understanding the CVP waveform components (a, c, v waves and x, y descents) is crucial for interpretation of volume status and right heart function.

## 8. CARDIAC IMPLANTABLE ELECTRONIC DEVICE (CIED) MONITORING

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Physics|Part A]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3: Perioperative Management and Monitoring|Part B]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Patient Care|Part C]]

**Cardiac implantable electronic devices require specialized perioperative monitoring and management to prevent electromagnetic interference and ensure appropriate device function.**

### 8.1. DEVICE CLASSIFICATION AND DEFINITIONS


* **Definition:** A cardiac implantable electronic device (CIED) refers to any permanently implantable cardiac pacemaker, implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD), or cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) device `[ASA2020]`
* **Device Categories:** `[ASA2020]`

  * **Pacemakers:** Provide electrical stimulation for bradyarrhythmias `[ASA2020]`

  * **Implantable Cardioverter-Defibrillators (ICDs):** Provide antitachycardia pacing and defibrillation for tachyarrhythmias `[ASA2020]`

  * **Cardiac Resynchronization Therapy (CRT) devices:** Improve cardiac output in heart failure patients `[ASA2020]`

  * **Combination devices:** CRT-D (with defibrillator) and CRT-P (pacemaker only) `[ASA2020]`

  * **Implantable Loop Recorders (ILRs):** Leadless cardiac monitoring devices for diagnostic purposes only `[Bryant2016]`

    * Indicated for recurrent unexplained syncope/palpitations `[Bryant2016]`

    * Long-term monitoring for atrial fibrillation patients `[Bryant2016]`

    * Self-contained electrodes capable of ECG recording `[Bryant2016]`

    * Automatically or patient-triggered activation `[Bryant2016]`

#### 8.1.1. DETAILED PACEMAKER INDICATIONS


* **Permanent Pacemaker (PPM) Indications:** `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Most common (58% in UK 2010):** Symptomatic bradycardia caused by atrioventricular block `[Bryant2016]`

    * First-degree block: P-R interval >0.2 s `[Bryant2016]`

    * Second-degree block: Intermittent interruption of conduction `[Bryant2016]`

    * Third-degree block: Complete interruption `[Bryant2016]`

    * Etiologies: Ischemic, degenerative, infiltrative processes, or iatrogenic (AV nodal ablation) `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Second most common (23.6% in 2010):** Sick sinus syndrome `[Bryant2016]`

    * Sinus node dysfunction with intermittent loss of P-waves `[Bryant2016]`

    * Sinus arrest causing symptomatic bradycardia `[Bryant2016]`
* **Biventricular Pacemaker (CRT) Indications:** `[Bryant2016]`

  * Symptomatic moderate-to-severe cardiac failure `[Bryant2016]`

  * Left ventricular ejection fraction <35% `[Bryant2016]`

  * Widened QRS interval `[Bryant2016]`

  * UK implantation rate: 145 per million population `[Bryant2016]`

  * Optimizes timing of RV and LV contraction `[Bryant2016]`
* **ICD Indications:** `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Secondary prevention (most common):** Survived cardiac arrest or significant hemodynamic compromise after ventricular arrhythmias `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Primary prevention:** 
    * Post-myocardial infarction with scar-related VT risk `[Bryant2016]`

    * Significant left ventricular dysfunction `[Bryant2016]`

    * High-risk familial conditions: Long QT syndrome, Brugada syndrome, cardiomyopathies `[Bryant2016]`

    * Selected congenital heart disease patients `[Bryant2016]`

  * **CRT-D devices:** Combination of CRT indications plus arrhythmia risk `[Bryant2016]`

#### 8.1.2. GENERIC PACEMAKER CODE


* **Purpose:** Describes PPM function using 4-position code `[Bryant2016]`
* **Code Structure (Positions 1-3):** `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Position I:** Chamber paced (O=None, A=Atrium, V=Ventricle, D=Dual) `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Position II:** Chamber sensed (O=None, A=Atrium, V=Ventricle, D=Dual) `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Position III:** Response to sensing (O=None, I=Inhibited, T=Triggered, D=Dual) `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Position IV:** Rate modulation (R=Rate modulation, O=None) `[Bryant2016]`
* **Common Pacing Modes:** `[Bryant2016]`

  * **VVI:** Ventricular paced, ventricular sensed, inhibiting response `[Bryant2016]`

  * **DDD:** Most commonly encountered mode with four possible responses `[Bryant2016]`
    1. Both chambers detected → inhibition of pacing `[Bryant2016]`
    2. Atrial pacing leads to intrinsic ventricular activity → inhibits ventricular pacing `[Bryant2016]`
    3. Sensed atrial activity triggers ventricular pacing when no spontaneous ventricular depolarization `[Bryant2016]`
    4. Pacing of both chambers if intrinsic activity undetected `[Bryant2016]`

#### 8.1.3. VENTRICLE-SPARING PACING MODES (ADIR/AAIR)


* **Rationale:** Evidence (MOST, DAVID trials) showed chronic RV apical pacing causes dyssynchrony and cardiomyopathy. Ventricle-sparing modes minimise unnecessary RV pacing.
* **ADIR Mode Explained:**
  * **A** (paced chamber) = Only the atrium is paced
  * **D** (sensed chamber) = Device listens to both atrium AND ventricle
  * **I** (response) = Inhibits pacing if activity sensed
  * **R** = Rate-responsive
* **Principle:** "Pace the atrium, but just watch the ventricle"
  * The device paces the atrium then asks: "Did my atrial pacing conduct through to the ventricle on its own?"
  * If yes → No ventricular pacing needed (AV node did the job)
  * If no → Device can switch modes or intervene
* **Ideal patient:** Sick sinus syndrome with intact AV conduction - fix the slow atrium, let conduction happen naturally
* **Related algorithms:** Medtronic MVP (Managed Ventricular Pacing) operates in ADIR/AAIR and switches to DDD only if AV block develops
* **Clinical interpretation:** The programmed mode tells you the *strategy*; the Vp percentage tells you the *reality* of how much the patient actually needs ventricular pacing

#### 8.1.4. ICD PHYSICS AND FUNCTION


* **ICD System Components:** `[Bryant2016]`

  * Larger generator than pacemakers containing: `[Bryant2016]`

    * Electronic circuitry `[Bryant2016]`

    * Batteries `[Bryant2016]`

    * Capacitor for defibrillation `[Bryant2016]`
* **Defibrillation Mechanism:** `[Bryant2016]`

  * Requires ~750 V to deliver 30-45 J output `[Bryant2016]`

  * Achieved by charging capacitor and dissipating energy `[Bryant2016]`

  * Shock delivery: Painful and utilizes more battery capacity than ATP `[Bryant2016]`
* **Arrhythmia Detection:** `[Bryant2016]`

  * Continuously senses and analyzes native electrical activity (R-R interval) `[Bryant2016]`

  * Detects VT or ventricular fibrillation `[Bryant2016]`

  * ⚠️ **EMI risk:** May be falsely interpreted as shockable rhythm leading to inappropriate shock delivery `[Bryant2016]`
* **Anti-Tachycardia Pacing (ATP):** `[Bryant2016]`

  * Programmed burst of overdrive pacing to terminate VT `[Bryant2016]`

  * If ATP fails or VF detected → capacitor charges and releases shock `[Bryant2016]`

  * Shock passes between defibrillator coils (both on ventricular lead OR box as one coil with single ventricular lead coil) `[Bryant2016]`
* **Anti-Bradycardia Function:** `[Bryant2016]`

  * ICD has pacemaker capabilities `[Bryant2016]`

  * Can pace in event of post-shock bradyarrhythmias `[Bryant2016]`
* **Special ICD Types:** `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Subcutaneous ICD (S-ICD):** Less invasive option `[Bryant2016]`

    * Lead placed subcutaneously in midline `[Bryant2016]`

    * Pulse generator in left axilla `[Bryant2016]`

    * Delivers 80 J biphasic shock `[Bryant2016]`

    * Indications: Difficult venous access, complex cardiac anatomy `[Bryant2016]`

    * Limitations: Shorter battery longevity, post-shock pacing only `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Leadless pacemakers:** Small sensing and pacing device entirely contained within right ventricle with no leads `[Bryant2016]`

#### 8.1.5. PACING DEPENDENCY: ASSESSMENT AND CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS


* **Definition:** Pacing-dependent patients rely on device pacing for adequate cardiac output `[ASA2020]`
* **Clinical indicators of pacing dependency:** `[ASA2020]`
  * Bradycardia causing syncope or symptoms resulting in device implantation `[ASA2020]`
  * Successful atrioventricular nodal ablation `[ASA2020]`
  * Device interrogation showing no spontaneous ventricular activity when programmed to non-tracking mode at lowest rate `[ASA2020]`
* **Interpreting Pacing Percentages from Interrogation:**

| Abbreviation | Meaning |
|--------------|---------|
| ApVs | Atrium paced, Ventricle sensed (conducted on its own) |
| ApVp | Atrium paced, Ventricle paced |
| AsVs | Atrium sensed, Ventricle sensed |
| AsVp | Atrium sensed, Ventricle paced |

  * **Vp (Ventricular pacing) percentage** = ApVp + AsVp = surrogate for "how much does this patient need the device to make the ventricles beat?"
  * High Vp (>40%) suggests significant AV conduction disease → patient may be Vp-dependent
  * Low Vp (<20%) suggests intact AV conduction → patient tolerates brief pacing inhibition
* **The Key Clinical Question:** "If EMI inhibits this pacemaker, will the patient have a ventricular rhythm?"

| Patient Type | What Happens if Pacing Inhibited | Result |
|--------------|----------------------------------|--------|
| Sick sinus + intact AV node (low Vp) | Slow native atrial rate conducts through working AV node | **Bradycardia** (tolerable) |
| AV block (high Vp) | Atrial activity cannot reach ventricles | **Asystole** (dangerous) |

* ⚠️ **Critical consideration:** Pacing-dependent patients are at highest risk for hemodynamic compromise if device malfunction occurs `[ASA2020]`

#### 8.1.6. CIED PHYSICS AND NOMENCLATURE


* **Pacemaker System Components:** `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Pulse generator:** Contains battery and electronic circuitry including silicon chip `[Bryant2016]`

  * **One or more pacing leads** `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Electronic sensing and output circuitry:** Analyzes cardiac rhythm and paces as programmed `[Bryant2016]`
* **Lead Configuration:** `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Unipolar leads:**
    * Single electrode at pacing tip (cathode) `[Bryant2016]`

    * PPM box serves as anode `[Bryant2016]`

    * More susceptible to EMI `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Bipolar leads (more common):**
    * Anode lies on lead proximal to cathode tip `[Bryant2016]`

    * Reduced susceptibility to EMI `[Bryant2016]`
* **Pacemaker Function Concepts:** `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Electrical capture:** Successful pacing-induced depolarization `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Stimulation threshold:** Minimum electrical stimulus that consistently exceeds excitation threshold to produce cardiac depolarization `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Sensing:** Device response to intrinsic electrical activity `[Bryant2016]`

    * Can inhibit or trigger pacing in one or more chambers `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Sensitivity:** Minimum intrinsic atrial or ventricular electrical activity (in millivolts) sensed by device `[Bryant2016]`

    * Set as margin around amplitude of sensed signal `[Bryant2016]`

    * Depends on origin (atrial/ventricular) and lead type (unipolar/bipolar) `[Bryant2016]`
* **Sensing Errors:** `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Under-sensing:** Device fails to detect intrinsic activity `[Bryant2016]`

    * Consequence: "Over-pacing" may occur with potential risk of triggering malignant tachyarrhythmias `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Over-sensing:** Device inappropriately registers myocardial activity when none exists `[Bryant2016]`

    * Consequence: Failure to pace despite no intrinsic electrical activity `[Bryant2016]`

    * ⚠️ **Most frequent PPM interaction with EMI** `[Bryant2016]`

    * Particularly dangerous in pacemaker-dependent patients `[Bryant2016]`

### 8.4. PREOPERATIVE DEVICE PROGRAMMING

#### 8.4.1. INDICATIONS FOR REPROGRAMMING


* **High EMI risk procedures requiring reprogramming:** `[ASA2020]`

  * Monopolar electrosurgery superior to umbilicus `[ASA2020]`

  * Radiofrequency ablation superior to umbilicus `[ASA2020]`

  * MRI procedures (conditional and non-conditional devices) `[ASA2020]`

  * Electroconvulsive therapy `[ASA2020]`

#### 8.4.2. PACEMAKER PROGRAMMING MODIFICATIONS

* **Magnet Effect on Pacemakers:** Switches device to **asynchronous pacing mode** (DOO, VOO, AOO) at a fixed rate
  * EMI from monopolar diathermy causes **oversensing** → pacemaker interprets noise as native cardiac activity → inappropriately inhibits pacing
  * Asynchronous mode eliminates sensing entirely → device paces regardless of EMI
  * Magnet rate provides diagnostic information - declining rate indicates battery depletion (elective replacement indicator)
* **Decision Framework for Magnet/Reprogramming:**

| Vp% | EMI Risk | Magnet/Reprogram? | Rationale |
|-----|----------|-------------------|-----------|
| High (>40%) | High (monopolar above umbilicus) | **Yes** | AV block → EMI inhibition causes asystole |
| High | Low | Consider | Lower risk but no escape rhythm if inhibited |
| Low (<20%) | High | **Probably not** (have magnet ready) | Intact AV node → bradycardia only, tolerable |
| Low | Low | No | Minimal risk |

* **Why Low Vp Patients May Not Need Magnet:**
  * In sick sinus syndrome with intact AV conduction, EMI inhibits pacing → patient falls back on (slow) native SA node output → conducts through intact AV node → **bradycardia but not asystole**
  * Trade-off accepted: brief, intermittent bradycardia during diathermy bursts is usually tolerable in an anaesthetised patient
  * If patient has documented sinus arrest with long pauses and no escape rhythm, approach more cautiously
* **R-on-T Risk with Magnet Application:**
  * Asynchronous pacing is "blind" - delivers spikes at fixed intervals regardless of native rhythm
  * R-on-T occurs when a pacing spike lands during the relative refractory period (T wave) of a native beat → requires **competing rhythms**
  * **In Vp-dependent patients (complete heart block):** No native impulses reach ventricles → no competing rhythm → asynchronous pacing captures every beat → **R-on-T risk minimal**
  * **In patients with intact AV node:** If native rate is close to asynchronous rate, intermittent competition may occur → risk present but usually low in bradycardic sick sinus patients (native rate typically slower than magnet rate of 85-100 bpm)
* **Pacing-dependent patients:** `[ASA2020]`
  * **Convert to asynchronous pacing mode** (VOO, DOO, AOO) `[ASA2020]`
  * **Disable rate-responsive pacing sensors** to prevent inappropriate tachycardia `[ASA2020]`
  * **Maintain appropriate pacing rate** for patient's clinical condition `[ASA2020]`
* **Non-pacing-dependent patients:** `[ASA2020]`
  * Consider disabling pacing output in low EMI risk procedures `[ASA2020]`
  * Monitor for EMI-induced inappropriate pacing `[ASA2020]`

#### 8.4.3. ICD PROGRAMMING MODIFICATIONS

* **Magnet Effect on ICDs:** **Suspends tachyarrhythmia detection and therapy** (no shocks delivered)
  * ⚠️ **Crucially:** Does NOT convert to asynchronous pacing (unlike pacemakers)
  * Prevents inappropriate shocks from EMI being misinterpreted as VF/VT
  * Must be removed promptly post-procedure to restore protection
* **Antitachycardia therapy suspension:** `[ASA2020]`
  * **Mandatory for high EMI risk procedures** `[ASA2020]`
  * **Ensure monitored environment** before suspension `[ASA2020]`
  * **Alternative methods:** Programming vs. magnet application `[ASA2020]`
* **Magnet considerations:** `[ASA2020]`
  * ⚠️ **Avoid indiscriminate magnet use** over ICDs `[ASA2020]`
  * **Magnet application:** Suspends antitachycardia therapies but does not reprogram device `[ASA2020]`
  * **Verification required:** Ensure suspension was achieved and intended `[ASA2020]`
* **ICD Magnet Practical Points:** `[Bryant2016]`
  * Place and secure (with surgical tape) medical grade magnet on device for duration of surgery `[Bryant2016]`
  * Works for all commonly encountered ICDs provided magnet function not disabled `[Bryant2016]`
  * **Audible tones (Medtronic/Boston Scientific):** Emitted from ICD to indicate magnet applied and defibrillator functions deactivated `[Bryant2016]`
  * ⚠️ **Removal at end of surgery:** Should promptly reactivate defibrillator function `[Bryant2016]`
* **Key Caveat:** Magnet responses are **manufacturer-specific and programmable** - some devices can have magnet response disabled entirely. Preoperative device interrogation remains essential.

### 8.6. EMI SOURCE-SPECIFIC MANAGEMENT

#### 8.6.1. ELECTROSURGERY MANAGEMENT


* **Risk minimization strategies:** `[ASA2020]`

  * **Dispersive electrode positioning:** Current pathway should not pass through device or leads `[ASA2020]`

  * **Avoid proximity:** Keep electrosurgical field away from generator and leads `[ASA2020]`

  * **Technique modification:** Use short, intermittent, irregular bursts at lowest feasible energy `[ASA2020]`

  * **Alternative methods:** Prefer bipolar electrosurgery or ultrasonic scalpel when possible `[ASA2020]`
* **Prohibited practices:** `[ASA2020]`

  * ⚠️ **Never wave activated electrode over device generator** `[ASA2020]`
* **Enhanced Diathermy Management:** `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Bipolar vs. monopolar:** Bipolar electrodes and engineered shielding have reduced EMI risk `[Bryant2016]`

    * Bipolar electrical diathermy considered safer than monopolar `[Bryant2016]`

    * Should be avoided where possible for both types `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Monopolar diathermy technique (if required):** `[Bryant2016]`

    * Use in short 1-2 second bursts with 10-second pauses `[Bryant2016]`

    * Prefer cutting rather than coagulation current where possible `[Bryant2016]`

    * Pathway from diathermy to return electrode should not pass near CIED `[Bryant2016]`

    * Current field should be at right angles to pacing leads `[Bryant2016]`

    * Keep diathermy cables well away from implant site `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Device proximity considerations:** `[Bryant2016]`

    * If device recently checked and surgical site remote, likelihood of malfunction minimal `[Bryant2016]`

    * ⚠️ **Risk stratification by anatomical location:** Over-sensing unlikely for procedures where diathermy and return electrode below umbilicus `[Bryant2016]`
* **Other EMI Source Management:** `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Mobile phones:** Generally considered safe but avoid direct placement onto CIED `[Bryant2016]`

    * Could potentially activate "magnet mode" `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Wireless medical equipment:** Infusion pumps, monitoring devices, ultrasound probes with wireless technology `[Bryant2016]`

    * Should be distanced from CIED as potential EMI source `[Bryant2016]`

#### 8.6.4. OTHER EMI SOURCES


* **Lithotripsy:** Avoid focusing beam near device generator `[ASA2020]`
* **Radiofrequency identification devices:** Maintain distance from device when possible `[ASA2020]`
* **Electroconvulsive therapy:** 
  * Program to asynchronous mode in pacing-dependent patients `[ASA2020]`

  * Suspend ICD antitachycardia functions `[ASA2020]`

  * Monitor for post-convulsive arrhythmias `[ASA2020]`
* **Enhanced Procedure-Specific Management:** `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Tissue expanders in breast surgery:** `[Bryant2016]`

    * Tissue expanders using magnets to orientate needle should be avoided `[Bryant2016]`

    * Close proximity to CIED may risk magnetic switch activation `[Bryant2016]`

    * Could cause conversion to asynchronous pacing or ICD failure to detect tachyarrhythmia `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) - detailed considerations:** `[Bryant2016]`

    * Brief (1-2 second) electrical stimulus may cause pacemaker inhibition (usually not clinically significant) `[Bryant2016]`

    * Subsequent seizure activity may cause more prolonged over-sensing `[Bryant2016]`

    * Reprogramming to asynchronous mode may be appropriate for selected patients `[Bryant2016]`

    * ⚠️ **Risk:** Asynchronous mode may also risk arrhythmias when intrinsic electrical activity exists `[Bryant2016]`

    * Defibrillator function should be disabled (seizure activity may trigger inappropriate shock from EMI or reactive sinus tachycardia) `[Bryant2016]`

    * **Challenge:** ECT often occurs in isolated sites adding complexity to planning `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Transcutaneous electric nerve stimulation (TENS):** `[Bryant2016]`

    * ⚠️ **Contraindicated in patients with CIEDs** `[Bryant2016]`

    * Transcutaneous impulses may cause inappropriate sensing `[Bryant2016]`

    * Can lead to pacemaker inhibition or inappropriate ICD shock administration `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Diagnostic radiation:** `[Bryant2016]`

    * Generally does not significantly impact CIED function `[Bryant2016]`

    * Rare case reports of inappropriate sensing and electronic reset with higher radiation doses from new-generation multi-slice CT scanners `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Radiation therapy:** `[Bryant2016]`

    * Usually considered safe `[Bryant2016]`

    * Direct radiation of CIED should be avoided `[Bryant2016]`

    * Device may need re-siting if located directly within radiation field `[Bryant2016]`

    * Device function should be verified frequently in case of radiation-induced reversion to backup safety mode (usually VVI, but manufacturer-specific) `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy:** `[Bryant2016]`

    * Shock waves are acoustic pressure waves from electromagnetic, piezoelectric, electroconductive, or electrohydraulic sources `[Bryant2016]`

    * Wave form comprises compressive and tensile phase generating acoustic energy for stone disintegration `[Bryant2016]`

    * Generally low risk of CIED dysfunction but devices should be checked within 1 month of procedure `[Bryant2016]`

    * Case reports of pacing suppression and backup safety mode reversion exist `[Bryant2016]`

    * **Precautions:** Keep lithotripter at least 6 inches away from CIED `[Bryant2016]`

    * Beam should not be focused near CIED `[Bryant2016]`

    * Lithotripsy pulses should be timed with ECG `[Bryant2016]`

    * Rate modulation should be deactivated `[Bryant2016]`
* **Implantable Loop Recorder (ILR) Management:** `[Bryant2016]`

  * Do not deliver treatment and present no risk to patient when EMI encountered `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Consideration:** Diathermy noise may fill memory banks and overwrite existing data `[Bryant2016]`

  * Prudent to contact cardiac physiology department to download relevant clinical data before surgery `[Bryant2016]`

### 8.7. EMERGENCY CARDIOVERSION/DEFIBRILLATION

#### 8.7.1. EMERGENCY PROTOCOL


* **Initial response for patients with ICDs:** `[ASA2020]`

  * **Remove EMI sources** and magnets to re-enable ICD function `[ASA2020]`

  * **Observe for appropriate ICD therapy** delivery `[ASA2020]`

  * **Determine if ICD antitachycardia therapy needs re-enabling** if disabled by programming `[ASA2020]`
* **Intraoperative Arrhythmia Management:** `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Preparation:** Equipment immediately available for external defibrillation and/or temporary pacing `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Pad placement:** External defibrillator/pacing pads should be attached before surgery (especially when chest wall access difficult) `[Bryant2016]`

    * Position at least 10-15 cm away from CIED edge `[Bryant2016]`

    * Avoids remote possibility of device damage or myocardial damage from excess current flow `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Treatment principles:** Follow standard ALS procedures `[Bryant2016]`

  * **External pacing:** If necessary, capture usually achieved at currents ~50-100 mA using external pads `[Bryant2016]`

  * **High-risk patient considerations:** Possibility of intraoperative arrhythmias particularly in ICD patients when defibrillator function temporarily disabled `[Bryant2016]`

#### 8.7.2. EXTERNAL DEFIBRILLATION TECHNIQUE


* **When ICD therapy fails or cannot be restored:** `[ASA2020]`

  * **Follow ACLS guidelines** for energy levels and pad placement `[ASA2020]`

  * **Pad positioning:** Avoid placing directly over device generator `[ASA2020]`

  * **Preferred configuration:** Anterior-posterior rather than anterior-lateral when possible `[ASA2020]`

  * **Energy levels:** Use clinically appropriate energy regardless of device presence `[ASA2020]`
* **Post-defibrillation management:** `[ASA2020]`

  * **Immediate device interrogation** after external cardioversion/defibrillation `[ASA2020]`

### 8.8. POSTOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT

#### 8.8.1. CONTINUED MONITORING REQUIREMENTS


* **Immediate postoperative period:** `[ASA2020]`

  * **Extended monitoring** until device settings restored to permanent configuration `[ASA2020]`
* **Equipment availability:** `[ASA2020]`

  * **Backup pacing and defibrillation equipment** immediately available until settings restored `[ASA2020]`
* **Enhanced Postoperative Management:** `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Ideal recovery environment:** High dependency setting with continuous monitoring and full resuscitation equipment `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Device function restoration:** Defibrillator function of ICD and any suspended rate modulator pacing function needs reactivating by cardiac physiologist `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Magnet use verification:** Device should be checked at earliest opportunity if magnet used intraoperatively `[Bryant2016]`

  * **Adverse incident management:** Any adverse incident relating to CIED should be addressed by cardiac physiologist as soon as possible `[Bryant2016]`

#### 8.8.2. DEVICE INTERROGATION INDICATIONS


* **Mandatory postoperative interrogation:** `[ASA2020]`

  * **Emergency surgery** without appropriate preoperative evaluation `[ASA2020]`

  * **Suspected antitachycardia therapy disability** rather than temporary suspension `[ASA2020]`

  * **Observed antitachycardia therapy delivery** `[ASA2020]`

* **Timing considerations:** `[ASA2020]`

  * **Immediate interrogation** when indicated clinically `[ASA2020]`

  * **Within 30 days** if not interrogated during immediate postoperative period `[ASA2020]`

#### 8.8.3. SETTINGS RESTORATION


* **Programming restoration requirements:** `[ASA2020]`

  * **Restore permanent settings** before discharge from monitored environment `[ASA2020]`

  * **Verify appropriate settings** for patient's current clinical condition `[ASA2020]`

  * **Reprogram to newly appropriate settings** if indicated by clinical changes `[ASA2020]`

#### 8.9.1. MULTIDISCIPLINARY APPROACH


* **Team coordination requirements:** `[ASA2020]`

  * **Anesthesiologist** responsible for perioperative device management `[ASA2020]`

  * **Device specialist consultation** when programming modifications needed `[ASA2020]`

#### 8.9.2. STANDARDIZED PROTOCOLS


* **Institutional protocol elements:** `[ASA2020]`

💡 **Clinical Pearls:**
* **Device interrogation within 3-6 months** of surgery is preferred by most experts `[ASA2020]`

* **Bipolar electrosurgery is safer** than monopolar but can still cause interference `[ASA2020]`

Success depends on multidisciplinary coordination, proper device interrogation, appropriate programming modifications for high-risk procedures, and maintaining emergency preparedness throughout the perioperative period. **Cardiac output measurement techniques provide quantitative assessment of circulatory function to guide clinical management.**
    * CVP, RV pressure, PA pressure, PCWP
 
    * Cardiac output, cardiac index
 
    * Stroke volume, stroke volume index
 
    * LV and RV stroke work index
 
    * SVR, pulmonary vascular resistance
 
    * Mixed venous oximetry (SvO₂)
 
    * Blood temperature
 
    * Degree of shunt
 
    * Oxygen delivery and consumption (calculated)
 
* **PHYSIOLOGICAL PRINCIPLES & INTERPRETATION** 

| Variable | Formula | Normal Range | Units |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cardiac index | Cardiac output (L/min) / Body surface area (m²) | 2.2-4.2 | L/min/m² |
| Total peripheral resistance | (MAP - CVP) × 80 / Cardiac output (L/min) | 1200-1500 | dynes·s·cm⁻⁵ |
| Pulmonary vascular resistance | (PA (Mean) - PAOP) × 80 / Cardiac output (L/min) | 100-300 | dynes·s·cm⁻⁵ |

* **Starling relationship**:

  * LV function relates to LV end-diastolic muscle fiber length

  * Fiber length proportional to end-diastolic volume if compliance is normal

  * LVEDP reflects fiber length with normal compliance

  * In normal mitral valve, left atrial pressure approximates LV pressure during filling
* **Pressure transmission principles**:

  * Left atrium connects to right heart via pulmonary vasculature

  * Wedged PA catheter isolates distal lumen from right-sided pressures

  * Distal opening exposed only to capillary pressure

  * With normal airway pressures and pulmonary vasculature, capillary pressure equals left atrial pressure

  * PAOP thus indirectly measures LVEDP
* **Cardiac output measurement (thermodilution principle)**:

  * Based on measuring blood flow through right ventricular outflow tract

  * Uses indicator dilution technique with temperature as indicator

  * Thermistor at catheter tip measures temperature changes

  * Can be performed as intermittent bolus or continuous measurement
* **Intermittent thermodilution cardiac output (TCO)**:

  * Injects room-temperature or chilled fluid bolus

  * Measures temperature drop as bolus mixes with blood

  * CO calculated from area under temperature curve
* **Continuous CO monitoring**:

  * Uses coiled filament creating pulsed heating signal

  * Tip thermistor detects temperature changes

  * Microcomputer creates thermodilution curve and calculates CO

  * Compares favorably with bolus measurements
* **Detailed CO measurement process:** 
  * **Traditional thermodilution method:**
    * 10 mL cold injectate bolused upstream via proximal lumen
 
    * Thermistor measures downstream blood temperature change
 
    * Temperature-time curve displayed for CO calculation
 
    * Accurate injectate volume required and rapid injection needed
 
    * Usually averages three readings
 
  
  * **Continuous cardiac output designs:**
    * Heating filament wrapped around catheter (14-25 cm from tip)
 
    * Periodically heats passing blood
 
    * Small temperature change measured by thermistor 4 cm from tip in PA
 
* **Advanced PA catheter capabilities:** 
  * **Continuous mixed venous oxygen saturation monitoring:**
    * Uses fiberoptic technology
 
  * **Cardiac pacing:**
    * Available in some designs 

### 9.1. COMPLICATIONS AND LIMITATIONS


* **LIMITATIONS & POTENTIAL ERRORS**
* **Unreliable PAOP-LVEDP relationship** during:

  * Mitral stenosis

  * Pulmonary vascular disease

  * High airway pressures

  * Changing left atrial or ventricular compliance
* **Thermodilution accuracy affected by**:

  * Incorrect catheter positioning

  * Reflux of injectate within introducer sheath

  * Catheter advanced too far (heating filament beyond pulmonic valve)

  * Hypothermia

  * Rapid administration of unwarmed IV fluids
* **Invalid TCO measurements with**:

  * Intracardiac shunts (create accessory blood flow paths)

  * Significant tricuspid regurgitation (permits retrograde flow)

  * Pediatric patients (alternative techniques needed)
* **COMPLICATIONS**
* **Common central venous catheterization complications**
* **Cardiovascular**:

  * Endocarditis

  * Thrombogenesis

  * Arrhythmias

  * Conduction abnormalities

  * Pulmonary valvular damage

  * Catheter knotting
* **Pulmonary**:

  * Pulmonary infarction

  * PA rupture and hemorrhage

    * ⚠️ Higher risk in: patients on anticoagulants, elderly, females, pulmonary hypertension

    * ⚠️ Hemoptysis is insidious sign of PA rupture → prompt placement of double-lumen ETT required
* **Duration limitation**: Should not exceed 72 hours

* **Detailed morbidity and complications:** 
  * **Overall patient morbidity:** 0.4%
 
  * **Categories of complications:**
    1. **Complications from central venous cannulation** (as previously described) 
    
    2. **Complications during catheter passage/advancement:**
       * Arrhythmias (ventricular ectopics, ventricular tachycardia, others)
 
       * Heart block
 
       * Knotting/kinking (more common in low-flow states and enlarged hearts)
 
       * Valvular damage
 
       * PA vessel perforation 
    
    3. **Complications from PA presence:**
       * Thrombosis (reduced with heparin-bonded catheters)
 
       * PA rupture (more common in elderly, may present as hemoptysis, often fatal)
 
       * Infection
 
       * Balloon rupture
 
       * Pulmonary infarction
 
       * Valve damage
 
       * Ongoing arrhythmias
 
* **PAOP-LVEDP relationship limitations:** 
  * Not accurate in:

    * Mitral stenosis/regurgitation
 
    * Pulmonary veno-occlusive disease
 
    * Left atrial myxoma
 
    * Ball valve thrombus
 
    * Total anomalous pulmonary venous drainage
 
    * Cardiac tamponade
 
    * Acute RV dilatation from:

      * RV infarction
 
      * Massive pulmonary embolism
 
      * Acute severe tricuspid regurgitation
 
  
  * **Catheter whip artifact:**
    * Caused by catheter coursing through right heart
 
    * Cardiac contractions produce "shock transients" or "whip" artifacts
 
    * Negative deflections can cause underestimation of PA pressures 

### 9.2. ALTERNATIVE MONITORING TECHNIQUES


* **Less invasive CO measurement options**:

  * Pulse contour analyses of arterial pressure waveform

  * Transpulmonary thermodilution

  * Bioimpedance measurements across chest

  * Femoral arterial thermodilution (FATD) in pediatric patients

    * Correlates closely with Fick-based calculations

    * Less hazardous than PA sampling (avoids pneumothorax risk)
* **Alternative oxygenation measures**:

  * Right atrial blood oxygen saturation vs. mixed venous saturation

  * Can assess tissue oxygen extraction adequacy
* **Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE)**:

  * More precise estimation of preload than PA catheter

  * Has contributed to declining use of PA catheters

### 9.3. EVIDENCE & OUTCOMES


* 🔑 **Current practice considerations**: Several large observational studies show worse outcomes in patients managed with PA catheters compared to similar patients without PA catheters
* Usage has diminished over last two decades due to less invasive alternatives
* Remains clinical gold standard for CO determination when absolutely necessary

💡 **Clinical Pearl**: Despite the historical importance of PA catheters in guiding hemodynamic management, their use should be carefully considered given the availability of less invasive alternatives and evidence suggesting potential harm. Reserve for situations where the specific data provided cannot be obtained through safer means.

**Bottom Line:** PA catheters provide comprehensive hemodynamic monitoring including direct pressure measurements, cardiac output, and mixed venous oxygen saturation. They help differentiate causes of hemodynamic instability and guide targeted therapy. Their use has declined as evidence does not show outcome benefit and less invasive alternatives have emerged. Major complications include arrhythmias, thrombogenesis, and PA rupture. When used, careful attention to insertion technique and monitoring for complications is essential.

## 10. CARDIAC OUTPUT MEASUREMENT METHODS

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Cardiovascular Physiology|Part A]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3: Perioperative Management and Monitoring|Part B]]

**Cardiac output measurement techniques provide quantitative assessment of circulatory function to guide clinical management.**

### 10.1. CLINICAL ASSESSMENT METHODS


* **Clinical methods** for evaluating cardiac output:

  * **Peripheral perfusion** signs:

    * Skin color, temperature and capillary refill time

  * **Respiratory rate**
  * **Conscious level**:

    * Measured by GCS

  * **Urine output**
* **Basic monitoring** indicators:

  * **Blood pressure**:

    * BP = CO x SVR

    * Blood pressure gives a surrogate marker of cardiac output, but can be misleading as the body may compensate by changing systemic vascular resistance

    * A slightly low blood pressure with warm hands and feet suggests adequate cardiac output in the presence of low resistance, as seen in anesthetized patients

    * A normal blood pressure in a clammy, pale patient with cold arms and legs suggests severe vasoconstriction and high SVR to compensate for low cardiac output

  * **Pulse oximetry**:

    * Gives an idea of pulse volume and variability and peripheral perfusion

  * **ETCO2**:

    * End tidal CO2 will drop in the presence of severely diminished cardiac output

    * Used as a marker of return of circulation in cardiac arrest management

  * **ECG**:

    * Can help diagnose the ***cause*** of low cardiac output, but doesn't offer specific information about cardiac output itself
* **Laboratory indicators**:

  * **Lactate** gives an idea of adequacy of perfusion to vital organs and degree of anaerobic respiration

  * **Venous oxygen saturations** from a central line indicate oxygen extraction and tissue perfusion

    * Low venous oxygen saturations are seen in ***stagnant*** hypoxia, where blood is moving so slowly that organs extract a greater proportion of available oxygen
* **Historical context of cardiac output monitoring:** 
  * PA catheter developed in 1970s - initially only bedside device for CO measurement
 
  * Gained widespread acceptance in intensive care and anesthesia practice
 
  * Later lost favor due to technical demands and studies suggesting increased mortality
 
  * Mortality concerns later refuted, but technical challenges remained
 
  * Led to development of less invasive technologies
 
  * Thermodilution remains gold standard for bedside CO measurement 

### 10.2. THERMODILUTION METHOD (PAC)

#### 10.2.1. PRINCIPLES OF OPERATION


* **Basic Physiologic Concept**: In a structurally normal heart (without septal defects or patent ductus arteriosus), time-averaged blood flow through the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) accurately approximates cardiac output (CO)

  * *Note*: Small amount of venous return to left heart from bronchial circulation and thebesian veins is neglected

  * Time averaging suppresses effects of beat-to-beat pulsatility
* **Pulmonary Artery Catheter (PAC) Placement**: Properly positioned PAC passes through the RVOT

  * CO monitoring using PAC depends on assessing blood flow rate through right heart

  * Measurement based on indicator dilution principle: dilution of known quantity by passing blood flow
* **Thermodilution Cardiac Output (TCO)**: Modern clinical practice uses temperature change as indicator

  * Depends on temperature measurement near PAC tip using thermistor

  * 🔑 Two methods: Intermittent bolus technique and continuous monitoring

#### 10.2.2. INTERMITTENT BOLUS TECHNIQUE


* **Methodology**: Cold saline injected into central vein → temperature change detected in pulmonary artery

  * Thermistor records temperature fall as bolus mixes with surrounding blood

  * CO estimated from area under temperature curve
* **Stewart Hamilton Equation**: Mathematical formula for calculating cardiac output from thermodilution data

![Stewart Hamilton Equation](CVS_MONITORING_CVS_MONITORING_nse-7921523409294180510-113090.jpg_2c6d02.jpg_fb31cc.jpg)


* **Calculation Factors**:

  * Specific heat capacity and gravity of blood

  * Specific heat capacity and gravity of injectate

  * Volume of injectate

  * Catheter size
* **Advantages**:

  * Safe, non-toxic injectate (saline)
* **Disadvantages**:

  * Variation (up to 10% between readings in an individual patient with no other changes)

  * Wrong volume of injectate can cause over or underestimation of cardiac output

  * Doesn't work reliably in atrial fibrillation or tricuspid regurgitation
* **Clinical Correlation**: When performed properly, TCO measurements correlate well with direct Fick or dye dilution estimates of CO

#### 10.2.3. CONTINUOUS CO MONITORING


* **Technical Components**:

  * Coiled filament creates pulsed, low-power heating signal in right atrium/ventricle

  * Heat signal follows proprietary pseudorandom sequence

  * Thermistor at PAC tip detects blood temperature changes
* **Processing Method**:

  * Microcomputer creates thermodilution curve based on pulsed inputs and measured temperatures

  * CO computed using conservation of heat equation

  * Effectively uses warmed bolus rather than chilled bolus
* **Performance**: Compares favorably with bolus CO measurements

  * ⚠️ Note: Time lag can exist

  * Maintains accuracy under conditions of varying patient temperature and CO

#### 10.2.4. PROPER USE AND INTERPRETATION


* **Critical Positioning Considerations**:

  * **Insufficient advancement**: If injection port remains within introducer sheath:

    * Reflux of injectate within sheath

    * "Lost" thermal energy

    * Smaller-than-expected temperature change at thermistor

    * Erroneously HIGH cardiac output reading

  * **Excessive advancement**: If heating filament lies beyond pulmonic valve:

    * Similar errors in measurement
* **Confounding Factors**:

  * Hypothermia

  * Rapid concurrent administration of unwarmed IV fluids

#### 10.2.5. THERMODILUTION CURVE CHARACTERISTICS


* **Interpretation**: After injection, temperature plotted as function of time produces thermodilution curve

  * Area under curve inversely related to cardiac output

  * Larger area = lower CO; Smaller area = higher CO
* **Shape Analysis**:

  * Normal curve: Smooth, rapid upstroke followed by exponential decay

  * Abnormal curves may indicate:

    * Catheter malposition

    * Tricuspid regurgitation

    * Intracardiac shunts

![Thermodilution Curve](CVS_MONITORING_CVS_MONITORING_Untitled_11.png_ae769a.png_8d205f.png)

### 10.3. TRANSPULMONARY THERMODILUTION

#### 10.3.1. TECHNIQUE & PRINCIPLES


* **Definition**: Minimally invasive hemodynamic monitoring technique
* **Methodology**: Cold saline injected into central vein → temperature change detected in central artery (femoral/axillary)
* **Measures LV rather than RV cardiac output**
* **Based on analysis of thermodilution curve using Stewart-Hamilton principle**

#### 10.3.2. MEASURED PARAMETERS


* **Global End-Diastolic Volume (GEDV)**
  * Volume in all four heart chambers

  * Better preload estimate than wedge pressure (actual volume vs pressure surrogate)

  * Avoids misinterpretation with stiff ventricles

  * Normal range: 680-800 mL/m²
* **Extravascular Lung Water (EVLW)**
  * Early index of pulmonary edema and fluid overload

  * Detects pulmonary edema before clinical symptoms

  * Normal range: 3-7 mL/kg

  * ⚠️ Values >10 mL/kg associated with increased mortality
* **Intrathoracic Blood Volume (ITBV)**
  * Includes blood volume in heart chambers and pulmonary vasculature

  * Calculated from GEDV × 1.25

  * Normal range: 850-1000 mL/m²

#### 10.3.3. COMPARTMENT CALCULATIONS


* **ITTV (Intrathoracic Thermal Volume)**
  * Complete distribution volume of cold indicator

  * Includes: All cardiac chambers + PBV + EVLW

  * Calculation: Cardiac output (CO) × Mean Transit Time (MTT)
* **PTV (Pulmonary Thermal Volume)**
  * Pulmonary component of thermal volume

  * Includes: PBV + EVLW

  * Calculation: Cardiac output (CO) × Exponential Decay Time (EDT)
* **Derived measurements**
  * GEDV = ITTV - PTV

  * ITBV = GEDV × 1.25

  * EVLW = ITTV - ITBV

![Transpulmonary Thermodilution](CVS_MONITORING_CVS_MONITORING_image_7.png_0a4f90.png_b99565.png)

#### 10.3.4. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS


* **Volume status assessment**
  * Superior to CVP and PAWP for guiding fluid therapy

  * Particularly useful in septic shock and ARDS
* **Pulmonary edema detection**
  * EVLW provides quantitative measure of pulmonary edema

  * Allows early intervention before clinical deterioration
* **Cardiac function monitoring**
  * Provides continuous cardiac output measurement

  * Cardiac function index (CFI) = CO/GEDV × 1000

#### 10.3.5. ADVANTAGES & LIMITATIONS


* **Advantages**
  * Less invasive than pulmonary artery catheter

  * Provides volumetric parameters rather than pressure surrogates

  * Continuous cardiac output monitoring

  * Early detection of pulmonary edema
* **Limitations**
  * Requires central venous and arterial access

  * Affected by significant valvular regurgitation

  * Less accurate in patients with intracardiac shunts

  * Thermal indicator recirculation may affect accuracy

#### 10.3.6. RELATED TECHNIQUES


* **PiCCO system** (Pulse index Continuous Cardiac Output)

  * Combines transpulmonary thermodilution with pulse contour analysis

  * Provides continuous cardiac output between calibrations
* **VolumeView/EV1000 system**
  * Uses similar principles but different algorithms

  * Claims improved accuracy in varied clinical conditions

### 10.4. ARTERIAL WAVEFORM ANALYSIS

#### 10.4.1. BACKGROUND AND PRINCIPLES


* **Pulmonary Artery Catheter (PAC) Considerations**
  * Gold standard for cardiac output (CO) determination

  * Not associated with reduced mortality in ICU patients

  * High rate of iatrogenic complications

  * ⚠️ ASA recommends only clinicians with regular ongoing experience should place PACs

  * Clinical challenge: Need for CO measurement without PAC-related risks
* **Core Principle of Arterial Waveform Analysis**
  * *Definition*: Technique to estimate cardiac output using arterial pressure waveforms

  * Directly measures arterial pressure fluctuations and heart rate (HR)

  * Estimates beat-to-beat stroke volume (SV)

  * Calculates CO using the formula: CO = HR × SV

#### 10.4.2. AVAILABLE DEVICES


* **Invasive Arterial Waveform Devices**
  * Require peripheral arterial cannula

  * Three main commercial systems:

    * FloTrac

    * PiCCO

    * LiDCOrapid
* **Non-invasive Arterial Waveform Device**
  * **ClearSight** (Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, CA)

  * Uses noninvasive finger blood-pressure cuff

  * *Methodology*: Dynamic inflation/deflation to track brachial artery pressure

  * Based on Peñáz method:
    1. Fingertip compressed by pressure cuff
    2. Simultaneous transillumination by infrared light
    3. Arterial pulsations → increased blood volume → increased infrared absorption
    4. System rapidly adjusts cuff pressure to maintain constant infrared absorption
    5. Constant absorption → constant blood volume → cuff pressure equals arterial pressure

  * System refinements include:

    * Compensation for pressure reflection in vascular tree

    * Adjustments for vertical fluid column between fingertip and heart

#### 10.4.3. MATHEMATICAL PRINCIPLES


* **Heart Rate Determination**
  * Straightforward measurement from arterial waveform tracing
* **Stroke Volume Estimation**
  * Technical challenge in arterial waveform analysis

  * Based on **Windkessel Model** (German: "air chamber")

    * *First modern mathematical description of arterial waveform shape*
    * Accounts for arterial distensibility despite blood incompressibility

    * Recognizes that instantaneous blood volumes entering/leaving arterial segment differ

    * Volumes balance only when averaged over complete cardiac cycle
* **Windkessel Model Mechanics**
  * Artery distends and stores blood during systole

  * Artery relaxes and ejects blood during diastole

  * Mathematically similar to compressible fluid in rigid vessels

  * Key arterial properties:

    * Resistance to flow (R)

    * Compliance (C)

    * Values predictable from patient demographics based on cadaveric studies

  ![Windkessel Model](CVS_MONITORING_CVS_MONITORING_image_8.png_5fdd04.png_3864eb.png)


* **Stroke Volume Calculation Principles**
  * Total SV = Volume flowing forward in systole (Qs) + Volume flowing forward in diastole (Qd)

  * Assuming competent aortic valve: SV = Qs + Qd

  * During diastole (no aortic inflow), Qd is proportional to pressure difference between aorta and arterial beds

  * End-systolic mean distending pressure (Pmd) represents this "pressure head"

  * Therefore: Qd = k × Pmd

    * k = constant of proportionality dependent on resistance and compliance

  * Volumes proportional to areas under pressure curve:

    * Qs proportional to systolic area (As)

    * Qd proportional to diastolic area (Ad)

  * Therefore, by derivation

    * Qs/As = Qd/Ad or Qs = Qd(As/Ad)

    * SV = Qd(1 + As/Ad) and SV = kPmd(1 + As/Ad)

  * Mathematical derivation leads to beat-to-beat SV estimates

  ![Stroke Volume Calculations](CVS_MONITORING_CVS_MONITORING_image_9.png_6c5b20.png_533831.png)

#### 10.4.4. DEVICE CALIBRATION APPROACHES


* **Calibrated Systems**
  * Require external calibration reading

  * Example: **PiCCO** device

  * Compares model prediction with established SV measurement method (TEE or thermodilution)
* **Uncalibrated Systems**
  * Use biophysical models based on patient demographics

  * Variables include: age, sex, height, weight

  * Examples: **FloTrac** and **LiDCOrapid** devices

#### 10.4.5. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS


* **Primary Clinical Utility**
  * Most useful for assessing *relative volume status*
  * Best for *evaluating response to fluid resuscitation*
  * 🔑 Provides trend monitoring rather than absolute values
* **Indications** (at practitioner discretion)

  * Cases with expected large fluid shifts

  * Situations where volume status assessment by clinical exam is difficult

  * Less invasive alternative to central venous catheter or PAC

  * Particularly useful when:

    * Patient already requires arterial line for other indications

    * Patient has comorbidities that contraindicate central line or PAC

  * Additional parameters like SVV and SPV (systolic pulse variation) help predict fluid responsiveness

#### 10.4.6. LIMITATIONS IN SPECIFIC CLINICAL SCENARIOS


* **Arrhythmias**
  * **Atrial fibrillation** → prone to inaccuracy due to beat-to-beat variability in preload and stroke volume
* **Altered Vascular Properties**
  * **SEPSIS** → deviation from expected population norms for SVR and arterial compliance

  * **Liver transplantation** → high-output state causes inaccuracy compared to PAC measurements

  * **Vasopressor administration** → iatrogenic reduction in arterial compliance affects readings
* **Valvular Disease**
  * **Aortic insufficiency** → violates assumption of no further inflow into arterial system at end of systole

  * *Aortic stenosis* → does not appear to impair accuracy of CO measurement
* **Mechanical Support**
  * **Intra-aortic balloon pump** → may distort arterial waveform to uninterpretable extent, rendering device inoperative
* **Response Assessment Reliability**
  * ✅ *Reliable*: Assessing changes in CO caused by fluid resuscitation

  * ⚠️ *Variable utility*: Detecting changes caused by inotropes

  * ❌ *Poor*: Assessing changes caused by vasopressors (phenylephrine, norepinephrine)

### 10.5. ESOPHAGEAL DOPPLER MONITORING
 

* **Definition:** Minimally invasive measurement of cardiac output
 
* **Clinical utility:** Allows quick estimation of cardiac output and response to therapeutic interventions
 
* **Advantages:**
  * Smooth esophageal muscle tone maintains probe position naturally
 
  * Close anatomical proximity to aorta minimizes signal interference from bone, tissue, lung
 
  * Has evolved from experimental technique to simple bedside procedure
 
  * Latest models incorporate both Doppler and echo-ultrasound in single probe
 
* **Clinical validation:**
  * Measurements correlate well with PA catheter-derived values
 
  * Used successfully for intravascular volume optimization in perioperative and critical care settings
 
  * Associated with reduced morbidity and hospital stay in cardiac, general, and orthopedic surgery
 
  * Requires adequate sedation due to mild discomfort from probe placement and positioning 

#### 10.5.1. COMPONENTS 

1. **Monitor housing:**
   * Screen for visual verification of signal measurement
 
   * Technology for beat-to-beat calculation of stroke volume and cardiac output 

2. **Esophageal probe:**
   * Insulated, thin, latex-free silicone
 
   * Contains Doppler transducer angled at 45 degrees
 
   * 6 mm diameter with internal spring coil for flexibility and rigidity
 
   * Pediatric version available for children >3 kg 

#### 10.5.2. OPERATING PRINCIPLES
 

* **Physical basis:** Doppler principle - frequency change when signal source moves relative to observer
 
* **Biological application:**
  * Ultrasound waves reflected by moving red blood cells experience frequency shift
 
  * Cells moving toward source create positive Doppler shift (higher frequency return)
 
  * Cells moving away create negative Doppler shift (lower frequency return)
 
  * Analysis of reflected frequencies determines blood flow velocity
 
* **Clinical procedure:**
  1. Lubricated probe inserted via mouth with bevel facing up at throat 
  2. Advanced to distal esophagus (35-40 cm from teeth) 
  3. Rotated and slowly pulled back while monitoring audible signal 
  4. Optimal position: level between T5-T6 vertebrae where descending aorta and distal esophagus run parallel 
  5. Superficially landmarked at third sternocostal junction anteriorly 
  6. High-frequency ultrasound (4 MHz) measures blood flow in aorta 
  7. Doppler signal waveform analyzed to compute stroke volume and cardiac output 
  8. Uses Doppler equation and normogram correcting for patient age, sex, body surface area
 

* **Doppler equation:** 
  * v = (c × fd) / (2 × fT × cos θ)

  * where:

    * v is flow velocity

    * c is speed of sound in body tissue (1540 m/s)

    * fd is Doppler frequency shift

    * cos θ is cosine of angle between sound beam and blood flow (45°)

    * fT is frequency of transmitted ultrasound (Hz)
* **Hemodynamic parameters obtained:** 
  * Cardiac output and stroke volume
 
  * Volumemic status
 
  * Systemic vascular resistance
 
  * Myocardial function 

#### 10.5.3. LIMITATIONS AND SAFETY
 

* **Practical limitations:**
  * Fully insulated probe safe with diathermy
 
  * Difficult to maintain correct position for extended periods - may require frequent repositioning
 
  * Primarily used in sedated, intubated patients (though use in awake patients with local anesthesia has been described)
 
* **Contraindications:**
  * Not recommended in patients with pharyngo-esophageal pathology (e.g., esophageal varices)
 
  * Role in children still being evaluated 

### 10.6. OTHER METHODS AND TECHNOLOGIES

#### 10.6.1. FICK METHOD


* **Principle**: CO = oxygen consumption / arteriovenous O₂ difference × 10 (in L/min)

  * Arteriovenous O₂ difference: (CaO₂ - CvO₂) = (1.34 × Hgb × SaO₂) - (1.34 × Hgb × SvO₂)
* **O₂ Consumption (VO₂) Determination**:

  * Estimated via nomogram based on age, sex, height, weight

  * Directly measured by exhaled breath analysis

  * 250 mL/min often used as standardized reference
* **Arteriovenous O₂ Difference Calculation**:

  * Requires arterial and mixed venous blood samples

  * Formula includes hemoglobin concentration, oxygen saturation values

#### 10.6.2. ECHOCARDIOGRAPHY-BASED CO MEASUREMENT


* **Pulsed Wave Doppler Technique**
  * Record LVOT velocity profile → determine velocity time integral (VTI)

  * *Formula*: SV = LVOT VTI × LVOT area (πr²)

  * CO = SV × HR

#### 10.6.3. INDICATOR DILUTION METHODS


* **Dye Dilution**
  * *Technique*: ICG or lithium injected via CVC → arterial sampling for substance detection

  * Area under indicator curve relates to CO

  * **LiDCO™ System**:

    * Small lithium chloride bolus injected

    * Lithium-sensitive electrode in arterial catheter measures concentration decay

    * Integrated with arterial pressure for beat-to-beat SV

#### 10.6.4. BIOIMPEDANCE


* **Principle**: Thoracic volume changes → resistance (bioimpedance) changes
* **Implementation**: Noninvasive, requires 6 electrodes
* **Limitations**: Susceptible to electrical interference; dependent on correct electrode positioning

### 10.7. LIDCO TECHNOLOGY
 

* **LiDCOrapid system description:** 
  * Cardiac output monitor using arterial pressure waveform analysis
 
  * Generates "nominal" cardiac output without calibration
 
  * Allows quick setup and assessment of fluid/inotrope effects
 
* **Components:** 
  * Monitor housing with screen displaying real-time cardiac output parameters
 
  * Software technology for computing cardiac output
 
  * Electrical connection to patient's bedside invasive pressure monitor
 
* **Mechanism of action:** 
  * Uses existing invasive blood pressure trace fed by cable into LiDCOrapid
 
  * Software algorithm generates nominal stroke volume and cardiac output
 
  * Displays heart rate, pressures (MAP, systolic, diastolic), stroke volume, cardiac output
 
  * Can generate dynamic preload parameters to assess fluid responsiveness
 
* **Limitations:** 
  * Performance compromised in:

    * Patients with aortic valve regurgitation
 
    * Patients with intra-aortic balloon pump or cardiac arrhythmias (disrupted waveform)
 
    * Highly damped peripheral arterial lines
 
    * Pronounced peripheral arterial vasoconstriction
 
  * LiDCOplus version offers calibration using lithium indicator dilution for higher accuracy 

**Bottom Line:** Multiple methods exist for cardiac output measurement, each with distinct advantages and limitations. Thermodilution via PAC remains the clinical gold standard but more minimally invasive techniques like transpulmonary thermodilution, arterial waveform analysis, and esophageal Doppler have gained popularity. Clinical assessment using signs like skin perfusion, urine output, and laboratory values remains crucial for evaluating circulatory adequacy. Method selection should consider the specific clinical scenario, available equipment, and operator expertise.

## 11. HEMODYNAMIC CALCULATIONS

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Cardiovascular Physiology|Part A]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3: Perioperative Management and Monitoring|Part B]]

**Hemodynamic calculations provide quantitative values for vascular resistance and cardiac performance to guide clinical management.**

### 11.1. RESISTANCES DERIVED FROM CO


* **Systemic Vascular Resistance (SVR)**:

  * SVR = [(MAP - CVP) / CO] × 80

  * Multiplier of 80 converts to Wood units

  * Normal range: 1200-1500 dynes·s·cm⁻⁵
* **Pulmonary Vascular Resistance (PVR)**:

  * PVR = [(mPAP - PCWP) / CO] × 80

  * Normal range: 100-300 dynes·s·cm⁻⁵

![SVR and PVR Calculation](CVS_MONITORING_CVS_MONITORING_image_11.png_5c28ce.png_a9f415.png)

### 11.2. CARDIAC PERFORMANCE INDICATORS


* **Other Common Hemodynamic Formulas**:

  * **Cardiac index** = Cardiac output (L/min) / Body surface area (m²)

    * Normal range: 2.2-4.2 L/min/m²

  * **Stroke volume** = Cardiac output (L/min) × 1000 / Heart rate (beats/min)

    * Normal range: 60-90 mL/beat

  * **Stroke index (SI)** = Stroke volume (mL/beat) / Body surface area (m²)

    * Normal range: 20-65 mL/beat/m²

  * **Right ventricular stroke-work index** = 0.0136 (PA (Mean) - CVP) × SI

    * Normal range: 30-65 g-m/beat/m²

  * **Left ventricular stroke-work index** = 0.0136 (MAP - PAOP) × SI

    * Normal range: 46-60 g-m/beat/m²

### 11.3. OXYGEN TRANSPORT CALCULATIONS


* **Oxygen Content (CaO₂)**:

  * CaO₂ = (1.34 × Hb × SaO₂) + (0.003 × PaO₂)

  * Normal range: 16-22 mL O₂/100 mL blood
* **Oxygen Delivery (DO₂)**:

  * DO₂ = CO × CaO₂ × 10

  * Normal range: 900-1100 mL/min
* **Oxygen Consumption (VO₂)**:

  * VO₂ = CO × (CaO₂ - CvO₂) × 10

  * Normal range: 200-250 mL/min
* **Oxygen Extraction Ratio (O₂ER)**:

  * O₂ER = VO₂/DO₂

  * Normal range: 22-30%

### 11.4. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS


* **Shock Classification**:

  * **Hypovolemic**: ↓CO, ↑SVR, ↓CVP, ↓PAOP

  * **Cardiogenic**: ↓CO, ↑SVR, ↑CVP, ↑PAOP

  * **Distributive**: ↑CO, ↓SVR, ↓/nl CVP, ↓/nl PAOP

  * **Obstructive**: ↓CO, ↑SVR, ↑CVP, ↓/nl PAOP
* **Targeted Therapy**:

  * SVR elevation: vasopressors indicated

  * SVR reduction: vasodilators indicated

  * ↓CO with elevated filling pressures: inotropes indicated

  * ↓CO with reduced filling pressures: volume indicated

**Bottom Line:** Hemodynamic formulas provide quantitative assessment of cardiovascular parameters, allowing for diagnosis of specific pathologies and tracking response to interventions. SVR and PVR are particularly important for distinguishing between different types of shock and guiding appropriate therapy. Indexes that adjust for body surface area allow for comparison between patients of different sizes.

## 12. ADDITIONAL MONITORING MODALITIES

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Physics|Part A]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3: Perioperative Management and Monitoring|Part B]]

**Additional monitoring modalities provide crucial information about patient status and supplement standard monitoring techniques.**

### 12.1. PULSE OXIMETRY
 

* **Definition:** Noninvasive measurement of arterial blood oxygen saturation at arteriolar level
 
* **Clinical value:** Continuous display of oxygenation (SpO2) through simple, accurate, rapid method
 
* **Applications:**
  * Operating theater
 
  * Recovery wards
 
  * Intensive care units
 
  * General wards
 
  * During transport of critically ill patients
 
* **Clinical importance:** Considered greatest recent technical advance in monitoring
 
* **Primary benefit:** Enables detection of arterial hypoxemia before tissue damage occurs 

#### 12.1.1. COMPONENTS AND OPERATION


* **Key components:** 
  1. **Probe:** Positioned on finger, toe, ear lobe or nose
 
     * Two light-emitting diodes (LEDs) produce red and infrared beams (660 nm and 940 nm)
 
     * Sensitive photodetector on opposite side
 
     * LEDs operate in sequence at about 30 times/s 
  
  2. **Processor unit:** Houses microprocessor that displays
 
     * Oxygen saturation
 
     * Pulse rate
 
     * Plethysmographic waveform of pulse
 
     * Alarm settings for saturation and pulse rate
 
* **Scientific principles:** 
  * Based on Beer's law (relation between light absorbed and solute concentration)
 
  * And Lambert's law (relation between absorption and thickness of absorbing layer)
 
  * Light transmission through pulsatile vascular tissue affects absorption
 
  * Light absorbed by non-pulsatile tissues (skin, soft tissues, bone, venous blood) is constant (DC)
 
  * Non-constant absorption (AC) results from arterial blood pulsations
 
  * AC component typically 1–5% of total signal
 
  * High LED frequency allows multiple samples during each pulse beat
 
  * Creates running averages to reduce "noise" (e.g., movement) effects
 
* **Signal processing:** 
  * Microprocessor analyzes DC and AC components at 660 and 940 nm
 
  * Calculates ratio of absorption at these frequencies (R:IR ratio)
 
  * Relates result to arterial saturation
 
  * Differential absorption of oxyhemoglobin and deoxyhemoglobin provides sensitivity
 
  * One isobestic point of these forms is at 805 nm
 
  * OFF measurement allows baseline for ambient light changes
 
  * Variable pitch beep provides audible signal of saturation changes
 
* **Advanced designs:** 
  * Multiple wavelengths to eliminate errors from carboxyhemoglobin and methemoglobin
 
  * Advanced oximeters use >7 light wavelengths
 
  * Can measure hemoglobin, oxygen content, carboxyhemoglobin, and methemoglobin 

#### 12.1.2. LIMITATIONS AND CONSIDERATIONS


* **Accuracy:**
  * ±2% in 70–100% range
 
  * Below 70%, readings are extrapolated
 
  * Highest accuracy at 90–100%, intermediate at 80–90%, lowest below 80%
 
  * Variability between probes due to LED wavelength differences
 
* **Sources of error:** 
  * Carbon monoxide poisoning (including smoking) - false high readings
 
  * Colored nail polish
 
  * Intravenous dyes (methylene blue, indocyanine green) - false low readings
 
  * Methemoglobinemia - false low readings
 
  * Fetal hemoglobin - no significant clinical change (similar absorption spectrum)
 
  * Sulfhemoglobin, bilirubin, dark skin - not clinically problematic
 
* **Physiological limitations:** 
  * Hypoperfusion and vasoconstriction reduce accuracy by diminishing AC component
 
  * Only monitors oxygenation, not oxygen delivery to tissues
 
  * Typically averages readings every 10–20 seconds
 
  * Cannot detect acute desaturation rapidly
 
  * Response time longer with finger probe (>60 seconds) vs. ear probe (10–15 seconds)
 
  * Movement artifacts (newer designs like Masimo claim better stability)
 
  * External fluorescent light interference
 
  * Venous pulsation (high airway pressures, Valsalva, impaired venous return) causes inaccuracy
 
  * ⚠️ Probe can cause pressure sores with continuous use - check regularly
 
  * ⚠️ Change application site every 2 hours in patients with impaired microcirculation
 
  * ⚠️ Burns reported in infants
 
  * Does not indicate carbon dioxide elimination 

#### 12.1.3. ADVANCED TECHNOLOGY - MASIMO


* **Signal extraction technology:** 
  * Allows venous blood saturation to be ignored
 
  * Uses two wavelengths but with advanced signal processing
 
  * Offers suite of innovative monitoring options 

### 12.2. PACEMAKERS AND DEFIBRILLATORS

##### 12.2.1.3. INTRAOPERATIVE RISKS ASSOCIATED WITH PACEMAKERS


* **Electrical interference**
  * Inappropriate triggering may lead to tachycardia

  * Diathermy

#### 12.2.2. DEFIBRILLATORS

##### 12.2.2.1. PRINCIPLES OF OPERATION


* **Definition**: An electrical medical device that applies an electrical current across the heart to convert a ventricular arrhythmia (fibrillation or tachycardia) into normal sinus rhythm
* **Mechanism**: By forcing the entire myocardium to contract at the same time, it creates a universal refractory period, after which the next impulse is likely to come from the sino-atrial node (as these pacemaker cells fire most frequently), potentially allowing sinus rhythm to continue
* **Technical operation**: The defibrillator uses a ***capacitor*** that has a voltage of 5000V applied across its plates

  * The energy stored is equal to half of the charge multiplied by the voltage

    $E=QV/2$


  * Given charge is equal to voltage multiplied by capacitance

    * $Q=VC$

  * The energy stored can therefore be expressed as voltage squared times capacitance over two

    * $E = V^2C/2$

## 13. DIALYSIS CATHETERS AND CONTINUOUS RENAL REPLACEMENT THERAPY
<!-- EPA-6:K7:dialysis_access EPA-6:EQ3:crrt_management EPA-6:S2:vascular_access -->

> **Syllabus**: [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3: Perioperative Management and Monitoring|Part B: C3]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Patient Care|Part C: PC]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Systems-Based Practice|Part C: SBP]]

**Dialysis catheters provide temporary or bridge vascular access for renal replacement therapy, while CRRT provides continuous solute removal and fluid management in critically ill patients.**

### 13.1. DIALYSIS CATHETER TYPES

#### 13.1.1. NON-TUNNELED (TEMPORARY) CATHETERS

**Non-tunneled catheters are used for immediate/emergent hemodialysis needs** [NKF-KDOQI]:

* **Indications**: Acute kidney injury requiring urgent dialysis; temporary access while awaiting permanent access maturation
* **Duration**: Suitable for <21 days (ideally <2-3 weeks)
* **Design options**:
  * Two-lumen: Standard dialysis access
  * Three-lumen: Allows concurrent medication administration
* **Characteristics**: Smaller bore; no subcutaneous cuff; easier insertion

#### 13.1.2. TUNNELED (CUFFED) CATHETERS

**Tunneled catheters are preferred for patients requiring dialysis >2-3 weeks** [NKF-KDOQI]:

* **Design features**:
  * Subcutaneous Dacron cuff creates inflammatory response
  * Tissue ingrowth fixes device under skin
  * Reduces bacteremia risk
* **Modern innovations**:
  * Split-tip and symmetric-tip designs reduce recirculation
  * Antibiotic or heparin coatings for infection/thrombosis prevention
* **Insertion**: Requires tunneling technique with cuff positioned ≥4 cm from venous entry site

### 13.2. INSERTION SITE SELECTION

**The NKF-KDOQI guidelines establish a clear hierarchy for dialysis catheter insertion sites**:

| Priority | Site | Rationale |
|----------|------|-----------|
| **1st** | Right internal jugular | Straight, short course to SVC; higher flow rates; shorter catheter length |
| **2nd** | Left internal jugular | More technically challenging; tortuous anatomy through innominate vein |
| **3rd** | Femoral | Higher infection rates; acceptable for immobile ICU patients |
| **Last resort** | Subclavian | High risk of venous stenosis (30-50%); jeopardizes future upper extremity fistula |

⚠️ **Safety Warning**: Subclavian vein catheterization carries high risk of subsequent venous occlusion or stenosis. NKF-KDOQI guidelines explicitly recommend avoiding subclavian access to preserve vein patency for future arteriovenous fistula creation.

### 13.3. INSERTION TECHNIQUE

#### 13.3.1. ULTRASOUND-GUIDED APPROACHES

**Ultrasound guidance significantly improves safety and success rates** [Meta-analysis evidence]:

**Out-of-Plane (Short-Axis) Approach:**
* Transducer perpendicular to vessel
* Needle inserted cranial to transducer at 45° angle
* Visualize needle tip as bright "star" entering vein
* Risk: Posterior wall perforation if needle advanced beyond visualization

**In-Plane (Long-Axis) Approach:**
* Transducer parallel to vessel course
* Needle inserted laterally with complete visualization from skin to vessel
* Transducer angled 10-15° cephalad
* **Advantages**: Better for tunneled catheters; straighter tunneling path behind sternocleidomastoid

💡 **Clinical Pearl**: In-plane approach enables lateral entry for straighter tunneling path, improving patient comfort and reducing cuff irritation compared to trajectory between sternocleidomastoid heads.

#### 13.3.2. CATHETER TIP POSITIONING

**Proper tip position is critical for catheter function and longevity**:

* **Target**: Mid-right atrium (highest flow channel)
* **Fluoroscopic landmarks**: SVC-RA junction ~2 vertebral bodies or 4 cm below carina on PA view
* **Left-sided catheters**: Place 2 cm deeper initially due to greater migration with upright positioning
* **Malposition consequences**: Fibrin sheath formation, thrombosis, venous stenosis

#### 13.3.3. TUNNELING STANDARDS

**Proper tunnel construction prevents complications**:

* **Cuff position**: ≥4 cm from venous entry site
* **Exit site**: 2 cm from cuff
* **Excessive tunnel length**: Increases migration risk
* **Insufficient tunnel length**: Causes patient discomfort and cuff irritation

### 13.4. COMPLICATIONS OF DIALYSIS CATHETERS

#### 13.4.1. EARLY (INSERTION-RELATED) COMPLICATIONS

| Complication | Incidence | Prevention/Management |
|--------------|-----------|----------------------|
| Hemorrhage/hematoma | <2% | Direct pressure; DDAVP for uremic coagulopathy |
| Arterial puncture | <1% | Ultrasound guidance; micropuncture needles |
| Pneumothorax | 0-1% | In-plane ultrasound; avoid subclavian |
| Air embolism | 0-1% | Valved peel-away sheaths; Trendelenburg |
| Arrhythmias | Variable | Withdraw wire/catheter from RA; adenosine if sustained SVT |
| Central vein laceration | Rare | Firm back tension on guidewire during dilation |

#### 13.4.2. LATE COMPLICATIONS

**Catheter-Related Bloodstream Infection (CRBSI):**
* Most common indication for catheter removal
* Incidence: 1.6-5.5 episodes per 1,000 catheter days
* Categories: Exit site infection, tunnel infection, bloodstream infection
* Prevention: Strict aseptic technique; chlorhexidine skin prep; antibiotic locks

**Fibrin Sheath Formation:**
* Present in 100% of catheters within 7 days
* 100% of fibrin sheaths colonized with bacteria
* **Classic presentation**: Able to flush but unable to aspirate
* **Management**: Catheter stripping with snare; exchange with balloon disruption; r-TPA instillation

**Thrombosis:**
* **Intraluminal**: Prevented with heparin/citrate locks; treated with r-TPA dwell (1-4 hours)
* **Vessel thrombosis**: Often asymptomatic; severe cases cause arm/neck swelling or SVC syndrome; treat with anticoagulation

**Central Venous Stenosis:**
* Interrelated complication involving fibrin sheath, thrombus, and vessel narrowing
* **Prevention is paramount**: Catheter alternatives when possible; proper tip positioning; minimize dwell time

### 13.5. CONTINUOUS RENAL REPLACEMENT THERAPY (CRRT)

#### 13.5.1. INDICATIONS FOR CRRT

**CRRT is preferred over intermittent hemodialysis in hemodynamically unstable patients** [KDIGO]:

| Indication | Description |
|------------|-------------|
| Hemodynamic instability | Cannot tolerate rapid fluid/solute shifts of IHD |
| Severe fluid overload | Requires continuous controlled removal |
| Cerebral edema/raised ICP | Slow solute removal prevents osmotic shifts |
| Multiorgan dysfunction | Continuous cytokine and toxin removal |
| Severe electrolyte disturbances | Gradual correction prevents arrhythmias |

#### 13.5.2. CRRT MODALITIES

**Three main continuous venovenous modalities are used in ICU practice**:

| Modality | Mechanism | Characteristics |
|----------|-----------|-----------------|
| **CVVH** (Hemofiltration) | Convection only | Higher clearance of middle molecules (1-20 kDa); UF rates 1-4 L/hr |
| **CVVHD** (Hemodialysis) | Diffusion only | Effective small molecule clearance; dialysate flow 9-34 mL/min |
| **CVVHDF** (Hemodiafiltration) | Convection + Diffusion | Combined benefits; most studied modality |

**Solute Removal Mechanisms:**

* **Diffusion**: Molecules move from high to low concentration across semipermeable membrane
  * Effective for small molecules (urea, creatinine, potassium)
  * Clearance decreases as molecular weight increases

* **Convection**: Solutes pass across membrane with plasma water (solvent drag)
  * Effective for larger molecules including inflammatory mediators
  * Independent of concentration gradient

* **Ultrafiltration**: Fluid removal via transmembrane pressure
  * Rate determined by hydrostatic pressure gradient
  * Allows precise fluid balance control

💡 **Clinical Pearl**: CVVH provides higher clearance for molecules 1,000-20,000 Daltons compared to CVVHD at equivalent effluent flow rates. This theoretical advantage in cytokine removal has led some centres to prefer hemofiltration in sepsis.

#### 13.5.3. CRRT PRESCRIPTION

**KDIGO Dose Recommendations:**

* **Target effluent dose**: 20-25 mL/kg/h
* **Prescription**: 25 mL/kg/h to achieve delivered dose of at least 20 mL/kg/h (accounting for downtime)
* **No survival benefit** from doses >25 mL/kg/h [RENAL, ATN trials]

**Pre-dilution vs Post-dilution Replacement Fluid:**

| Approach | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|----------|------------|---------------|
| **Pre-dilution** | Reduces hemoconcentration and clotting; prolongs filter life | Dilutes blood, reducing solute clearance by ~15-20% |
| **Post-dilution** | Better solute clearance | Higher clotting risk; may require higher anticoagulation |

**Blood Flow Rate:**
* Typical range: 150-300 mL/min
* Minimum for adequate clearance: 150-200 mL/min
* Higher flows improve clearance but increase recirculation risk

### 13.6. ANTICOAGULATION FOR CRRT

#### 13.6.1. REGIONAL CITRATE ANTICOAGULATION (RCA)

**RCA is the KDIGO-recommended first-line strategy for CRRT anticoagulation**:

**Mechanism:**
* Citrate chelates ionized calcium (essential coagulation cofactor)
* Target pre-filter iCa: <0.25 mmol/L (typically 0.25-0.35 mmol/L)
* Citrate concentration: 4-6 mmol/L in circuit
* Systemic calcium infusion maintains normal systemic levels

**Advantages:**
* Filter lifespan: 45-49 hours (vs 26-28 hours with heparin)
* Reduced bleeding complications: RR 0.34 (95% CI 0.17-0.65)
* No systemic anticoagulation effect
* Suitable for patients at high bleeding risk

**Complications and Monitoring:**

| Complication | Mechanism | Monitoring |
|--------------|-----------|------------|
| Citrate accumulation | Impaired hepatic metabolism | Total calcium/iCa ratio >2.5 |
| Hypocalcemia | Inadequate calcium replacement | Systemic iCa <1.0 mmol/L |
| Metabolic alkalosis | Citrate metabolized to bicarbonate | Serial ABGs |

**Contraindications to RCA:**
* Severe liver failure
* Severe shock states
* Severe hypoxemia (impairs citrate metabolism)
* Severe lactic acidosis

#### 13.6.2. HEPARIN ANTICOAGULATION

**Heparin is used when citrate is contraindicated or in low bleeding risk patients**:

* **Unfractionated heparin (UFH)**: Loading dose 1000-5000 IU; infusion 5-20 IU/kg/h; target aPTT 1.5-2x normal
* **Low molecular weight heparin (LMWH)**: Alternative option; less monitoring required
* **Disadvantages**:
  * Bleeding risk: 10-50%
  * Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) risk
  * Shorter filter life (26-28 hours)

### 13.7. CRRT TROUBLESHOOTING

#### 13.7.1. CIRCUIT CLOTTING

**Signs of imminent circuit failure:**
* Increasing transmembrane pressure (TMP)
* Darkening of blood in circuit
* Visible clots in venous bubble trap
* Decreasing filter efficiency (rising urea)

**Management approach:**
1. Check vascular access function (flush/aspirate)
2. Optimize anticoagulation (adjust citrate dose or heparin)
3. Evaluate catheter position (kinking, malposition)
4. Consider filter exchange if TMP >250-300 mmHg

#### 13.7.2. ACCESS DYSFUNCTION

| Problem | Cause | Solution |
|---------|-------|----------|
| Cannot flush or aspirate | Intraluminal thrombus | r-TPA lock (1-2 mg/lumen for 1-4 hours) |
| Can flush, cannot aspirate | Fibrin sheath | Exchange over wire with balloon disruption |
| High venous pressures | Kinking, stenosis | Reposition; consider angiography |
| Recirculation >15% | Tip malposition | Catheter repositioning |

#### 13.7.3. DRUG DOSING DURING CRRT

**Many drugs require dose adjustment during CRRT** [Clinical pharmacology considerations]:

* **Factors affecting clearance**:
  * Molecular weight (convection removes larger molecules)
  * Protein binding (only free drug cleared)
  * Volume of distribution (larger Vd = less clearance impact)
  * CRRT modality (convection vs diffusion)

* **Commonly adjusted drugs**:
  * **Antibiotics**: Vancomycin, aminoglycosides, beta-lactams - often require supplemental dosing
  * **Anticonvulsants**: Levetiracetam, phenobarbital - significant CRRT clearance
  * **Sedatives**: Morphine metabolites accumulate; propofol unaffected

💡 **Clinical Pearl**: Consult pharmacy and use CRRT-specific dosing references. Generic renal dosing guidelines often underestimate drug requirements during high-flux CRRT.

**Bottom Line:** Dialysis catheters require meticulous insertion technique with ultrasound guidance, preferentially via the right internal jugular vein. CRRT provides gentle, continuous solute and fluid removal suitable for hemodynamically unstable ICU patients. Regional citrate anticoagulation is first-line for CRRT, offering superior filter life and reduced bleeding risk compared to heparin. Successful CRRT management requires attention to vascular access, anticoagulation optimization, and appropriate drug dose adjustments.

[Sources: NKF-KDOQI Guidelines; KDIGO AKI Guidelines; Lok CE et al. Semin Dial 2016; Critical Care 2020]
