# 1. PHARMACOKINETICS OF INHALATION ANESTHETICS
<!-- EPA-2:K2:applied_pharmacology EPA-2:EQ1:inhalational_agents -->







## 1.2. UNIQUE FEATURES OF INHALED ANESTHETICS

**Inhaled anesthetics differ fundamentally from other drugs in their gaseous state and route of administration.**

* **Speed:** Among the most rapidly acting drugs in existence [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Quick action provides margin of safety
  * Rapid titration of anesthetic levels possible
  * Efficient operating room turnover times
  
* **Gaseous State:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Only N₂O and xenon are true gases
  * Other "potent" anesthetics are vapors of volatile liquids
  * All referred to as "gases" as they are in gas phase when administered
* **Route of Administration:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Delivered via lungs - a unique route available in all patients
  * Allows rapid increase and decrease of plasma concentrations
  * No need for facilitated diffusion or active transport (unlike many IV drugs)
  * Rapid diffusion due to nonionized state and low molecular weights
* **Sequential Phases:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * 1. Uptake (absorption phase)
  * 2. Distribution
  * 3. Metabolism (biotransformation)
  * 4. Elimination
* **Primary Goal:** Achieve alveolar gas partial pressure that equilibrates with CNS for anesthesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Inhaled anesthetics' gaseous state and pulmonary delivery route provide unique pharmacokinetic advantages including rapid onset, precise titration, and quick elimination compared to other medications.

## 1.3. PARTIAL PRESSURE AND EQUILIBRIUM

**The anesthetic state is achieved by establishing specific partial pressure of anesthetic in the brain, which equilibrates with alveolar and arterial partial pressures.**

* **Fundamental Principle:** It is the partial pressure (not concentration) that determines anesthetic effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * At higher altitudes (Patm < 760 mmHg):
	* Same concentration → lower partial pressure
	* Results in reduced anesthetic effect
* **Equilibrium State:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * PCNS = Parterial blood = Palveoli (at equilibrium with normal cardiopulmonary function) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * This equilibrium results from:
	* Rapid bidirectional transfer via lungs
	* Low capacity of plasma/tissues to absorb anesthetics relative to delivery
	* Minimal metabolism compared to rate of delivery/removal
* **Partial Pressure Concepts:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Dalton's law: Sum of partial pressures equals total pressure of mixture
  * In solution, partial pressure refers to pressure gas would exert if present in gas phase at equilibrium
  * Gases equilibrate based on partial pressures, not concentrations
* **Henry's Law:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Concentration of gas in solution = solubility constant × partial pressure
  * Doubling pressure of gas doubles its concentration in solution
  * Concentration in tissue depends on partial pressure and tissue solubility
* **Terminology:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Gas phase: Expressed as fractional concentration (percent by volume)
  * Fractional concentration = partial pressure ÷ ambient pressure
  * When measured using kPa, concentration and partial pressure are virtually the same at atmospheric pressure which approximates to 100 kPa [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** At equilibrium, anesthetic partial pressure equalizes between alveoli, blood, and brain. This partial pressure (not the concentration) determines the anesthetic effect, which is why monitoring alveolar concentration provides a reliable index of anesthetic depth.

## 1.4. FI/FA RATIO

**The ratio of fractional concentration of alveolar anesthetic to inspired anesthetic (FA/FI) is a key measure of anesthetic uptake over time.**

* **Definition:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * FA = Fraction (concentration) of anesthetic in alveoli
  * FI = Fraction (concentration) of anesthetic inspired
  * FA/FI ratio tracks the approach to equilibrium
* **Clinical Significance:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Faster FA rise relative to FI = faster induction speed
  * FA is proportional to PA (alveolar partial pressure)
  * PA = Pblood = PCNS when equilibrated
  
* **Circuit Dynamics:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * FI rises according to first-order kinetics
  * Time constant (τ) = Circuit Volume (VC) ÷ Fresh Gas Flow (FGF)
  * 95% of maximum reached after three time constants, 98% after 4 time constants
  
* **Methods to Speed FI Rise:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Increase Fresh Gas Flow (FGF)
  * Decrease circuit capacity (collapse rebreathing bag)
  * Use high flows (>4 L/min) to prevent complete mixing
  * Start with higher vaporizer setting initially
* **Alveolar Rise of FA:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Without uptake: FA would rise according to τ = FRC ÷ Minute Ventilation
  * With uptake: Rise affected by blood solubility of the agent
  * Less soluble agents (desflurane, N₂O) show faster rise in FA/FI
  * Different agents approach FA/FI ratio of 1 at different rates; agents with low blood:gas partition coefficient reach equilibrium more rapidly [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

![FI/FA Ratio](INHALATIONAL_INHALATIONAL_Untitled.png_5765c7.png_65d4b4.png)

**Bottom Line:** The FA/FI ratio reflects how quickly alveolar anesthetic concentration approaches inspired concentration. Less soluble agents like desflurane and N₂O equilibrate faster than more soluble agents like isoflurane, leading to more rapid induction.

## 1.5. UPTAKE

**Uptake is the movement of anesthetic gas from alveoli into pulmonary circulation, directly affecting the speed of induction.**

* **Key Concept:** Higher uptake → larger FA/FI difference → slower induction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * FA lags behind FI during induction due to pulmonary uptake
  
* **Three Major Factors Affecting Uptake:**

  1. **Blood Solubility:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	 * Higher solubility = more gas needed to saturate blood
	 * Blood:gas partition coefficient reflects solubility
	 * DESFLURANE (lowest solubility coefficient 0.42) = fastest FA/FI rise
	 * Higher solubility agents like halothane (2.5) rise more slowly
	 
  2. **Alveolar Blood Flow (Cardiac Output):** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	 * High CO = "larger tank" → slower FA/FI rise (more effect on soluble agents)
	 * Low CO = "smaller tank" → faster rise (risk of overdose)
	 * Alveolar blood flow = cardiac output (without shunt)
	 * Poorly soluble agents: minimal CO effects
	 
  3. **Alveolar-venous Partial Pressure Difference:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

* **Blood Uptake Equation:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * V̇B = δb/g × Q × (PA - Pv)/PB
  * Where: V̇B is blood uptake, δb/g is blood:gas partition coefficient, Q is cardiac output, PA is alveolar partial pressure, Pv is venous partial pressure, PB is barometric pressure
* **Shunt Effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Right-to-Left:
	* Mechanism: Non-anesthetic blood dilutes arterial anesthetic
	* Result: Slower induction (especially with poorly soluble agents)
	* Note: IV anesthetics work faster (bypass lungs)
  * Left-to-Right:
	* Minimal effect on induction speed

**Bottom Line:** Anesthetic uptake is primarily determined by the agent's blood solubility, cardiac output, and alveolar-venous partial pressure gradient. Less soluble agents like desflurane and N₂O show faster induction due to limited uptake and rapid alveolar concentration rise.

## 1.6. FACTORS AFFECTING INDUCTION/RECOVERY SPEED [PART A/B]

### 1.6.1. VENTILATION

**Ventilation significantly impacts the rate of anesthetic induction and elimination.**

* **Key Points:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Higher alveolar ventilation counteracts uptake effects
  * Most notable with soluble anesthetics
  * Minimal effect on insoluble agents (FA/FI rises rapidly regardless)
  * Ventilation depression (from anesthetics/opioids) → slower alveolar concentration rise
* **Physiological Basis:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Increased alveolar ventilation results in faster rise in PA
  * Consequently, PB increases more rapidly and induction is faster
  * A large functional residual capacity (FRC) dilutes the inspired concentration causing slower induction
  * Patients with small FRC have faster induction due to limited dilution effect
* **Spontaneous vs. Controlled Ventilation:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Spontaneous ventilation is not static - varies with anesthetic depth
  * Respiratory depression at high concentrations slows FA/FI rise
  * This creates a negative feedback that may add safety margin by preventing overdose
  * Controlled ventilation eliminates this safety mechanism
* **Clinical Applications:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Increasing minute ventilation accelerates induction with soluble agents
  * Less effect with poorly soluble agents (desflurane, sevoflurane)
  * 💡 In pediatric patients, high minute ventilation relative to FRC contributes to faster inductions

![Factors Affecting FA/FI](INHALATIONAL_INHALATIONAL_Untitled_1.png_ee5105.png_307c6e.png)

**Bottom Line:** Ventilation has a significant impact on the rate of rise of alveolar anesthetic concentration, particularly for more soluble agents. Higher minute ventilation speeds induction, while anesthetic-induced respiratory depression creates a natural negative feedback mechanism during spontaneous ventilation.

### 1.6.2. CONCENTRATION/2ND GAS EFFECT

**Higher inspired concentrations accelerate induction through concentration and second gas effects.**

* **Overpressurization:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Definition: Administration of higher partial pressure than target FA
  * Analogous to an IV bolus
  * Speeds induction significantly
* **Concentration Effect:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * ↑ FI leads to both ↑ FA and ↑ rate of FA/FI equilibration
  * Has two components:
	1. Concentrating effect
	2. Augmented gas inflow effect
  * Describes the disproportionate rate of rise of the alveolar fraction compared with the inspired fraction when high concentrations of N₂O are inspired
  * Observed primarily with N₂O because it's the only agent used at sufficiently high concentration
  * Example at 50% uptake: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* 20% inspired → 11% alveolar (10/90 parts)
	* 80% inspired → 67% alveolar (40/60 parts)
	* 4x increase in FI → 6x increase in FA
  * Mechanism: Large amounts of N₂O are absorbed into pulmonary capillaries despite being considered insoluble (blood:gas coefficient 0.47)
  * Gas from conducting airways is drawn into alveoli to maintain volume, changing alveolar concentrations
* **Second Gas Effect:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Definition: One gas's concentration effect augments another gas
  * Mechanism: Rapid N₂O uptake → ↑ relative concentration of second gas
  * Direct result of the concentration effect
  * Oxygen and volatile agents used alongside high concentrations of N₂O are concentrated by the rapid uptake of N₂O and augmented alveolar ventilation
  * Example: 70% N₂O with 2% potent agent
	* If 50% of N₂O is taken up, the potent agent concentration increases from 2% to 3.1%
  * Clinical relevance questionable during induction/emergence
  * Most pronounced with N₂O due to high concentration used (50-70%)
* **Diffusion Hypoxia:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * At the end of anesthesia when N₂O/O₂ is replaced by air (N₂/O₂), the reverse of the second gas effect occurs
  * Volume of N₂O entering the alveolus will be greater than the volume of N₂ entering the pulmonary capillaries
  * Results in dilution of all alveolar gases including oxygen
  * Prevented by changing from N₂O/O₂ to 100% O₂ at the end of anesthesia

**Bottom Line:** Higher inspired concentrations accelerate induction through concentration effects. This is particularly notable with nitrous oxide, which can enhance the uptake of a second anesthetic gas. While these effects are mathematically significant, their clinical impact varies with different agents. The concentration and second gas effects are most relevant when using high concentrations of N₂O.

### 1.6.3. CARDIAC OUTPUT

**Cardiac output affects anesthetic uptake and induction speed, particularly with soluble agents.**

* **Key Points:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Low cardiac output will accelerate the rise of FA/FI
  * Effect is more pronounced with soluble agents
  * Less effect with poorly soluble agents (desflurane, sevoflurane)
  * A high cardiac output tends to maintain a concentration gradient between the alveolus and pulmonary blood, causing PA to rise slowly
  
* **Mechanism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Higher CO = greater blood flow through lungs
  * More anesthetic molecules transferred from alveoli to blood
  * Creates larger arterial-venous concentration gradient
  * Results in slower rise in FA/FI
* **Clinical Implications:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * As inspired concentration increases → cardiovascular depression
  * This reduces anesthetic uptake → increases rate of rise of FA/FI
  * Creates positive feedback that can lead to profound cardiovascular depression
  * ⚠️ Particularly dangerous with soluble agents and high concentrations
* **Contraindications:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Caution with high concentrations in patients with:
	* Hypovolemia
	* Heart failure
	* Other conditions with low/compromised CO

**Bottom Line:** Low cardiac output accelerates induction by reducing anesthetic uptake from the lungs. This creates a potentially dangerous positive feedback loop where anesthetic-induced cardiovascular depression further accelerates anesthetic depth, requiring careful titration, especially with soluble agents.

### 1.6.4. VENTILATION-PERFUSION MISMATCHING

**V/Q mismatching affects the rate of rise of alveolar and arterial anesthetic concentrations.**

* **Ventilation-Perfusion Mismatch Effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Normal matching: PA (alveolar) and Pa (arterial) rise together
  * With mismatching (e.g., mainstem intubation):
	* 50% intrapulmonary shunt:
	  * Increased ventilation to intubated lung
	  * No ventilation to non-intubated lung
	  * Slight increase in perfusion to intubated lung
	  * Slight decrease in perfusion to non-intubated lung
* **Effects on Anesthetic Uptake:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * For poorly soluble anesthetics:
	* PA rises faster (less uptake overall)
	* Pa rises slower (dilution effect)
	* Net result: Slower induction despite faster alveolar rise
  * For soluble anesthetics:
	* Increased ventilation in intubated lung increases PA on that side
	* Less dilution effect on Pa
	* Induction less delayed compared to poorly soluble agents

![V/Q Mismatch Effects](INHALATIONAL_image-8.png_d88f94.png)

**Bottom Line:** Ventilation-perfusion mismatching, such as from mainstem intubation, affects induction speed differently for soluble versus poorly soluble agents. While alveolar concentrations may rise faster, arterial concentrations (which determine brain levels) can rise more slowly, delaying induction particularly with poorly soluble anesthetics.

## 1.7. ELIMINATION

**Elimination of inhaled anesthetics occurs primarily through exhalation, with minor contributions from metabolism and other routes.**

* **Modes of Elimination:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Exhalation (most important)
  * Biotransformation (metabolism)
  * Transcutaneous loss (insignificant)
* **Percutaneous and Visceral Loss:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Small amount of loss via skin (greatest for N₂O)
  * Some loss across exposed viscera during open surgery
  * ⚠️ N₂O can diffuse into enclosed air spaces (pneumothorax, gas-containing bowel, etc.)
* **Diffusion Between Tissues:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Five-compartment model explains anesthetic distribution
  * Intertissue diffusion can account for up to 1/3 of uptake during long cases
  * Particularly relevant for adipose tissue adjacent to lean tissue
* **Metabolism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Hepatic cytochrome P450 (CYP2E1) metabolizes the C-(halogen) bond to release halogen ions
  * The C-F bond is stable and minimally metabolized, unlike C-Cl, C-Br, and C-I which are progressively easier to metabolize
  * Metabolism rates vary significantly between agents:
	* N₂O: <0.01%
	* Halothane: 20%
	* Sevoflurane: 3.5%
	* Enflurane: 2%
	* Isoflurane: 0.2%
	* Desflurane: 0.02%
* **Exhalation and Recovery:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Primary determinants:
	* Anesthetic solubility (most important)
	* Cardiac output
	* Minute ventilation
  * Recovery expressed as FA/FA₀ ratio (current to initial concentration)
  * Important differences from induction:
	* No "underpressurization" possible (unlike overpressurization for induction)
	* Different tissues begin with different anesthetic concentrations
	* Redistribution from vessel-rich group to muscle/fat initially speeds recovery
* **Diffusion Hypoxia:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Occurs during recovery from N₂O
  * Rapid washout of N₂O can lower alveolar O₂ and CO₂
  * Can cause hypoxemia and depress respiratory drive
  * 💡 Administer 100% O₂ briefly after N₂O discontinuation

![Elimination Rates](INHALATIONAL_INHALATIONAL_Untitled_6.png_075543.png_b05174.png)

**Bottom Line:** Anesthetic elimination occurs primarily through pulmonary exhalation and follows similar principles to uptake, but in reverse. Recovery speed is primarily determined by blood:gas solubility, with less soluble agents (desflurane, sevoflurane) allowing faster emergence, especially after prolonged administration.

# 2. PHYSICAL AND CHEMICAL PROPERTIES







## 2.2. BLOOD:GAS PARTITION COEFFICIENT

**The blood:gas partition coefficient represents the ratio of anesthetic in blood to that in alveolar gas at equilibrium.**

* **Definition:** Ratio of dissolved gas (by volume) in blood vs gas at equilibrium [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Represents the relative solubility of the anesthetic in blood compared to air
  * For equal volumes of blood and gas at the same pressure and temperature (37°C)
  * Defined as the ratio of the amount of anesthetic in blood and gas when the two phases are of equal volume and pressure and in equilibrium at 37°C
* **Clinical Significance:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Determines speed of induction and emergence
  * Lower coefficient = faster equilibration = faster onset/offset
  * Key determinant of FA/FI curve characteristics
  * Agents with low blood:gas partition coefficient exhibit rapid onset and offset of action
  
* **Values for Common Agents:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Desflurane: 0.42 (lowest, fastest onset/offset)
  * Sevoflurane: 0.65
  * N₂O: 0.47
  * Isoflurane: 1.40
  * Halothane: 2.50 (highest among common agents, slowest onset/offset)
  * Xenon: 0.14 (extremely low, fastest onset/offset)
  
* **Effect on Anesthetic Uptake:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Higher solubility = greater blood capacity for anesthetic
  * Greater blood capacity = slower rise in alveolar concentration
  * Example: If blood and lung volumes are equal
	* 71.4% of delivered halothane moves to blood (coefficient 2.5)
	* Only 29.6% of desflurane moves to blood (coefficient 0.42)
	* Results in slower equilibration for halothane
* **Effect on Recovery:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Primary determinant of elimination rate
  * Less soluble agents allow faster washout
  * Especially important after long procedures

**Bottom Line:** The blood:gas partition coefficient is a fundamental property that determines how quickly an anesthetic will reach equilibrium between alveoli and blood. Agents with lower coefficients like desflurane and sevoflurane allow for faster induction and emergence, making them valuable for outpatient procedures.

## 2.3. OIL:GAS PARTITION COEFFICIENT

**The oil:gas partition coefficient relates to anesthetic potency and determines the MAC value.**

* **Definition:** Ratio of anesthetic concentration in oil vs gas at equilibrium [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Reflects lipid solubility of the anesthetic
  * Corresponds to Meyer-Overton hypothesis linking lipid solubility to potency
* **Clinical Significance:**
  * Determines potency of the anesthetic agent
  * Higher coefficient = higher lipid solubility = greater potency = lower MAC
  * Related to the agent's ability to interact with lipophilic sites of action
* **Values for Common Agents:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Desflurane: 19 (lowest, least potent)
  * Sevoflurane: 47
  * Isoflurane: 98
  * Halothane: 224
  * Xenon: Not listed in table but considered high potency despite low blood:gas coefficient
* **Relationship to MAC:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Inverse relationship between oil:gas coefficient and MAC
  * Higher coefficient = lower MAC value
  * Example: Desflurane (oil:gas 19) has MAC of 6.6%
  * Example: Isoflurane (oil:gas 98) has MAC of 1.15%
* **Tissue Partition Coefficients:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Different tissues have different affinities for anesthetics
  * Fat has highest partition coefficients (27-51 for potent agents)
  * Muscle and vessel-rich groups have lower coefficients

**Bottom Line:** The oil:gas partition coefficient reflects the lipid solubility of an anesthetic agent and directly corresponds to its potency. Higher lipid solubility generally results in greater potency (lower MAC), illustrating the Meyer-Overton principle that lipid solubility correlates with anesthetic effect.

## 2.4. SATURATED VAPOR PRESSURE

**Saturated vapor pressure determines the maximum concentration of anesthetic that can be delivered at a given temperature.**

* **Definition:** Pressure exerted by molecular collisions of gas against container walls at equilibrium [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Independent of liquid volume as long as liquid remains in container
  * Directly proportional to temperature
  * Pressure exerted by molecular collisions of gas against container walls at equilibrium
* **Clinical Significance:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Determines volatility of an anesthetic
  * Affects vaporizer design requirements
  * Higher SVP = more volatile = easier to vaporize
* **Values for Common Agents at 20°C:** [PART A, 116]
  * Desflurane: 669 mmHg (highest, most volatile)
  * Isoflurane: 240 mmHg
  * Sevoflurane: 160 mmHg
  * Halothane: 243 mmHg
  * Nitrous oxide: >760 mmHg (above atmospheric pressure)
* **Relationship to Boiling Point:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Boiling point = temperature at which vapor pressure exceeds atmospheric pressure
  * Lower boiling point = higher vapor pressure at room temperature
  * ⚠️ Desflurane's boiling point (23.5°C) is near room temperature, requiring special vaporizer
* **Vaporizer Implications:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * SVP determines maximum possible concentration at a given temperature
  * Desflurane requires heated, pressurized vaporizer due to high SVP
  * Other agents use variable-bypass vaporizers

**Bottom Line:** Saturated vapor pressure is a critical physical property that determines how easily an anesthetic can be vaporized at a given temperature. Desflurane's high SVP necessitates a special heated vaporizer, while other agents can be delivered using conventional variable-bypass systems.

## 2.5. BOILING POINTS

**Boiling points of anesthetic agents determine their physical state at room temperature and impact delivery requirements.**

* **Definition:** Temperature at which vapor pressure equals atmospheric pressure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Substance changes from liquid to gas phase
  * Lower boiling point = more volatile agent
* **Values for Common Agents:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Desflurane: 23.5°C (near room temperature)
  * Sevoflurane: 58.6°C
  * Isoflurane: 48.5°C
  * Halothane: 50.2°C
  * Nitrous oxide: -88.5°C (gas at room temperature)
  * Xenon: -108°C (gas at room temperature)
* **Clinical Implications:**
  * Agents with boiling points above room temperature exist as liquids in vaporizers
  * Desflurane (boiling point near room temperature) requires special handling
  * Nitrous oxide and xenon are true gases at room temperature, stored in compressed cylinders
  * Storage requirements differ based on physical state
* **Delivery System Considerations:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Desflurane requires heated, pressurized vaporizer to prevent boiling
  * Other potent agents use standard vaporizers
  * N₂O and xenon delivered as gases from cylinders

**Bottom Line:** The boiling point of an anesthetic agent determines its physical state at room temperature, which dictates storage requirements and delivery systems. Most potent agents are liquids at room temperature, while desflurane's low boiling point requires a specialized vaporizer to prevent boiling and inconsistent delivery.

## 2.6. PHYSICAL CHARACTERISTICS TABLE

**Physical properties of inhaled anesthetics determine their pharmacokinetic behavior and clinical effects.**

* **Key Properties:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * MAC (%) - indicates potency
  * Blood:Gas coefficient - determines speed of onset/offset
  * Oil:Gas coefficient - relates to potency
  * SVP (mmHg) - affects vaporization

* **Property Comparisons:**
  * Desflurane: Lowest blood:gas coefficient (0.42), fastest onset/offset, pungent odor
  * Isoflurane: More potent than desflurane (MAC 1.15%), pungent/irritant odor
  * Sevoflurane: Pleasant odor, intermediate properties, good for induction
  * Nitrous oxide: Low potency (MAC 104%), cannot achieve 1 MAC alone, odorless
  * Xenon: Extremely low blood:gas coefficient (0.14), high potency (MAC 71%), odorless
* **Clinical Implications:**
  * Physical properties directly determine clinical characteristics
  * Choosing agent involves balancing multiple properties
  * No single "ideal" agent for all situations

**Bottom Line:** The physical characteristics of inhaled anesthetics determine their clinical utility. Understanding these properties allows anesthesiologists to select appropriate agents based on specific requirements for speed of onset/offset, potency, and other clinical considerations.

## 2.7. THE IDEAL INHALED ANESTHETIC

**While agents in use today demonstrate many favorable characteristics, no single agent has all the desirable properties of an ideal inhaled anesthetic.**

* **Physical Properties:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Stable to light and heat
  * Inert when in contact with metal, rubber, and soda lime
  * Preservative-free
  * Not flammable or explosive
  * Pleasant odor
  * Not a greenhouse gas
  * Cost-effective
* **Biochemical Properties:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * High oil:gas partition coefficient (low MAC, high potency)
  * Low blood:gas partition coefficient (rapid onset/offset)
  * Not metabolized
  * Non-toxic
  * Only affects the CNS (minimal effects on other systems)
  * Not epileptogenic
  * Some analgesic properties
* **Comparison to Existing Agents:**
  * Sevoflurane: Closest to ideal for induction (pleasant odor, low blood:gas coefficient)
  * Desflurane: Best for recovery due to lowest blood:gas coefficient
  * Xenon: Closest overall to ideal (inert, stable, no metabolism, minimal CV effects, rapid onset/offset) but prohibitively expensive
  * All currently used agents have some non-ideal characteristics (metabolism, cardiovascular effects, environmental impact)
* **Environmental Considerations:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * All volatile anesthetics have greenhouse gas effects
  * Desflurane has significantly worse environmental profile than other agents
  * Nitrous oxide has ozone-depleting potential
  * Only xenon has no environmental impact

**Bottom Line:** No single inhaled anesthetic possesses all the ideal characteristics. The "ideal" agent would have high potency, rapid onset/offset, pleasant odor, lack of metabolism, cardiovascular stability, absence of toxicity, and no environmental impact. Clinical use involves selecting the agent that best matches requirements for a specific case while acknowledging inevitable compromises.

# 3. PHARMACODYNAMICS







## 3.2. MECHANISM OF ACTION

**The precise mechanism of action for inhaled anesthetics remains incompletely understood despite decades of research.**

* **Current Understanding:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * No single clear mechanism identified
  * Direct binding to amphiphilic cavities in proteins
  * Unclear how this binding produces anesthesia
  * Likely enhancement of inhibitory channels and attenuation of excitatory channels
  * Multiple receptors involved: GABA, NMDA, glycine
  
* **Historical Theories:**
  * **Meyer-Overton Hypothesis:** Anesthetic potency correlates with lipid solubility
	* Suggests non-specific mechanism in lipid bilayers
	* Cannot explain stereospecificity of some agents
  
  * **Membrane Expansion Theory:** Anesthetics increase membrane thickness
	* Reduces action potential transmission
	* Cannot explain why hyperthermia (which also increases membrane thickness) increases anesthetic requirements
* **Current Evidence:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Specific protein targets more likely than lipid effects
  * Protein binding sites are lipophilic, explaining correlation with lipid solubility
  * Enhancement of inhibitory neurotransmission (primarily GABA)
  * Inhibition of excitatory neurotransmission (glutamate/NMDA)
  * Modulation of potassium channels

**Bottom Line:** While the precise mechanism remains unclear, inhaled anesthetics likely work through multiple effects on specific protein receptors, particularly by enhancing inhibitory neurotransmission (GABA) and suppressing excitatory pathways (NMDA). The correlation between potency and lipid solubility reflects the lipophilic nature of the binding sites.

## 3.3. MEYER-OVERTON HYPOTHESIS

**The Meyer-Overton hypothesis describes the relationship between anesthetic potency and lipid solubility.**

* **Core Principle:** Anesthetic potency correlates strongly with lipid solubility (oil:gas partition coefficient) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Higher lipid solubility = more potent anesthetic (lower MAC)
  * Relationship holds across diverse chemical structures
* **Supporting Evidence:**
  * Consistent correlation across many anesthetic compounds
  * Aligns with observed potency (MAC values) of clinically used agents
  * Explains why structurally diverse compounds can all produce anesthesia
* **Limitations:**
  * Cannot explain stereospecificity of some anesthetics
	* Some enantiomers have identical lipid solubility but different potencies
  * Does not account for the "cutoff effect" (very lipid-soluble long-chain alcohols lack anesthetic properties)
  * Temperature effects contradict prediction (hyperthermia increases lipid fluidity but increases anesthetic requirements)
* **Modern Interpretation:**
  * Lipid solubility likely correlates with ability to interact with hydrophobic protein binding sites
  * Not necessarily indicating direct membrane effect
  * Represents necessary but not sufficient property for anesthetic action

![Meyer-Overton Hypothesis](INHALATIONAL_INHALATIONAL_Meyer-Overton_Hypothesis.png_b1d8ed.png_9715e1.png)

**Bottom Line:** While the Meyer-Overton hypothesis correctly identifies the correlation between lipid solubility and anesthetic potency, modern understanding suggests this relationship exists because the protein binding sites for anesthetics have hydrophobic characteristics, not because the lipid bilayer itself is the primary site of action.

## 3.4. SHARED PROPERTIES [PART A/B]

**All volatile anesthetics share common pharmacodynamic effects across major organ systems despite chemical differences.**

![Shared Properties](INHALATIONAL_INHALATIONAL_Untitled_3.png_f6ca54.png_ce65b4.png)

* **Neurological Effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * CMRO₂ ↓ (except N₂O: CMRO₂ ↑)
  * Cerebral vascular resistance ↓ → CBF ↑ → ICP ↑
  * Dose-dependent depression of EEG activity
  * For sevoflurane/desflurane/isoflurane:
	* 0.5 MAC: CMRO₂ ↓ counteracts cerebral vasodilation → CBF ↔
	* 1 MAC: CMRO₂ ↓ maximal, so vasodilatory effects predominate → CBF ↑
  * ⚠️ Can increase ICP, particularly at >1 MAC or with pre-existing intracranial pathology
* **Cardiovascular Effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Dose-related ↓ SVR → ↓ MAP (CO usually maintained)
  * Myocardial depression (most pronounced with halothane)
  * Baroreceptor depression at >1 MAC
  * Sensitization to catecholamines varies (halothane > others)
  * Arrhythmogenic potential varies between agents
* **Pulmonary Effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * ↓ Tidal volume, ↑ respiratory rate → relatively preserved minute ventilation
  * Dose-dependent ↓ of ventilatory response to hypercapnia and hypoxemia
  * ↑ Bronchodilation (beneficial in reactive airway disease)
  * Inhibition of hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (minimal clinical effect)
  * Suppression of mucociliary function
* **Other System Effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Renal: ↓ renal blood flow and ↓ GFR
  * MSK: ↑ muscle relaxation (except N₂O)
  * ↓ Uterine tone (dose-dependent)
  * EEG Effects:
	* Halothane/isoflurane/desflurane/sevoflurane: Burst suppression
	* Enflurane: Epileptiform activity (3 Hz spike and wave)

**Bottom Line:** Despite chemical differences, inhaled anesthetics share similar effects across organ systems including reduced cerebral metabolic rate with increased cerebral blood flow, reduced vascular resistance with maintained cardiac output, respiratory depression, and bronchodilation. Understanding these common properties allows prediction of clinical effects across agents.

# 4. MINIMUM ALVEOLAR CONCENTRATION (MAC)







## 4.2. DEFINITION AND CLINICAL RELEVANCE

**MAC (Minimum Alveolar Concentration) is the fundamental measure of potency for inhaled anesthetics.**

* **Definition:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Alveolar concentration of anesthetic at 1 atmosphere that prevents movement in response to surgical incision in 50% of patients
  * Equivalent to ED50 (effective dose for 50% of population)
  * Measured as volume percent (vol%)
  * Based on partial pressure rather than concentration
  * MAC at steady state that prevents reaction to a standard surgical stimulus (skin incision) in 50% of subjects at one atmosphere
* **Clinical Significance:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Standardized method to compare potency between agents
  * Allows rational dosing and titration
  * Provides target for anesthetic delivery
  * End-tidal concentration monitoring allows real-time assessment of anesthetic depth
  
* **Relationship to Anesthetic Depth:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * MAC is movement response, not awareness
  * Loss of consciousness occurs at approximately 0.4-0.5 MAC
  * ED95 (no movement in 95% of patients) ≈ 1.2-1.3 MAC
  * MAC values are additive between different agents
	* 0.5 MAC isoflurane + 0.5 MAC N₂O = 1.0 MAC (equivalent effect)
* **Physiologic Basis:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Reflects primarily spinal cord effects (prevention of movement)
  * Loss of consciousness reflects primarily brain effects
  * Different endpoints reflect different sites of action

**Bottom Line:** MAC is the cornerstone concept for quantifying and comparing inhaled anesthetic potency. It provides a standardized measure that allows clinicians to predict anesthetic effects, compare agents, and titrate delivery. Understanding that prevention of movement (MAC) requires higher concentrations than prevention of awareness is essential for safe anesthetic practice.

## 4.3. TYPES OF MAC [PART A/B]

**Multiple MAC derivatives describe different clinical endpoints beyond the standard movement response.**

* **MAC-Movement (Standard MAC):** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Prevents movement in 50% of patients in response to surgical incision
  * The standard reference value (e.g., sevoflurane MAC = 2.1%)
  
* **MAC-Awake (MAC-Awareness):** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Concentration at which patient opens eyes to command
  * Approximately 0.3-0.5 standard MAC
  * Varies significantly between agents
  * Shows hysteresis (typically takes less to regain consciousness than to lose it)
  
* **MAC-BAR (MAC-Blocking Adrenergic Response):** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Concentration preventing sympathetic response to noxious stimuli
  * Prevents tachycardia, hypertension, pupillary dilation
  * Approximately 1.5-1.7 standard MAC
  * Opioids significantly reduce MAC-BAR requirement
  
* **MAC-EI (MAC-Endotracheal Intubation):** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Concentration preventing laryngeal response to intubation
  * Approximately 1.3 MAC
  * ED95 level (95% of patients will not respond)
  * More relevant in pediatric practice (adults typically receive muscle relaxants)
* **Other MAC Derivatives:**
  * MAC-Amnesia: Prevents memory formation, ~0.25 MAC
  * MAC-BS (Burst Suppression): Produces EEG burst suppression, ~1.5-2 MAC

**Bottom Line:** Different MAC derivatives describe specific clinical endpoints from preventing awareness (MAC-Awake) to blunting sympathetic responses (MAC-BAR). Understanding these different values helps guide anesthetic delivery for specific clinical goals, recognizing that preventing movement, blunting autonomic responses, and ensuring unconsciousness require different anesthetic concentrations.

## 4.4. FACTORS AFFECTING MAC

**Numerous physiological and pharmacological factors can increase or decrease MAC values.**

* **Factors Decreasing MAC (Less Anesthetic Required):** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Age:**
	* 6% decrease per decade after age 40
	* MAC peaks at 6 months, then declines throughout life
	* 💡 MAC for 80-year-old is ~70% of MAC for 40-year-old
	* During neonatal period, sevoflurane has the highest MAC
	* In general, infants > neonates > adults (except sevoflurane - neonates > infants > adults)
	
  * **Physiological States:**
	* Hypothermia (5% MAC reduction per °C)
	* Pregnancy (up to 30% reduction)
	* Hypotension (MAP < 50 mmHg)
	* Hypoxemia (PaO₂ < 38 mmHg)
	* Anemia (< 4.3 mL O₂/dL blood)
	* Hypothyroidism
	
  * **Acid-Base Disturbances:**
	* Metabolic acidosis
	* Hypo-osmolality
	* Hyponatremia
	
  * **Pharmacological:**
	* Sedatives/hypnotics (benzodiazepines, barbiturates, propofol)
	* Opioids (significant MAC reduction)
	* Alpha-2 agonists (dexmedetomidine, clonidine)
	* Lithium
	* Lidocaine and other local anesthetics
	* Acute ethanol administration
	* Verapamil
	* Acute opioid use (chronic opioid use increases MAC)
	* Acute alcohol intake (chronic alcohol intake increases MAC)
	* Chronic amphetamine intake
	
* **Factors Increasing MAC (More Anesthetic Required):** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Physiological States:**
	* Hyperthermia
	* Hyperthyroidism
	* Hypernatremia
	* Infancy (except neonates)
	
  * **Pharmacological:**
	* Chronic alcohol abuse
	* Stimulants (acute amphetamine, cocaine, ephedrine)
	* MAO inhibitors
	* Levodopa (acute administration)
	* Catecholamines and sympathomimetics
	* Chronic opioid use
	
  * **Genetic Factors:**
	* Red hair (possible 19% increase in some studies)
	* Genetic variations in MAC requirements
* **Factors Not Affecting MAC:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Gender
  * Duration of anesthesia
  * PaCO₂ levels (within normal range)
  * Thyroid function (unless pathological)
  * Magnesium levels

![Factors Decreasing MAC](INHALATIONAL_INHALATIONAL_Untitled_7.png_18fe46.png_9671b4.png)

**Bottom Line:** MAC requirements vary substantially with age, temperature, physiological state, and concurrent medications. Age is particularly important clinically, with older patients requiring significantly less anesthetic. Understanding these factors allows appropriate adjustment of anesthetic delivery to achieve desired clinical effects while minimizing side effects.

## 4.5. MAC VALUES FOR COMMON AGENTS

**MAC values differ significantly between anesthetic agents, reflecting their relative potencies.**

* **MAC Values of Common Agents (Adult, 40 years old):** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Desflurane: 6.6% (least potent among halogenated agents)
  * Sevoflurane: 2.1%
  * Isoflurane: 1.15% (most potent among currently used agents)
  * Halothane: 0.75% (historical reference)
  * Enflurane: 1.68%
  * Nitrous oxide: 104% (cannot achieve 1 MAC alone at atmospheric pressure)
  * Xenon: 71%
  
* **MAC-Age Relationship:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * MAC decreases approximately 6% per decade after age 40
  * Formula: MAC(age) = MAC(40) × 10^(-0.00269(Age-40))
  * Example: Sevoflurane MAC at age 80 = 2.1 × 10^(-0.00269×40) = 1.45%
  
* **Clinical Applications:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * MAC values are additive between agents
  * Example: 0.5 MAC sevoflurane (1.05%) + 0.5 MAC nitrous oxide (52%) = 1.0 MAC
  * ED95 (preventing movement in 95% of patients) is approximately 1.2-1.3 × MAC
  
* **Relationship to Physical Properties:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Inverse correlation with oil:gas partition coefficient (lipid solubility)
  * Higher lipid solubility = lower MAC = greater potency
  * Reflects Meyer-Overton principle

![Age Effects on MAC](INHALATIONAL_image-7.png_c34699.png)

**Bottom Line:** MAC values vary widely between agents, with isoflurane being the most potent and desflurane the least potent among the halogenated agents. MAC decreases significantly with age, requiring adjustment of delivered concentrations for older patients. When combining agents, the MAC values are additive, allowing for reduced concentrations of individual agents.

# 5. DELIVERY OF VOLATILE ANESTHETICS







## 5.2. VAPORIZERS

**Modern anesthetic machines use specialized vaporizers to deliver precise concentrations of volatile anesthetics.**

* **Basic Principle:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Vaporizers convert liquid anesthetic to vapor and mix with carrier gas
  * Create saturated vapor in equilibrium with liquid
  * Deliver precise concentration regardless of flow rate (variable bypass design)
  
* **Types of Vaporizers:**
  * **Variable Bypass (Most Common):**
	* Used for isoflurane, sevoflurane
	* Split flow: portion of gas passes through vaporization chamber
	* Concentration controlled by splitting ratio
	* Temperature compensated
	
  * **Measured Flow/Direct Injection (Desflurane):**
	* Used for desflurane due to high vapor pressure
	* Electrically heated to 39°C (above boiling point)
	* Pressurized to 2 atmospheres
	* Directly injects measured amount of vapor into carrier gas
	
* **Vaporization Principles:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Liquid anesthetic evaporates up to saturated vapor pressure (SVP)
  * SVP depends on temperature and specific agent
  * Output concentration determined by:
	* SVP of agent
	* Temperature
	* Barometric pressure
	* Flow rate (minimal effect in variable bypass design)

![Vaporizer Diagram](INHALATIONAL_INHALATIONAL_Untitled_4.png_b9f200.png_b90dde.png)

**Bottom Line:** Modern vaporizers accurately deliver volatile anesthetics by creating and precisely controlling a saturated vapor in equilibrium with liquid anesthetic. Desflurane requires a specialized vaporizer due to its high vapor pressure and low boiling point, while other agents can use conventional variable-bypass vaporizers.

## 5.3. FRESH GAS FLOW CONSIDERATIONS

**Fresh gas flow (FGF) rates affect efficiency, circuit dynamics, and potential byproduct exposure.**

* **Impact on Circuit Dynamics:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Higher FGF = faster rise in circuit concentration
  * Time constant (τ) = Circuit Volume ÷ FGF
  * 95% of steady state reached after 3 time constants
  
* **Low Flow Anesthesia Benefits:**
  * Conservation of anesthetic gases
  * Reduced environmental impact (less waste gas)
  * Better heat and humidity preservation
  * Cost savings
  
* **Low Flow Anesthesia Concerns:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Slower changes in delivered concentration
  * Potential accumulation of degradation products
	* Compound A with sevoflurane (increased at low flows)
	* However, clinical renal injury not demonstrated
  * Slower washout during emergence
  
* **Recommended Flow Rates:**
  * High flow (≥4 L/min): Rapid changes, induction, emergence
  * Medium flow (2-4 L/min): Standard maintenance
  * Low flow (0.5-1 L/min): Efficient maintenance after equilibration
  * Minimal flow (<0.5 L/min): Specialized techniques
  
* **Clinical Applications:**
  * Induction: High flow (4-6 L/min) for rapid rise in concentration
  * Maintenance: Can reduce to 1-2 L/min once stable
  * Emergence: Increase to 4-6 L/min for rapid washout

**Bottom Line:** Fresh gas flow rates significantly impact the speed of concentration changes, efficiency of gas usage, and environmental impact. Higher flows enable faster changes in anesthetic concentration, while lower flows conserve anesthetic agents and reduce pollution but slow response to changing vaporizer settings.

## 5.4. EFFECTS OF ALTITUDE

**Changes in barometric pressure at different altitudes affect anesthetic delivery and potency.**

* **Fundamental Principles:**
  * Anesthetic effect depends on partial pressure (not concentration)
  * Partial pressure = concentration × barometric pressure
  * At higher altitudes, barometric pressure decreases
  
* **Effects on Standard Vaporizers (Sevoflurane, Isoflurane):** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * At higher altitudes, output concentration increases, but partial pressure remains constant
  * Modern vaporizers compensate automatically
  * Same dial setting produces same clinical effect despite higher % concentration
  * Example: In Denver (5,280 ft), setting 2% sevoflurane may deliver 2.3% but same partial pressure as 2% at sea level
  
* **Effects on Desflurane Vaporizer:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Delivers fixed percent concentration, NOT fixed partial pressure
  * At higher altitudes, partial pressure is reduced due to lower barometric pressure
  * Required dial setting = desired % × (760 mmHg ÷ current atmospheric pressure)
  * Example: For 1 MAC at 380 mmHg, would need to set dial to 12% (vs. 6% at sea level)
  
* **Clinical Management:**
  * Most vaporizers automatically compensate (sevoflurane, isoflurane)
  * For desflurane, adjust setting based on altitude
  * Monitor clinical signs of anesthetic depth
  * Consider using end-tidal concentration monitoring calibrated for altitude

**Bottom Line:** While most vaporizers automatically compensate for altitude, the desflurane TEC-6 vaporizer requires manual adjustment. Understanding that anesthetic effect depends on partial pressure, not concentration percentage, is key to appropriate dosing at different altitudes.

## 5.5. CALCULATIONS FOR ANESTHETIC DELIVERY [PART A/B]

**Mathematical relationships allow prediction of anesthetic concentrations based on physical properties and environmental conditions.**

* **Saturated Vapor Pressure Calculations:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * SVP/PT = VA/(VC+VA)
	* SVP = Saturated vapor pressure
	* PT = Total pressure (usually atmospheric pressure)
	* VA = Agent vapor volume
	* VC = Carrier gas volume
  
  * For calculating agent volume: VA = [SVP/(PT-SVP)] × VC
  
  * For calculating % concentration: % Volatile = [VA/(FGF+VA)] × 100
  
* **Vaporizer Output Principles:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * For standard vaporizers (sevoflurane, isoflurane):
	* Output is function of SVP in proportion to atmospheric pressure
	* Falling atmospheric pressure increases output volume but maintains partial pressure
  
  * For desflurane vaporizer:
	* Delivers anesthetic at fixed percent concentration
	* Does not compensate automatically for altitude
	
* **Example Calculations:**
  * If sevoflurane has SVP of 160 mmHg at 20°C, and PT is 760 mmHg:
	* At vaporizer setting of 2%, actual delivered concentration is close to 2%
	* If altitude decreases PT to 600 mmHg, vaporizer still delivers partial pressure equivalent to 2% at sea level
	
  * For desflurane at altitude with PT of 600 mmHg:
	* To achieve same partial pressure as 6% at sea level, must set vaporizer to 6% × (760/600) = 7.6%

![Vapor Pressure Calculations](INHALATIONAL_INHALATIONAL_Untitled_5.png_279988.png_aa32e7.png)

**Bottom Line:** Understanding the mathematical relationships between vapor pressure, atmospheric pressure, and anesthetic concentration allows prediction of vaporizer output and appropriate adjustments for environmental conditions. Most vaporizers automatically compensate for pressure changes, but desflurane vaporizers require manual adjustment at altitude.

# 6. INDIVIDUAL AGENTS [PART A/B]







## 6.2. ISOFLURANE [PART A/B]

**Isoflurane is a halogenated methyl ethyl ether that has been a mainstay of anesthetic practice for decades.**

* **Physical Properties:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Chemical structure: Halogenated methyl ethyl ether
  * Structural isomer of enflurane
  * MAC: 1.15% (most potent of currently used volatile anesthetics)
  * Blood:Gas coefficient: 1.4 (intermediate)
  * Oil:Gas coefficient: 98
  * Pungent odor, highly irritating to airways
  * SVP at 20°C: 240 mmHg
  * Boiling point: 48.5°C
  
* **Pharmacokinetics:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Metabolized by CYP2E1 (only 0.2%)
  * Primarily eliminated via lungs
  * Intermediate onset/offset due to solubility
  * Produces trifluoroacetic acid and Fas metabolites
  
* **Clinical Effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Cardiovascular:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Dose-dependent ↓ SVR and BP
	* Modest direct myocardial depression
	* Reflex tachycardia common
	* Previously implicated in "coronary steal" phenomenon (now disputed)
	* As long as coronary perfusion is maintained, coronary steal does not occur
	* May have myocardial protective properties via effects on ATP-dependent potassium channels
  
  * **Respiratory:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Dose-dependent respiratory depression
	* Airway irritant (not ideal for inhalational induction)
	* Bronchodilation
	* Minute volume decreased while respiratory rate and PaCO₂ increased
  
  * **CNS:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Decreases CMRO₂
	* Increases CBF (minimal at <1 MAC, more significant at >1 MAC)
	* Can increase ICP (usually at >1 MAC)
	* At concentrations up to 1 MAC cerebral autoregulation is preserved
	* At 2 MAC produces electrically silent EEG
	* Stimulation of the area postrema at the base of the fourth ventricle in the medulla is the likely mechanism leading to nausea and vomiting
  
* **Metabolism and Toxicity:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Only 0.2% is metabolized and none of the products has been linked to toxicity
  * Due to the presence of a –CHF₂ group in its structure it may react with dry soda lime (or baralyme), producing carbon monoxide
  * Reports relate to circle systems left with dry gas circulating over a weekend
  * Enflurane and desflurane also possess –CHF₂ groups and may react similarly
* **Clinical Uses:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Maintenance of general anesthesia
  * Cost-effective option (least expensive among current agents)
  * Good stability and long shelf life
  * Not commonly used for mask induction due to pungency

**Bottom Line:** Isoflurane is a potent, cost-effective anesthetic with intermediate blood solubility and minimal metabolism. Its pungent odor limits use for inhalational induction, but it provides reliable maintenance of anesthesia with predictable cardiovascular and respiratory effects. Despite historical concerns about coronary steal, it has a long record of safe use in diverse patient populations.

## 6.3. DESFLURANE [PART A/B]

**Desflurane is a fluorinated methyl ethyl ether with extremely low blood solubility allowing rapid emergence.**

* **Physical Properties:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Chemical structure: Similar to isoflurane but with fluorine substituted for chlorine
  * MAC: 6.6% (least potent volatile anesthetic)
  * Blood:Gas coefficient: 0.42 (lowest, fastest onset/offset)
  * Oil:Gas coefficient: 19 (lowest, explaining low potency)
  * Boiling point: 23.5°C (near room temperature)
  * Vapor pressure: 669 mmHg (highest, close to atmospheric pressure)
  * Extremely volatile, dangerous to administer via conventional vaporizer
  
* **Pharmacokinetics:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Minimal metabolism (0.02%)
  * Strong carbon-fluorine bonds resist metabolism
  * Rapid elimination via lungs
  * Fastest onset/offset among halogenated agents
  
* **Clinical Effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Cardiovascular:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Dose-dependent ↓ SVR and BP
	* ⚠️ Rapid increases in concentration can cause sympathetic activation, tachycardia, hypertension
	* Can cause myocardial ischemia in susceptible patients if rapidly increased without adequate opioid coverage
	* In patients with ischemic heart disease, concentrations above 1 MAC may produce cardiovascular stimulation
	* Similar to isoflurane, but with greater risk of tachycardia
  
  * **Respiratory:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Very pungent, can cause coughing, breath-holding, laryngospasm
	* Dose-dependent respiratory depression
	* Can cause transient airway irritation and increased resistance
	* Not suitable for induction of anesthesia
  
  * **CNS:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Decreases CMRO₂
	* Increases CBF and potentially ICP at >1 MAC
	* EEG effects can revert to continuous activity despite unchanging MAC (unique to desflurane)
  
* **Special Considerations:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Requires special heated, pressurized vaporizer (TEC-6) that heats desflurane to 39°C at 2 atmospheres
  * ⚠️ Can form CO in desiccated CO₂ absorbent (more than other volatiles)
  * Significant greenhouse gas effect (20 times worse than sevoflurane)
  * Low blood:gas partition coefficient (0.42) ensures rapid onset and offset
* **Clinical Uses:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Most advantageous for long cases requiring rapid emergence
  * Particularly useful in obese patients due to low fat solubility
  * Not suitable for inhalational induction due to airway irritation
  * Volatile agent of choice for prolonged surgery or bariatric surgery

**Bottom Line:** Desflurane's extremely low blood solubility enables the fastest onset/offset among volatile anesthetics, making it valuable for outpatient surgery and cases requiring rapid emergence. However, its airway irritancy, propensity to trigger sympathetic activation with rapid increases, and need for a specialized vaporizer are significant limitations.

## 6.4. SEVOFLURANE [PART A/B]

**Sevoflurane is a fluorinated methyl isopropyl ether known for its pleasant odor and suitability for inhalational induction.**

* **Physical Properties:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Chemical structure: Fluorinated methyl isopropyl ether
  * Achiral polyflurinated isopropyl methyl ether
  * MAC: 2.1% (intermediate potency)
  * Blood:Gas coefficient: 0.65 (low, fast onset/offset)
  * Oil:Gas coefficient: 47
  * Pleasant, non-pungent odor
  * Vapor pressure: 160 mmHg (conventional vaporizer suitable)
  
* **Manufacture & Storage:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Methods: One pot method or chloro-fluoro method
  * Storage concerns: At water concentration below 100 ppm, susceptible to attack by Lewis acids at ether and halogen bonds, releasing toxic hydrofluoric acid (HF)
  * Formulated with 300 ppm water as Lewis acid inhibitor
  * Stored in polyethylene napthalate bottles rather than glass to prevent degradation by Lewis acids
  * Dry formulation (<130 ppm water) available in aluminum bottle lined with epoxyphenolic resin lacquer
  
* **Pharmacokinetics:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Metabolized to approximately 3.5% extent
  * Produces inorganic fluoride but not associated with renal toxicity
  * Not metabolized to trifluoroacetate (unlike other agents)
  * Hepatic metabolism by cytochrome P450 (isoform 2E1) to hexafluoroisopropanol and inorganic F-
  * Rapid elimination primarily via lungs
  
* **Clinical Effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Cardiovascular:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Dose-dependent ↓ SVR and BP
	* Often without compensatory tachycardia (unlike isoflurane)
	* Half as potent a coronary vasodilator as isoflurane
	* The systemic vascular resistance falls and due to unchanged heart rate, blood pressure falls
	* Cardiac contractility is unaffected and heart not sensitized to catecholamines
  
  * **Respiratory:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Non-irritating to airways
	* Dose-dependent respiratory depression
	* Good bronchodilator properties
	* Useful for induction of anesthesia due to pleasant odor and favorable physical properties
	* Predictable depression of ventilation with reduction in minute volume and rise in PaCO₂
  
  * **CNS:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Decreases CMRO₂
	* Preserves autoregulation up to approximately 1 MAC
	* Potential for EEG abnormalities/excitation at high concentrations
	* Possible seizure-like activity in predisposed patients
	* Evidence that children exhibit higher incidence of post-operative agitation and delirium compared to halothane
	* Delirium may extend beyond initial recovery period
  
* **Special Considerations:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Interacts with CO₂ absorbents to form Compound A
	* Most with barium hydroxide lime (Baralyme)
	* Less with calcium hydroxide absorbents
	* No evidence of human nephrotoxicity despite theoretical concern
  * Compounds A-E formed when sevoflurane used with carbon dioxide absorbents
  * Formation favored with potassium hydroxide rather than sodium-hydroxide-based absorbents, especially when dry
  * Reaction releases heat and consumes sevoflurane
  * Lethal concentration of compound A in 50% of rats is 300-400 ppm after 3 hours
  * Studies suggest human nephrotoxic threshold of 150-200 ppm
  * Recent studies show even with flow rates of 0.25 L/min for 5 hours, compound A peaks at <20 ppm with no renal function abnormalities
* **Clinical Uses:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Ideal for inhalational induction due to pleasant odor
  * Popular for pediatric anesthesia despite possible emergence agitation
  * Good choice for patients with reactive airway disease
  * Second fastest emergence after desflurane

**Bottom Line:** Sevoflurane's pleasant odor and low blood solubility make it ideal for inhalational induction, particularly in pediatrics and patients with difficult IV access. Its rapid onset/offset, minimal airway irritation, and excellent bronchodilator properties make it suitable for diverse applications despite theoretical concerns about Compound A formation.

## 6.5. NITROUS OXIDE [PART A/B]

**Nitrous oxide is a low-potency inhalational anesthetic commonly used as an adjunct to other agents.**

* **Physical Properties:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * True gas at room temperature (not a volatile liquid)
  * MAC: 104% (cannot achieve surgical anesthesia alone at atmospheric pressure)
  * Blood:Gas coefficient: 0.47 (low, fast onset/offset)
  * Storage: Compressed liquid in cylinders
  * Stored at 50 atmospheres (745 psi)
  * Weight, not pressure, indicates remaining N₂O in tank
  * French blue cylinders (C = 450 liters up to G = 9,000 liters) with gauge pressure of 51 bar at 20°C
  * Filling ratio (mass of N₂O in cylinder/mass of water cylinder could hold) is 0.75 in temperate regions, 0.67 in tropical regions
  * Critical temperature: 36.5°C, critical pressure: 72 bar
  
* **Manufacture:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Manufactured by heating ammonium nitrate to 250°C
  * NH₄NO₃ → N₂O + 2H₂O
  * Potential contaminants: NH₃, N₂, NO, NO₂, HNO₃
  * Impurities removed by passage through scrubbers, water, and caustic soda
  
* **Pharmacokinetics:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Minimal metabolism (<0.01%)
  * Rapid elimination via lungs
  * 💡 Rapid washout can cause diffusion hypoxia without supplemental O₂
  
* **Clinical Effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Cardiovascular:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Mild sympathetic stimulation at 40% concentration
	* Minimal direct myocardial effects
	* Although N₂O has mild direct myocardial depressant effects, it increases sympathetic activity through central effects
	* In health, circulatory system changes very little
	* In cardiac failure patients unable to increase sympathetic drive, direct myocardial depressant effects may significantly reduce cardiac output
	* Does not sensitize the heart to catecholamines
  
  * **Respiratory:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Minimal respiratory depression
	* Does not produce bronchodilation
	* Small fall in tidal volume offset by increased respiratory rate so minute volume and PaCO₂ remain unchanged
  
  * **CNS:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* ⚠️ Unlike other agents, increases CMRO₂
	* Increases CBF
	* Sometimes avoided in patients with raised intracranial pressure
	* NMDA antagonist properties contribute to analgesic effects
	* Despite MAC of 105%, potential to cause anesthesia in certain patients (the elderly) should not be ignored
  
  * **Other Effects:**
	* Mild analgesic properties (NMDA antagonism)
	* Does not produce skeletal muscle relaxation
	* Probably increases risk of post-operative nausea and vomiting (PONV), especially in those with risk factors
	* ⚠️ Oxidizes vitamin B₁₂ cobalt atom, inhibiting methionine synthase
	  * Prolonged exposure can lead to megaloblastic anemia and neuropathies
	  * Clinical significance for short exposures minimal
	  * Exposure of only a few hours may result in megaloblastic changes in bone marrow
	  * Prolonged exposure (days) may result in agranulocytosis
	  * Recovery governed by synthesis of new methionine synthetase (taking a few days)
	  * May be helped by folinic acid administration
  
* **Special Considerations:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Diffuses into closed air spaces more rapidly than nitrogen diffuses out
	* Can expand pneumothorax to 2-3× size in 10-30 minutes
	* Can increase pressure in bowel, middle ear, ET tube balloons, etc.
	* Contraindicated in pneumothorax, bowel obstruction, pneumocephalus, middle ear surgery
  * Not a trigger for malignant hyperthermia
  * Potent greenhouse gas and ozone depleter
  * Concentration effect and second gas effect (described in section 1.5.2)
  * In properly scavenged environment where N₂O concentrations are <50 ppm, no effect on DNA synthesis
  * In unscavenged dental surgeries with large amounts used, chronic exposure resulted in neurological syndromes similar to subacute combined degeneration of the cord
  * In experimental conditions, shown to be teratogenic to rats (prevented by folinic acid)
  * Often not used in first trimester when anesthesia required, though never unequivocally demonstrated in humans
* **Entonox:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * 50:50 mixture of N₂O and O₂
  * Two gases effectively dissolve into each other (Poynting effect)
  * Used commonly for analgesia during labor and other painful procedures
  * Stored as gas in French blue cylinders (G = 3,200 liters; J = 6,400 liters) with white and blue checked shoulders, at 137 bar
  * Separates into constituent parts below its pseudo-critical temperature (about -7°C), most likely at 117 bar
  * Higher or lower pressures reduce likelihood of separation
  * When delivered via pipeline at 4.1 bar, pseudo-critical temperature is <-30°C
  * If cylinder used following separation, inspired gas will initially produce little analgesia (mainly O₂), becoming progressively potent and hypoxic as it approaches 100% N₂O
* **Clinical Uses:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Adjunct to potent volatile agents (reduces volatile requirements)
  * Analgesic component of balanced anesthesia
  * Commonly used in dental procedures
  * 💡 Should administer 100% O₂ for 5-10 min after discontinuation to prevent diffusion hypoxia

**Bottom Line:** Nitrous oxide provides modest analgesia and anesthetic-sparing effects but cannot produce surgical anesthesia alone. Its rapid onset/offset and minimal cardiovascular effects are advantages, but its expansion of gas-filled spaces and potential for diffusion hypoxia require careful patient selection and technique.

## 6.6. XENON [PART A/B]

**Xenon is an inert noble gas with anesthetic properties and minimal side effects.**

* **Physical Properties:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Inert noble gas occurring in air at 0.0000087% (0.05 ppm)
  * MAC: 71% (more potent than N₂O but still requires high concentration)
  * Blood:Gas coefficient: 0.115 (extremely low, fastest onset/offset)
  * Non-explosive, non-pungent, odorless
  * Makes up 0.0000087% of the atmosphere
  
* **Manufacture:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Produced by the fractional distillation of air
  * About 2,000 times the cost of producing N₂O
  
* **Pharmacokinetics:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * No metabolism (chemically inert)
  * Eliminated entirely via lungs
  * Extremely rapid onset/offset due to very low blood solubility
  
* **Clinical Effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Cardiovascular:**
	* Minimal myocardial depression
	* Does not alter coronary blood flow
	* Stable hemodynamic profile
	* May result in small decrease in heart rate
  
  * **Respiratory:**
	* Non-irritating to airways
	* Minimal respiratory depression
	* Unlike other inhaled anesthetics, xenon slows respiratory rate while tidal volume increases so minute volume remains constant
	* Higher density (3x N₂O) and viscosity (1.5x N₂O) might increase airway resistance when used in high concentrations, but clinical significance minimal
	* Does not appear to cause diffusion hypoxia like N₂O
  
  * **CNS:**
	* Provides analgesia (NMDA receptor inhibition)
	* Possible neuroprotective properties
	* Inhibits NMDA receptor glycine site
	* May increase cerebral blood flow in a variable manner
	* Use in anesthesia for neurosurgery not recommended
  
* **Environmental Impact:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * No greenhouse gas effect or ozone depletion
  * Not a pollutant or occupational hazard
  
* **Limitations:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Extremely expensive (extraction by fractional distillation of air)
  * Limited availability
  * High MAC (71%) requires high concentration
  * Requires specialized delivery systems for efficiency

**Bottom Line:** Xenon has many characteristics of an "ideal" anesthetic including rapid onset/offset, minimal side effects, inherent analgesic properties, and no environmental impact. However, its scarcity and high cost limit its clinical use despite these favorable properties.

## 6.7. HISTORICAL AGENTS (HALOTHANE, ENFLURANE)

**Halothane and enflurane are older volatile anesthetics with historical significance but limited current use.**

* **Halothane:**
  * **Physical Properties:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* First widely used halogenated anesthetic
	* Halogenated hydrocarbon
	* MAC: 0.75% (high potency)
	* Blood:Gas coefficient: 2.5 (high, slow onset/offset)
	* Oil:Gas coefficient: 224 (high, explaining potency)
	* Sweet non-irritant odor
	* Unstable when exposed to light, corrodes certain metals
	* Stored with 0.01% thymol to prevent liberation of free bromine
	* Dissolves into rubber and may leach into breathing circuits after vaporizer turned off
  
  * **Pharmacokinetics:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Significant metabolism (15-20%)
	* Forms trifluoroacetic acid metabolites
	* Up to 25% undergoes oxidative metabolism by hepatic P450 to produce trifluoroacetic acid, Brand Cl-
	* Reductive metabolism producing Fand other reduced metabolites predominate when liver becomes hypoxic
  
  * **Clinical Effects:**
	* **Cardiovascular:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	  * Direct myocardial depression
	  * ⚠️ Sensitizes myocardium to catecholamines (arrhythmias)
	  * Minimal effect on SVR
	  * Cardiac output reduced indirectly by increased vagal tone and directly by myocardial depressant effect
	  * Local anesthetic/adrenaline preparations should be used with caution
  
	* **Respiratory:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	  * Bronchodilation (used historically for status asthmaticus)
	  * Respiratory depression
	  * May be used to induce anesthesia due to sweet non-irritant odor
	  * Higher concentrations significantly reduce minute ventilation
	  * Significant bronchodilator properties useful in asthmatic patients
  
	* **CNS:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	  * Increases cerebral blood flow more than any other volatile agent
	  * Can increase intracranial pressure above 0.6 MAC
	  * Reduces cerebral oxygen requirements
  
	* **Hepatic:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	  * ⚠️ Halothane hepatitis - rare but serious complication
	  * Type 1: Mild, self-limiting transaminitis (common, subclinical, often associated with hepatic hypoxia)
	  * Type 2: Fulminant, potentially fatal immune-mediated hepatic necrosis (rare)
	  * Trifluoroacetyl chloride (oxidative metabolite) may act as hapten, binding covalently with hepatic proteins, inducing antibody formation
	  * Diagnosis based on exclusion of all other forms of liver damage
	  * Incidence in children: 1 in 80,000-200,000; in adults: 1 in 2,500-35,000
	  * Risk factors: Multiple exposures, obesity, middle age, female sex
	  * Mortality rate: 50-75%
	  * Should be avoided if administered within previous 3 months, history of previous adverse reaction, or pre-existing liver disease
  
  * **Replaced due to:**
	* Hepatotoxicity concerns
	* Cardiac sensitization
	* Slower kinetics than newer agents
	* Retains niche use but largely superseded by sevoflurane
* **Enflurane:**
  * **Physical Properties:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Structural isomer of isoflurane
	* Halogenated ethyl methyl ether
	* MAC: 1.7%
	* Blood:Gas coefficient: 1.9 (higher than isoflurane)
	* Superseded by other agents
  
  * **Pharmacokinetics:**
	* Metabolized to approximately 2% extent
	* Produces fluoride ions (potential nephrotoxicity)
	* Avoided in patients with renal impairment as metabolism produces Fions (known to produce reversible nephropathy)
  
  * **Clinical Effects:**
	* **CNS:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	  * ⚠️ Can produce epileptiform EEG activity at high concentrations
	  * At high concentrations produces 3 Hz spike and wave pattern on EEG, consistent with grand mal activity
	  * Contraindicated in epilepsy
  
	* **Hepatic:**
	  * Has been reported to cause hepatic necrosis like halothane
	  * Incidence much lower due to lower rate of metabolism
  
  * **Replaced due to:**
	* CNS excitatory effects
	* Fluoride production and potential nephrotoxicity
	* Availability of safer alternatives

**Bottom Line:** Halothane and enflurane represent earlier generations of volatile anesthetics with significant historical importance but have been largely replaced by newer agents with more favorable safety profiles, pharmacokinetics, and side effect profiles.

# 7. ORGAN SYSTEM EFFECTS [PART A/B]







## 7.2. CENTRAL NERVOUS SYSTEM [PART A/B]

**Volatile anesthetics exert profound and dose-dependent effects on cerebral function and hemodynamics.**

* **Effects on Cerebral Metabolism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Dose-dependent decrease in cerebral metabolic rate (CMRO₂)
  * Nonlinear reduction - steep decline at 0.3-0.6 MAC
  * Exception: N₂O increases CMRO₂
  * Maximum CMRO₂ suppression at ~1 MAC
  * Burst suppression on EEG at 1.5-2 MAC
  * Isoelectric EEG at higher concentrations
* **Effects on Cerebral Blood Flow (CBF):** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * All volatile agents increase CBF in dose-related manner
  * Mechanisms:
	* Direct cerebral vasodilation
	* Impaired autoregulation at >1 MAC
	* Uncoupling of flow-metabolism relationship
  * Agent comparison:
	* Halothane: Most potent CBF increase
	* Isoflurane/Sevoflurane/Desflurane: Less increase per MAC
	* Minimal CBF changes below 1 MAC
* **Effects on Intracranial Pressure (ICP):** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Parallel changes with CBF
  * Mild increases for isoflurane, sevoflurane, desflurane >1 MAC
  * Can be blunted/prevented by:
	* Hypocapnia (hyperventilation)
	* Barbiturate coadministration
  * ⚠️ More significant concern in patients with reduced intracranial compliance or mass lesions
* **EEG and Evoked Potentials:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Dose-dependent depression of:
	* EEG activity
	* Sensory-evoked potentials
	* Motor-evoked potentials
  * Early excitation followed by progressive depression
  * MEPs extremely sensitive to volatile suppression
  * Avoid volatile agents or use low doses for neuromonitoring cases
  * EEG Effects:
	* Halothane/isoflurane/desflurane/sevoflurane: Burst suppression
	* Enflurane: Epileptiform activity (3 Hz spike and wave)
* **Cerebral Protection:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * May improve neurologic outcome after incomplete cerebral ischemia
  * Protective mechanisms:
	* Decreased CMRO₂
	* Maintenance of tissue oxygenation
	* Inhibition of excitotoxicity
  * All modern volatiles show some protective properties
* **Postoperative Cognitive Dysfunction:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Causal relationship to volatile anesthetics difficult to establish
  * Animal models link to neuroinflammation and neurotransmitter imbalance
  * Clinical studies show similar outcomes between regional and general anesthesia
  * Monitoring depth may reduce but not eliminate post-op delirium

![CBF Changes](INHALATIONAL_image.png_8645e1.png)

**Bottom Line:** Volatile anesthetics cause dose-dependent reduction in cerebral metabolism with paradoxical increase in cerebral blood flow, potentially increasing intracranial pressure. This uncoupling effect is most pronounced at higher concentrations and may be mitigated with hyperventilation. Concerns regarding cerebral effects are most relevant in neurosurgical patients with impaired intracranial compliance.

## 7.3. CARDIOVASCULAR SYSTEM [PART A/B]

**Volatile anesthetics cause dose-dependent cardiovascular depression through multiple mechanisms.**

* **Hemodynamic Effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Dose-dependent decrease in arterial blood pressure
  * Primary mechanism: Decreased systemic vascular resistance
  * Minimal direct effects on cardiac output at clinical concentrations
  * Comparison at equi-MAC concentrations:
	* Similar blood pressure reduction across modern agents
	* Different heart rate responses (sevoflurane causes minimal change)
  
* **Heart Rate Effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Isoflurane: Reflex tachycardia (from vasodilation)
  * Sevoflurane: Minimal heart rate change up to 1.5 MAC
  * Desflurane:
	* Modest increase at steady-state
	* ⚠️ Significant tachycardia with rapid increases in concentration
  
* **Myocardial Contractility:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * All agents cause dose-dependent depression of contractility
  * Similar effects at equipotent concentrations
  * Despite reductions, maintain adequate response to preload increases
  * Minimal effect on diastolic function
  
* **Cardiovascular Reflexes:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Dose-dependent depression of baroreceptor function
  * Impaired compensatory responses to hypovolemia above 1 MAC
  * Desflurane: Unique sympathoexcitatory effect with rapid increases
	* HR and BP surges last 4-5 minutes
	* Blunted with opioids or α₂-agonists
  
* **Coronary Blood Flow:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * All agents dilate coronary vessels
  * Historical concerns about "coronary steal" with isoflurane
  * Current evidence: No increase in perioperative ischemia with any agent
  * Safe in coronary disease with attention to hemodynamic stability
  
* **Cardioprotection:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Preand post-conditioning effects similar to ischemic preconditioning
  * Mechanisms:
	* Reactive oxygen species signaling
	* Protein kinase C activation
	* KATP channel opening
	* Reduced calcium loading
  * Clinical significance in cardiac surgery debated
	* Some meta-analyses show benefit
	* Recent trials show minimal effect on mortality

![Cardiovascular Effects](INHALATIONAL_image-2.png_91420f.png)

![Cardiovascular Effects](INHALATIONAL_image-3.png_6d0f98.png)

**Bottom Line:** All volatile anesthetics cause dose-dependent vasodilation and myocardial depression, resulting in reduced blood pressure. While specific agents have unique characteristics (sevoflurane's heart rate stability, desflurane's sympathetic activation with rapid increases), cardiovascular effects are generally similar at equipotent concentrations and can usually be managed with careful titration and appropriate volume status.

## 7.4. RESPIRATORY SYSTEM [PART A/B]

**Volatile anesthetics produce significant dose-dependent effects on respiratory function and control.**

* **Ventilatory Effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Dose-dependent decrease in tidal volume
  * Compensatory increase in respiratory rate
  * Net effect: Moderate decrease in minute ventilation
  * Progressive increase in PaCO₂ with increasing depth
  * All agents similar except isoflurane (does not increase rate above 1 MAC)
  
* **Ventilatory Control:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Profound depression of ventilatory responses to:
	* Hypercapnia (dose-dependent)
	* Hypoxemia (extremely sensitive, even at 0.1 MAC)
  * Clinical implications:
	* ⚠️ Particularly important in patients reliant on hypoxic drive
	* Potentially dangerous in recovery period
	* May persist into PACU with residual volatile concentration
  
* **Airway Effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Bronchodilation (all agents)
  * Airway irritancy varies significantly:
	* Sevoflurane: Minimal irritation, suitable for inhalational induction
	* Isoflurane: Moderate irritation
	* Desflurane: Significant irritation, can cause coughing, laryngospasm
  * Inhibition of hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction
	* Minimal clinical effect at usual concentrations
  
* **Respiratory Mechanics:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Decrease in functional residual capacity (FRC)
  * Altered diaphragm and intercostal muscle activity
  * Impaired response to increased respiratory load
  * Decreased mucociliary function
	* ⚠️ Particularly problematic in smokers
  
* **Pulmonary Vascular Resistance:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Minimal direct effect on pulmonary vessels
  * Small vasodilatory effect offset by decreased cardiac output
  * Attenuation of hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction
	* Minimal clinical impact in routine cases
	* May exacerbate V/Q mismatch in one-lung ventilation

![Respiratory Effects](INHALATIONAL_image-4.png_e70b5d.png)

**Bottom Line:** Volatile anesthetics cause dose-dependent respiratory depression, including reduced tidal volume, depressed ventilatory responses to CO₂ and hypoxemia, and altered respiratory mechanics. The bronchodilatory properties are beneficial for reactive airways, but depression of protective reflexes and mucociliary clearance increases the risk of respiratory complications, particularly in vulnerable patients.

## 7.5. HEPATIC EFFECTS [PART A/B]

**Modern volatile anesthetics have minimal direct hepatic effects compared to historically used agents.**

* **General Effects on Liver:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Decrease hepatic blood flow secondary to:
	* Reduced cardiac output
	* Modest splanchnic vasoconstriction
  * Minimal effect on hepatic enzyme function at clinical doses
  * Minimal metabolism compared to older agents like halothane
  
* **Hepatic Protection:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Evidence suggests volatile anesthetics may protect against hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury
  * In CABG surgery, markers of hepatic dysfunction lower with sevoflurane vs. propofol
  * Protective mechanisms similar to cardioprotection:
	* Preconditioning effect
	* Anti-inflammatory actions
	* Improved microcirculation
  
* **Metabolism and Potential Toxicity:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Modern agents undergo minimal hepatic metabolism:
	* Desflurane: 0.02%
	* Isoflurane: 0.2%
	* Sevoflurane: 3.5%
	* Halothane: 20%
  * Metabolic pathways:
	* CYP2E1 primary enzyme for all agents
	* Formation of trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) from desflurane and isoflurane
	* Sevoflurane metabolized to hexafluoroisopropanol (not TFA)
  
* **Historical Context - Halothane Hepatitis:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Type 1: Mild, transient elevation of liver enzymes (common)
  * Type 2: Severe, immune-mediated hepatic necrosis (rare)
	* Antibodies against trifluoroacetylated liver proteins
	* Risk factors: Female, middle-aged, obesity, multiple exposures
  * Modern agents rarely associated with immune-mediated hepatitis
	* Sevoflurane does not form TFA, no antibody formation
	* Isoflurane and desflurane: Minimal metabolism limits antigen formation
  * The C-F bond is stable and minimally metabolized, unlike C-Cl, C-Br, and C-I which are progressively easier to metabolize

**Bottom Line:** Unlike halothane, modern volatile anesthetics have minimal adverse effects on the liver and may even provide hepatoprotection against ischemic injury. Their limited metabolism greatly reduces the risk of immune-mediated hepatitis, though they can still reduce hepatic blood flow as part of their systemic hemodynamic effects.

## 7.6. NEUROMUSCULAR SYSTEM [PART A/B]

**Volatile anesthetics significantly affect skeletal muscle function and interact with neuromuscular blocking agents.**

* **Direct Effects on Skeletal Muscle:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Dose-dependent relaxation of skeletal muscle
	* Direct effect, independent of central effects
	* Significant above 1.0 MAC
	* All potent agents produce this effect
	* N₂O does not affect skeletal muscle relaxation
  * Effect enhanced by 40% in myasthenia gravis
  
* **Potentiation of Neuromuscular Blocking Drugs:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Reduce dosing requirements for NMBs by 30-40%
  * Mechanism:
	* Primarily postsynaptic effect at nicotinic acetylcholine receptors
	* Impaired calcium mobilization
	* Direct effect on muscle membrane excitability
  * Similar potentiation across modern agents
  * Clinical implication: Lower doses of NMBs required with volatile-based anesthesia
  
* **Malignant Hyperthermia:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * All volatile anesthetics are triggers for MH in susceptible patients
  * N₂O and xenon are considered safe in MH-susceptible patients
  * Clinical manifestations:
	* Uncontrolled increase in skeletal muscle metabolism
	* Rapidly rising temperature (up to 1°C every 5 minutes)
	* Increased CO₂ production and oxygen consumption
	* Mixed metabolic/respiratory acidosis
	* Muscle rigidity
	* Rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalemia, arrhythmias
  * Treatment with dantrolene
  * Previously 70% mortality, now 5% with dantrolene

**Bottom Line:** Volatile anesthetics provide direct muscle relaxation and significantly potentiate neuromuscular blocking drugs, reducing NMB dosing requirements. All volatile agents are triggers for malignant hyperthermia in susceptible patients, requiring alternative anesthetic techniques in these individuals.

## 7.7. UTERINE EFFECTS [PART A/B]

**Volatile anesthetics cause dose-dependent relaxation of uterine smooth muscle relevant to obstetric anesthesia.**

* **Effects on Uterine Tone:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Dose-dependent decrease in spontaneous myometrial contractility
  * Inhibition of frequency and amplitude of oxytocin-induced contractions
  * Similar effects across volatile agents
  * Mechanism similar to effects on vascular smooth muscle
  
* **Clinical Implications:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Uterine relaxation/atony at >1 MAC
  * Can delay newborn respiratory onset
  * May increase blood loss during and after cesarean delivery
  * Common technique for cesarean delivery:
	* Low concentrations (0.5-0.75 MAC)
	* Combined with N₂O
	* Minimizes uterine atony and blood loss
  
* **Beneficial Applications:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Brief high concentration useful for:
	* Removing retained placenta
	* Acute uterine relaxation when needed
	* Rapid onset and offset allows precise control
  
* **Effects on Fetal Development:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * No teratogenicity in humans despite animal model concerns
  * Transient depression of neonatal neurobehavioral scores following general anesthesia for cesarean delivery
	* Resolves within 24 hours
	* No long-term neurodevelopmental effects from brief exposure
  
* **Neurodevelopmental Considerations:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Potential for neurotoxicity in rapidly developing brain remains controversial
  * Primate and rodent studies show apoptosis with prolonged exposure
  * Single brief anesthetic exposure (<4 hours) not associated with adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes
  * Multiple or prolonged exposures may have effects on behavior

**Bottom Line:** Volatile anesthetics relax uterine smooth muscle in a dose-dependent manner, which can be either advantageous (removing retained placenta) or problematic (potential bleeding during cesarean delivery). Lower concentrations are typically used in obstetric anesthesia to minimize uterine atony, while briefly higher concentrations can be used therapeutically when uterine relaxation is desired.

## 7.8. RENAL EFFECTS

**Volatile anesthetics have both indirect hemodynamic effects on renal function and direct effects via metabolism.**

* **Hemodynamic Effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Decreased renal blood flow secondary to:
	* Reduced systemic blood pressure
	* Altered renal vascular tone
  * Decreased glomerular filtration rate
  * All agents produce similar effects at equipotent concentrations
  * Generally reversible upon discontinuation
  
* **Metabolism and Fluoride Production:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Agent-specific metabolism rates:
	* Sevoflurane: ~5% metabolism
	* Isoflurane: ~0.2% metabolism
	* Desflurane: ~0.02% metabolism
  * Sevoflurane metabolism produces inorganic fluoride
	* Historically concerning for nephrotoxicity
	* Peak levels typically below toxic threshold (50 μM)
	* Rapid decline in fluoride levels after discontinuation
	* Minimal renal defluorination (unlike methoxyflurane)
  
* **Historical Context - Methoxyflurane:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Extensive metabolism (50-70%)
  * Produced high, sustained fluoride levels
  * Caused concentration-dependent renal injury
  * Presented as renal concentrating defect unresponsive to vasopressin
  
* **Compound A Concerns with Sevoflurane:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Formation during interaction with CO₂ absorbents
  * Nephrotoxic in rats at high concentrations
  * No evidence of human renal injury:
	* Multiple studies using standard and experimental markers of renal function
	* Safe even in patients with pre-existing renal disease
	* No FGF restrictions in most countries

**Bottom Line:** Modern volatile anesthetics have minimal direct renal effects despite transient hemodynamic-mediated reductions in renal blood flow. While sevoflurane metabolism produces fluoride and can generate Compound A with CO₂ absorbents, extensive clinical evidence has not demonstrated nephrotoxicity in humans, even with low-flow anesthesia or in patients with pre-existing renal impairment.

# 8. SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS AND COMPLICATIONS [PART B/C]







## 8.2. CARBON DIOXIDE ABSORBENT INTERACTIONS

### 8.2.1. COMPOUND A

**Sevoflurane undergoes base-catalyzed degradation in carbon dioxide absorbents to form potentially nephrotoxic compounds.**

* **Formation Process:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Sevoflurane breaks down in alkaline CO₂ absorbents
  * Forms vinyl ether called Compound A
  * Enhanced by:
	* Low-flow or closed-circuit systems
	* Warm or desiccated CO₂ absorbents
	* Barium hydroxide lime (Baralyme) > soda lime
  * Compounds A, B, C, D and E all identified when sevoflurane used with CO₂ absorbents
  * Only compounds A and B (which is less toxic) present in sufficient amount for analysis
  * Formation favored with potassium hydroxide rather than sodium-hydroxide-based absorbents, particularly when dry
  * Reaction releases heat and consumes sevoflurane, both readily detectable
  
* **Exposure Levels:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Typical inspired concentrations:
	* 8-24 ppm with soda lime
	* 20-32 ppm with barium hydroxide lime
  * Total exposures up to 320-400 ppm/h studied
  
* **Nephrotoxicity Evidence:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Established nephrotoxicity in rats
	* Proximal tubular necrosis at high concentrations
	* Dose and time-dependent injury
	* Lethal concentration in 50% of rats is 300-400 ppm after 3 hours exposure
	* Human nephrotoxic threshold estimated at 150-200 ppm
  
  * Extensive human studies show no evidence of renal injury:
	* No effect on standard clinical markers (creatinine, BUN)
	* No effect on experimental markers (proteinuria, enzymuria)
	* Safe in patients with pre-existing renal disease
	* Recent studies show even with flow rates of 0.25 L/min for 5 hours, Compound A peaks at <20 ppm with no renal function abnormalities
  
* **Mechanism of Species Difference:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Rats metabolize Compound A to nephrotoxic thiols via β-lyase pathway
  * Humans have minimal renal β-lyase activity
  * Explains lack of human toxicity despite rat findings
  
* **Clinical Management:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * No fresh gas flow restrictions in most countries
  * FDA recommends flows ≥2 L/min with older absorbents
  * Modern calcium hydroxide absorbents produce minimal Compound A

![Compound A Levels](INHALATIONAL_image-5.png_18576f.png)

**Bottom Line:** While sevoflurane degradation produces Compound A, which is nephrotoxic in rats, extensive clinical evidence has not demonstrated renal injury in humans even with low-flow anesthesia. The lack of nephrotoxicity in humans is likely due to differences in renal enzyme pathways. Modern CO₂ absorbents further reduce Compound A formation, making this a theoretical rather than practical concern.

### 8.2.2. CARBON MONOXIDE AND HEAT [PART B/C]

**Desiccated carbon dioxide absorbents can degrade volatile anesthetics, producing carbon monoxide and heat.**

* **Carbon Monoxide Formation:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Occurs when absorbent water content is <5% (normal is 13-15%)
  * Strong bases in absorbent degrade anesthetics containing difluoromethoxy group
  * Agent-specific CO production (dry absorbent at room temperature):
	* Desflurane: Up to 8,000 ppm (highest)
	* Isoflurane: Moderate
	* Sevoflurane: 79 ppm (lowest)
  * Isoflurane, enflurane, and desflurane all possess –CHF₂ groups that may react with dry soda lime (or baralyme), producing carbon monoxide
  * Reports relate to circle systems left with dry gas circulating over a weekend
  
* **Risk Factors:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Desiccation of absorbent:
	* High FGF through absorbent overnight
	* Prolonged gas flow when not in use
  * Absorbent type:
	* Barium hydroxide lime (highest risk)
	* Soda lime (intermediate risk)
	* Modern calcium hydroxide absorbents (minimal risk)
  
* **Clinical Implications:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * CO poisoning:
	* Reported cases with high carboxyhemoglobin levels
	* Delayed emergence
	* Potential end-organ injury
  
  * Exothermic reactions:
	* Sevoflurane produces most heat despite less CO
	* Can reach extremely high temperatures
	* ⚠️ Reported fires and patient injuries
  
* **Prevention Strategies:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Turn off all gas flow when machine not in use
  * Change absorbent regularly per manufacturer guidelines
  * Use modern calcium hydroxide absorbents (eliminates degradation)
  * Machine checkout should include absorbent inspection
  * ⚠️ If absorbent is abnormally warm, replace before using

**Bottom Line:** Desiccated CO₂ absorbents can degrade volatile anesthetics to produce carbon monoxide and extreme heat, with desflurane producing the most CO and sevoflurane the most heat. These reactions can lead to patient injury through CO poisoning or thermal injury. Using modern calcium hydroxide absorbents, regular absorbent changes, and proper machine management significantly reduce these risks.

## 8.3. ANESTHETIC METABOLISM [PART A/B]

**Inhaled anesthetics undergo varying degrees of metabolism, potentially producing toxic metabolites.**

* **Metabolic Pathways:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Primarily via cytochrome P450 enzymes, especially CYP2E1
  * Metabolizes the C-(halogen) bond to release halogen ions (F⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻), which may cause hepatic or renal damage
  * The C–F bond is stable and minimally metabolized, unlike C–Cl, C–Br, and C–I which are progressively easier to metabolize
  * Agent-specific metabolism rates:
	* Halothane: 20%
	* Enflurane: 2%
	* Sevoflurane: 3.5%
	* Isoflurane: 0.2%
	* Desflurane: 0.02%
	* Nitrous oxide: <0.01%
  
* **Metabolic Products:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Trifluoroacetic acid (TFA):
	* From halothane, isoflurane, desflurane
	* Potential antigen for immune-mediated hepatitis
  
  * Inorganic fluoride:
	* From all fluorinated anesthetics
	* Sevoflurane produces most significant levels
	* Historically linked to renal injury (methoxyflurane)
  
  * Compound A and other degradation products:
	* From sevoflurane in CO₂ absorbents
	* Discussed in previous section
  
* **Fluoride-Induced Nephrotoxicity:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Historical problem with methoxyflurane
  * Traditionally linked to:
	* Peak fluoride level >50 μM
	* Duration of elevated fluoride (area under curve)
  
  * Modern agents:
	* Sevoflurane can produce fluoride levels above 50 μM
	* No renal concentrating defect observed
	* Safer profile attributed to:
	  * Rapid decline in fluoride levels
	  * Minimal renal defluorination
	  * Different intrarenal distribution
* **Clinical Implications:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Minimal risk with modern agents at typical durations
  * Extensive monitoring has not demonstrated clinically significant toxicity
  * No dosing or duration limitations for renal concerns with current agents

**Bottom Line:** Modern volatile anesthetics undergo limited metabolism compared to older agents, significantly reducing the risk of toxic metabolite formation. While sevoflurane produces measurable fluoride levels, it has not been associated with the renal injury seen historically with methoxyflurane, likely due to differences in pharmacokinetics and intrarenal handling of fluoride.

## 8.4. ENVIRONMENTAL IMPACT [PART B/C]

**Volatile anesthetics have significant greenhouse gas effects contributing to global warming.**

* **Global Warming Potential:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * All volatile anesthetics have greater GWP than CO₂
  * 100-year GWP compared to CO₂ (reference = 1):
	* Desflurane: 2,540 (highest impact)
	* Isoflurane: 510
	* Sevoflurane: 130
	* Nitrous oxide: 265 (298 in supplementary source)
  
  * Atmospheric lifetime:
	* Desflurane: 14 years (longest among volatiles)
	* Isoflurane: 3.2 years
	* Sevoflurane: 1.1 years (shortest among volatiles)
	* Nitrous oxide: 114 years
  
* **Environmental Impact Characteristics:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Radiative Efficiency (RE): Measure of influence on incoming and outgoing atmospheric energy
	* RE for isoflurane and desflurane are similar
	* Due to its five-fold atmospheric lifespan, desflurane has much larger GWP100
  * Desflurane is least potent volatile agent, requiring more for anesthesia, further worsening its profile
  * Globally, desflurane makes up ~80% of CO₂e of all volatile agents despite being vented in roughly equal quantities by mass
  * Nitrous oxide has largest CO₂e, but <4% of N₂O emissions are from medical use (majority agricultural)
  * N₂O is currently the dominant ozone depleting substance expected to remain so throughout the 21st century
  * Only isoflurane has ozone depleting potential due to chlorine, but minimal due to short atmospheric lifetime
* **Comparative Impact:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Driving equivalence (1 MAC-hour at 1 L/min FGF):
	* Desflurane: 190 car miles
	* Isoflurane: 8 car miles
	* Sevoflurane: 4 car miles
  
  * N₂O additional concerns:
	* Ozone layer destruction
	* Both greenhouse gas and ozone depleter
  
* **Reduction Strategies:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Reduce FGF during maintenance phase
	* Target 0.7-1 L/min FGF when possible
	* Maintain higher flows only during induction/emergence
  
  * Prefer lower-impact agents when clinically appropriate
	* Consider sevoflurane instead of desflurane when either is suitable
	* Limit N₂O use
  
  * Other approaches:
	* Use adjuvants (opioids, regional techniques) to reduce volatile requirements
	* Consider TIVA for very long cases
	* Ensure proper scavenging and waste gas management

![Environmental Impact Strategies](INHALATIONAL_INHALATIONAL_Untitled.png_5565e1.png_c82282.png)

**Bottom Line:** Volatile anesthetics, particularly desflurane, have substantial global warming potential far exceeding that of CO₂. Reducing environmental impact requires a combination of lower fresh gas flows during maintenance, preferential use of agents with lower impact when clinically appropriate, and use of adjuvant techniques to minimize volatile agent requirements.

## 8.5. SIGNS OF LIGHT ANESTHESIA

**Recognizing signs of light anesthesia is critical for preventing awareness and patient movement.**

* **Clinical Indicators:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Autonomic Signs:**
	* Tearing (lacrimation)
	* Sympathetic activation: Dilated pupils, sweating
	* Increase in heart rate or blood pressure by 20% above baseline
	* ⚠️ Note: These do not reliably predict awareness
  
  * **Movement Indicators:**
	* Coughing or bucking
	* Patient movement
	* Swallowing
  
  * **Respiratory Patterns:**
	* Irregular breathing
	* Breath-holding
	* Tachypnea
  
  * **Monitoring Parameters:**
	* Signs of consciousness on EEG monitor (BIS, Sedline)
	* Entropy or spectral edge frequency changes
	* End-tidal anesthetic concentration below MAC-awake
  
* **Response to Light Anesthesia:**
  * Increase anesthetic depth:
	* Increase volatile agent concentration
	* Consider bolus of IV agent (propofol)
	* Assess need for additional analgesics
  
  * Address contributing factors:
	* Surgical stimulation intensity
	* Inadequate neuromuscular blockade if using
	* Equipment issues (vaporizer empty, disconnection)
  
  * Reassess anesthetic plan:
	* Verify end-tidal concentrations
	* Consider adding adjuvants to reduce MAC requirements
	* Evaluate for tolerance (chronic substance use)

**Bottom Line:** Multiple clinical signs may indicate light anesthesia, including autonomic responses, movement, and respiratory pattern changes. However, autonomic signs alone are unreliable predictors of awareness. Monitoring end-tidal anesthetic concentration and brain function monitors provides additional objective data to guide appropriate depth of anesthesia.

# 9. MALIGNANT HYPERTHERMIA [PART B/C]







## 9.2. DEFINITION AND PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

**Malignant hyperthermia is a potentially fatal hypermetabolic crisis triggered by susceptible patients' exposure to volatile anesthetics or succinylcholine.**

* **Definition:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Life-threatening genetic hypermetabolic muscle disease
  * Occurs when susceptible patients are exposed to triggering agents
  * Characterized by uncontrolled increase in muscle metabolism and intracellular calcium
  
* **Underlying Defect:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Abnormally increased Ca²⁺ levels in skeletal muscle
  * Results in sustained muscle contraction
  * Calcium pump attempts clearance → increased ATP usage
  * Leading to hypermetabolism and heat production
  
* **Genetic Basis:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * 80% of cases: RYR-1 receptor mutation (affects calcium release channel in sarcoplasmic reticulum)
  * Autosomal dominant inheritance with variable penetrance and expression
  * Autosomal recessive forms also described (especially with King-Denborough syndrome)
  * At least 6 chromosomal loci identified, >80 genetic defects associated with MH
  * Linked to several rare musculoskeletal disorders:
	* Central core disease
	* King-Denborough syndrome
	* Multi-minicore myopathy
  
* **Pathophysiologic Sequence:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  1. Trigger exposure causes mutant RYR-1 receptor to remain open
  2. Unregulated calcium release from SR into intracellular space
  3. Sustained muscle contraction and increased Ca-ATPase activity
  4. Heat generation, accelerated metabolism, increased oxygen consumption
  5. CO₂ production, lactic acidosis, and rhabdomyolysis
  6. Multiple system involvement: hyperkalemia, arrhythmias, DIC, organ failure

**Bottom Line:** Malignant hyperthermia is a pharmacogenetic disorder in which volatile anesthetics or succinylcholine trigger uncontrolled calcium release in skeletal muscle via abnormal ryanodine receptors. This leads to hypermetabolism, hyperthermia, acidosis, and potentially fatal complications if not promptly recognized and treated.

## 9.3. EPIDEMIOLOGY AND RISK FACTORS

**Malignant hyperthermia is a rare but potentially fatal complication with specific demographic and genetic risk patterns.**

* **Incidence:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Rare overall: 1:15,000 pediatric vs. 1:40,000 adult anesthetics
  * Geographic variation (highest in upper Midwest US)
  * More common in young males
  * Almost no cases in infants; few in adults >50 years
  
* **Risk Factors:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Personal or family history of MH
  * Pediatric age
  * Comorbid myopathies (central core disease, multi-minicore disease)
  * History of unexplained fevers, cramps, weakness
  * History of exercise-induced rhabdomyolysis
  * Caffeine intolerance
  * Trismus on induction (precedes 15-30% of MH cases)
  
* **Triggering Agents:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * All halogenated inhalational anesthetics:
	* Sevoflurane
	* Desflurane
	* Isoflurane
	* Halothane
	* Enflurane
  * Succinylcholine
  * ⚠️ Not triggered by:
	* Nitrous oxide
	* Xenon
	* Propofol
	* Etomidate
	* Barbiturates
	* Opioids
	* Non-depolarizing neuromuscular blockers
  
* **Important Considerations:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * MH may occur on a patient's 2nd exposure to triggers
  * Nearly 50% of MH episodes occurred in patients with at least one prior uneventful anesthetic
  * All closely related family members considered susceptible in absence of testing

**Bottom Line:** Malignant hyperthermia occurs most frequently in young males with specific genetic predispositions. All volatile anesthetics and succinylcholine are triggers, while other anesthetic agents including nitrous oxide are safe. Prior uneventful anesthesia does not rule out MH susceptibility, as many cases occur on subsequent exposures to triggering agents.

## 9.4. CLINICAL PRESENTATION

**Malignant hyperthermia presents with a characteristic constellation of symptoms reflecting hypermetabolism and muscle involvement.**

* **Early Warning Signs:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Masseter muscle rigidity (trismus)
	* Most common if succinylcholine used
	* Not all cases with trismus develop MH
	* If other muscles become rigid, association with MH is stronger
  
  * Increased end-tidal CO₂ (earliest, most sensitive sign)
	* Persistent despite increased minute ventilation
	* Rapid rise without other explanation
  
  * Tachycardia and hypertension
	* Reflect sympathetic surge
	* Often precede temperature rise
  
* **Progression of Symptoms:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  1. **Hypermetabolism:**
	 * Increased CO₂ production and metabolic acidosis
	 * Increased O₂ consumption (decreased ScvO₂)
	 * Mixed respiratory/metabolic acidosis
	 * Body will compensate with tachypnea
  
  2. **Hyperthermia:**
	 * A late sign of MH
	 * Temperature can rise 1-2°C every 5 minutes
	 * Can exceed 42°C in fulminant cases
  
  3. **Cell Damage & Rhabdomyolysis:**
	 * Leakage of K⁺, myoglobin, CK
	 * Dark-colored urine due to myoglobinuria
	 * Muscle rigidity
  
  4. **Secondary Systemic Manifestations:**
	 * Hyperkalemia → arrhythmias
	 * Acute renal failure from myoglobinuria
	 * DIC, hemorrhage, compartment syndrome
	 * Cerebral edema
	 * Whole body edema from increased cellular permeability
* **Differential Diagnosis:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Neuroleptic malignant syndrome
  * Thyroid storm
  * Pheochromocytoma
  * Serotonin syndrome
  * Sepsis
  * Inadequate anesthesia with sympathetic response
  * CO₂ absorber exhaustion or breathing circuit malfunction

![MH Differential Diagnosis](INHALATIONAL_INHALATIONAL_Untitled_1.png_29d0ae.png_0f9ad0.png)

**Bottom Line:** The clinical presentation of MH typically begins with unexplained hypercarbia, tachycardia, and muscle rigidity, followed by hyperthermia, acidosis, and evidence of rhabdomyolysis. Early recognition of these signs is critical, as the syndrome can rapidly progress to multi-organ failure. An unexplained, persistent rise in end-tidal CO₂ is the most reliable early indicator.

## 9.5. TREATMENT [PART B/C]

**Prompt recognition and immediate treatment are essential for successful management of a malignant hyperthermia crisis.**

* **Immediate Actions:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Discontinue triggering agents:**
	* Turn off all volatile anesthetics
	* Switch to non-triggering anesthetics (propofol, opioids, non-depolarizing NMBs)
	* Hyperventilate with 100% oxygen at high flows
  
  * **Call for help:**
	* Activate MH protocol
	* Bring MH cart/dantrolene
	* Assign team roles
  
  * **Administer dantrolene:**
	* Initial dose: 2.5 mg/kg IV
	* Repeat 1 mg/kg as needed up to 10 mg/kg total
	* Mix with sterile water (not saline)
	* Give through large-bore IV (pH 9.5, irritant)
	* Takes approximately 15 minutes to work
  
* **Supportive Management:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Active cooling:**
	* Cold IV fluids
	* Surface cooling (ice packs to axilla, groin)
	* Lavage of open body cavities if applicable
	* Target temperature <38.5°C
  
  * **Treat metabolic derangements:**
	* Hyperkalemia: Calcium, insulin/glucose, bicarbonate
	* Acidosis: Hyperventilation, sodium bicarbonate
	* Arrhythmias: Standard ACLS protocols (avoid calcium channel blockers)
  
  * **Prevent complications:**
	* Monitor for DIC (consider clotting studies)
	* Maintain urine output >1-2 mL/kg/hr with fluids and diuretics
	* Monitor CK every 6-12 hours for 48 hours
	* Prepare for potential compartment syndrome
  
* **Ongoing Management:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Transfer to ICU for at least 24 hours
  * Continue dantrolene 1 mg/kg IV q4-6h for at least 24 hours
  * Monitor for recrudescence (can occur up to 48 hours later)
  * Serial labs: ABG, electrolytes, CK, coagulation studies, myoglobin

![MH Treatment](INHALATIONAL_INHALATIONAL_Untitled_2.png_e7c0f1.png_e50fd7.png)

**Bottom Line:** Successful management of MH requires immediate discontinuation of triggering agents, prompt administration of dantrolene, aggressive cooling, and supportive care addressing metabolic derangements. With early recognition and proper treatment, mortality has decreased from 70% to less than 5%. ICU monitoring for at least 24 hours is essential to detect recrudescence.

## 9.6. PREVENTION AND MANAGEMENT IN SUSCEPTIBLE PATIENTS [PART B/C]

**Safe anesthesia for MH-susceptible patients requires specific preparation and technique modifications.**

* **Preoperative Considerations:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Identify susceptible patients through history
  * Document family history, previous reactions, genetic testing results
  * Consider preoperative dantrolene controversial (not routinely recommended)
  * Schedule case early in the day if possible
  
* **Machine Preparation:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Ideally, use dedicated "clean" machine without volatile capability
  * If not available, prepare standard machine:
	* Remove vaporizers if possible
	* Replace circuit and CO₂ absorbent
	* Flush with high O₂ flow (10 L/min) for at least 20 minutes
	* Consider activated charcoal filters
	* Follow manufacturer guidelines for specific machine model
  
* **Anesthetic Technique:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Safe agents:**
	* Propofol
	* Etomidate
	* Barbiturates
	* Benzodiazepines
	* Opioids
	* Non-depolarizing neuromuscular blockers
	* Nitrous oxide
	* Local anesthetics
  
  * **Avoid:**
	* All volatile anesthetics
	* Succinylcholine
  
  * **Recommended approaches:**
	* Total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA)
	* Regional/neuraxial techniques when appropriate
	* Combined techniques
  
* **Intraoperative Monitoring:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Standard monitors plus:
	* Core temperature monitoring
	* Consider arterial line for long/complex cases
	* End-tidal CO₂ with particular attention to unexpected rises
  
* **Postoperative Care:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Standard PACU monitoring
  * Observe for signs of delayed MH
  * Consider longer observation for complex cases
  * MH cart should remain available

**Bottom Line:** Safe anesthetic management of MH-susceptible patients involves machine preparation to remove volatile agents, use of non-triggering anesthetics (typically TIVA or regional techniques), vigilant monitoring, and readiness to treat MH should it occur despite precautions. With proper preparation, these patients can safely undergo anesthesia with minimal additional risk.

## 9.7. SUSCEPTIBILITY TESTING [PART B/C]

**Various testing methods can identify MH susceptibility in patients with suspected risk.**

* **Caffeine-Halothane Contracture Test (CHCT):** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Procedure:**
	* Takes fresh muscle biopsy (typically vastus lateralis)
	* Exposes muscle to caffeine and halothane
	* Measures contracture response
  
  * **Performance:**
	* Gold standard for MH testing
	* High sensitivity (>97%)
	* Specificity 80-93%
	* 10-20% false positive rate but zero false negative rate
  
  * **Limitations:**
	* Invasive procedure requiring muscle biopsy
	* Limited availability (specialized centers)
	* Requires viable tissue (must be performed promptly)
	* Costly and resource-intensive
  
* **Molecular Genetic Testing:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Procedure:**
	* Blood sample for DNA analysis
	* Screens for RYR1 and CACNA1S mutations
  
  * **Performance:**
	* Low sensitivity (screens only 20-30% of recognized mutations)
	* High specificity (rule-in but cannot rule-out)
  
  * **Indications:**
	* Patients with positive CHCT
	* Relatives of known MH-susceptible individuals
	* Patients with highly suspicious MH episode
	* Patients with associated myopathies (e.g., central core disease)
  
* **Other Testing Methods:**
  * In vitro contracture test (IVCT) - European protocol
  * Calcium-induced calcium release (CICR) rate
  * 4-chloro-m-cresol contracture test
  * Currently under investigation: metabolomics, proteomics approaches
* **Special Considerations:**
  * All first-degree relatives of known MH-susceptible individuals should be considered susceptible until proven otherwise
  * Previous uneventful anesthesia does not exclude susceptibility
  * Patient with one negative genetic test may still be MH susceptible due to unidentified mutations

**Bottom Line:** The caffeine-halothane contracture test remains the gold standard for diagnosing MH susceptibility despite its invasive nature. Genetic testing can identify specific mutations but cannot rule out susceptibility due to limited sensitivity. Testing should be performed at specialized centers and is recommended for patients with suspicious reactions and family members of known MH-susceptible individuals.

# 10. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS







## 10.2. INDUCTION OF ANESTHESIA

**Inhaled anesthetics can be used for induction of anesthesia, particularly when intravenous access is difficult or in pediatric patients.**

* **Advantages of Inhalational Induction:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Avoids need for IV access initially
  * Preserves spontaneous ventilation
  * Patients regulate their own depth (respiratory depression limits overdosing)
  * Gradual transition from consciousness to unconsciousness
  * Particularly useful in:
	* Pediatric patients
	* Patients with difficult IV access
	* Patients with intellectual disabilities
	* Anticipated difficult airway
  
* **Agent Selection:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Sevoflurane:**
	* Agent of choice for inhalational induction
	* Non-pungent, pleasant odor
	* Well-tolerated without coughing/laryngospasm
	* Clinical unconsciousness in ~60 seconds with 8% concentration
	* Laryngeal mask placement possible within 2 minutes
  
  * **Alternatives:**
	* Halothane (historically used, less available now)
	* Isoflurane/desflurane not suitable due to pungency and airway irritation
  
* **Technique Options:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Vital capacity induction:**
	* Patient takes single deep breath and holds
	* Rapid induction but requires cooperation
	* Typically with 8% sevoflurane
  
  * **Incremental induction:**
	* Start with low concentration, gradually increase
	* More gradual, better tolerated by anxious patients
	* Less cooperation required
  
  * **Modifications:**
	* Adding N₂O accelerates induction minimally
	* Pretreatment with benzodiazepines improves acceptance
	* ⚠️ Opioid pretreatment may cause apnea
  
* **Patient Acceptance:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * High success rate (>90% in studies)
  * May be preferred by patients with needle phobia

**Bottom Line:** Inhalational induction with sevoflurane provides a valuable alternative to intravenous induction, particularly in pediatrics and difficult IV access scenarios. Its pleasant odor, low airway irritancy, and ability to maintain spontaneous ventilation make it well-suited for this purpose. Technique can be adapted based on patient cooperation and clinical circumstances.

## 10.3. MAINTENANCE OF ANESTHESIA

**Volatile anesthetics are the most common agents used for maintenance of general anesthesia worldwide.**

* **Advantages of Volatile Maintenance:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Easily administered via inhalation
  * Readily titrated to effect
  * End-tidal monitoring provides real-time assessment of tissue levels
  * High safety ratio for preventing awareness
  * Effective regardless of age or body habitus
  * Beneficial properties:
	* Skeletal muscle relaxation
	* Preservation of cardiac output
	* Cerebral blood flow maintenance
	* Organ protection from ischemic injury
  
* **Agent Selection Considerations:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Sevoflurane:**
	* Intermediate solubility
	* Rapid onset/offset
	* Minimal airway irritation
	* Good hemodynamic stability
	* Popular for outpatient/pediatric cases
  
  * **Desflurane:**
	* Lowest solubility, fastest emergence
	* Particularly advantageous for:
	  * Long cases requiring quick wake-up
	  * Obese patients
	  * Outpatient procedures
	* Limitations: Airway irritancy, sympathetic activation with rapid increases
  
  * **Isoflurane:**
	* Most potent (lowest MAC)
	* Most cost-effective
	* Longer context-sensitive half-time
	* Suitable for procedures without rapid emergence requirements
  
* **Balanced Anesthesia Approach:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Combining volatile anesthetics with:
	* Opioids for analgesia
	* Neuromuscular blockers for muscle relaxation
	* Benzodiazepines for amnesia
	* Regional techniques for site-specific analgesia
  
* **Limitations and Drawbacks:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Lack of intrinsic analgesic effects (except N₂O)
  * Association with PONV
  * Potential neurobehavioral concerns in neonates
  * Environmental impact (greenhouse effect)

**Bottom Line:** Volatile anesthetics remain the foundation of maintenance anesthesia due to their ease of administration, titratability, and real-time end-tidal concentration monitoring. Agent selection should consider case duration, patient characteristics, and specific clinical requirements, with each agent offering unique advantages for different scenarios.

## 10.4. EMERGENCE CONSIDERATIONS

**The pharmacokinetics of volatile anesthetics significantly impact emergence characteristics and recovery.**

* **Pharmacokinetic Determinants of Emergence:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Blood:gas partition coefficient (primary determinant)
  * Duration of anesthesia
  * Agent-specific recovery profiles:
	* Desflurane: Fastest emergence regardless of duration
	* Sevoflurane: Fast emergence, slightly slower than desflurane
	* Isoflurane: Emergence speed more affected by duration
  
* **Techniques to Optimize Emergence:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Progressive downtitration:**
	* Gradual reduction of concentration in anticipation of surgical end
	* Guided by processed EEG monitor or clinical experience
	* Can produce fast wake-ups even with isoflurane
  
  * **End of case management:**
	* Increase fresh gas flow (4-6 L/min) to accelerate washout
	* Use 100% oxygen (unless contraindicated)
	* Consider adding N₂O toward end to speed emergence via second gas effect
	* ⚠️ If N₂O used, switch to 100% O₂ for several minutes before extubation to prevent diffusion hypoxia
  
* **Clinical Considerations:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Case Duration Effects:**
	* Long cases (>4 hours): More significant differences between agents
	* Short cases (<1 hour): Less difference between modern agents
	* Desflurane shows greatest advantage in prolonged cases
  
  * **Patient-Specific Factors:**
	* Obesity: Greater advantage of low-solubility agents
	* Elderly: May be more sensitive to residual effects
	* Patients with OSA: Rapid emergence may be beneficial
  
  * **Emergence-Related Concerns:**
	* Pediatric emergence delirium (more common with sevoflurane)
	* Coughing/bucking with desflurane
	* PONV risk with all agents

![Recovery Time by Duration](INHALATIONAL_image-6.png_ec7537.png)

**Bottom Line:** The speed of emergence from volatile anesthetics depends primarily on their blood solubility, with desflurane offering the fastest recovery followed by sevoflurane and isoflurane. The advantage of less soluble agents becomes more pronounced with longer procedures and in obese patients. Techniques such as progressive downtitration and increased fresh gas flow can optimize emergence regardless of the agent chosen.

## 10.5. SIGNS OF LIGHT ANESTHESIA

**Recognizing insufficient anesthetic depth is crucial for preventing awareness and patient movement.**

* **Clinical Indicators:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Tearing (lacrimation)
  * Sympathetic activation:
	* Dilated pupils
	* Sweating
	* Increase in HR or BP by 20% above baseline (not reliable predictor of awareness)
  * Coughing or bucking
  * Patient movement
  * Signs of consciousness on EEG monitor (Bispectral Index or Sedline)
* **Response to Light Anesthesia:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Increase anesthetic depth
  * Assess need for additional analgesics
  * Consider contributing factors such as surgical stimulation intensity
  * Verify end-tidal concentrations

**Bottom Line:** Multiple clinical signs may indicate light anesthesia, including autonomic responses and movement. Promptly increasing anesthetic depth and addressing contributing factors is essential to prevent awareness and ensure patient comfort and safety.

# 11. NON-ANESTHETIC MEDICAL GASES [PART A/B]







## 11.2. OXYGEN [PART A/B]

**Oxygen is a fundamental medical gas used to prevent and treat hypoxemia.**

* **Manufacture and Storage:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Manufactured by fractional distillation of air or by oxygen concentrator (zeolite mesh adsorbs N₂)
  * Stored as gas in black cylinders with white shoulders at 137 bar
  * Also stored as liquid in vacuum insulated evaporator (VIE) at 10 bar and -180°C
  * VIE must be located outside and rests on three legs; two are hinged while the third serves as weighing device
* **Physiochemical Properties:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Boiling point: -182°C
  * Critical temperature: -119°C
  * Critical pressure: 50 bar
* **Clinical Uses:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Prevention and treatment of hypoxemia
  * Should be prescribed like other therapeutic interventions
* **Measurement Methods:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Gas mixture: Mass spectrometer, paramagnetic analyzer, fuel cell
  * Blood dissolved: Clarke electrode, transcutaneous electrode, pulse oximetry
  * In vitro blood samples: Bench or co-oximetry
* **Physiological Effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Cardiovascular:**
	* Improves cardiovascular parameters when correcting hypoxemia
	* Prolonged 100% O₂ administration directly reduces cardiac output slightly
	* Causes coronary artery vasoconstriction
	* Decreases pulmonary vascular resistance and pulmonary artery pressure
  
  * **Respiratory:**
	* Mild respiratory depression in healthy subjects at high concentrations
	* ⚠️ In patients truly dependent on hypoxic drive, even modest concentration may prove fatal
* **Toxicity:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Caused by free radicals
  * CNS effects: Anxiety, nausea, seizures when partial pressure exceeds 200 kPa
  * Alveolar-capillary membrane undergoes lipid peroxidation causing regions of lung collapse
  * Neonates susceptible to retrolental fibroplasia (possibly from vasoconstriction of developing retinal vessels)

**Bottom Line:** Oxygen is a fundamental medical gas for preventing and treating hypoxemia. While it improves oxygenation, prolonged high-concentration administration can have adverse effects including CNS symptoms at high partial pressures, pulmonary damage with prolonged use, and potentially fatal respiratory depression in patients reliant on hypoxic drive.

## 11.3. NITRIC OXIDE [PART A/B]

**Nitric oxide is an endogenous signaling molecule used therapeutically to treat pulmonary hypertension and improve ventilation-perfusion matching.**

* **Synthesis:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Manufactured as by-product in nitric acid synthesis
  * Potential contaminant in nitrous oxide cylinders
  * In vivo synthesized from L-arginine in process catalyzed by nitric oxide synthase (NOS)
  * Two forms of NOS:
	* Constitutive: Normally present in endothelial, neuronal, skeletal muscle, cardiac tissue, platelets
	* Inducible: Seen only after exposure to endotoxin or certain cytokines
* **Physiological Effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Cardiovascular:**
	* Vasodilator tone in small arteries and arterioles depends on continuous supply of locally synthesized NO
	* Shear stresses increase NO production (flow-dependent vasodilation)
	* Endothelium-derived NO inhibits platelet aggregation
	* In septic shock, overproduction causes hypotension and capillary leakage
  
  * **Respiratory:**
	* Provides important basal vasodilator tone in pulmonary and bronchial vessels
	* When inhaled (up to 40 ppm), reduces V/Q mismatching in ARDS
	* Reduces pulmonary hypertension in neonates
	* No effect on systemic circulation (rapid inactivation within red blood cells)
	* Affinity for hemoglobin 1,500 times that of CO
	* No bronchodilator properties
  
  * **Other Systems:**
	* Immune: Synthesized in macrophages/neutrophils to combat pathogens
	* Hematological: Inhibits platelet aggregation
	* Neuronal: Widely distributed throughout CNS (arousal, pain perception, programmed cell death)
	* Peripheral neurons containing NO control regional blood flow in corpus cavernosum
* **Clinical Applications:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **UK Guidelines for Use in Adult ICU:**
	* Indications: Severe ARDS (PaO₂ < 12 kPa with FiO₂ = 1) or right-sided cardiac failure
	* Maximum dose: 40 ppm (use minimum effective dose)
	* Equipment: Synchronized inspiratory injection system optimal; continuous delivery must use calibrated flowmeter
	* Monitoring: Chemiluminescence or electrochemical analyzers (accurate to 1 ppm)
	* Methemoglobinemia monitoring: Check before and after starting, then daily
	* Exposure limits: Environmental NO should not exceed 25 ppm for 8 hours
	* Contraindications: Methemoglobinemia, bleeding diathesis, intracranial hemorrhage, severe left ventricular failure
* **Related Medications:**
  * N-monomethyl-L-arginine (L-NMMA): Guanidino substituted analogue of L-arginine that inhibits NOS
  * Sodium nitroprusside and organic nitrates (e.g., glyceryl trinitrate): Exert effect by spontaneous release of NO or metabolism to NO in smooth muscle cells

**Bottom Line:** Nitric oxide is an important signaling molecule with therapeutic applications primarily in treating pulmonary hypertension and improving ventilation-perfusion matching in ARDS. Its pulmonary selectivity when inhaled makes it valuable for treating pulmonary hypertension without systemic effects, though careful monitoring for methemoglobinemia is required.

## 11.4. HELIUM [PART A/B]

**Helium is an inert gas used primarily to reduce airway resistance in upper airway obstruction due to its low density.**

* **Physical Properties:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Inert gas
  * Presented as Heliox (79% He, 21% O₂) in brown cylinders with white shoulders
  * Also available as 100% helium in brown cylinders at 137 bar
  * Does not support combustion
  * Key characteristic: Lower density than both air and oxygen
  * Specific gravity compared to other gases:
	* Helium: 0.178
	* Heliox: 0.337
	* Oxygen: 1.091
	* Air: 1.000
* **Clinical Applications:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Airway Obstruction:**
	* During turbulent flow, velocity is higher with Heliox
	* Reduces work of breathing and improves oxygenation in upper airway obstruction
	* Particularly useful for:
	  * Tumors causing airway obstruction
	  * Stridor
	  * Upper airway edema
	  * Post-extubation stridor
  
  * **Diving Medicine:**
	* Used for deep water diving to avoid nitrogen narcosis
  
  * **Physical Effects:**
	* Lower density produces higher frequency vocal sounds (squeaky voice)

**Bottom Line:** Helium's primary clinical use is in reducing airway resistance during turbulent flow conditions due to its low density. This makes it valuable in treating upper airway obstruction, where it reduces work of breathing and improves gas exchange. It's typically administered as Heliox (79% He, 21% O₂) for respiratory applications.

## 11.5. CARBON DIOXIDE [PART A/B]

**Carbon dioxide is a colorless gas with multiple physiological effects and specific clinical applications.**

* **Physical Properties:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Colorless gas with pungent odor at high concentrations
  * Stored as liquid at 51 bar at 20°C in grey cylinders (C = 450 liters up to E = 1,800 liters)
  * Boiling point: -78.5°C
  * Critical temperature: 31°C
  * Critical pressure: 73.8 bar
* **Clinical Uses:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Insufflating gas for laparoscopic procedures
  * Occasionally used to stimulate respiration following general anesthesia
  * Used in cryotherapy
* **Physiological Effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Cardiovascular:**
	* Increases heart rate, blood pressure, cardiac output through sympathetic stimulation
	* Dilates coronary arteries
	* Arrhythmias more likely with raised PaCO₂
  
  * **Respiratory:**
	* Respiratory center and peripheral chemoreceptors respond to raised PaCO₂
	* Increases minute volume and causes bronchodilation
	* PaCO₂ above 10 kPa may result in respiratory depression
  
  * **Central Nervous System:**
	* As PaCO₂ rises, cerebral blood flow and intracranial pressure increase
	* Beyond 10 kPa, narcosis may ensue

**Bottom Line:** Carbon dioxide has multiple clinical applications including laparoscopic insufflation and respiratory stimulation. Its significant physiological effects include sympathetic activation with cardiovascular stimulation, respiratory stimulation at moderate levels but depression at high levels, and increased cerebral blood flow and ICP with rising partial pressure.

## 11.6. CARBON DIOXIDE ABSORBENTS [PART A/B]

**Carbon dioxide absorbents remove CO₂ from breathing circuits to prevent rebreathing in circle systems.**

* **Composition:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * UK: Mainly sodium-hydroxide-based (sodalime)
  * US: Primarily potassium-hydroxide-based (baralyme)
* **Chemical Reaction:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Three-step process:
	1. H₂O + CO₂ → H₂CO₃
	2. H₂CO₃ + 2NaOH → Na₂CO₃ + 2H₂O
	3. Na₂CO₃ + Ca(OH)₂ → CaCO₃ + 2NaOH

* **Interactions with Volatile Anesthetics:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Carbon Monoxide Production:**
	* Occurs when absorbent water content is <5% (normal 13-15%)
	* Strong bases degrade anesthetics containing difluoromethoxy group
	* Agent-specific production:
	  * Desflurane: Up to 8,000 ppm (highest)
	  * Isoflurane/enflurane: Moderate
	  * Sevoflurane: Lowest CO production
  
  * **Compound A Production:**
	* Sevoflurane breaks down in alkaline absorbents to form Compound A
	* Enhanced by low-flow, desiccated absorbents, and barium hydroxide lime
	* Compound A nephrotoxic in rats but no human nephrotoxicity demonstrated
  
  * **Exothermic Reactions:**
	* Sevoflurane produces most heat despite less CO
	* Can reach extremely high temperatures
	* Reported fires and patient injuries
* **Prevention of Adverse Reactions:**
  * Turn off all gas flow when machine not in use
  * Change absorbent regularly per manufacturer guidelines
  * Use modern calcium hydroxide absorbents with lower strong base content
  * Machine checkout should include absorbent inspection
  * If absorbent is abnormally warm, replace before using

**Bottom Line:** Carbon dioxide absorbents remove CO₂ to allow rebreathing in circle systems through a series of chemical reactions. When desiccated, they can interact with volatile anesthetics to produce carbon monoxide (especially with desflurane) or Compound A (with sevoflurane), and generate significant heat. Regular changes, maintaining hydration, and using modern formulations reduce these risks.
