# 1. FUNDAMENTAL PRINCIPLES OF ELECTRICITY

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|Part A: Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C4 Trauma Resuscitation and Crisis Management|Part B: C4]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Patient Care and Procedural Skills|Part C: Patient Care]]

## 1.1. BASIC PHYSICS TERMS AND ELECTRICAL ANALOGS

**Electrical principles have direct analogs to familiar mechanical concepts, providing a useful framework for understanding.**

* **Force and Motion**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]]:
  * In mechanics: Force imparted on an object tends to induce motion (e.g., pushing a car).
  * In electricity: Electromotive force (voltage) moves electrical charges through a circuit.
* **Work and Energy**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]]:
  * In mechanics: Energy imparted to an object is called work (e.g., pushing a car uphill creates gravitational potential energy).
  * In electricity: Electrical energy is transferred when voltage drives current through a circuit.
* **Power**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]]:
  * In mechanics: Rate at which work is done (pushing a car more quickly requires more power).
  * In electricity: Rate at which electrical energy is transferred, measured in watts.
* **Electric Current (I)**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]]:
  * Flow of electrons through a conductor past a given point per unit of time    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]] 
  * Propelled by driving force (voltage or potential difference)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]] 
  * Measured in amperes (A)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]] 
  * One ampere represents flow of 6.24 × 10^18 electrons (one coulomb of charge) per second    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]] 
  * Two primary types:
	* **Direct Current (DC)**: Current flows in one direction (e.g., battery)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]] 
	* **Alternating Current (AC)**: Flow of electrons reverses direction at regular intervals    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]] 
* **Potential Difference or Voltage (V)**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]]:
  * Electrical force that drives electrical current    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]] 
  * When 1 ampere current dissipates 1 watt of power between two points, potential difference between those points is 1 volt    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]] 
  * In UK, mains power is supplied at 240 volts (RMS)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]] 

## 1.2. OHM'S LAW AND CIRCUIT PROPERTIES

**Ohm's law describes the relationship between voltage, current, and resistance in an electrical circuit.**

* **Fundamental Equation**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]]:
  * V = I × R
  * V is voltage (volts)
  * I is current (amperes)
  * R is resistance (ohms)
* **Rearranged Form**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]]:
  * I = V/R
  * Current is proportional to voltage and inversely proportional to resistance
  * Higher voltage increases current; higher resistance decreases current
* **Power Equation**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]]:
  * P = V × I = (I × R) × I = I² × R
  * P is power (watts)
  * Electrical work = power × time (watt-seconds or joules)
  * Larger units include kilowatt-hours (used for electrical energy storage capacity)
* **Electrical Resistance (R)**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]]:
  * Resistance along a conductor to flow of electrical current (Direct Current - DC)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]] 
  * Not dependent on frequency of current    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]] 
  * Measured in ohms (Ω)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]] 
  * Substances with high resistance are insulators; those with low resistance are conductors    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]] 

## 1.3. ELECTRICAL CIRCUITS WITH OSCILLATING VOLTAGES

**The behavior of electrical circuits depends significantly on how electrical energy is delivered.**

* **Direct Current (DC)**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]]:
  * Involves constant unidirectional flow of charge
  * Electromotive force always in same direction
  * Example: Battery-powered devices
* **Alternating Current (AC)**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]]:
  * Involves circuit voltage that oscillates between positive and negative
  * In US, standard frequency is 60 Hz (cycles per second)
  * In UK, standard frequency is 50 Hz (cycles per second)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]] 
  * Charge carriers move "forward" and "backward" in the circuit
  * Standard for mains power delivery
* **Mains Power Supply**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]] 
  * Current travels from power station to substation where it is converted to main power voltage by a transformer
  * From substation, current travels in two conductors: the live and neutral wires
  * Live wire is at a potential of 240 V
  * Neutral is connected to earth at the substation, keeping potential approximately same as earth
  * Live wire carries potential to equipment, neutral wire returns current to source, completing circuit

## 1.4. IMPEDANCE, CAPACITANCE, AND INDUCTANCE

**All real-world electrical circuits have some degree of capacitance and inductance, which create frequency-dependent behaviors.**

* **Impedance (Z)**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]]:
  * General form of Ohm's law: V = I × Z
  * Dynamic resistance that varies with time and current frequency
  * Encompasses both resistance and reactance (capacitive and inductive)
  * Sum of forces that oppose movement of electrons in an AC circuit    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]] 
  * Measured in ohms (Ω)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]] 
  * Includes resistors, capacitors, and inductors    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]] 
  * Impedance through capacitors and inductors related to frequency (Hz) of AC - called reactance - reactance is the opposition of flow to AC caused by capacitance and inductance in a circuit (X)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]] 
* **Capacitance**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]]:
  * Two conductive plates with insulating material between them
  * Stores electrical energy when charged
  * Resists current flow at low frequencies; transmits at high frequencies
  * Relevant in the context of electrolyte solutions (saline acts as a capacitor)
  * Measure of ability of conductor or system to store electrical charge    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]] 
  * Unit for capacitance is the farad    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]] 
  * With AC, plates change polarity at current frequency, causing electrons to move back and forth between plates    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]] 
  * Impedance of capacitor = distance between plates/(current frequency × plate area)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]] 

![A capacitor consists of two parallel conductors separated by an insulator](ELECTRICAL_image-1.png_85860c.png)

* **Inductance**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]]:
  * Example: Helical coil of conductive wire
  * Resists rapid changes in voltage
  * Resists current flow at high frequencies; transmits at low frequencies
  * The inverse behavior of capacitance
  * Occurs when electrons flow in wire resulting in magnetic field around wire    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]] 
  * If wire coiled around iron core (as in transformer), magnetic field can be very powerful    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]] 
  * Blocks AC (high reactance) but passes DC (low resistance)

![Capacitance vs Inductance frequency behavior](electrical_capacitance_vs_inductance_f4n8x1_1734163960000.png)
* **Universality of Electrical Physics**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Basic Physical and Mathematical Principles|ⓘ]]:
  * Electrical behavior extends beyond wires
  * Current, electric fields, magnetic fields interact with surroundings
  * Relevant to safety considerations when unexpected current pathways develop

**Bottom Line:** Understanding fundamental electrical principles is crucial for anesthesia providers as they form the basis for electrical safety in the OR. Current behavior is especially important to grasp, as it relates directly to electrical shock hazards and patient safety. The principles of impedance, capacitance, and inductance explain how different components in medical equipment function and interact with frequencies.

## 1.5. OTHER COMPONENTS

- Transformer: 
	- Device which transfers electrical energy between 2 or more circuits through electromagnetic induction
	- Used to step up or step down the voltages of AC
	- Consists of 2 inductors wound around the same core
	- Current changes in 1 circuit can induce current in the 2d circuit due to coupling effects of the magnetic field
- Earth
	- System of electrical safety where there is an electrical connection to ground
	- Protects people from effects of faulty insulation in electrically powered equipment
- Diode (Rectifier): Allows current to flow in 1 direction only
- Transducer: Device that changes 1 form of energy into another, normally into an electric signal for interpretation and recording
- Amplifier
	- Converts a small signal to a larger signal output
	- Can be electrical or non-electrical (Bourdon gauge)
	- Bandwidth = range of freq that an amplifier works over most efficiently
	- Gain = Difference in size of the input signal and the amplified signal
	- Noise = amplification of unwanted signals
	- Signal = measure of performance of the system
	- Noise ratio is the amount of noise introduced compared to the signal
	- Can act as filters via
		- Differential amplifiers (aka operational amplifiers)
		- Bandwidth filter, notch, high/low pass filters
- Differential amplifiers
	- Measure the difference between 2 separate inputs
	- Signals that is the same at both sources eg 50Hz from main interference will be eliminated
	- Remaining signal will be amplified
	- Common mode rejection: Common mode rejection ratio = ratio of unwanted signal to the wanted signal i.e. CMRR of 10,000 to 1 = interference signal at both electrodes needs to be 10,000x greater than desired signal applied at 1 electrode to achieve same output
- Filters
	- Bandwidth: Filter and amplifies a certain frequency range
	- High or low pass
		- High pass filter - eliminates all currents with a low frequency, allowing high frequency current to pass through
		- Low pass filter - eliminates high frequency current and allow low frequency current to be analysed
	- Notch: Rejects particular frequency e.g. 50Hz
# 2. ELECTRICAL SHOCK AND SAFETY

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|Part A: Safety]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C4 Trauma Resuscitation and Crisis Management|Part B: C4]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Systems-Based Practice|Part C: Systems-Based Practice]]

## 2.1. DEFINITION AND TYPES

**Hazardous electrical shock is a potentially dangerous flow of electricity through a human body.**

* **Definition**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Electrical shock occurs when an individual becomes part of the electric circuit    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Person must be in contact with circuit at two points with potential difference between them for electrons to flow    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Can happen with both AC and DC, though DC typically requires higher current to cause ventricular fibrillation    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
* **Two Primary Types**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * **Microshock**: Small current flows that can become physiologically disruptive (e.g., inducing ventricular fibrillation)
	* Tissue generally not injured
	* Current causes disruption of normal physiological function
	* Patients more susceptible when they have conductive pathways to electrically sensitive tissues
  
  * **Macroshock**: Larger current flows that dissipate energy as heat, causing thermal injury
	* Occurs when current exceeds skin resistance
	* Causes direct tissue damage
* **Factors Affecting Severity**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Current density within affected tissues
  * Frequency of the current
  * Duration of the current
  * Path of current flow through the body
  * Size of current (amperes) per unit area    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Current pathway through body - current through chest may cause ventricular fibrillation or respiratory muscle tetany    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Duration of contact - shorter contact causes less damage    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Type of current (AC/DC) and frequency - 50 Hz is almost most lethal frequency as myocardium is most susceptible at this frequency    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 

## 2.2. CAUSES OF ELECTRIC SHOCK

**Electric shock occurs when a person becomes part of an electrical circuit with sufficient voltage to drive current through tissues.**

* **Unintended Contact**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Person contacts a voltage source
  * Normal contact with equipment in setting of equipment fault
  * Fault that causes harmless current flows to become harmful
  * Can happen with faulty high-leakage current or direct connection to main power source    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Main power source frequency (50 Hz) can cause muscle spasm or tetany    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
* **Current Density Considerations**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Current has three-dimensional flow from source to sink with broad distribution within body
  * Example: Electrosurgery blade (small contact area) vs. return pad (large contact area)
  * Small contact areas create high current density capable of burning tissue
  * Current density is amount of current per unit area of tissues    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Body current diffusion tends to be in all directions    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Larger current or smaller application area means higher current density    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
* **Proximity to Sensitive Tissues**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Current applied directly to heart is much more dangerous than same current at skin
  * 100 mA at skin required to induce ventricular fibrillation
  * Only 0.1 mA required when applied directly to myocardium
  * For heart, 100 mA (100,000 μA) required to cause ventricular fibrillation when applied to body surface    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Only 0.05-0.1 mA (50-100 μA) required when applied directly to myocardium (microshock)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
* **Skin Impedance**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Main impedance is skin resistance, which can vary from hundreds of thousands to one million ohms
  * Skin impedance can be reduced in inflamed areas or when covered with sweat
  * According to Ohm's law, current flow is inversely proportional to impedance, so high impedance reduces current flow
  * Main power source supplies are maintained at constant voltage (240 V in UK)

## 2.3. CURRENT THRESHOLDS AND HUMAN EFFECTS

**The human body responds to electrical current in predictable ways that vary by current magnitude.**

* **Perception Threshold**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * 1 mA (0.001 A) at 60 Hz: Threshold of sensory perception
  * Important because microshock can occur below this threshold without provider awareness
* **Key Current Thresholds (60 Hz, 1-second contact)**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:

| Current | Effect                                                                                                                                                          |
| ----------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Macroshock** |                                                                                                                                                                 |
| 1 mA (0.001 A) | Threshold of perception, tingling pain    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]                                                                                                                 |
| 5 mA (0.005 A) | Accepted as maximum harmless current intensity, pain    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]                                                                                                   |
| 10-20 mA (0.01-0.02 A) | "Let-go" current before sustained muscle contraction                                                                                                            |
| 15 mA | Tonic muscle contraction and pain    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]                                                                                                                      |
| 50 mA (0.05 A) | Pain, possible fainting, mechanical injury; heart and respiratory functions continue. Tonic contraction of respiratory muscles and respiratory arrest    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]  |
| 75-100 mA (0.075-0.1 A) | Ventricular fibrillation will start, but respiratory center remains intact    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]                                                              |
| 6,000 mA (6 A) | Sustained myocardial contraction, followed by normal heart rhythm; temporary respiratory paralysis; burns if current density is high                            |
| 1,000 mA (1 A) | Extensive burns and charring    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]                                                                                                            |
| **Microshock** |                                                                                                                                                                 |
| 100 μA (0.1 mA) | Ventricular fibrillation                                                                                                                                        |
| 10 μA (0.01 mA) | Recommended maximum 60-Hz leakage current                                                                                                                       |

![Current thresholds and physiological effects](electrical_current_thresholds_scale_r9j2w5_1734163960000.png)

* **Effect of Frequency**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Lower frequency (50-100 Hz) easily stimulates sustained muscle contraction
  * Very high frequency (>500,000 Hz, as in electrosurgery) generally does not stimulate muscle contraction
  * High-frequency currents don't sufficiently separate charges to actuate voltage gates on cell membranes
  * As frequency increases, stimulation effect decreases but heating effect increases    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * With frequency over 100 kHz, heating is the only effect    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 

![Effect of frequency on muscle stimulation and heating](electrical_frequency_physiological_effects_t5h7y3_1734163960000.png) 

## 2.4. MICROSHOCK HAZARDS

**Microshock poses a unique risk to vulnerable patients and requires specific preventive measures.**

* **Definition and Risk Factors**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Small imperceptible current flows that can become physiologically disruptive
  * Major risk: Conductive pathways to the heart (pacing wires, saline-filled catheters)
  * Current threshold: As low as 100 μA (0.1 mA)
  * Below perception threshold (1 mA), making it difficult to detect
* **Protection Mechanisms**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Functioning ground wire provides low-resistance pathway for leakage current
  * Proper grounding of all equipment in patient care areas
	  * At home, power supply is grounded and appliances used may be grounded or ungrounded
	  * But in OT, power supply is ungrounded and appliances should always be grounded
  * Isolation between high-voltage power and sensor connections
  * Wearing non-conductive gloves when handling central/PA catheters

![The electrically susceptible patient is protected from microshock by intact equipment ground wire](ELECTRICAL_image-3.png_68c535.png)

![A broken equipment ground wire results in a significant hazard](ELECTRICAL_image-4.png_9335d7.png)

* **Risk Mitigation Behaviors**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Avoid handling a patient's central/PA catheter while touching plugged-in electrical equipment
  * Be aware of personal grounding (wet shoes, standing in puddles)
  * Maintain high-quality equipment ground connections
  * Extra vigilance with electrically susceptible patients

## 2.5. METHODS TO REDUCE RISK OF ELECTROCUTION

**Various strategies can minimize electrical risks in healthcare environments.**

* **General Measures**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Adequate maintenance and testing of electrical equipment at regular intervals
  * Wearing antistatic shoes and having antistatic flooring in theaters
  * Ensuring patient is not in contact with earthed objects
* **Equipment Design**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * All medical equipment used in theaters must comply with British Standard 5724 and International Electro-technical Committee (IEC) 60601-1
  * These standards describe protection methods and degree of protection
* **Safety Features**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Equipotentiality - ensures all metalwork is at same potential to prevent current flow between devices
  * Isolated circuits - current not allowed to flow between electrical source and earth
  * Circuit breakers (COELCB) - detect leakage current and break circuit
  * Proper maintenance of equipment - checking earth leakage (<0.5 mA) and earth continuity (<0.1 Ω)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
* **Coupling Mechanisms**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Body can form part of electrical circuit through:
	* Capacitance coupling (acting as plate of capacitor without direct contact)
	* Resistive coupling (acting as electrical resistance)
  * Resistive coupling can occur through:
	* Faulty equipment allowing contact with live wire
	* Leakage current due to imperfect insulation

**Bottom Line:** Electrical shock hazards in healthcare settings involve both macroshock (perceptible, tissue-damaging) and microshock (imperceptible, potentially fatal in vulnerable patients). Understanding current thresholds and practicing appropriate precautions is essential, especially with patients who have conductive pathways to the heart. Multiple safety systems including proper grounding, isolation, and circuit protection are essential for minimizing these risks.

# 3. ELECTRICAL POWER SYSTEMS

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|Part A: Equipment and Safety]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C4 Trauma Resuscitation and Crisis Management|Part B: C4]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Systems-Based Practice|Part C: Systems-Based Practice]]

## 3.1. GROUNDED ELECTRICAL POWER

**A grounded electrical system has a direct connection between the neutral line and earth ground.**

* **System Architecture**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Neutral line connected to earth ground at circuit breaker box
  * Three wires in modern outlets: hot (black), neutral (white), ground (green/bare)
  * Hot line carries driving voltage from power utility
  * Neutral line completes the circuit back to source
  * Ground line serves as safety backup

![Modern electrical outlet with ground wire](ELECTRICAL_image-5.png_799ae6.png)

* **Ground Connection Purpose**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Prevents excessive charge buildup on neutral line
  * Maintains neutral line at same potential as earth
  * Provides safe path for current in case of fault
  * Acts like overflow drain in reservoir during unusual events

![In a neutral grounded power system](ELECTRICAL_image-6.png_909c54.png)

* **Function of Ground Wire**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Connected to casing of electrical device
  * No current flows under normal circumstances
  * Provides safe, low-resistance pathway if hot line contacts casing
  * Allows high current to flow, triggering circuit breaker/fuse
  * Prevents shock hazard to users touching the device
  * Protective earth connected to device casing via green-and-yellow lead in three-pin plug    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Reduces casing to zero potential, though doesn't guarantee complete protection    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]] 

![When a faulty piece of equipment without an equipment ground wire](ELECTRICAL_image-7.png_06b683.png)

![When a faulty piece of equipment containing an equipment ground wire](ELECTRICAL_image-8.png_897a8e.png)

* **Grounded vs. Ungrounded Devices**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * **Grounded Devices (Class I)**: Single-insulated with ground wire (three-prong plug)
  * **Ungrounded Devices (Class II)**: Double-insulated with no ground wire (two-prong plug)
	* Two sources of insulation: internal wires and nonconductive casing
	* ⚠️ Note: Electrolyte solution intrusion can bypass insulation in OR setting
* **Ground Types** 
  * **Protective Earth**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]] 
	* Equipment has its own earth connection via green-and-yellow lead in three-pin plug
	* Connected to external case of equipment, reducing potential to zero
	* Provides some protection but not guaranteed
  * **Functional Earth**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]] 
	* Part of main circuit where current is returned via neutral wire back to substation
	* Required for proper functioning of electrical equipment
	* Not primarily a safety feature
	* In older equipment, same symbol may have denoted protective earth
  * **Additional Protective Earth**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]] 
	* Extra protection against electric shock in cases of single-fault condition
  * **Equipotentiality**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]] 
	* Ensures all metalwork is normally at or near zero voltage
	* Under fault conditions, all metalwork increases to same potential
	* No current flows during simultaneous contact between two metal appliances
	* Provides protection by joining all metal appliances and connecting to earth

### 3.1.1. COMMON SAFETY FEATURES

**Circuit breakers, fuses, and GFCIs protect electrical systems from overloads and ground faults.**

* **Circuit Breakers/Fuses**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Protect wiring from overheating due to excess current flow
  * Trip when current exceeds capacity (typically 15A or 20A in OR)
  * Important for fire prevention
  * 💡 OR circuits easily overwhelmed by multiple high-current devices

* **Ground Fault Circuit Interrupters (GFCIs)**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Continuously measure current flow on hot and neutral lines
  * Disconnect circuit if they detect imbalance (current leaking to ground)
  * Typically trigger at 5-6 mA leakage within 25-40 milliseconds
  * Used in wet areas with good electrical conduction to earth ground
  * ⚠️ Limited use in ORs as they cut power without warning
* **Current-Operated Earth Leakage Circuit Breakers (COELCB)**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Also known as earth trip or residual circuit breakers
  * Consist of transformer with core having equal windings of live and neutral wire
  * Connected via third winding to relay coil operating circuit breaker
  * Under normal conditions, magnetic fluxes cancel out (current in live/neutral wires equal)
  * During fault (excessive leakage current), current in live/neutral differs, creating magnetic field
  * Induces current in third winding, causing relay to break circuit
  * Very sensitive - small current (e.g., 30 mA) for short period triggers, reducing electrocution risk

![A GFCI electrical outlet with integrated test and reset buttons](ELECTRICAL_image-10.png_73be65.png)

### 3.1.2. POWER RECEPTACLES

**Power receptacles come in various standardized configurations to ensure safe connections.**

* **NEMA Standards**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * North American receptacle standards specified by National Electrical Manufacturers Association
  * Designed to prevent accidental connections between incompatible power sources
  * Most common in healthcare: NEMA 5-15 and 5-20
* **OR Receptacles**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Typically NEMA 5-20 (20 amp capacity)
  * Often installed "ground pin up" for safety
  * Usually labeled with circuit identification
  * ⚠️ Remember: Multiple outlets on same circuit can be overloaded even if each device draws less than circuit capacity
* **Hospital-Grade Receptacles and Plugs**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Denoted by green dot
  * Higher durability specifications
  * Ground pin longer to ensure first contact
  * Solid pins and thicker plug bodies to reduce risk of breaking
  * Power strips with splash-resistant covers for OR use

![A hospital-grade plug and outlet](ELECTRICAL_image-11.png_5e9666.png)

## 3.2. GROUND-ISOLATED ELECTRICAL POWER

**ORs typically use isolated power systems (IPS) to enhance electrical safety.**

* **Purpose and Prevalence**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * ORs account for 40% of all hospital electrical accidents despite small footprint
  * Higher risk environment due to:
	* Mission-critical electrical equipment
	* Frequent use of electrolyte solutions
	* Staff focused on patient care rather than electrical safety
* **How Isolation Works**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Neutral line not tied to earth ground ("floating ground")
  * Same 120V differential between lines
  * No potential circuit path between hot line and earth ground
  * Implemented using line isolation transformer

![Isolated power panel showing circuit breakers, LIM, and isolation transformer](ELECTRICAL_image-13.png_002d75.png)

* **Line Isolation Transformer**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Equal coil windings on each side (no voltage transformation)
  * Power transferred via magnetic field - no direct electrical connection
  * Creates electrical separation between primary (source) and secondary (OR) circuits
  * Within secondary circuit, terms "hot" and "neutral" become "line 1" and "line 2"
  * Used to isolate individual equipment    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Has two coils insulated from each other    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Main power source circuit is earthed, while patient's circuit is not (floating)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Current flowing through main power source coil creates electromagnetic field, inducing current in patient's coil    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * To complete patient's circuit, both wires must be connected - contact with only one wire won't complete circuit even if person is earthed    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]] 

![In the OR, the isolation transformer converts grounded power to ungrounded system](ELECTRICAL_image-14.png_e8ea87.png)

* **Safety Benefits**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Protects grounded person from shock with single point of contact to circuit
  * Allows faulty equipment to be used without immediate power loss
  * Prevents single fault from causing circuit breaker trip that might disable critical life support
  * 💡 No short circuit occurs between line and ground in an IPS, unlike in grounded systems

![A safety feature of the isolated power system](ELECTRICAL_image-15.png_f48da1.png)

![A faulty piece of equipment plugged into the isolated power system](ELECTRICAL_image-16.png_8d48d9.png)

* **"Second Fault" Concept**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * First fault converts IPS to effectively grounded system
  * Second fault can then cause shock hazard or power interruption
  * Understanding this concept crucial for managing LIM alarms

### 3.2.1. LINE ISOLATION MONITOR (LIM)

**The LIM measures impedance between the IPS and earth ground to assess isolation integrity.**

* **Function**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Essentially an ohmmeter between earth ground and each line of the IPS
  * Reports impedance as "hazard current" in milliamperes
  * Indicates how much current could flow in case of a ground fault
  * Typical alarm thresholds: 5 mA for modern LIMs, 2 mA for older devices

![The capacitance in AC power lines results in small leakage currents](ELECTRICAL_image-17.png_44c015.png)

![[Pasted image 20250715193939.png]]

![Meters of various LIMs calibrated in milliamperes](ELECTRICAL_image-18.png_a632fd.png)

* **Contributors to Hazard Current**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * **Equipment Faults**: Sudden unintended connection between IPS line and ground
  * **Cumulative Leakage**: Multiple properly functioning devices with small leakage currents
  * ⚠️ No way to distinguish between these causes when alarm triggers without observing the before/after readings

### 3.2.2. MANAGING LIM ALARMS

**LIM alarms require appropriate response based on clinical context and patient needs.**

* **Best Practices**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * **For Non-Essential Equipment**: Disconnect offending device, ensure alarm clears, have device serviced later
  * **For Essential Equipment**: Can safely use for duration of surgery with understanding that IPS is no longer isolated
  * **After First Fault**: Reduce risk by avoiding connecting more devices to affected circuit unless necessary
  * **Remember**: Single ground fault not inherently dangerous but removes extra layer of protection
* **Ground Wire Importance**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Broken ground wire creates "silent first fault" scenario
  * Device can have faulty connection to casing without LIM detection
  * Many devices have metal peg near power connection for ground continuity testing
* **GFCIs vs. LIMs**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * GFCIs cut power without warning - potential patient safety issue
  * LIMs warn without interrupting power - allows clinical decision-making
  * NFPA 99 code requires single-outlet GFCIs if used in ORs
  * Case report: Multiple devices including anesthesia machine lost power when single GFCI tripped

**Bottom Line:** Understanding electrical systems is crucial for anesthesia providers. Isolated power systems provide an additional layer of safety in ORs by preventing single faults from causing immediate power loss to critical equipment and reducing shock hazards to patients and providers. Proper response to LIM alarms requires considering both electrical safety and patient care needs.

## 4.1. MEDICAL EQUIPMENT CLASSIFICATIONS

**Medical electrical equipment is classified according to protection methods and degree of protection.**

### 4.1.1. EQUIPMENT CLASSES (I, II, III) 

* **Class I Equipment**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Offers basic protection where live, neutral, and earth wires don't contact each other
  * Secondary protection: touchable parts (metal case) insulated from live electricity and connected to earth via plug
  * Fuses positioned on live and neutral supply in equipment, plus third fuse on live wire in UK plugs
  * Fuse melts during faults (deteriorating insulation or short circuit) to protect user
  * Some tiny non-fault leakage currents always present as insulation is never 100% perfect
  * Requires faultless earth connection for protection to function
* **Class II Equipment**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Also called double-insulated equipment
  * Has double or reinforced insulation protecting accessible parts
  * No need to earth this equipment
  * Power cable has only 'live' and 'neutral' conductors with single fuse
* **Class III Equipment**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Doesn't need electrical supply exceeding 25V AC or 60V DC
  * Voltage used called safety extra low voltage (Self)
  * Although doesn't cause electrical shock, still risk of microshock
  * May contain internal power source or connect to main power supply via adaptor or Self transformer
  * Power input port designed to prevent accidental connection with another cable

### 4.1.2. EQUIPMENT TYPES (B, BF, CF) 

* **Type B Equipment**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * May be class I, II, or III main source-powered equipment or have internal power source
  * Designed with low leakage currents even in fault conditions (0.5 mA for Class I, 0.1 mA for Class II)
  * Max leakage current must not exceed 100uA
  * Can connect to patient externally or internally but not considered safe for direct heart connection
  * May include defibrillator protection
* **Type BF Equipment**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Similar to Type B but part applied to patient is isolated from all other equipment parts using isolated (floating) circuit
  * Isolation ensures allowable leakage current under single-fault conditions not exceeded even when 1.1 times rated voltage applied
  * Maximum AC leakage: 0.1 mA under normal conditions, 0.5 mA under single-fault condition
  * Safer than Type B but still not safe enough for direct heart connection
  * May include defibrillator protection
* **Type CF Equipment**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Can be Class I or II equipment powered by mains or internal source
  * Considered safe for direct connection to heart
  * Uses isolated circuits
  * No risk of electrocution by leakage currents (allows 0.05 mA per electrode for Class I, 0.01 mA per electrode for Class II)
  * Leakage current must not exceed 10uA
  * Provides high degree of protection against shock
  * Used in ECG leads, pressure transducers, and thermodilution computers
  * May include defibrillator protection

![Medical equipment protection types B, BF, CF](electrical_equipment_types_bf_cf_q8k4n6_1734163960000.png)

### 4.1.3. INTERNATIONAL STANDARDS AND MARKINGS 

* **Warning Symbols**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * **Attention**: Black triangle on yellow background with exclamation mark means no standardized symbol for hazard
  * **High Voltage**: Warning of risk of electrocution
* **Protection Ratings**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * **Drip proof, splash proof, water tight**: Based on nature and use of equipment
  * **Anaesthetic-proof (AP)**: Standards based on ignition energy for most flammable ether/air mixture
	* Can use within 5-25 cm of gas escaping from breathing system
	* Temperature must not exceed 200°C
	* Less stringent requirement
  * **Anaesthetic-proof Category G**: Standards based on ignition energy for most flammable ether/oxygen mixture
	* Can use within 5 cm of gas escaping from breathing system
	* Temperature must not exceed 90°C
	* More stringent requirement (energy less than 1 mJ)
* **Conformity Markings**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * **UKCA**: UK conformity mark that supersedes European CE mark
  * **CE**: Conformity met according to Council of Europe Directive 93/42/EEC for medical devices

## 4.2. SAFETY FEATURES WITHIN MONITORS

**Patient monitoring devices require special safety features since they directly connect to patients.**

* **Design Challenges**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Conductive wiring links patient's skin to electrical device
  * Requires isolation between sensor wiring and high-voltage internal electronics
* **Isolation Methods**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * High resistance between sensors and power supply voltage
  * Optical isolators (optocouplers): Convert electrical signal to optical then back to electrical
  * Creates non-conductive break in the electrical pathway
* **Connector Safety**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * FDA reported 24 incidents (5 fatal) of children/infants electrocuted by ECG/apnea monitor wires (1985-1994)
  * 1997 FDA rule: Only female connectors allowed on patient side
  * Male connectors on monitor side must be housed within protective casing
  * Reduces risk from exposed conductors

## 4.3. ELECTROSURGERY (DIATHERMY)

**Electrosurgical units (ESUs) provide surgical benefits but present hazards requiring careful management.**

### 4.3.1. COMPONENTS AND MECHANISM OF ACTION 

* **Components**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Active or live electrode
  * Patient's neutral or passive plate
  * Diathermy case where frequency and voltage can be adjusted
  * Isolating capacitor between patient's plate and earth
* **Mechanism of Action**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Heat generated when current passes through resistor, depending on current density (current per unit area)
  * Heat generated (H) proportional to square of current (I²) divided by area (A) (H = I²/A)
  * Smaller area generates greater heat
  * Current density around active electrode can reach 10 A/cm²
  * Large amount of heat at diathermy forceps tip (small size, high current density)
  * No heat or burning at patient's plate due to large surface area (low current density)
  * Uses high-frequency current (radiofrequency range) of 500,000-1,000,000 Hz
  * High-frequency current behaves differently from 50 Hz current:
	* Passes across precordium without causing ventricular fibrillation
	* Low tissue penetration without exciting contractile cells
  * Isolating capacitor has low impedance to high-frequency current (diathermy) but high impedance to 50 Hz current, protecting against shock

![Surgical diathermy showing isolating capacitor between patient's plate and earth](ELECTRICAL_pdf_ELECTRICAL.pdf_p17_img1_44a388.jpeg)

* **Types of Diathermy**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * **Earth-free circuit diathermy**: Patient, forceps tip, and patient plate not connected to earth
	* Reduces patient burn risk
	* Known as floating patient circuit
  * **Cutting diathermy**: Uses continuous sine waveform at 250-3000V
  * **Coagulation diathermy**: Uses modulated waveform
	* Achieved by fulguration or desiccation
  * **Blended modes**: Variable mixture of cutting and coagulation
  * **Bipolar diathermy**: No patient plate required
	* Current flows through one side of forceps, through patient, back through other side
	* Same current density and heating effect at both electrodes
	* Usually lower power with good coagulation but less cutting ability
	* Frequently used in neurosurgery or ophthalmic surgery

### 4.3.2. HAZARDS AND SAFETY FEATURES

* **Basic Principles**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * High-frequency AC voltage applied across two electrodes in contact with patient
  * Current flows through patient from one electrode to other
  * Cutting blade electrode vs. harmless return electrode differentiated only by size/current density
  * Radio frequency AC (500,000-1,000,000 Hz) used to avoid stimulating cellular action potentials
* **Hazards and Complications**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Fire risk when oxygen and combustible materials present
  * Interference with monitors and implanted electrical devices
  * Burns at return pad electrode or other metal contact points
  * Rare cases of ventricular fibrillation despite theoretical safety
  * Problems with reduced contact area between plate and patient    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
	* Risk of burns at plate site if area reduced
	* If plate completely detached, current might flow through any earth contact point
	* Modern machines won't function under these conditions
  * Electrical interference with monitoring devices - solved with electrical filters    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Interference with cardiac pacemakers - can damage circuits or change programming    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
	* More hazardous with cutting than coagulation diathermy
	* Modern pacemakers protected against diathermy
  * Fires/explosions from sparks igniting flammable materials (skin cleaners, bowel gas)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 

![A properly applied ESU return plate](ELECTRICAL_image-20.png_ef94a3.png)

![An improperly applied ESU return plate](ELECTRICAL_image-21.png_3245b3.png)

* **Return Pad Safety**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Improperly placed/partially adherent pads create high current density and burns
  * Modern ESUs use "contact quality-monitoring" return pads
  * Two separate conductive zones allow impedance monitoring
  * Alarms if impedance too high (indicating poor contact)
  * ⚠️ Return pad should never be in contact with conductive casing of ESU
* **Patient Grounding Considerations**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Patient contact with earth ground increases risk during ESU use
  * Modern ESUs are isolated from earth ground for safety
  * Pre-1968 ESUs connected return lines to earth ground (more dangerous)
  * ESU current may still return via alternate pathways despite isolation
* **Metal Jewelry Considerations**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * No published case reports of burns caused by metal piercings
  * Laboratory testing shows no significant heating of piercings between ESU and return pad
  * Only direct contact/proximity with active electrode (5-10mm) causes significant heating
  * 💡 While removing easily removable jewelry is prudent, trauma from forceful removal may pose greater risk than leaving it in place when distant from surgical site
* **Pacemaker and AICD Interference**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Interference with pacemaker sensing during ESU use is common
  * Mitigation strategies:
	* Avoid ESU entirely if possible
	* Use bipolar rather than unipolar ESU
	* Place ESU use and return pad below umbilicus
	* Place pacer in nonsensing mode (magnet may achieve this)
  * AICDs may misinterpret ESU noise as tachydysrhythmia and deliver shock
  * Consider disabling defibrillation functionality temporarily
  * Have external defibrillator available when AICD is disabled
## 4.4. ELECTROMAGNETIC INTERFERENCE

**Electronic devices can interfere with medical equipment through electromagnetic radiation.**

* **Types of Interference**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Direct current flows across device's sensing electrodes
  * Electromagnetic radiation (intentional or incidental)
  * EMI is difficult to study - laboratory detection may not reflect clinical significance
* **Research Findings**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Hayes study (1997): 20% of tests showed detectable interference, 6.6% clinically significant in worst-case scenarios
  * von Olshausen study (2005-2013): 1.87% detectable EMI event rate per patient per year, 0.27% clinically significant
  * Modern devices less susceptible than older generations
* **Frequency Considerations**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Devices more sensitive to low-frequency EMI (two-way radio, high-voltage power switching)
  * Higher-frequency EMI less problematic
  * Modern wireless standards use higher frequencies (WiFi: 2.4→5GHz, mobile: 900MHz→40GHz)
  * EMI risk lower with modern wireless communication devices than in past decades
* **Practical Recommendations**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * FDA recommends keeping mobile devices at least 6 inches from cardiac implantable electronic devices
  * Normal use of wireless devices unlikely to cause clinically significant EMI
  * Be aware of magnetic components in modern devices (can trigger magnet-responsive functions)
  * 💡 Clinically significant EMI is rare but possible under abnormal conditions

## 4.5. STATIC ELECTRICITY 

**Static electricity buildup poses fire and explosion risks in operating theaters.**

* **Preventive Measures**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Electrical impedance of equipment should allow leakage of charge to earth but not be so low as to risk electrocution/burns
  * Specific measures include:
	* Using carbon-containing rubber for tubing, reservoir bags, and face masks (black with yellow labels)
	* Staff wearing antistatic footwear
	* Trolleys with conducting wheels
	* Maintaining relative humidity above 50% with room temperature above 20°C
  * Significance questionable with modern anesthesia as flammable agents (ether, cyclopropane) are no longer used

## 4.6. EMERGENCY BACKUP POWER

**Healthcare facilities require reliable backup power for patient safety during utility power loss.**

* **Regulatory Requirements**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * NFPA 99/110 codes require backup power within 10 seconds of utility loss
  * Typically implemented as fuel-powered generators
  * Sometimes supplemented with batteries/flywheel systems for instantaneous coverage
  * Must be able to run at least 2 days without utility power
  * Regular testing required
* **Power Distribution**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Red outlets denote emergency power coverage
  * Mission-critical equipment should always use red outlets
  * Non-essential equipment should use regular outlets to preserve capacity
* **Contingency Planning**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Natural disasters and construction mishaps common causes of power loss
  * Construction mishaps particularly problematic as they may break internal connections
  * Hospitals need contingency plan for power loss in every anesthesia location
  * Plan should include scenarios where backup power isn't available
* **Equipment Considerations**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Battery-powered light sources essential
  * Anesthesia machines with battery backup
  * Provider education about power loss management
  * 💡 Even electronically controlled anesthesia machines can be manually ventilated after power/battery failure

## 4.7. REGULATIONS AND BUILDING CODES

**Regulatory frameworks establish minimum standards for electrical safety in healthcare settings.**

* **Key Regulatory Bodies**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) - building codes
  * NFPA 99 (healthcare facilities), 101 (life safety), 110 (emergency power)
* **Historical Evolution**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Pre-1984: Isolated power required due to flammable anesthetics
  * 1984 revision: Requirement removed as flammable anesthetics phased out
  * Current standards based on "wet procedure location" classification
* **Current Requirements**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * ORs presumed "wet procedure locations" unless risk assessment determines otherwise
  * Wet procedure locations require either isolated power or ground fault circuit interrupters
  * If GFCIs used, each outlet should have its own separate GFCI (2015 revision)
* **Practical Implications**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Ongoing debate about necessity of IPSs in ORs
  * Stakeholders include cost-conscious administrators and safety-focused clinicians
  * Anesthesia providers should understand implications of classifying ORs as "dry"
  * 💡 As stakeholders directly affected by electrocution risk, anesthesia providers should engage in these regulatory discussions

**Bottom Line:** Electrical safety in healthcare requires specialized equipment design, awareness of electromagnetic interference risks, reliable backup power, and adherence to appropriate building codes. Medical equipment is classified according to protection methods (Class I, II, III) and degree of protection (Type B, BF, CF) with specific safety standards. Modern electrosurgical units provide important surgical capabilities but require careful management to prevent electrical hazards, burns, and fire risks.

## 5.1. FIRE TRIANGLE/TETRAHEDRON

**Understanding the components necessary for fire is essential for prevention strategies.**

* **Fire Triangle Components**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * **Oxidizer**: Substance that supports combustion (O₂, N₂O)
  * **Fuel**: Material that burns (drapes, alcohol, plastic, hair)
  * **Ignition Source**: Provides energy to start combustion (ESU, laser)

![The fire triangle](ELECTRICAL_image-22.png_579864.png)

* **Fire Tetrahedron**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Adds fourth component: uninhibited chemical chain reaction
  * Required for fire to continuously oxidize fuel and sustain itself
  * Distinguishes ongoing fire from flash or explosion

![The fire tetrahedron](ELECTRICAL_image-23.png_d9db32.png)

* **OR Team Control of Components**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Anesthesia provider controls oxidizer (FiO₂/N₂O)
  * Surgeon operates ignition sources (ESU/laser)
  * Circulating nurse/scrub tech manages many fuels (prep solutions, drapes, sponges)
  * Anesthesia provider also determines if airway is open or sealed
* **OR Fire Triangle Components**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:

| Oxidizers | Fuels | Ignition Sources |
|-----------|-------|-----------------|
| Air | Alcohol | Electrosurgery |
| Oxygen | Drapes (paper > cloth) | Laser |
| Nitrous oxide | Gauze | Bipolar ESU |

## 5.2. FLAMMABILITY AND OXYGEN CONCENTRATION

**The relationship between oxygen concentration and flammability is critical to understanding fire risk.**

* **Oxygen Impact on Flammability**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Flammability increases with any oxygen enrichment
  * Nonlinear increase in flammability with increasing oxygen concentration
  * Ignition time decreases dramatically with increased oxygen concentration
* **Research Findings (Culp et al.)**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Median ignition time: 0.9s in 21% O₂, 0.4s in 50% O₂, 0.2s in 100% O₂
  * Some materials that don't burn in room air ignite in <0.5s in 50% oxygen
  * With each doubling of oxygen concentration, approximate halving of ignition time

| Materials | Ignition Time in 21% O₂ (s) | Ignition Time in 50% O₂ (s) | Ignition Time in 100% O₂ (s) |
|-----------|------------------------------|------------------------------|-------------------------------|
| Laparotomy sponge | 0.9 [0.8, 1.4] | 0.3 [0.1, 1.0] | 0.1 [0.0, 0.1] |
| Utility drape | Did not ignite | 0.2 [0.1, 0.4] | 0.2 [0.1, 0.4] |
| Surgical gown | Did not ignite | 0.4 [0.3, 0.4] | 0.3 [0.2, 0.4] |
| Blue OR towel | 1.6 [1.3, 1.9] | 0.5 [0.4, 0.5] | 0.1 [0.1, 0.2] |

![Oxygen concentration and ignition time](electrical_oxygen_ignition_time_graph_m3b9v2_1734163960000.png)

* **Safety Thresholds**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * General recommendation: Do not exceed 30% ambient oxygen where fire risk exists
  * Fire will generally not propagate if oxygen concentration is <16% (not clinically practical)
  * Carbon dioxide (as insufflation gas) dilutes oxidizing gases and is fire quenching
* **Physical Properties and Accumulation Risk**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Oxygen (15.9994 g/mol) denser than nitrogen (14.0067 g/mol)
  * Oxygen sinks and can accumulate in patient's pillow/hair or under drapes
  * ⚠️ Relevant during open delivery of supplemental oxygen
  * 💡 Suction under drapes near face speeds dissipation of accumulated oxygen
* **Nitrous Oxide Considerations**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * N₂O is an oxidizer, additive to oxygen
  * Flammability index with N₂O approximately half that of oxygen
  * Conservative approach: Treat O₂ and N₂O concentrations as additive
  * Best practice: Avoid N₂O when fire risk is present

## 5.3. FIRE RISK ASSESSMENT

**Systematic fire risk assessment should be performed before all procedures.**

* **ASA Practice Advisory**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Published in 2013 with comprehensive recommendations
  * Emphasizes prevention and preparedness

![ASA fire safety algorithm](ELECTRICAL_image-24.png_23ffe2.png)

* **Key Points for Oxygen Management**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Question if patient truly needs supplemental oxygen
  * Room air preferred if oxygen saturation remains acceptable
  * Secure airway if >0.3 FiO₂ needed with high fire risk
  * Use air/oxygen flowmeters or blender to deliver reduced oxygen concentration
* **Risk Assessment Scoring**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:

| Risk Factor | Score |
|-------------|-------|
| Procedure above xiphoid | 1 (yes) 0 (no) |
| Alcohol-based skin preparation | 1 (yes) 0 (no) |
| Open oxidizer source (O₂/N₂O via nasal cannula, face mask, uncuffed ETT) | 1 (yes) 0 (no) |
| Ignition source (cautery, laser, fiberoptic light) | 1 (yes) 0 (no) |

* **High-Risk Procedures (Score ≥3)**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Allow alcohol-containing skin preparations to dry for at least 3 minutes
  * Ensure no pooling of preparation solution (can form fuel vapor "bomb")
  * Hair may take considerably longer to dry completely
  * Confirm no tunneling space under drapes between oxygen source and surgical field
  * Limit oxygen concentration to 30% or less if possible, otherwise seal airway
  * Use moistened sponges
  * Have basin/bottle of sterile irrigating fluid readily available

## 5.4. TYPES OF OR FIRES

**Different surgical contexts create distinct fire risks requiring specific prevention strategies.**

### 5.4.1. ON-PATIENT VERSUS IN-PATIENT FIRES

**Most OR fires occur on the patient rather than inside body cavities.**

* **On-Patient Fires**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Most common type
  * Associated with open airway and supplemental oxygen delivery
  * Overwhelmingly associated with procedures around upper thorax, head, and neck
  * Common examples: port placement, temporal artery biopsy, blepharoplasty
  * ⚠️ Drapes do not isolate cautery from oxygen presence
  * Takes many minutes for ambient oxygen to dissipate after stopping flow
* **Oxygen Concentration Considerations**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Delay in achieving reduced circuit oxygen concentration even in intubated patients
  * Requires several minutes to reach <30% oxygen depending on:
	* Starting FiO₂
	* Fresh gas flow rate
	* Breathing circuit configuration
	* Minute ventilation
  * Expired oxygen concentration lags inspired concentration until FRC washout complete

### 5.4.2. AIRWAY FIRES

**Airway fires are particularly devastating due to thermal injury and toxic inhalation.**

* **General Characteristics**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Occur when ignition source interacts with fuel in oxygen-enriched environment
  * Particularly devastating due to:
	* Direct thermal injury
	* Associated tissue destruction
	* Inhalation injury from toxic combustion products
  * PVC endotracheal tube will burn in 25% O₂

![A burning ET tube produces a large amount of debris](ELECTRICAL_image-26.png_00cf5e.png)

* **Airway Fire Response**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Immediately remove endotracheal tube from patient
  * Stop flow of gases
  * Remove all flammable materials from airway
  * Pour saline into airway

#### 5.4.2.1. LASER-RELATED AIRWAY FIRES

**Laser surgery poses specific risks for airway fires with unique prevention strategies.**

* **Mechanism and Risk**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Most common cause of airway fires
  * Laser beam contacts flammable tube/cuff or charred tissue in oxygen-rich environment
  * Creates "blowtorch" effect
  * Unintended laser strikes common (50% of tubes show evidence in one study)
* **Laser Types**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * CO₂ lasers: Interact with water and any surface, require eye protection
  * KTP, blue light, Nd:YAG lasers: Interact with tissue pigment, require special tinted glasses
  * Different wavelengths and tissue penetration characteristics
* **Prevention Strategies**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Avoid endotracheal tube altogether (intermittent apnea/mask ventilation)
  * Use intermittent/low-frequency jet ventilation with Sanders injector
  * High-frequency jet ventilation
  * Use pulsed lasing mode at low power
  * Reduce oxygen concentration environment
  * Avoid lasering adipose and charred tissue
* **Special Endotracheal Tubes**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Laser-resistant (not immune) tubes available
  * Design approaches: copper foil coating, aluminum foil coating, corrugated stainless-steel wrapper
  * Still have flammable cuffs and PVC at tip and inside
  * May not be resistant to all laser types

![Laser-Flex laser resistant ET tube](ELECTRICAL_image-27.png_223ba1.png)

* **Best Practices**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Limit FiO₂ to no greater than 0.3
  * Use only moistened gauze sponges
  * Moisten exterior of endotracheal tube
  * Inflate cuffs with colored saline rather than air
  * Set laser on intermittent mode at lowest clinically useful energy level
* **Pediatric Considerations**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Laser-resistant tubes not available in smallest sizes
  * Smallest laser-resistant tubes not cuffed
  * Leak around uncuffed tube allows oxygen into operative field
  * Maintain lowest possible circuit oxygen concentration and peak inspiratory pressure
  * Consider intermittent apneic technique or low-frequency jet ventilation
* **Airway Laser Surgery Response**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Switch off laser immediately
  * Flood surgery site with saline
  * Disconnect breathing system
  * Remove tracheal tube
  * Ventilate patient with air using bag-valve-mask system

#### 5.4.2.2. TRACHEOSTOMY FIRES

**Tracheostomy procedures present unique fire risks due to the transition from closed to open airway.**

* **Natural Airway Approach**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Supply minimum clinically tolerable supplemental oxygen
  * Connect to breathing circuit or common gas outlet on anesthesia machine
  * Drape widely and avoid drape tunneling
  * Place suction under drape to inhibit oxygen accumulation
  * Encourage bipolar over monopolar cautery (less sparking)
  * ⚠️ Enter trachea with knife, not cautery, to eliminate ignition source
* **Intubated Patient Approach**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Consider transitional nature of procedure:
	* Starts with closed oxygen administration
	* Becomes open when trachea entered
	* Returns to closed when tracheostomy cuff inflated
  * FiO₂ typically increased before tracheal entry
  * ⚠️ Enter airway with knife, not electrosurgery
  * Avoid electrosurgery until airway resecured
  * Obtain hemostatic field prior to entering trachea
* **High-Flow Nasal Cannula Considerations**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * THRIVE technique creates oxygen-rich environment around face
  * Humidification may provide some fire retardance
  * Adipose tissue gases released by electrosurgery can also burn

#### 5.4.2.3. TONSILLECTOMY FIRES

**Tonsillectomy procedures create fire risk due to proximity of fuel, oxygen, and ignition sources.**

* **Risk Factors**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Fire tetrahedron present in close proximity
  * Fuel source: endotracheal tube or tonsil tissue bed
  * Elevated risk with uncuffed endotracheal tube (especially if PIP ≥12 cmH₂O)
  * Leak around tube causes pharyngeal oxygen concentration similar to breathing circuit
* **Prevention Strategies**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Use cuffed endotracheal tube
  * Maintain FiO₂ ≤30%
  * Avoid nitrous oxide
  * Wait for expired oxygen concentration to decrease before electrosurgery begins
  * 💡 Room-air ventilation reasonable in otherwise healthy patient during cautery

### 5.4.3. EYE SURGERY FIRES

**Eye surgery presents significant fire risks due to proximity to oxygen sources and common ignition sources.**

* **Research Findings**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Closed claims review of 168 eye surgeons showed:
	* 88% of fires occurred with supplemental oxygen
	* 59% under monitored anesthesia care
	* 41% with monopolar cautery
	* 41% with high-temperature thermal cautery
  * Hair most common fuel (40%), cotton gauze second (19%)
  * 12% occurred in room air (less severe injury)
  * Bipolar cautery appeared less likely to cause fire than monopolar
* **Prevalence**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * 44% of surgeons had experienced at least one OR fire
  * Average experience: only 17.7 years in practice
  * 12.5% had experienced multiple fires
  * Another survey (258 oculoplastic surgeons): 32% experienced at least one fire
  * Eyelid surgeries most common (90%)
  * Estimated 14 fires annually related to oculoplastic surgery alone

### 5.4.4. ABDOMINAL SURGERY/COLONOSCOPY FIRES

**Intestinal gases can create fire hazards even in room air environments.**

* **Open Abdominal Procedures**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Room air provides adequate oxidizer for flammable intestinal gases (hydrogen, methane)
  * Risk with bowel perforation or entry into viscus with cautery
  * Similar conditions exist during colonoscopy
* **Laparoscopic Considerations**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Carbon dioxide insufflation reduces fire risk (fire quenching)
  * Increasingly used during gastrointestinal endoscopy
  * ⚠️ Accidental use of oxygen for insufflation has caused fires

### 5.4.5. THORACIC SURGERY FIRES

**Thoracic procedures create unique fire risks due to direct exposure of lung tissue to oxygen and ignition sources.**

* **Risk Factors**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Violation of lung parenchyma creates oxygen-enriched environment
  * Combination of cautery and dry lap sponges
  * Higher FiO₂ typically used in thoracic and cardiac surgery
  * Lung isolation with apneic oxygenation to deflated lung
* **Protective Factors**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Carbon dioxide insufflation in thoracoscopic surgery provides some protection

### 5.4.6. ELECTRICAL EQUIPMENT FIRES

**Electrical equipment itself can be a source of fire in the OR environment.**

* **Mechanisms**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Wires vulnerable to wear and tear
  * Contain flammable materials
  * Ignition typically from short circuit
  * Forced air warmers and other devices can fail and catch fire

## 5.5. FIRE PREVENTION

**Fire prevention depends on communication, awareness, and specific preventive measures.**

* **Team Communication**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Assess fire risk at beginning of each case
  * Discuss mitigation strategies
  * Recognize that alcohol-based skin preparation always creates some risk
  * Assign roles for each team member
* **Role-Specific Strategies**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * **Anesthesia Provider**:
	* Secure airway with supraglottic device or ETT if needed
	* Minimize oxygen concentration
	* Use air:oxygen mixture when possible
  * **Surgeon**:
	* Consider knife instead of cautery
	* Use lower energy settings if ESU necessary
  * **Nurse/Technician**:
	* Ensure adequate preparation drying time
	* Check for pooled solution
	* Have moistened sponges and extinguishing solution available
* **Dynamic Risk Assessment**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Recognize that fire risk may change during case
  * Increase risk: patient's oxygen requirement increases, skin reprepped
  * Decrease risk: tracheostomy secured, procedure moves away from airway

## 5.6. FIRE RESPONSE

**Prompt, coordinated response to fire is essential to minimize patient harm.**

* **ERASE Mnemonic**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * **E**xtinguish: Remove burning fuel from patient, use water/saline, smother flame
  * **R**escue: Evacuate patient and personnel if extinguishing unsuccessful
  * **A**ctivate: Trigger fire alarm
  * **S**hut: Close OR doors, shut off medical gas supply
  * **E**valuate: Assess and treat patient
* **Fire Extinguisher Use**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]]:
  * Usually not required (removing fuel and smothering sufficient)
  * Carbon dioxide extinguisher preferred if needed
  * Can be used on energized electrical equipment
  * Non-water-based fire extinguisher should be used immediately    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Equipment and Safety|ⓘ]] 

**Bottom Line:** OR fires are preventable but remain a significant threat. The fire risk should be assessed before every procedure, with particular attention to the triad of oxidizer, fuel, and ignition source. Reducing oxygen concentration, controlling ignition sources, and managing flammable materials are essential for prevention. If fire occurs, a prompt coordinated response following the ERASE protocol can minimize patient harm.

# 6. LASERS IN HEALTHCARE

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|Part A: Safety]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C4 Trauma Resuscitation and Crisis Management|Part B: C4]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Systems-Based Practice|Part C: Systems-Based Practice]]

## 6.1. BASIC PRINCIPLES AND TYPES 

**Lasers produce focused, high-energy light beams with specific medical applications.**

* **Definition**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * LASER stands for Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation
  * Produces non-divergent light beam of single color/frequency (monochromatic)
  * High-energy intensity with very small cross-sectional area
  * Energy depends on frequency
* **Types of Lasers**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * **Solid-state laser**: e.g., Nd-YAG laser emitting light at 1064 nm (infrared)
  * **Semiconductor laser**: e.g., gallium arsenide (GaAs) laser - typically lower power output
  * **Liquid laser**
  * **Gas laser**:
	* Helium-neon laser (emits red light)
	* Carbon dioxide laser (emits infrared light)
	* Argon laser (emits green light)
* **Medical Applications**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Precise tissue cutting with excellent hemostasis
  * Used in:
	* Thoracic surgery (excision of central airway tumors)
	* ENT surgery (vocal cord tumors)
	* Gynecology (endometriosis)
	* Urology (benign prostatic hyperplasia)
	* Dermatology (skin lesions)
	* Ophthalmology (myopia)

## 6.2. HAZARDS AND SAFETY CONSIDERATIONS 

**Laser use presents unique hazards requiring specific safety precautions.**

* **Hazard Considerations**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Increasing distance from laser offers little safety improvement due to non-divergent beam
  * High energy can cause:
	* Permanent damage to eye retina or optic nerve head (visible portion)
	* Damage to cornea, lens, aqueous and vitreous humors (infrared light)
	* Skin burns
* **Safety Measures**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * All staff should wear eye protection appropriate for specific laser type within controlled area
  * Protection must guard against accidental main beam exposure
  * Standard spectacles don't provide reliable peripheral visual field protection
  * Non-water-based fire extinguisher must be readily available
  * All doors should be locked and windows covered to protect those outside
* **Controlled Access**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Restricted entry to laser treatment areas
  * Clear signage indicating laser in use
  * Appropriate safety measures for non-treatment staff

## 6.3. CLASSIFICATION OF LASER PRODUCTS 

**Lasers are classified according to their hazard potential.**

* **Classification System**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 

| Class | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| Class 1 | Power doesn't exceed maximum permissible eye exposure; safe due to engineering design |
| Class 2 | Visible laser beam only (400-700 nm), powers up to 1 mW, eye protected by blink-reflex time of 0.25 second |
| Class 2m | As Class 2, but not safe when viewed with visual aids such as eye loupes |
| Class 3a | Relaxation of Class 2 - up to 5 mW for visible spectrum radiation provided beam expanded so eye still protected by blink reflex. Maximum irradiance must not exceed 25 W/m² for intrabeam viewing. For other wavelengths, hazard no greater than Class 1 |
| Class 3b | Powers up to 0.5 W; direct viewing hazardous; can be any wavelength from 180 nm to 1 mm |
| Class 4 | Powers over 0.5 W; any wavelength from 180 nm to 1 mm; capable of igniting flammable materials; extremely hazardous |

## 6.4. AIRWAY LASER SURGERY 

**Airway laser surgery presents significant fire risks requiring specific preventive strategies.**

* **Risk Factors**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * High fire risk due to combination of oxygen-enriched environment and high thermal energy from laser
  * Proximity of flammable airway devices to oxygen and laser energy
* **Risk Reduction Strategies**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * Avoid nitrous oxide
  * Use lower oxygen concentrations (25% or less)
  * Use laser-resistant tracheal tubes
  * Protect surrounding tissues with wet swabs
  * Use non-reflective matt-black surgical instruments to reduce beam reflection
* **Emergency Response**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Safety|ⓘ]] 
  * If fire occurs:
	1. Switch off laser immediately
	2. Flood surgery site with saline
	3. Disconnect breathing system
	4. Remove tracheal tube
	5. Ventilate patient with air using bag-valve-mask system

**Bottom Line:** Lasers provide valuable capabilities for precise surgical procedures but present significant hazards, particularly with respect to fire risk, eye damage, and skin burns. Safety measures include appropriate eye protection, controlled access to laser areas, and specific protocols for airway procedures. Understanding laser classification helps in assessing risks and implementing appropriate precautions.
