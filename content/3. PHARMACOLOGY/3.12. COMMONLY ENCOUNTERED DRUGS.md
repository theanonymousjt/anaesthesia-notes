# 1. ANTIBIOTICS









## 1.1. CLASSIFICATION OF BACTERIA








### 1.1.1. INFECTIOUS AGENTS

* **Viruses:**
  * Protein envelope structure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Contain either DNA or RNA [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Bacteria:**
  * **Classification:**
	* **Gram-positive vs. Gram-negative:** Differentiated by cell wall structure and gram stain reaction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* **Aerobic vs. anaerobic:** Based on oxygen requirements for growth [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Epidemiology:**
	* Most community-acquired infections are due to gram-positive organisms [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Most hospital-associated infections are gram-negative [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Structure:**
	* Prokaryotes with DNA in loops [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Cell walls (target of many antibiotics) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Cell membranes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Ribosomes (30S and 50S subunits - targets of many antibiotics) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Fungi:**
  * Eukaryotes with defined cellular structures [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Cell membrane made from ergosterol (rather than cholesterol in human cells), allowing for selective targeting [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Prions:**
  * Infectious proteins [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * No specific antimicrobial treatment currently available [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Understanding bacterial classification is essential for selecting appropriate antibiotics, as cellular structures specific to bacteria provide targets for antibacterial agents while minimizing toxicity to human cells.

## 1.3. BETA-LACTAM ANTIBIOTICS








### 1.3.1. PENICILLINS

**First discovered antibiotics that remain clinically important for many common infections.**

#### 1.3.1.1. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* **β-lactam ring inhibits cell wall synthesis:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Stops peptidoglycan cross-link formation by irreversibly binding to penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs) in the bacterial cell wall [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Bactericidal activity:** Kills actively dividing bacteria [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * 💡 Less effective against inactive bacterial cells as they aren't actively synthesizing cell wall components [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Synergy with aminoglycosides:** Gentamicin penetrates cells more effectively when cell wall is damaged by penicillins [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Differential effectiveness:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * More effective against gram-positive bacteria than gram-negative [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * In gram-negative bacteria, damaging the thin peptidoglycan wall weakens the cell, but the lipopolysaccharide outer membrane helps maintain structural integrity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 1.3.1.2. PHARMACOKINETICS

* **Absorption:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Some are well absorbed from the stomach if resistant to stomach acid:
	* Amoxicillin
	* Penicillin V (phenoxymethylpenicillin)
  * Others are broken down by stomach acid with no oral bioavailability:
	* Benzylpenicillin (Penicillin G)
* **Distribution:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Good tissue penetration generally
  * 💡 Can only penetrate blood-brain barrier and bone in the presence of inflammation, which is why benzylpenicillin is effective in meningitis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Metabolism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Up to 20% metabolized
  * Short half-life:
	* Penicillin G: 30 minutes
	* Ampicillin: 120 minutes
* **Excretion:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Approximately 70% undergoes renal excretion unchanged
	* 💡 This excretion is blocked by probenecid, which can be co-administered to increase penicillin potency and duration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * 10% excreted in bile

#### 1.3.1.3. CLINICAL ADJUNCTS

* **Probenecid:** Inhibits renal excretion of penicillins, increasing effective dose [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Clavulanic acid:** Inhibits β-lactamase and has weak antibacterial action itself [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Tazobactam:** β-lactamase inhibitor [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 1.3.1.4. ALLERGIES

* **Reported allergy incidence:** 10% of population [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
* **True anaphylaxis:** Only 0.01% [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
* **Cross-reaction:** Up to 10% cross-reaction with cephalosporins or carbapenems [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]

#### 1.3.1.5. SIDE EFFECTS

* **Generally well tolerated** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
* **Gastrointestinal:**
  * Diarrhea (common) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
  * Ampicillin can cause pseudomembranous colitis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
* **Dermatological:**
  * Rash in up to 10% of patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
* **Neurological:**
  * ⚠️ Benzylpenicillin can cause seizures in high doses [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]

#### 1.3.1.6. BACTERIAL RESISTANCE MECHANISMS

* **β-lactamase production:** Bacterial enzymes that inactivate β-lactam antibiotics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Multiple types of lactamase enzymes exist [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Reduced membrane permeability:** Decreases drug entry into bacteria [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Modified penicillin-binding proteins:** Reduced affinity for penicillin binding on cell wall [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 1.3.1.7. CLASSIFICATION OF PENICILLINS 

Penicillins can be classified based on their spectrum of activity:

* **Narrow-spectrum, naturally occurring:**
  * Benzylpenicillin (Penicillin G)
  * Phenoxymethylpenicillin (Penicillin V)
  * **Indications:** Gram-positive cocci (meningococcal infections) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Narrow-spectrum, semisynthetic:**
  * Flucloxacillin
  * **Indications:** Penicillinase-producing staphylococcal infections [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Narrow-spectrum, staphylococcal β-lactamase resistant:**
  * Temocillin
  * **Indications:** Gram-negative lactamase-producing bacteria (not pseudomonas) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Broad-spectrum penicillins:**
  * Ampicillin, Amoxicillin, Co-amoxiclav
  * **Indications:** Gram-positive and some gram-negative coverage; Co-amoxiclav has increased activity by combining amoxicillin with clavulanic acid (β-lactamase inhibitor) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Antipseudomonal penicillins:**
  * Piperacillin, Ticarcillin
  * **Indications:** Broad spectrum activity against gram-positive, gram-negative bacteria and anaerobes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

**Bottom Line:** Penicillins remain fundamental antibiotics that inhibit bacterial cell wall synthesis. While generally well-tolerated with good tissue penetration, their effectiveness can be limited by bacterial resistance mechanisms, primarily through β-lactamase production.

### 1.3.2. CEPHALOSPORINS







**Second major class of beta-lactam antibiotics with expanded gram-negative coverage in higher generations.**

#### 1.3.2.1. STRUCTURE AND MECHANISM

* **Fused β-lactam ring:** Less sensitive to β-lactamase enzymes, giving broader spectrum of activity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * 💡 The ring is fused to a dihydrothiazine ring to form a cephem nucleus [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Mechanism of action:** Similar to penicillins [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Bactericidal
  * Prevents peptidoglycan cross-linking in bacterial cell wall

#### 1.3.2.2. PHARMACOKINETICS

* **Distribution:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Large volume of distribution
  * Cross placenta and blood-brain barrier (especially higher generation agents)
* **Metabolism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Half-life typically 60-90 minutes
  * 💡 Ceftriaxone has longer half-life (6-10 hours), allowing once-daily dosing [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Cefuroxime not significantly metabolized
  * Cefotaxime undergoes hepatic metabolism
* **Excretion:** Primarily renal [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 1.3.2.3. GENERATIONS AND TARGETS

* **First Generation:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Examples:** Cefalexin, Cefaclor, Cefradine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * **Spectrum:** Primarily gram-positive cocci [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Second Generation:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Examples:** Cefuroxime, Cefaclor, Cefoxitin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * **Spectrum:** Improved gram-negative coverage including Haemophilus influenzae [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Third Generation:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Examples:** Ceftriaxone, Cefotaxime, Ceftazidime [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * **Spectrum:** Enhanced gram-negative coverage including Pseudomonas [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * 💡 Better CNS penetration, crosses blood-brain barrier [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Fourth Generation:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Examples:** Cefepime, Cefpirome (not commonly used in UK) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * **Spectrum:** Enterobacter, Pseudomonas [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Fifth Generation:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Examples:** Ceftaroline, Ceftobiprole [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * **Spectrum:** Includes MRSA coverage [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 1.3.2.4. PENICILLIN CROSS-REACTIVITY

* **Historical context:** Cephalothin (1st cephalosporin) marketed in 1964 had 5-10% cross-reactivity with penicillin allergy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
  * This led to over-generalization about all cephalosporins [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
  * Some cross-reactivity historically may have been due to manufacturing contamination [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
* **Current understanding:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
  * Cross-reaction rate between penicillin and 1st/2nd generation cephalosporins: 1-10% [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
  * Cross-reaction rate between penicillin and 3rd generation cephalosporins: approaches 0% [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
  * 💡 History of penicillin allergy may be a general risk factor for allergic manifestations to antibiotics, not specific to cephalosporins [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]

#### 1.3.2.5. SIDE EFFECTS 

* **Hypersensitivity:** Cross-reactivity with penicillins (see above section)
* **Gastrointestinal:** Antibiotic-associated colitis, especially with third-generation agents
* **Other:** Thrombophlebitis and pain at injection site

**Bottom Line:** Cephalosporins are beta-lactam antibiotics with progressively increased gram-negative and specialized coverage through five generations. Despite historical concerns, cross-reactivity with penicillin allergies is relatively low, especially with third-generation agents.

### 1.3.3. CARBAPENEMS







**Broad-spectrum beta-lactam antibiotics typically reserved for serious infections.**

#### 1.3.3.1. CHARACTERISTICS AND COVERAGE

* **Spectrum:** Broad-spectrum β-lactam antibiotics with excellent coverage against both gram-positive and gram-negative organisms, including aerobic and anaerobic bacteria [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
* **Examples:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Ertapenem
  * Meropenem
  * Imipenem
* **Limitations:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
  * Do not effectively target MRSA or Enterococcus faecalis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ When used alone, can promote Pseudomonas resistance [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]

#### 1.3.3.2. STRUCTURE AND MECHANISM 

* **Structure:** Part of the β-lactam group but with subtle modifications of the β-lactam ring resulting in significant resistance to most β-lactamases
* **Resistance:** Carbapenem-hydrolyzing β-lactamases have been increasingly isolated from gram-negative organisms and may limit therapy
* **Action:** Bactericidal drugs that disrupt cell wall synthesis like other β-lactams

#### 1.3.3.3. SPECIFIC AGENTS 

* **Imipenem:**
  * Resistant to β-lactamase but only moderately effective against Clostridium perfringens
  * Induces β-lactamase expression in Pseudomonas, reducing effectiveness of other β-lactam antibiotics
  * Metabolized by renal dehydropeptidase, so often combined with inhibitor cilastatin
  * ⚠️ Very high doses associated with neurotoxicity, especially with inflamed meninges, limiting use in meningitis
* **Meropenem:**
  * Similar to imipenem but not metabolized by renal dehydropeptidase (no cilastatin needed)
  * Less neurotoxic, making it suitable for bacterial meningitis
* **Ertapenem:**
  * Narrower spectrum than meropenem or imipenem
  * Less activity against pseudomonas and some gram-positive bacteria
  * Long half-life allowing once-daily dosing

#### 1.3.3.4. PHARMACOKINETICS 

* All carbapenems need dose reduction in renal dysfunction

**Bottom Line:** Carbapenems are powerful, broad-spectrum antibiotics typically reserved for serious infections, but require careful stewardship to prevent resistance development.

## 1.4. GLYCOPEPTIDE ANTIBIOTICS








### 1.4.1. MECHANISM AND PROPERTIES

* **Agents:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Teicoplanin
  * Vancomycin
* **Mechanism of action:** Attack the bacterial cell wall and prevent peptidoglycan reinforcement but are not β-lactam agents [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Activity spectrum:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Bactericidal
  * Effective against aerobic and anaerobic gram-positive bacteria [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 1.4.2. CLINICAL USE

* **Indications:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
  * MRSA infections
  * Endocarditis
  * 💡 Vancomycin not absorbed from GI tract, making it useful for Clostridioides difficile infection [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
* **Administration:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
  * Vancomycin can be given intrathecally for CNS infections [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
  * Teicoplanin is approximately three times as potent as vancomycin [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
  * Teicoplanin has longer duration, allowing once-daily dosing after a loading period of twice daily for 3-4 doses [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]

### 1.4.3. PHARMACOKINETICS

* **Excretion:** Mostly excreted unchanged in the urine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 1.4.4. SIDE EFFECTS

* **Ototoxicity:** Can cause hearing loss [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
* **Nephrotoxicity:** ⚠️ Reversible renal impairment [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
* **Infusion reactions:** "Red man syndrome" due to histamine release if given rapidly [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
  * 💡 Can be minimized by slow infusion (typically over 1-2 hours) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
* **Hematologic:** Neutropenia and thrombocytopenia possible [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]

#### 1.4.4.1. ADDITIONAL FEATURES 

* **Structure:** Vancomycin and Teicoplanin are naturally occurring first-generation glycopeptide antibiotics; they are glycosylated non-ribosomal heptapeptides that do not contain a β-lactam ring
* **Semi-synthetic derivatives:** Second generation agents include Dalbavancin and Telavancin
* **Pharmacodynamics:** Glycopeptides have time-dependent bactericidal action; efficacy is related to time spent above the MIC
* **Monitoring:** Vancomycin trough levels should be maintained between 10-20 mg/L due to its relatively short duration of action, minimal post-antibiotic effect, and efficacy being related to time spent above the MIC
* **Anaphylaxis risk:** Teicoplanin is used widely for surgical prophylaxis and has been found to be 17 times more likely to cause anaphylaxis compared to alternatives

**Bottom Line:** Glycopeptide antibiotics are valuable for treating resistant gram-positive infections, particularly MRSA. Administration considerations (especially slow infusion to prevent red man syndrome) and monitoring for ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity are essential for safe use.

## 1.5. QUINOLONES








### 1.5.1. MECHANISM AND PROPERTIES

* **Mechanism of action:** Prevent DNA replication by inhibiting DNA gyrase and other topoisomerase enzymes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Examples:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Ciprofloxacin
* **Distribution:** Good tissue and blood-brain barrier penetration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 1.5.2. SIDE EFFECTS

* **Neurological:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Can lower seizure threshold in epileptic patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
* **Musculoskeletal:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Achilles tendinopathy and tendon rupture, especially in elderly or steroid-treated patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
* **Cardiac:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ QT prolongation leading to risk of arrhythmias [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Quinolones are effective broad-spectrum antibiotics with good tissue penetration, but carry important side effect risks including tendinopathy, seizure threshold lowering, and QT prolongation.

## 1.6. MACROLIDES








### 1.6.1. COVERAGE AND SPECIFICS

* **Spectrum:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Gram-positive coverage
  * Gram-negative anaerobes
* **Examples:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Erythromycin
  * Clarithromycin
* **Specialized coverage:** Clarithromycin has enhanced activity against Streptococcus, Listeria, and Legionella species [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]

### 1.6.2. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* **Target:** Bind to 50S subunit of bacterial ribosome [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Effect:** Prevents protein synthesis by interfering with tRNA function [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 1.6.3. DRUG INTERACTIONS

* **Enzyme interactions:** CYP450 and P-glycoprotein inhibitors [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Can significantly increase levels of many drugs including statins, warfarin, and digoxin [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]

### 1.6.4. SIDE EFFECTS

* **Cardiovascular:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Arrhythmias and QT prolongation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
* **Gastrointestinal:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]
  * Nausea and vomiting
  * Diarrhea
  * 💡 Erythromycin is sometimes used in intensive care as a prokinetic agent for treatment of ileus [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction|ⓘ]]

#### 1.6.4.1. ADDITIONAL PHARMACOLOGICAL DETAILS 

* **Structure:** Macrolides are derived from erythromycin and have a lactone ring at their center; newer agents have different groups attached to the lactone ring resulting in improved oral tolerance
* **Pharmacokinetics:**
  * Can be administered orally or parenterally
  * CSF penetration is poor even with inflamed meninges, but sputum and lung penetration are good
  * Macrolides are frequently concentrated in leukocytes facilitating transport to the site of infection
  * Metabolized and excreted mainly via the liver
* **Immunomodulatory properties:** Macrolides may have immunomodulatory properties separate from their antimicrobial action, useful in certain diseases such as cystic fibrosis

**Bottom Line:** Macrolides offer good coverage for respiratory and atypical infections but require careful consideration of QT prolongation risk and significant drug interactions as CYP450 inhibitors.

#### 1.7.4.1. EXTENDED INTERVAL DOSING 

* Extended interval dosing (administering larger doses less frequently) may be associated with improved outcomes and less toxicity compared with multiple daily injection regimens
* Advantages based on three pharmacological features:
  * Concentration-dependent killing
  * Rapid elimination
  * Prolonged post-antibiotic effect
* Larger doses increase target site concentrations while minimizing potential toxicity by allowing sufficient intervals for drug concentrations to fall
* Therapeutic drug monitoring helps reduce toxicity by ensuring low drug levels between doses

#### 1.7.4.2. STRUCTURE AND ADDITIONAL AGENTS 

* **Structure:** Characterized by a core structure of amino sugars connected via glycosidic linkages to a dibasic aminocyclitol
* **Additional agents:**
  * **Tobramycin:** Similar activity to gentamicin but can be given via nebulized route for chronic Pseudomonas infection in cystic fibrosis
  * **Streptomycin:** Active against Mycobacterium tuberculosis and used only in this setting
  * **Neomycin:** Too toxic for parenteral therapy, only used orally for decontamination of the GI tract or topically

**Bottom Line:** Aminoglycosides provide valuable gram-negative coverage but require careful monitoring of levels, renal function, and auditory function due to significant ototoxic and nephrotoxic potential.

### 1.10.3. CROSS-REACTIVITY PATTERNS

#### 1.10.3.1. ADDITIONAL INFORMATION ON ALLERGY TO PENICILLIN AND RELATED DRUGS 

* Penicillin allergy is reported commonly by patients; however, more than 90% of patients with a reported penicillin allergy do not have IgE-mediated sensitivity when skin testing is performed
* Reasons for overreporting include:
  * Inappropriate labeling of patients
  * Incorrect diagnosis of other side effects
  * Prior allergies that resolved with time
* Penicillins remain one of the most widespread causes of anaphylaxis in anesthesia and intensive care practice
* Multiple antigenic components in the penicillin molecule can cause reactions
* The appropriate strategy is to take a detailed history of the reaction to determine if it was:
  * An IgE-mediated anaphylactic reaction
  * Another type of severe reaction (e.g., Stevens-Johnson syndrome)
  * Misreporting of a side effect (nausea, diarrhea)
* Current evidence suggests:
  * Up to 10% of patients with true penicillin allergy will react to cephalosporins
  * 99% of patients with positive skin tests to penicillin will tolerate carbapenems
  * No evidence of immunologic cross-reactivity between penicillins and monobactams (e.g., aztreonam)

**Bottom Line:** Antibiotic allergies are frequently reported but less commonly represent true allergies. Understanding the patterns of cross-reactivity, particularly that 3rd generation cephalosporins rarely cross-react with penicillins, enables safer antibiotic selection in patients with reported allergies.

# 2. ANTIARRHYTHMIC DRUGS









## 2.1. CLASSIFICATION SYSTEMS






### 2.1.1. VAUGHAN-WILLIAMS CLASSIFICATION

* **Traditional system** based on target ion channels and cellular effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Limitations:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Doesn't adequately cover commonly used drugs such as digoxin and adenosine
  * Some drugs like amiodarone and sotalol don't fit neatly into a single group and have multiple actions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 2.1.1.1. DETAILED CLASSIFICATION 

* **Class I** (sodium channel blockers): Inhibit rapid sodium influx during phase 0 of the action potential
  * **Class Ia:** Prolong refractory period; act in atria, ventricles, and accessory pathways (quinidine, procainamide, disopyramide)
  * **Class Ib:** Shorten refractory period; act primarily in ventricles (lidocaine, mexiletine, phenytoin)
  * **Class Ic:** No effect on refractory period; act in atria, ventricles, and accessory pathways (flecainide, propafenone)
* **Class II** (beta blockers): Inhibit sympathetic tone (propranolol, atenolol, esmolol)
* **Class III** (potassium channel blockers): Prolong action potential duration (amiodarone, bretylium, sotalol)
* **Class IV** (calcium channel blockers): Inhibit calcium channels (verapamil, diltiazem)
* **Others**: Digoxin, MgSO4, Adenosine, Atropine

### 2.1.2. CLASSIFICATION BY CLINICAL INDICATION

* **SVT agents:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Adenosine
  * Beta blockers
  * Verapamil
  * Digoxin
* **VT agents:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Lidocaine
* **SVT and VT agents:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Amiodarone

* **Special indications:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Phenytoin used for digoxin toxicity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 2.1.3. CARDIAC ACTION POTENTIAL EFFECTS

![Antiarrhythmics.png](OTHER_COMMON_OTHER_COMMON_OTHER_COMMON_OTHER_COMMON_OTHER_COMMON_OTHER_COMMON_Antiarrhythmics.png_3995b6.png__52895d.png)

* **Mnemonic: “some block potassium channels”
* **Class I** (sodium channel blockers): [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Inhibit rapid sodium influx during phase 0 of the action potential [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Reduce phase 4 depolarization rate in pacemaker cells [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Subclass effects:**
	* **IA:** Prolong refractory period; act in atria, ventricles, and accessory pathways [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* **IB:** Shorten refractory period; act primarily in ventricles [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* **IC:** No effect on refractory period; act in atria, ventricles, and accessory pathways [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Class II** (beta blockers): [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Inhibit sympathetic tone with three main effects:
	* Reduce phase 4 depolarization slope (pacemaker cells) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Reduce maximum rate of depolarization (phase 0) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Prolong action potential duration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Class III** (potassium channel blockers): [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Prolong action potential duration and refractory period by inhibiting potassium channels [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Class IV** (calcium channel blockers): [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Modify plateau phase in non-pacemaker cells [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Inhibit phase 0 depolarization in pacemaker cells by blocking calcium channels [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
- **Limitations of the Vaughan-Williams Classification**
	- Some drugs don’t fall under the class 1-4 system.
	- Ivabradine acts on phase 4 by selectively inhibiting the HCN funny channel, hence decreasing the inward If current and decreasing HR. And does not decrease inotropy or action potential duration.
	- Digoxin indirectly slows phase 4 by promoting the release of ACh at cardiac muscarinic receptors. The downstream effects of parasympathetic stimulation is membrane hyperpolarization through increased potassium permeability
	- Adenosine acts via adenosine A1 receptors, leading to opening of adenosine-sensitive K channels and membrane hyperpolarization (potassium efflux)

#### 2.1.3.1. CARDIAC ACTION POTENTIAL PHYSIOLOGY 

* **Cardiac tissue types:**
  * **Pacemaker tissue:** Found in the SA node in the right atrium; has the fastest rate of spontaneous depolarization, setting the heart rate
  * **Conducting tissue:** Specialized pathways transmitting electrical impulses
  * **Contractile tissue:** Responsible for mechanical contraction
* **Pacemaker action potential:**
  * Slow spontaneous depolarization (pre-potential) due to increased Ca²⁺ conductance
  * At -40 mV, slow voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels (L channels) open, causing membrane depolarization
  * Na⁺ conductance changes very little
  * Repolarization occurs due to increased K⁺ conductance as Ca²⁺ channels close
  ![[Pasted image 20250521195523.png]]
* Phase 0 (depolarization): calcium influx (via slow L-type voltage-gated calcium channels)
* Phase 3 (repolarization): potassium efflux (via voltage-gated potassium channels)
* Phase 4 (spontaneous diastolic depolarization): slow sodium influx and calcium influx (via hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide-gated [HCN] channels and T-type voltage gated calcium channels)
- Hence, phase 0 is blocked by CCBs (class 4); Phase 3 is blocked by potassium channel blockers (class 3); Additionally, beta blockers (class 2) produce a negative chronotropic effect by acting at phase 4 (prolonged diastolic depolarisation) and phase 0 (decreased maximum rate of depolarisation)
* **Contractile tissue action potential:**
  * More stable resting potential at -80 mV
  * Five phases:
	* **Phase 0:** Rapid depolarization (<1 millisecond) due to increased Na⁺ (and possibly some Ca²⁺) conductance through voltage-gated Na⁺ channels
	* **Phase 1:** Closure of Na⁺ channels with Cl⁻ expulsion
	* **Phase 2 (Plateau):** Due to Ca²⁺ influx via voltage-sensitive type-L Ca²⁺ channels, lasting up to 150 milliseconds (absolute refractory period)
	* **Phase 3:** Ca²⁺ channel inactivation with increased K⁺ conductance returning membrane potential to resting value (relative refractory period)
	* **Phase 4:** Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase maintains ionic gradient at -80 mV with variable spontaneous 'diastolic' depolarization

#### 2.1.3.2. ARRHYTHMIA MECHANISMS 

* **Tachyarrhythmias may originate from:**
  * **Enhanced automaticity:** Resting potential of contractile tissue loses stability, reaching threshold for depolarization before the SA node (seen in ischemia and hypokalemia)
  * **Afterpotentials:** Oscillations of membrane potential in ischemic myocardium reaching threshold potential
  * **Re-entry/circus mechanisms:** Development of ectopic focus leading to tachyarrhythmias
* **Bradyarrhythmias:**
  * Due to failure of conduction from SA node to surrounding tissue
  * Clinically significant in second-degree and third-degree block
  * May require atropine, β-stimulation, or pacing

**Bottom Line:** Antiarrhythmic drugs can be organized by the Vaughan-Williams classification targeting specific ion channels and action potential phases, or more pragmatically by their clinical indications for specific arrhythmias.

**Bottom Line:** Class I antiarrhythmics block sodium channels with varying effects on action potential duration. While effective against specific arrhythmias, they require careful monitoring for neurological side effects, conduction disturbances, and hypotension.

* **Absorption:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

* **Distribution:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Lipid-soluble agents cross membranes more easily, including the blood-brain barrier [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Protein binding varies significantly between agents [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Metabolism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Lipid-soluble agents undergo hepatic metabolism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Excretion:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Lipid-soluble: metabolites undergo renal excretion
  * Lipophobic agents: excreted unchanged by kidneys [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 2.3.5.1. ESMOLOL 

* **Properties:** Relatively cardioselective β-blocker with rapid onset and offset
* **Presentation:** Clear liquid (2.5g or 100mg in 10ml); larger strengths should be diluted
* **Dosing:** Infusion 50-200 µg/kg/min or titrated in 10mg boluses to effect
* **Uses:** Short-term management of tachycardia and hypertension in perioperative period; acute SVT
* **Pharmacology:** No intrinsic sympathomimetic activity or membrane stabilizing properties
* **Side effects:**
  * Exhibits β2 antagonism at high doses (caution in asthmatics)
  * May precipitate heart failure but effects limited by short duration
  * Irritant to veins; extravasation may lead to tissue necrosis
* **Pharmacokinetics:**
  * 60% plasma protein-bound
  * Volume of distribution: 3.5 L/kg
  * Rapidly metabolized by red blood cell esterases to inactive acid metabolites
  * Half-life: 10 minutes
  * Esterases responsible are distinct from plasma cholinesterase (no interaction with suxamethonium)

**Bottom Line:** Beta blockers provide effective management of various cardiovascular conditions through competitive beta-receptor antagonism. Selection between cardioselective (β1) and non-selective agents should consider the patient's comorbidities, particularly respiratory conditions.

  * Weak enzyme inhibitor of CYP2C9, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4
  * Metabolite desethylamiodarone inhibits CYP1A1, 1A2, 2B6, and 2D6 [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Anesthetic considerations:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Patients on amiodarone undergoing general anesthesia may develop bradycardia and complete AV nodal block resistant to atropine, epinephrine, and norepinephrine, potentially requiring temporary pacing [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Amiodarone is a complex antiarrhythmic with multiple mechanisms of action and extremely variable pharmacokinetics. While effective for resistant arrhythmias, it requires careful monitoring for numerous potential toxicities, particularly thyroid, pulmonary, and hepatic effects.

## 2.5. CLASS IV AGENTS (CALCIUM CHANNEL BLOCKERS)






### 2.5.1. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* **Cardiac electrophysiologic effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Modify plateau phase in non-pacemaker cells
  * Inhibit phase 0 depolarization in pacemaker cells by blocking calcium channels [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 2.5.1.1. VERAPAMIL 

* **Classification:** Competitive calcium channel antagonist
* **Presentation:** Film-coated and modified-release tablets and solution for IV injection (2.5 mg/ml)
* **Uses:** Treatment of SVT, atrial fibrillation/flutter (may slow or convert to sinus rhythm), prophylaxis of angina, hypertension
* **Mechanism of action:**
  * Prevents calcium influx through voltage-sensitive slow (L) channels in SA and AV nodes, reducing automaticity
  * Less marked effect on contractile cardiac tissue, but reduces calcium influx during plateau phase 2
  * Reduces rate of conduction through AV node and causes coronary artery dilation
* **Side effects:**
  * **Cardiac:** May precipitate VT in Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome due to increased conduction across accessory pathway
  * May precipitate cardiac failure in patients with poor left ventricular function
  * May cause serious bradycardia and AV block when used with other agents that slow AV conduction (digoxin, beta-blockers, halothane)
  * May increase serum digoxin levels
  * May cause hypotension through vascular smooth muscle relaxation
  * **Non-cardiac:** Cerebral artery vasodilation
* **Pharmacokinetics:**
  * Used orally and intravenously
  * ~90% absorbed from gut, but first-pass metabolism reduces oral bioavailability to ~25%
  * ~90% protein-bound
  * Metabolized in liver to at least 12 inactive metabolites excreted in urine
  * Volume of distribution: 3-5 L/kg
  * Elimination half-life: 3-7 hours (prolonged with higher doses as hepatic enzymes become saturated)

**Bottom Line:** Class IV antiarrhythmics work by blocking calcium channels, primarily affecting nodal tissues, slowing conduction and reducing automaticity.

## 2.6. OTHER ANTIARRHYTHMICS








### 2.6.1. ADENOSINE







**Ultra-short-acting agent used primarily for diagnosis and treatment of supraventricular tachycardias.**

#### 2.6.1.1. PROPERTIES AND PRESENTATION

* **Nature:** Endogenous purine nucleoside [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Formulation:** Clear colorless solution for injection (3mg/ml) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Dosing:** Given in 3mg, 6mg, and 12mg boluses as needed [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

#### 2.6.1.2. INDICATIONS

* **Primary use:** Treatment of SVT by temporarily blocking AV node [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Can convert to sinus rhythm or help identify underlying tachyarrhythmia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * 💡 Does not affect ventricular arrhythmias [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Usually effective for AV nodal re-entry tachycardias but typically doesn't cardiovert atrial fibrillation or flutter [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

#### 2.6.1.3. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* **Target:** A1 receptors at sinoatrial and atrioventricular nodes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Effect:** Opens potassium channels, hyperpolarizing nodal cells [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Effect enhanced by dipyridamole (inhibits cellular reuptake)
* **Duration:** Extremely short half-life of approximately 10 seconds [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Metabolized to inosine and adenosine monophosphate

#### 2.6.1.4. SIDE EFFECTS

* **Cardiovascular:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Transient AV block and bradycardia
  * ⚠️ Contraindicated in severe heart block and sick sinus syndrome [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Respiratory:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Can trigger bronchospasm in susceptible patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Neurological:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Anxiety and sense of doom or impending death
  * Typically very transient due to short half-life [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Adenosine provides rapid, short-lived AV nodal blockade useful for terminating or diagnosing SVTs. Its ultra-short half-life minimizes the duration of side effects but also necessitates rapid administration.

### 2.6.2. DIGOXIN







**Cardiac glycoside with positive inotropic and negative chronotropic effects.**

#### 2.6.2.1. CLASSIFICATION AND MECHANISM

* **Class:** Cardiac glycoside [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Mechanism of action:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Reversibly inhibits Na+/K+/ATPase pump, causing increased intracellular sodium and subsequently calcium, improving contractility [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Parasympathomimetic effect inhibiting AV node by increasing acetylcholine release at muscarinic receptors, reducing conduction and heart rate [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Increased calcium prolongs phases 0 and 4 and increases AV node refractory period [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Shortens QT interval but prolongs PR; reduces dromotropy while increasing inotropy

#### 2.6.2.2. INDICATIONS

* **Heart failure:** Improves cardiac contractility [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Arrhythmias:** Induces AV blockade, especially when other treatments have failed [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

#### 2.6.2.3. SIDE EFFECTS

* **Cardiovascular:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Can trigger ventricular tachyarrhythmias [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Neurological:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Headache
* **Endocrine:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Gynecomastia
  * Causes hyperkalaemia
* **Musculoskeletal:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Rash
  * Weakness

#### 2.6.2.4. TOXICITY

* **Toxic level:** ⚠️ 2.0 ng/ml can cause fatal arrhythmias [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Potentiated by hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, hypercalcaemia
* **Manifestations:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Nausea and vomiting
  * Xanthopsia (yellow/green visual changes)
  * ⚠️ Hyperkalemia due to Na+/K+/ATPase inhibition [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Management:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Atropine or pacing for symptomatic bradycardia
  * Phenytoin or lidocaine (Class I anti-arrhythmic) for ventricular arrhythmias
  * Digibind (digoxin-specific antibody fragments) for severe cases [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Correct hypokalaemia and other precipitating electrolyte imbalances (i.e. hypomagnaesaemia, hypercalcaemia)

#### 2.6.2.5. CONTRAINDICATIONS

* **Absolute:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Acute MI or myocarditis
  * Allergy
  * Hypomagnesemia or hypokalemia
  * Conduction pathway abnormality such as WPW [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

#### 2.6.2.6. DRUG INTERACTIONS

* **Increased digoxin levels with:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Macrolides and antifungals via P-glycoprotein inhibition
  * Loop diuretics (causing electrolyte disturbances)
  * ⚠️ IV calcium administration (can precipitate "stone heart") [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

#### 2.6.2.7. ADDITIONAL PHARMACOLOGICAL DETAILS 

* **Presentation:** Available as oral (tablets of 62.5-250 μg, elixir 50 μg/ml) and intravenous (100-250 μg/ml) preparations
* **Dosing:** Treatment starts with a loading dose of 1.0-1.5 mg in divided doses over 24 hours followed by a maintenance dose of 125-500 μg per day
* **Therapeutic range:** 1-2 μg/L
* **ECG signs:** Prolonged PR interval, characteristic ST segment depression, T wave flattening/inverted/biphasic, and shortened QT interval (not signs of toxicity), U waves, J point depression
* **DC cardioversion:** Severe ventricular arrhythmias may be precipitated in patients with toxic levels; recommended to withhold digoxin for 24 hours before elective cardioversion
* **Pharmacokinetics:**
  * Oral bioavailability >70%
  * About 25% plasma protein-bound
  * Volume of distribution: 5-10 L/kg (increased in thyrotoxicosis, decreased in hypothyroidism)
  * Minimal hepatic metabolism
  * Excreted mainly unchanged by filtration at the glomerulus and active tubular secretion
  * Elimination half-life: ~35 hours (significantly increased in renal failure)
* **Treatment of toxicity:**
  * Cautious use of gastric lavage (vagal stimulation may worsen bradycardia)
  * Correct electrolyte abnormalities, especially potassium
  * Atropine or pacing for symptomatic bradycardia
  * Digoxin-specific Fab indicated for severe toxicity (plasma levels >20 μg/L, life-threatening arrhythmias, uncontrolled hyperkalemia)

**Bottom Line:** Digoxin improves cardiac contractility while slowing heart rate through Na+/K+/ATPase inhibition. Its narrow therapeutic window requires careful monitoring of drug levels and electrolytes, particularly potassium and magnesium.

## 3.2. PERIOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT##
- Hypokalemia is common in hypertensive patients on long-term diuretic therapy (~25%), and is associated with increased arrythmias risks (especially for cardiac surgery)
- Preoperative diuretics associated with intraoperative hypotension and AKI (Kheterpal et al, J Cardiothorac Vasc Anesth, 2008; Tagawa, PLoS One, 2015)
- Generally recommended to withhold diuretics before surgery
	- Unless patient has volume overload, or requires diuresis for clinical optimisation
## 3.3. LOOP DIURETICS








### 3.3.1. USES

* **Heart failure** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Renal failure** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 3.3.2. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* **Primary target:** Inhibition of Na+/K+/2Cltransporter in thick ascending limb of loop of Henle [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Physiological effect:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Prevents the countercurrent multiplier from functioning effectively
  * Reduces osmotic tension of the interstitium in the renal medulla
  * Reduces water reabsorption [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 3.3.3. EFFECTS AND SIDE EFFECTS

* **Cardiovascular:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Reduced circulating volume
  * Arterial vasodilation causing decreased SVR
  * Venodilation reducing preload, especially with IV bolus administration [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Renal:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Increased renal perfusion
* **Metabolic:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Hypokalaemia
  * ⚠️ Hyponatraemia
  * ⚠️ Hypomagnesaemia
  * ⚠️ Hypochloraemic alkalosis
  * ⚠️ Hyperuricaemia
  * ⚠️ Hypocalcaemia (unlike thiazides which cause hypercalcaemia)
  * Rarely hyperglycaemia and hypertriglyceridaemia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Toxic effects:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Ototoxic if large dose given quickly
  * Bumetanide can cause muscle pain [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 3.3.4. PHARMACOKINETICS

* **Absorption:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Good oral absorption and bioavailability
	* 65% for furosemide
	* 95% for bumetanide [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Distribution:** >90% protein bound [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Metabolism:** Minimal [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Excretion:** Renal [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Loop diuretics are potent agents that inhibit Na+/K+/2Clcotransport in the thick ascending limb, providing rapid diuresis. While effective for heart failure and fluid overload, they require careful monitoring for electrolyte abnormalities, particularly hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia.

## 3.4. THIAZIDE DIURETICS








### 3.4.1. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* **Target site:** Distal convoluted tubule [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Primary effect:** Inhibit sodium and chloride reabsorption from the tubule back into blood [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Secondary effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Increased sodium excretion causes increased water excretion
  * Increased sodium in distal tubule favors sodium/potassium and sodium/hydrogen ion exchange
	* Results in hypokalaemia and hypochloraemic alkalosis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 3.4.2. EFFECTS AND SIDE EFFECTS

* **Cardiovascular:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Reduction in SVR and blood pressure by reducing circulating volume [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Renal:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Decreased renal perfusion and GFR [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Metabolic:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Hypokalaemia
  * ⚠️ Hypochloraemia
  * ⚠️ Metabolic alkalosis
  * ⚠️ Hypercalcaemia (unlike loop diuretics)
  * ⚠️ Hypomagnesaemia
  * ⚠️ Hyperuricaemia and gout
  * ⚠️ Hyperglycaemia due to inhibition of glycogenesis
  * ⚠️ Hypertriglyceridaemia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

#### 3.4.2.1. ADDITIONAL DETAILS 

* **Chemical structure:** Thiazides are chemically related to sulfonamides
* **Uses:** Widely used for mild heart failure and hypertension, alone or in combination with other drugs
  * Also used in diabetes insipidus (complex mechanism not fully understood)
  * Used in renal tubular acidosis
* **Main differences between agents:** Rate of absorption from gut due to differences in lipid solubility, and rate of onset/duration of action due to handling by renal tubule
* **Specific agents:** Bendroflumethiazide, Chlorothiazide, Metolazone
  * Metolazone has powerful synergistic effects when combined with loop diuretics (useful in severe edema)
* **Additional side effects:**
  * **Haematological:** Various blood dyscrasias including aplastic and hemolytic anemia, leucopenia, agranulocytosis, and thrombocytopenia
  * **Miscellaneous:** Impotence, rash, photosensitivity, rarely pancreatitis with bendroflumethiazide
  * **Interactions:** Hypokalaemia may prolong duration of non-depolarizing muscle relaxants; NSAIDs antagonize thiazide effects
* **Pharmacokinetics:**
  * Bendroflumethiazide well absorbed from gut vs. chlorothiazide (incompletely/variably absorbed)
  * Both produce effects within 90 minutes of oral administration
  * Bendroflumethiazide has twice the duration of action (18-24 hours)
  * Chlorothiazide eliminated solely via kidney (none metabolized)
  * Bendroflumethiazide 70% metabolized in liver to active metabolites, rest eliminated unchanged in urine

**Bottom Line:** Thiazide diuretics inhibit sodium and chloride reabsorption in the distal convoluted tubule. While effective for hypertension management, they can cause multiple metabolic derangements, particularly affecting potassium, calcium, glucose, and lipid metabolism.

## 3.5. POTASSIUM-SPARING DIURETICS








### 3.5.1. ALDOSTERONE ANTAGONISTS







**Competitive antagonists of aldosterone action in the distal tubule.**

#### 3.5.1.1. PROPERTIES AND INDICATIONS

* **Examples:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Spironolactone
  * Eplerenone
  * Triamterene
* **Uses:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Nephrotic syndrome
  * Ascites
  * Conn's syndrome (primary hyperaldosteronism) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

#### 3.5.1.2. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* **Primary effect:** Competitive aldosterone antagonism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Result:** Reduced sodium reabsorption in distal convoluted tubule, increasing water excretion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 3.5.1.3. EFFECTS AND SIDE EFFECTS

* **Metabolic:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Hyperkalemia (opposite of loop and thiazide diuretics)
  * Hyponatremia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Endocrine:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Gynecomastia (spironolactone)
  * Irregular menstruation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

#### 3.5.1.4. PHARMACOKINETICS

* **Absorption:** Poorly absorbed [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Distribution:** Highly protein bound [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Metabolism:** Almost completely metabolized in liver [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Excretion:** Renal [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 3.5.1.5. ADDITIONAL DETAILS 

* **Onset of effects:** Due to mechanism of action, effects take a few days to become established
* **Parenteral form:** Potassium canrenoate is available parenterally and metabolized to canrenone (also a metabolite of spironolactone)
* **Limitations:** Diuresis produced is limited as only 2% of Na+ reabsorption is under aldosterone control
* **Contraindications:** Addison's disease

**Bottom Line:** Aldosterone antagonists provide potassium-sparing diuresis through competitive blockade of aldosterone receptors. Their risk of causing hyperkalemia requires careful monitoring, particularly in patients with renal impairment or taking other potassium-retaining medications.

### 3.5.2. AMILORIDE





**Direct epithelial sodium channel blocker in the distal tubule.**

#### 3.5.2.1. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* **Drug class:** Potassium-sparing diuretic [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Primary effect:** Inhibits sodium-potassium exchange in distal tubule [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Result:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Increases sodium and water excretion
  * Reduces potassium excretion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 3.5.2.2. ADDITIONAL DETAILS 

* **Uses:** Weak diuretic frequently used in combination with loop diuretics to prevent hypokalemia
* **Pharmacokinetics:**
  * Poorly absorbed from gut
  * Minimally protein-bound
  * Not metabolized

**Bottom Line:** Amiloride directly blocks epithelial sodium channels in the distal tubule, providing potassium-sparing diuresis through a mechanism different from aldosterone antagonists.

## 3.6. OSMOTIC DIURETICS








### 3.6.1. MANNITOL

* **Mechanism for ICP reduction:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Mannitol cannot cross the intact blood-brain barrier
  * Increases oncotic pressure of blood, drawing fluid from brain interstitium into vasculature
  * ⚠️ If blood-brain barrier is damaged, mannitol can cross into brain tissue and potentially worsen cerebral edema [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Renal mechanism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Freely filtered at glomerulus
  * Not reabsorbed
  * Creates osmotic gradient drawing fluid into renal tubule
  * Generates significant diuresis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Dosing for raised ICP:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Initial dose: 1 g/kg
  * Maintenance: 0.1 g/kg as needed
  * ⚠️ Discontinue if serum osmolality exceeds 320 mosmol/kg [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

#### 3.6.1.1. ADDITIONAL DETAILS 

* **Chemical nature:** Mannitol is a carbohydrate derivative (polyhydric alcohol)
* **Preparation:** Made in water as 10% or 20% solution
* **Additional uses:** Preserving perioperative renal function in jaundiced patients and during major vascular surgery
* **Effects:**
  * **Cardiovascular:** Initially increases circulating volume, preload, and cardiac output (may precipitate heart failure in susceptible patients)
  * **Renal:** Produces osmotic diuresis and increased renal blood flow that may reduce intravascular volume
  * **CNS:** Acute reduction in ICP is not sustained during prolonged administration; may reduce cerebrospinal fluid formation rate
* **Allergic reactions:** Rare
* **Pharmacokinetics:**
  * Minimally absorbed from gut
  * After IV administration, distributes throughout extracellular fluid space
  * Freely filtered at glomerulus and not reabsorbed
  * Elimination half-life: 100 minutes
  * Not significantly metabolized

**Bottom Line:** Mannitol reduces intracranial pressure by creating an osmotic gradient that draws fluid from brain tissue into the vasculature, but requires an intact blood-brain barrier to be effective and safe.

## 3.7. CARBONIC ANHYDRASE INHIBITORS








### 3.7.1. ACETAZOLAMIDE

* **Clinical uses:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Glaucoma
  * Acute mountain sickness
  * Occasionally as a diuretic [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Mechanism of action:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Inhibits carbonic anhydrase, preventing the conversion of CO₂ into HCO₃⁻ and H⁺ ions
  * Particularly affects proximal convoluted tubule
  * Reduces H⁺ excretion, lowering blood pH
  * 💡 Treats acute respiratory alkalosis in hyperventilating patients with acute mountain sickness [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Pharmacokinetics:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Significantly protein bound
  * Excreted unchanged by kidneys [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 3.7.1.1. ADDITIONAL DETAILS 

* **Diuretic properties:** Weak diuretic
* **Effects:**
  * Inhibits H+ excretion and HCO3reabsorption, leading to alkaline urine
  * Slightly increases Na+ and water excretion, providing increased drive for K+ secretion
  * Produces alkaline urine in the presence of hyperchloremic acidosis
* **Pharmacokinetics:**
  * Oral bioavailability >95%
  * Highly protein-bound
  * Excreted unchanged in urine

**Bottom Line:** Acetazolamide inhibits carbonic anhydrase in the proximal tubule, reducing bicarbonate reabsorption. Beyond its diuretic effects, it has important applications in glaucoma management and acute mountain sickness.

# 4. DIABETES MEDICATIONS









## 4.1. INSULIN








### 4.1.1. MECHANISM OF ACTION





**Endogenous peptide hormone essential for glucose regulation.**

#### 4.1.1.1. STRUCTURE AND CHEMICAL PROPERTIES

* **Composition:** 51 amino acid polypeptide composed of A and B chains connected by disulfide bridges [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Production site:** β cells in pancreatic islets of Langerhans [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

#### 4.1.1.2. PHYSIOLOGICAL CONTROL

* **Release mechanism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Glucose enters β cells
  * Increases intracellular ATP
  * Closes potassium channels
  * Membrane depolarization
  * Calcium influx
  * Insulin release [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 

#### 4.1.1.3. CELLULAR MECHANISM

* **Receptor binding:** Binds to tyrosine kinase insulin receptor on the α subunit [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Post-binding:** Forms dimerized complex drawn into the cell [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 4.1.1.4. METABOLIC EFFECTS

* **General action:** Anabolic hormone [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Glucose metabolism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Stimulates glucose uptake, particularly in liver and muscle
  * Inhibits glycogenolysis
* **Lipid metabolism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Increases lipogenesis
  * Inhibits lipolysis
* **Protein metabolism:** Stimulates protein synthesis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 4.1.1.5. CLINICAL INDICATIONS

* **Primary indications:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Diabetes and hyperglycemia management
* **Secondary uses:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Hyperkalaemia treatment
  * β-blocker overdose
  * Calcium channel antagonist overdose [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Insulin is an essential anabolic hormone that regulates glucose, lipid, and protein metabolism through binding to the insulin receptor and triggering intracellular signaling cascades.

### 4.1.2. PREPARATIONS AND TYPES







**Various insulin formulations with different onset and duration profiles.**

#### 4.1.2.1. STANDARD FORMULATION

* **Concentration:** Usually 100 units/ml colorless solution [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 4.1.2.2. CLASSIFICATION BY DURATION

* **Fast-acting:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Novorapid (Aspart)
  * Humalog (Lispro)
* **Intermediate-acting:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Neutral Protamine Hagedorn (isophane)
* **Long-acting:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Levemir (Detemir)
  * Glargine (Lantus)

#### 4.1.2.3. PHARMACOKINETICS

* **Half-lives:** Range from 8 hours (fast-acting) to 36 hours (long-acting) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Modifications:** Different durations achieved by complexing insulin with substances such as protamine and zinc [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 4.1.2.4. INSULIN TYPES AND CHARACTERISTICS 

* **Human insulins:** Produced by recombinant DNA technology, identical to endogenous human insulin
* **Insulin analogues:** Modified human insulins with altered molecular structures resulting in different kinetic profiles
* **Short-acting insulins:**
  * **Human insulin (e.g., Actrapid, Humulin S):** Onset 30 minutes after subcutaneous injection
  * **Rapid-acting analogues (e.g., Aspart, Lispro, Glulisine):** Onset within 15 minutes, duration up to 5 hours
  * Used for meal coverage or in variable rate intravenous insulin infusions (VRIII)
* **Intermediate-acting insulins:**
  * Onset 1-2 hours after subcutaneous injection
  * Peak at 3-12 hours
  * Duration 11-24 hours
  * Often combined with rapid-acting insulins in premixed formulations
* **Long-acting insulins:**
  * Duration up to 36 hours
  * Minimal peaks/troughs at steady state
  * Used in basal-bolus regimens to mimic endogenous basal insulin production

#### 4.1.2.5. SAFE ADMINISTRATION 

* **Standard concentration:** 100 units/ml in the UK
* **Specialized preparations:** Some Type 2 diabetics may use high-concentration insulin (200-300 units/ml)
* **Safety considerations:**
  * Always use dedicated insulin syringes calibrated to 100 units/ml
  * Prescribe using brand name written in full
  * Avoid abbreviations like "U" or "IU" which have led to serious errors
  * When prescribing insulin, always also prescribe hypoglycemia treatment
  * When used intravenously, continuous carbohydrate substrate must be simultaneously infused

**Bottom Line:** Insulin preparations are available in various formulations with different onset and duration profiles, allowing for tailored regimens based on individual patient needs.

## 4.2. ORAL HYPOGLYCEMIC AGENTS








### 4.2.1. CLASSIFICATIONS AND MECHANISMS

* **Seven main categories of commonly used anti-diabetic agents:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Alpha glucosidase inhibitors
  * Biguanides

  * Meglitinides
  * Sodium glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT-2)
  * Sulfonylureas
  * Thiazolidinediones (TZDs)

### 4.2.2. SULFONYLUREAS







**Insulin secretagogues that stimulate pancreatic beta-cell insulin release.**

#### 4.2.2.1. AGENTS AND MECHANISM

* **Examples:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Gliclazide
  * Glibenclamide
* **Mechanism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Stimulate insulin secretion from β-cells of pancreatic islets
  * May also increase insulin sensitivity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 4.2.2.2. SIDE EFFECTS

* **Metabolic:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Hypoglycemia risk when nil by mouth (major perioperative concern) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **General:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Stimulates appetite and weight gain
  * Can trigger cholestasis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

#### 4.2.2.3. ADDITIONAL DETAILS 

* **Classification:**
  * **First generation:** Tolbutamide
  * **Second generation:** Glibenclamide, Gliclazide, Glipizide
* **Pharmacokinetics:**
  * Well absorbed from gut with >80% oral bioavailability
  * Extensively bound to albumin in plasma
  * Second-generation agents bind to albumin by non-ionic forces (unlike tolbutamide which binds by ionic forces)
  * Significant fraction excreted unchanged in urine (half-life prolonged in renal failure)
  * Most are metabolized in liver to inactive metabolites (except glibenclamide which has active metabolites)
* **Drug interactions:**
  * Cimetidine inhibits metabolism, potentiating actions
  * Drugs increasing blood sugar (thiazides, corticosteroids, phenothiazines) antagonize effects
  * Second-generation sulfonylureas less susceptible to displacement by anionic drugs (phenylbutazone, warfarin, salicylate)

**Bottom Line:** Sulfonylureas stimulate insulin secretion from pancreatic beta cells but carry significant hypoglycemia risk when patients are fasting, necessitating careful perioperative management.

### 4.2.3. BIGUANIDES







**Agents that reduce hepatic glucose production and enhance peripheral insulin sensitivity.**

#### 4.2.3.1. AGENT AND MECHANISM

* **Example:** Metformin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Mechanism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Slows absorption of glucose
  * Increases sensitivity to insulin
  * Reduces gluconeogenesis in the liver [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 4.2.3.2. PHARMACOKINETICS

* **Metabolism:** Not metabolized [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 4.2.3.3. SIDE EFFECTS

* **Gastrointestinal:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Nausea and diarrhea (common) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Metabolic:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Risk of lactic acidosis, especially in renal impairment [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

#### 4.2.3.4. METFORMIN-ASSOCIATED LACTIC ACIDOSIS 

* **Physiological context:**
  * In health, the body produces pyruvate by glycolysis which enters the Krebs cycle after conversion to Acetyl Co-A
  * Pyruvate and lactate exist in equilibrium (normally 1:10 lactate:pyruvate ratio)
  * Normally, 20 mmol/kg/day of lactate is produced and metabolized
  * Lactate is a vital metabolic fuel, especially during stress
* **Metformin effects on lactate metabolism:**
  * Increases cellular glucose uptake, leading to more pyruvate and lactate production
  * Reduces lactate clearance through inhibition of gluconeogenesis
* **Risk factors:**
  * Poor renal function (reduced metformin clearance)
  * Impaired hepatic and renal metabolism of lactate
  * Increased production from sepsis, hypoperfusion, or metabolic stress
  * Reduced capacity to utilize pyruvate in Krebs cycle (e.g., thiamine deficiency)

**Bottom Line:** Metformin reduces hepatic glucose production and improves insulin sensitivity without causing hypoglycemia when used alone, but carries a risk of lactic acidosis in patients with renal impairment.

### 4.2.4. DPP-IV INHIBITORS





**Agents that prolong the action of incretin hormones.**

#### 4.2.4.1. EXAMPLES

* Linagliptin
* Saxagliptin
* Sitagliptin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 4.2.4.2. MECHANISM AND EFFECTS 

* **Target:** Inhibit dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) enzyme which normally rapidly inactivates endogenous GLP-1
* **Effect:** Increases duration of action of endogenous GLP-1, enhancing:
  * Insulin secretion from pancreatic β-cells (glucose-dependent)
  * Reduction in glucagon secretion from pancreatic α-cells
  * Delayed gastric emptying
  * Reduced appetite
* **Administration:** Oral agents
* **Considerations:** Generally require dose modification in renal dysfunction (except linagliptin)
* **Side effects:** Associated with pancreatitis

**Bottom Line:** DPP-IV inhibitors prevent the breakdown of incretin hormones, enhancing the insulin response to meals and reducing glucagon secretion.

### 4.2.5. THIAZOLIDINEDIONES







**Insulin sensitizers working through nuclear receptor activation.**

#### 4.2.5.1. AGENT AND MECHANISM

* **Example:** Pioglitazone [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Mechanism:** Stimulates PPAR (peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor) gamma receptor, increasing insulin sensitivity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 4.2.5.2. SIDE EFFECTS

* **Hepatic:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Can cause liver dysfunction requiring monitoring [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Fluid-related:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Can cause fluid retention (concern in heart failure patients) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

#### 4.2.5.3. ADDITIONAL DETAILS 

* **Class context:** Pioglitazone is the only remaining thiazolidinedione available in the UK
* **Withdrawn agents:** Rosiglitazone was withdrawn after association with heart attacks and strokes
* **Onset of effects:** May take weeks to be fully established due to mechanism of action
* **Contraindications:** Heart failure (due to fluid retention risk)
* **Pharmacokinetics:** Extensively metabolized by hepatic cytochrome P450
* **Active metabolites:** Some metabolites remain active
* **Excretion:** Metabolites excreted in feces and urine

**Bottom Line:** Thiazolidinediones improve insulin sensitivity through PPAR-gamma activation but require liver function monitoring and caution in patients with heart failure due to fluid retention.

### 4.2.6. SGLT2 INHIBITORS (GLIFLOZINS)







**Agents that promote urinary glucose excretion.**

#### 4.2.6.1. AGENT AND MECHANISM

* **Example:** Dapagliflozin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Mechanism:** Prevents glucose reabsorption by inhibiting renal SGLT2 (sodium-glucose cotransporter-2) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 4.2.6.2. SIDE EFFECTS

* **Urogenital:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Glycosuria can cause urinary tract infections [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Metabolic:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Weight loss (generally beneficial)
  * ⚠️ Can trigger euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

#### 4.2.6.3. ADDITIONAL DETAILS 

* **Physiological context:** Glucose freely passes into kidney ultrafiltrate and is normally almost completely reabsorbed via SGLT2 in the proximal convoluted tubule
* **Effects:** SGLT2 inhibition prevents this reabsorption, reducing blood glucose
* **Expected side effects:** Polyuria, polydipsia, and risk of dehydration
* **Serious complications:** Reports of euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA without markedly raised glucose levels)
* **Testing recommendation:** Patients on SGLT2 inhibitors should be tested for ketones when presenting with symptoms of acidosis to avoid delayed diagnosis

**Bottom Line:** SGLT2 inhibitors lower blood glucose by promoting urinary glucose excretion, but carry risks of urinary tract infections and euglycemic DKA, particularly in the perioperative period.

### 4.2.7. MEGLITINIDES





**Rapid-acting insulin secretagogues.**

#### 4.2.7.1. AGENT AND MECHANISM

* **Example:** Metiglinide [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Mechanism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Inhibit potassium channels in pancreas
  * Causing increased insulin secretion
* **Characteristic:** Short-acting [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 4.2.7.2. ADDITIONAL DETAILS 

* **Examples:** Repaglinide, Nateglinide
* **Clinical use:** May be helpful for patients with erratic eating habits due to short action duration
* **Administration:** Taken just before meals
* **Detailed mechanism:** Block potassium channels in pancreatic β-cells, closing ATP-dependent potassium channels and opening calcium channels, leading to insulin secretion
* **Pharmacokinetics:**
  * Repaglinide has 50% oral bioavailability
  * 98% plasma protein-bound
  * Metabolized by CYP3A4 (potential drug interactions)
  * Elimination half-life: 1 hour

**Bottom Line:** Meglitinides stimulate insulin secretion similar to sulfonylureas but have a shorter duration of action, allowing for more flexible dosing with meals.

### 4.2.8. GLP-1 ANALOGUES





**Injectable incretin mimetics that enhance glucose-dependent insulin secretion.**

#### 4.2.8.1. EXAMPLES

* Exenatide
* Liraglutide [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 4.2.8.2. ADDITIONAL DETAILS 

* **Expanded examples:** Dulaglutide, Lixisenatide
* **Administration:** Subcutaneous route (endogenous GLP-1 rapidly degraded in gut)
* **Dosing variations:** Some preparations administered weekly with prolonged effects
* **Mechanism:**
  * Mimic actions of endogenous GLP-1
  * Increase insulin secretion (glucose-dependent)
  * Reduce glucagon secretion
  * Delay gastric emptying
  * Increase satiety and reduce appetite
* **Benefits:** Associated with weight loss and improved cardiovascular profiles
* **Side effects:**
  * Nausea (common)
  * Associated with pancreatitis
* **Contraindications:** Most are contraindicated in moderate renal impairment

**Bottom Line:** GLP-1 analogues mimic the action of incretin hormones, stimulating insulin secretion in a glucose-dependent manner, slowing gastric emptying, and suppressing glucagon release.

## 4.3. PERIOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT OF DIABETIC PATIENTS








### 4.3.1. VARIABLE RATE INSULIN INFUSION CONSIDERATIONS

* **Indications:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Consider VRII if more than one meal will be missed [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 4.3.2. MEDICATION MANAGEMENT

* **Pre-day of surgery:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Continue normal treatment up to and including day before surgery [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Day of surgery decisions:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Morning list:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Continue metformin and pioglitazone
	* Omit any other oral agents
	* Reduce insulin dose [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Afternoon list:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Omit sulfonylureas, GLP-1 analogues, and gliptins
	* Continue metformin and acarbose [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

#### 4.3.2.1. ADDITIONAL PERIOPERATIVE GUIDELINES 

* **Terminology:** Variable rate intravenous insulin infusions (VRII) preferred over "sliding scales"
* **VRII indications:** Generally needed when starvation period exceeds one missed meal
* **Long-acting insulin considerations:** When VRII indicated, continue long-acting insulin alongside VRII (at 20% dose reduction) to prevent rebound hyperglycemia when VRII ends
* **Oral hypoglycemic management:**
  * All can be taken normally the day before surgery
  * On surgery day with only one missed meal:
	* GLP-1 agonists, DPP-4 inhibitors, and pioglitazone can be taken normally
	* All others should be suspended
  * Metformin controversy: Some maintain at reduced dose for day surgery with good renal function not receiving IV contrast; many hospitals recommend suspension for all surgery
  * With VRII, suspend all oral agents except GLP-1 agonists

**Bottom Line:** Perioperative diabetes management requires a tailored approach based on the patient's medication regimen, surgical timing, and expected duration of fasting, with particular attention to minimizing hypoglycemia risk.
