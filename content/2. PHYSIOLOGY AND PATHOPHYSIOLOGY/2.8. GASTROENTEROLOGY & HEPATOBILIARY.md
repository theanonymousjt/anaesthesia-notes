# 1. GASTROINTESTINAL PHYSIOLOGY

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|Part A: GI Physiology]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C]]

## 1.2. STRUCTURE AND FUNCTION OF THE GI TRACT

**The gastrointestinal tract consists of specialized layers and tissues working together for digestion and absorption.**

### 1.2.1. STRUCTURE

* **Five layers of the GI tract** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Mucosa (innermost layer)
  * Submucosa
  * Circular muscle
  * Longitudinal muscle
  * Outer serosa layer

### 1.2.2. ENTERIC NERVOUS SYSTEM

* **Two major plexuses integrated into the autonomic nervous system** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * **Auerbach's myenteric plexus** - located between the circular and longitudinal muscle layers
  * **Meissner's submucosal plexus** - located in the submucosa
* **Submucosa contains** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Nerve cells of Meissner's plexus
  * Blood vessels
  * Lymphatic vessels
  * Elastic connective tissue

### 1.2.3. AUTONOMIC INNERVATION

* **Parasympathetic innervation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Vagal input to proximal GI tract
  * Sacral input to distal GI tract
  * Effect: Increases GI motility ("rest and digest")
* **Sympathetic innervation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * T5-L1 spinal levels
  * Effect: Reduces motility, increases sphincter tone

### 1.2.4. MOTILITY PATTERNS

* **Two main muscle movements** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * **Peristalsis** - coordinated, progressive contractions that propel contents forward
	* Smooth muscle fibers are joined longitudinally to allow action potentials to travel along the GI tract
	* Stimulated by: chyme entering duodenum, CCK, and gastrin
	* Inhibited by: glucagon and secretin
  * **Mixing** - localized contractions that mix contents with digestive secretions
* **Migrating motor complex** - occurs during prolonged fasting to clear excess secretions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** The GI tract has a specialized multi-layered structure with its own nervous system (enteric) that works in coordination with the autonomic nervous system to control motility and secretion.

## 1.3. SALIVA

### 1.3.1. FUNCTIONS OF SALIVA

**Saliva serves multiple important functions in digestion and oral health.**

* **Volume and composition** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * 500-1000 mL secreted daily by parotid, submandibular, and sublingual glands
  * 98% water, 2% solutes including:
	* **Electrolytes** - hypotonic with lower Na+ but higher K+ than plasma
	* **Proteins and enzymes** - mucin, haptocorrin, α-amylase, lingual lipase
	* **Bactericidal substances** - thiocyanate, lysozyme, lactoferrin, immunoglobulin A
* **Primary functions** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **Lubrication of food** - protects pharyngeal and esophageal mucosa during swallowing
  * **Digestion**:
	* α-amylase begins carbohydrate breakdown (can cleave up to 75% of starch before denaturation)
	* Lingual lipase starts triglyceride digestion (especially important in neonates)
	* Haptocorrin binds vitamin B12, protecting it from stomach acid
  * **Neutralization of acid** - dilutes and neutralizes gastric acid during reflux or vomiting
  * **Antibacterial effects** - contains multiple bactericidal components, though evidence for significant bacteriostatic action in humans is limited

### 1.3.2. PRODUCTION OF SALIVA

**Saliva production occurs in two phases involving acinar and ductal cells.**

* **Secretory mechanism** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **Acinar cells** produce primary secretion:
	* Active transport of electrolytes followed by passive water movement
	* Primary secretion is approximately isotonic
	* Na+, Cl−, and HCO3− concentrations resemble plasma
  * **Ductal cells** modify primary secretion:
	* Na+ and Cl− are reabsorbed
	* K+ and HCO3− are secreted
	* Reabsorption exceeds secretion, making saliva hypotonic
* **Effect of secretion rate** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Higher rates: saliva rich in Na+ and HCO3−
  * Lower rates: greater proportion of K+ and Cl−
  * Aldosterone increases Na+ reabsorption and K+ secretion (similar to kidney)

### 1.3.3. INNERVATION OF SALIVARY GLANDS

**Salivary glands receive both parasympathetic and sympathetic innervation.**

* **Parasympathetic effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Produces vasodilatation of blood vessels supplying acini
  * Causes myoepithelial cell contraction
  * Results in secretion of serous, electrolyte-rich saliva
  * **Origin of fibers**:
	* Parotid gland: glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX) with synapse at otic ganglion
	* Submandibular and sublingual glands: facial nerve (CN VII) with synapse at submandibular ganglion
* **Sympathetic effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Produces vasoconstriction of blood vessels supplying acini
  * Causes myoepithelial cell contraction
  * Results in brief increase in mucous, amylase-rich saliva followed by decreased production

**Bottom Line:** Saliva is produced by specialized glands with dual innervation, creating a complex fluid that serves digestive, protective, and lubricating functions in the oral cavity and upper GI tract.

## 1.4. ESOPHAGUS AND SWALLOWING

### 1.4.1. PHASES OF SWALLOWING

**Swallowing is a complex process involving coordinated muscular activity in three phases.**

* **Oral phase** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Only voluntary phase of swallowing
  * Food bolus pushed against hard palate by tongue
  * Sensory information from hard palate fed back to medulla via glossopharyngeal nerve
  * Triggers initiation of involuntary phases
* **Pharyngeal phase** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Under involuntary control from medullary swallowing center
  * Coordinated actions include:
	* Closure of nasopharynx by soft palate
	* Protection of laryngeal inlet by vocal cord adduction and aryepiglottic fold adduction
	* Hyoid elevation (by digastric and stylohyoid muscles)
	* Food bolus propelled by successive contractions of pharyngeal constrictors
	* Relaxation of upper esophageal sphincter (cricopharyngeus)
  * Breathing is involuntarily ceased for 1-2 seconds during this phase
* **Esophageal phase** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Involuntary process once bolus enters esophagus
  * Upper esophageal sphincter closes
  * Lower esophageal sphincter (LOS) partially relaxes
  * Peristaltic waves propel food bolus:
	* **Primary peristalsis** - initiated by medullary swallowing center
	* **Secondary peristalsis** - initiated by food bolus stretching esophageal wall
  * LOS fully relaxes as peristaltic wave approaches, then contracts to prevent reflux
* **Clinical relevance: aspiration pneumonia** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Occurs when foreign materials enter tracheobronchial tree
  * Common causes include:
	* Decreased conscious level (alcohol, drugs, anesthesia)
	* Esophageal disorders (reflux, stricture, fistula)
	* Swallowing problems from neurological or muscular disorders

### 1.4.2. FUNCTIONAL ANATOMY OF ESOPHAGUS

**The esophagus has specialized features to facilitate food transit.**

* **Key anatomical features** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Upper third: skeletal (striated) muscle
  * Lower two-thirds: smooth muscle
  * Upper esophageal sphincter (cricopharyngeus): high resting pressure (up to 100 mmHg)
  * Lower esophageal sphincter (LOS): lower resting pressure, assisted by diaphragmatic contraction
  * Acute angle at diaphragmatic passage reducing reflux

### 1.4.3. LOWER ESOPHAGEAL SPHINCTER

**The lower esophageal sphincter (LOS) is a specialized muscle that prevents gastric reflux.**

* **Function** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Maintains closure between swallows
  * Closes at around 20 mmHg above intragastric pressure
  * Prevents reflux of gastric contents into the esophagus
* **Barrier pressure** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Defined as difference between LOS pressure (20-30 mmHg) and intragastric pressure (5-10 mmHg)
* **Factors affecting LOS tone** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:

| Decreases sphincter tone            | Increases sphincter tone |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| Atropine and glycopyrrolate         | Acetylcholine            |
| Swallowing                          | Gastrin                  |
| Dopamine                            | Metoclopramide           |
| Thiopentone                         | Cyclizine                |
| Alcohol                             | Suxamethonium            |
| Estrogen and progesterone           |                          |
| Hiatus hernia (loss of acute angle) |                          |

* **Clinical relevance: gastro-oesophageal reflux disease** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Caused by gastric acid entering esophagus resulting in mucosal damage
  * Contributing factors:
	* Raised abdominal pressure (obesity, pregnancy, acute abdomen)
	* Reduced LOS barrier pressure
	* Anatomical changes (hiatus hernia)
  * Mendelson's syndrome: pneumonitis from pulmonary aspiration during anesthesia
  * Prevention strategies:
	* Preoperative fasting
	* Nasogastric drainage for intestinal obstruction
	* H2-receptor antagonists or non-particulate antacids
	* Rapid sequence induction with cricoid pressure
	* Semi-recumbent positioning for induction

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Awareness of factors affecting LOS tone is essential for anesthetic management, particularly in patients at risk for aspiration.

**Bottom Line:** The LOS is a critical barrier preventing gastroesophageal reflux, with its tone influenced by multiple medications commonly used in anesthesia. The swallowing mechanism involves coordinated activity across three phases, with important implications for aspiration risk.

## 1.5. STOMACH

### 1.5.1. FUNCTION AND STRUCTURE

**The stomach is a muscular, expandable organ that stores, mixes, and begins digestion of food.**

* **Primary functions** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Storage of food (up to 4 liters)
  * Mechanical digestion (grinding and mixing)
  * Chemical digestion (acid and enzymes)
  * Secretion of hormones
  * Regulation of passage of food into the duodenum
* **Additional functions** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Temporary storage of large meals
  * Secretion of digestive enzymes
  * Mixing via vigorous contractions
  * Secretion of gastric acid for immune defense
  * Secretion of intrinsic factor for vitamin B12 absorption
  * Endocrine function (secreting hormones to control gastric emptying)
* **Muscular structure** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Forms a syncytium via gap junctions
  * Contraction intensity determined by the **frequency** of **spike potentials**
  * Activity increased by parasympathetic and decreased by sympathetic stimulation

### 1.5.2. GLANDS AND SECRETIONS

**The stomach contains specialized glands that produce various secretions.**

* **Oxyntic glands** (fundus and body) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * **Chief cells** - produce pepsinogen
  * **Mucous cells** - produce protective mucus
  * **Parietal cells** - produce hydrochloric acid and intrinsic factor
* **Pyloric glands** (antrum) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * **Mucous cells** - produce protective mucus
  * **G-cells** - produce gastrin
* **Substances secreted by the stomach** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Approximately 2L of gastric fluid produced daily
  * **Hydrochloric acid** (HCl) - secreted by parietal cells to concentrations of up to 150 mmol/L (pH 0.8)
  * **Pepsinogen** - proenzyme secreted by chief cells, converted to pepsin in acidic environment
  * **Gastrin** - peptide hormone from G cells that stimulates acid secretion, pepsinogen release, and gastric motility
  * **Intrinsic factor** - glycopeptide from parietal cells essential for vitamin B12 absorption
  * **Mucus** - secreted by mucous cells for protection and lubrication
* **Cell types and secretions summary** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:

| Cell type | Chemical secreted |
| --- | --- |
| Chief Cell | Pepsinogen |
| Mucous Cell | Mucus |
| Parietal Cell | HCl, Intrinsic factor |
| D cell | Somatostatin (endocrine hormone) |
| G Cell | Gastrin (endocrine hormone) |

### 1.5.3. GASTRIC ACID AND ITS ROLE

* **Functions of stomach acid** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Immune function - destruction of pathogens
  * Activation of enzymes such as pepsin for protein digestion
  * Improved solubility of ions such as calcium and iron
* **Parietal cell acid secretion mechanism** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * CO₂ diffuses into parietal cell from blood
  * CO₂ reacts with water to form H₂CO₃ (catalyzed by carbonic anhydrase)
  * H₂CO₃ dissociates into H⁺ and HCO₃⁻
  * H⁺/K⁺-ATPase (proton pump) actively secretes H⁺ into gastric lumen
  * HCO₃⁻ exchanged for Cl⁻ at basolateral membrane (creating "alkaline tide" in blood)
  * Cl⁻ and K⁺ diffuse into secretory canaliculi
  * Na-K ATPase maintains high intracellular K+ levels for concentration gradient for K+ to diffuse doen
  * Net result: HCl secretion into stomach and NaHCO₃ into bloodstream
  ![[Pasted image 20250602172049.png]]
* **Stimuli for acid secretion** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Histamine (most important) - acts on H₂ receptors, increasing cAMP
  * Acetylcholine - via vagus nerve acting on muscarinic M₃ receptors
  * Gastrin - least important direct stimulus but triggers histamine release from ECL cells

### 1.5.4. REGULATION OF GASTRIC SECRETION

* **Phases of secretion** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * **Cephalic phase** - sight and smell of food triggers vagus nerve, increasing gastric secretion
  * **Gastric phase** - food enters stomach and stimulates vagus nerve and local reflexes; triggers gastrin release
  * **Intestinal phase** - gastric content, particularly protein, enters duodenum, causing further stimulation
* **Phases of gastric secretion** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **Cephalic phase** (30% of total acid per meal) - anticipation, smell, sight of food activates vagus nerve
  * **Gastric phase** (60% of total acid) - gastric distension triggers gastrin release
  * **Intestinal phase** - acidic chyme enters duodenum, triggering secretin release which:
	* Stimulates pancreatic HCO₃⁻ secretion
	* Inhibits gastrin release and thus reduces acid production
* **Factors that increase gastric secretion** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Acetylcholine (from vagus nerve)
  * Histamine
  * Gastrin
  * Amino acids
  * Caffeine
  * Alcohol
* **Factors that reduce gastric secretion** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Prostaglandins
  * Enterogastric reflex
  * Secretin (released in response to stomach distension, increased pH, protein, and fat)

### 1.5.5. PHASES OF GASTRIC RESPONSE TO FOOD

1. Fasting State
2. Relaxation
	- Receptive relaxation: vago-vagal reflex triggered by passage of food along esophagus
	- Adaptive relaxation: triggered by presence of food in the stomach
3. Peristalsis
	- Fundus > Pylorus
	- Changes in membrane potential (slow waves)
	- Pacemaker cells within longitudinal muscle (interstitial cells of Cajal)
	- Vagal stimulation > increased frequency of slow waves
	- Gastrin / Ach > increased amplitude and force of contractions
4. Retropulsion
	- Antral contraction > retrograde movement
	- Back and forth movement from peristalsis and retropulsion > food broken down, mixed with gastric sections ("chyme")

### 1.5.6. GASTRIC EMPTYING

* **Factors affecting gastric emptying time** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:

| Delays gastric emptying | Accelerates gastric emptying |
| --- | --- |
| Atropine and glycopyrrolate | Stomach stretch |
| High fat and protein content entering duodenum | Acetylcholine and anticholinesterases |
| Distension of duodenum | Prokinetics such as metoclopramide |
| Stress, pain, trauma | |
| Opioids | |
| Alcohol | |

* **Gastric emptying control** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **Factors affecting rate**:
	* **Consistency** - liquids pass faster than solids (pylorus constricts for solids)
		* Half time of liquids emptying ~20min; 
		* Solids have a lag time of ~30min before emptying starts
	* **Volume** - increased volume promotes emptying > vago-vagal reflexes + increased gastrin secretion > increased antral pump activity and emptying
	* **Content** - protein emptied most rapidly, followed by carbohydrate, then fat (?NUH says carbohydrate > protein > fat)
  * **Four duodenal factors slowing emptying**:
	* **Duodenal distension** - reflex inhibition via enteric nervous system
	* **Acid** - low duodenal pH triggers secretin release, inhibiting gastrin secretion
	* **Fat** - triggers CCK release, increasing pyloric tone
	* **Hyperosmolarity** - hyperosmolar chyme inhibits emptying via neural reflexes
  * **Other inhibitory factors**:
	* Sympathetic nervous system activity
	* Pain
	* Drugs (especially opioids)
	* Diseases (diabetic autonomic neuropathy, acute abdomen, ileus)
 -  **Migratory Motor Complex**: peristaltic wave every 60-90min starting from esophagus down the whole GIT (clears remnant food in stomach)
* **Gastric emptying duration** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **Solids** - initial 20-30 minute lag period followed by linear emptying
  * **Liquids** - exponential emptying pattern without lag phase
  * **Hyperosmolar, acidic, or fat-containing liquids** - slower emptying similar to solids
* **Measurement methods** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Ultrasound
  * Applied potential tomography
  * Systemic absorption of paracetamol
* **Clinical relevance: preoperative fasting** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Purpose: reduce volume and acidity of gastric fluid to minimize aspiration risk
  * Harms of excessive starvation:
	* Patient discomfort, dehydration, electrolyte disturbance
	* Increased PONV, glycemic disturbance, muscle catabolism
  * Current European evidence-based fasting recommendations:
	* 6 hours for solids
	* 4 hours for breastfeeding infants (formula milk counts as "solid food")
	* 2 hours for clear fluids (including carbohydrate drinks and tea/coffee with milk up to one-fifth of total volume)

⚠️ **Warning:** Delayed gastric emptying increases aspiration risk during anesthesia. Factors like opioids, pain, and critical illness significantly extend emptying time.

**Bottom Line:** Gastric emptying is a complex process influenced by multiple factors. Delayed emptying increases aspiration risk during anesthesia, while understanding the factors affecting it helps in perioperative risk assessment and management.

## 1.6. INTESTINES

### 1.6.1. STRUCTURE AND FUNCTION

* **Small intestine** - approximately 6 meters long, divided into:
  * Duodenum (25 cm)
  * Jejunum (2.5 m)
  * Ileum (3.5 m)
* **Large intestine** - approximately 1.5 meters long, divided into:
  * Cecum
  * Colon (ascending, transverse, descending, sigmoid)
  * Rectum
* **Absorption sites** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Most dietary nutrients - jejunum
  * Vitamin B12 and bile salts - terminal ileum
  * Iron - duodenum
  * Dietary fat and water - throughout small intestine
* **Small intestine layers** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **Adventitia** - outermost layer of loose connective tissue
  * **Muscularis externa** - outer longitudinal and inner circular smooth muscle layers
  * **Submucosa** - contains Meissner's plexus, blood and lymphatic vessels
  * **Mucosa** - innermost layer divided into:
	* Muscularis mucosae - smooth muscle layer for continuous agitation
	* Lamina propria - contains blood vessels and immune cells
	* Epithelium - absorptive enterocytes
* **Surface area enhancement** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **Valvulae conniventes** - mucosal folds projecting into lumen
  * **Villi** - finger-like projections (with goblet cells and intestinal crypts between)
  * **Microvilli** - microscopic projections forming brush border
  * Total absorptive surface area exceeds 250 m²
* **Villus vasculature** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **Arteriole** - single vessel giving rise to capillary network at villus tip
  * **Venule** - capillary network drains into venule returning to portal vein
  * **Lacteal** - single lymphatic capillary transporting absorbed fats as chylomicrons to thoracic duct

### 1.6.2. MOTILITY AND CONTROL MECHANISMS

* **Small intestine motility** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Segmentation (mixing movements)
  * Peristalsis (propulsive movements)
  * Controlled by intrinsic and extrinsic neural mechanisms
* **Large intestine motility** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Mass movements
  * Haustrations
  * Slower than small intestine
* **Fed vs. fasted motility** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **Fed state**:
	* **Segmental contractions** - compartmentalize sections of bowel, mix chyme with enzymes
	* **Propulsive contractions** - coordinated contractions propel chyme along GI tract
  * **Fasted state**:
	* Infrequent, irregular contractions
	* Migrating motor complex (MMC) every 90 minutes - intense coordinated contractions from duodenum to ileocecal valve
* **Motility control mechanisms** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **Electrical basis**:
	* Smooth muscle cell membrane potential fluctuates 20-30 times per minute (slow waves)
	* Food bolus stretches intestinal walls, triggering neurotransmitter release
	* Threshold potential exceeded during slow wave, creating spike potential
	* Smooth muscle contraction triggered
  * **Control systems**:
	* **Enteric nervous system** - semi-autonomous with myenteric and submucosal plexuses
	  * Sympathetic input inhibits motility
	  * Parasympathetic input increases motility ("rest and digest")
	* **Endocrine system**:
	  * Motilin - released every 90 min during fasting, stimulates MMC
	  * Vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP) - increases intestinal motility and secretion
	  * Gastrin, CCK, Motilin increases slow wave amplitude; Glucagon, Secretin decreases it

### 1.6.3. DIGESTIVE FUNCTIONS

* **Small intestine** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  - Secretes Alkaline muscus from Brunner's glands, goblet cells, H20 from epithelial cells
  * Primary site of nutrient absorption
	  * Glucose: 80% diffusion, 20% active (Na/Glu co-transport)
	  * Protein: Active Na-dpdt carrier / facilitated diffusion (basic amino acids)
	  * Fat: Diffusion from micelle through enterocyte membrane
	  * H20: 7-8L/day, passive down osmotic gradient
	  * Electrolytes: Na/Cl co-transport (main), Ca2+ carrier-mediated
	  * Vit ADEK (via micelles), B12 (terminal ileum, requires intrinsic factor), C/Folic acid via passive diffusion
  * Receives pancreatic enzymes and bile
  * Completes carbohydrate, protein, and fat digestion
* **Large intestine** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Secretes mucus from globlet cells
  * Water and electrolyte absorption (5-7L/day; passive absorption proximally, active absorption of Cl- (coupled with HCO3- secretion))
  * Formation of feces
  * Bacterial fermentation of undigested materials

**Bottom Line:** The intestines complete digestion and are the primary site of nutrient absorption, with specialized regions for different nutrients and controlled motility patterns that ensure proper processing of food.
### 1.6.4. OTHER FUNCTIONS OF SMALL INTESTINES VS LARGE INTESTINES

- **Small Intestines**:
  - **Immune**: Peyer's patches (T and B cells), Plasma cells in lamina propria (IgA, IgM, IgG)
  - **Endocrine**: Gastrin, CCK (signals satiety), Secretin, Motilin, VIP, GIP
  - **Digestion**: Carbohydrates, Protein, Fat breakdown
- **Large Intestines**:
  - Flatus: N2 (12-60%), CO2 (40%), Methane (<20%)
## 1.7. GASTROINTESTINAL HORMONES

**GI hormones regulate various digestive processes and are released in response to food intake.**

### 1.7.1. GASTRIN

* **Production** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Produced by G cells in the stomach antrum
  * Stimulated by vagal input, protein in stomach, and gastric stimulation
  * Inhibited by acid and secretin
* **Effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Stimulates gastric acid secretion
  * Stimulates release of mucus and enzymes
  * Increases gastric motility
* **Control mechanism** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Secretion controlled by negative feedback
  * High acid concentration releases somatostatin from δ cells
  * Somatostatin inhibits further gastrin release from G cells

### 1.7.2. INTRINSIC FACTOR

* **Structure and function** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Glycoprotein produced by parietal cells
  * Binds and protects vitamin B12
  * Allows vitamin B12 to reach terminal ileum for absorption
* **Clinical significance** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Deficiency causes megaloblastic anemia
  * Important consideration post-gastrectomy
* **Vitamin B12 absorption process** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Vitamin B12 released from ingested animal proteins during digestion
  * In stomach's acidic environment, intrinsic factor has low binding affinity
  * Vitamin B12 initially bound by haptocorrin (protects structure)
  * In duodenum, trypsin digests haptocorrin, releasing vitamin B12
  * In higher-pH duodenal environment, intrinsic factor avidly binds vitamin B12
  * In terminal ileum, intrinsic factor receptors allow absorption of IF-B12 complex
  * In pernicious anemia, autoimmune destruction of parietal cells leads to intrinsic factor deficiency

### 1.7.3. OTHER GI HORMONES

* **Secretin** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Released from S cells in duodenum in response to acidic chyme
  * Stimulates pancreatic bicarbonate secretion
  * Inhibits gastrin release and gastric acid secretion
* **Cholecystokinin (CCK)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Released from I cells in duodenum and jejunum
  * Stimulates pancreatic enzyme secretion
  * Stimulates gallbladder contraction
* **Glucose-dependent insulinotropic peptide (GIP)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Released from K cells in duodenum and jejunum
  * Stimulates insulin release

**Bottom Line:** Gastrointestinal hormones form a complex regulatory network that controls digestive processes, with specific roles in secretion, motility, and metabolic coordination.

# 2. NAUSEA AND VOMITING

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|Part A: Nausea & Vomiting Physiology]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B: PONV Management]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C]]

### 2.1.2. PROCESS OF VOMITING

 **Vomiting sequence** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **Pre-ejection phase**:
	* Nausea
	* Decreased gastric motility
	* Reverse peristalsis of small intestine
	* Secretion of HCO₃⁻-rich saliva (parasympathetic)
	* Sweating and tachycardia (sympathetic)
  * **Retching phase**:
	* Deep inspiration then glottic closure (airway protection)
	* Rhythmic contraction of intercostal muscles, diaphragm, and abdominal muscles against closed glottis
	* Mixing of alkaline small intestine contents with stomach to increase pH
	* Compression of esophagus to prevent reflux
  * **Ejection phase**:
	* Continued glottic closure
	* Pyloric contraction pushing gastric contents into body/fundus
	* LOS and esophageal relaxation
	* Sudden increase in intra-abdominal pressure from muscle contraction
	* Soft palate occlusion of nasopharynx
	* Reverse peristalsis in esophagus expelling contents upward

**Bottom Line:** Multiple classes of antiemetics targeting different pathways are available for PONV management. Combination therapy with agents from different classes provides additive efficacy and is recommended for moderate-to-high-risk patients.
### 2.1.3. METABOLIC CONSEQUENCES OF VOMITING

- Hypovolaemia
- Metabolic alkalosis 
	- Mainly seen in pyloric stenosis (loss of only acidic content)
	- **Paradoxical aciduria**: Kidneys initially excrete HCO3- > as volume deficit progresses, RAAS activated > preservation of Na and H20 in exchange for K+ and H+ excretion 
	- Volume contraction and Cl-/K+ deficiency impairs kidney's ability to rapidly excrete excess HCO3-
		- Decreased K+ > decreased renal tubular cells intracellular pH > increased proximal tubular HCO3- reabsorption
		- Increased Angiotensin II > increased HCO3- absorption in proximal and distal nephron > increased net H+ excretion in distal nephron
		- Decreased distal Cl- delivery > decreased HCO3- secretion
- Loss of electrolytes - Na, Cl
## 2.2. VOMITING CENTER
**The vomiting center coordinates the complex reflex of vomiting.**

### 2.2.1. NEUROLOGICAL CONTROL

* **Location and structure** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Located in the medulla oblongata, dorsal part of lateral reticular formation
  * Not a distinct anatomical nucleus but a distributed group of neurons forming a central pattern generator

### 2.2.2. INPUTS TO THE VOMITING CENTER

* **Four main inputs** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * **Chemoreceptor trigger zone (CTZ)** - detects chemical stimuli in blood and CSF
  * **Cerebellum** - vestibular system input (motion sickness)
  * **Higher centers** - psychological factors, memory, anticipation
  * **Solitary tract nucleus** - receives input from visceral afferents

### 2.2.3. SOLITARY TRACT NUCLEUS

* **Function and inputs** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Adjacent to the CTZ
  * May serve as the final common pathway for all inputs to the vomiting center
  * Coordinates various inputs including:
	* **Stomach and pharynx**
	  * Chemoreceptors - detect toxins, extreme pH changes, and irritants
	  * Mechanoreceptors - detect overdistension
	* **Spinal cord** - spino-reticular afferent impulses (nausea associated with severe physical injury)
	* **Heart** - cardiac afferents

**Bottom Line:** The vomiting center integrates signals from multiple sources to coordinate the complex vomiting reflex, making it a target for different classes of antiemetic medications.

## 2.3. CHEMORECEPTOR TRIGGER ZONE
**The CTZ detects circulating emetic substances and transmits this information to the vomiting center.**

* **Location and structure** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Located on the dorsal surface of the medulla oblongata
  * Also known as the area postrema
  * Lies on the floor of the fourth ventricle
  * Outside the blood-brain barrier (allowing detection of blood-borne substances)
* **Receptor types** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * **Mu (opioid) receptors**
  * **D2 (dopamine) receptors**
  * **5HT-3 (serotonin) receptors**
  * **Probable additional receptors**:
	* Kappa (opioid) receptors
	* NK1 (neurokinin-1) receptors
	* H1 and H2 (histamine) receptors
* **Location advantage** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Receives blood directly from systemic circulation (outside blood-brain barrier)
  * Provides faster vomiting response to emetic stimuli
  * Important clinically because antiemetic drugs don't need to cross BBB to reach receptors

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The diversity of receptors in the CTZ explains why different antiemetic classes may be effective for different causes of nausea and vomiting, and why combination therapy is often more effective than monotherapy.

**Bottom Line:** The CTZ is a critical chemosensitive region that triggers vomiting in response to blood-borne substances, serving as a major target for antiemetic medications.

## 2.4. POSTOPERATIVE NAUSEA AND VOMITING



### 2.4.1. RISK FACTORS
**PONV is influenced by multiple factors categorized as patient-related, anesthetic-related, and surgery-related.**

* **Patient-related factors** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Female gender (strongest predictor)
  * History of PONV or motion sickness
  * Non-smoker status
  * Younger age
  * Anxiety
* **Anesthetic-related factors** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Volatile anesthetics (including N₂O)
  * Postoperative opioids
  * Neostigmine (especially in high doses)
  * Aggressive hydration causing gut edema
* **Surgery-related factors** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Duration of surgery (higher risk if > 2 hours)
  * Type of surgery:
	* Highest risk: laparoscopic, bariatric, gynecological, and cholecystectomy
	* Moderate risk: ENT, neurosurgery, breast, plastic, strabismus

![PONV risk factors](GASTRO_AND_HPB_GASTRO_AND_HPB_Untitled_2.png_f33c66.png_6044a7.png)

### 2.4.2. RISK ASSESSMENT TOOLS
**The Apfel score is the most widely used tool for PONV risk assessment.**

* **Apfel score factors** (1 point each) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Female gender
  * Non-smoker status
  * History of PONV/motion sickness
  * Postoperative opioid use
* **Risk by Apfel score** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * 0 factors: 10% risk
  * 1 factor: 20% risk
  * 2 factors: 40% risk
  * 3 factors: 60% risk
  * 4 factors: 80% risk

![Apfel score chart](GASTRO_AND_HPB_GASTRO_AND_HPB_Untitled_4.png_0cec1f.png_3b319d.png)

### 2.4.3. PREVENTION AND MANAGEMENT 

**A multimodal approach to PONV prevention based on risk assessment is recommended.**

* **Prevention strategies** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]:
  * **Based on Apfel score**:
	* Score 0-1: No prophylaxis or single agent
	* Score 2: 1-2 antiemetics
	* Score 3-4: ≥2 antiemetics from different classes
  
  * **Current recommendation**: At least 2 prophylactic drugs in patients with at least one risk factor
* **Non-pharmacological strategies** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]:
  * Use regional anesthesia instead of general anesthesia when possible
  * Consider total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA)
  * Avoid nitrous oxide and volatile anesthetics
  * Minimize intraoperative and postoperative opioids
  * Avoid hypotension and cerebral hypoxia
  * Ensure adequate hydration
  * Consider acupuncture, acupressure, or transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation
  * Use sugammadex instead of neostigmine for reversal

⚠️ **Warning:** Try not to order agents for treatment in PACU that have already been used for prophylaxis (e.g., re-administration of ondansetron in PACU is not as effective if already used for prophylaxis).

**Bottom Line:** PONV risk assessment guides multimodal prophylaxis, while management should incorporate both pharmacological and non-pharmacological strategies tailored to the individual patient's risk profile.

## 2.5. ANTIEMETIC MEDICATIONS 




### 2.5.1. 5HT3 RECEPTOR ANTAGONISTS
**These agents block serotonin receptors in the CTZ and GI tract.**

* **Examples** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Ondansetron**
	* **Structure**: Synthetic carbazole
	* **Mechanism**: 5HT3 receptor antagonism in CTZ and GI tract
	* **Pharmacokinetics** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	  * 60% oral bioavailability
	  * 75% protein bound
	  * Hepatic metabolism (dose reduction in hepatic impairment) - CYP450 > hepatic hydroxylation and gluronidation 
	  * Renal and hepatic excretion
		  * Dose reduction only necessary for hepatic insufficiency
	  * T1/2: 3H
	* **Dosing**: 4-8 mg IV or 0.1 mg/kg in children (>2y), 30 min before
		* PO and Rectal formulations available
	* **Side effects**:
	  * GIT: constipation, rectal irritation (PR)
	  * CVS: Bradycardia, QT prolongation, Heart block
	  * CNS: Headache, flushing (especially if administered rapidly), dizziness, seizures (caution in those with epilepsy), rare extrapyramidal side effects
  * **Granisetron**: 0.1-1 mg IV
* **Efficacy**: More effective at preventing emesis than preventing nausea alone [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 2.5.2. HISTAMINE RECEPTOR ANTAGONISTS
**These agents primarily work on vestibular and cerebellar pathways.**

- **Example: Diphenhydramine**:
  - **Mechanism**: H1 antagonism (mainly vestibular/cerebellar) with antimuscarinic activity (M1) > decreased signals to vestibular nuclei, NTS, vomiting centre
  **Side effects**:
	* Sedation (H1)
	* Anticholinergic 
* **Example: Cyclizine** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Structure**: Piperazine derivative
  * **Mechanism**: H1 antagonism (mainly vestibular/cerebellar) with antimuscarinic activity
  * **Dosing**: 25-50 mg q8h in adults, 0.1 mg/kg in children
	  * IV formulation includes lactic acid (pH 3.2) > IV / IM pain on injection
	  * Good GIT absorption, PO bioavailability 75%
	  * T1/2 10h
  * **Side effects**:
	* Tachycardia
	* Dry mouth
	* Drowsiness
	* Blurred vision
	* Increased lower esophageal sphincter tone
	* Sedation (rare in cyclizine)
  * **Pharmacokinetics**: Hepatic metabolism, renal excretion

### 2.5.3. DOPAMINE RECEPTOR ANTAGONISTS
**These agents block dopamine receptors primarily in the CTZ. D2, muscarinic, noradrenergic (alpha 1 and 2), H1, 5-HT3 receptors. Also preents noradrenaline (uptake 1) into sympathetic nerves**

* **Phenothiazines** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Chlorpromazine**:
	* **Structure**: Phenothiazine neuroleptic
	* **Mechanism**: D2 antagonism in CTZ
	* **Dosing**: IM/PO/PR
		* PO (30% bioavailability despite good GIT absorption due to large hepatic first pass metabolism) and PR formulation available
	* **Pharmacokinetics**:
		* Protein bound >90%
		* Hepatic metabolism
		* Excreted in urine, bile, small % unchanged in urine 
	* **Side effects**:
	  * CNS
		  * Extrapyramidal side effects (central dopamine antagonism)
			  * Parkinsonism
			  * Oculogyric crisis
		  * Neuroleptic malignant syndrome
		  * Decreased growth hormone secretion
		  * Increased prolactin section (blocks dopamine which inhibits release of prolactin)
		  * Hypothermia (altered temperature regulation)
		  * Seizure threshold reduction
	  * CVS
		  * Heat loss from peripheral vasodilation (alpha ADR inhibition)
		  * QT prolongation
	  * Hematological
		  * Cholestatic jaundice
		  * Agranulocytosis
		  * Leukopenia, leukocytosis
		  * Haemolytic anaemia
		  * Venous thromboembolism
	  * Anticholinergic side effects: dry mouth
	  * Contact dermatitis
	  * Increased appetite, weight gain
  * **Promethazine**: Similar mechanism to Chlorpromazine
	  * **Pharmacokinetics**
		- PO bioavailability 25%
		- Large hepatic first pass metabolism
		- Protein bound 93%
		- T1/2 16-19H
		- Excreted via urine/bile
	- **Side effects**:
		- EPS (tardive dyskinesia)
		- Confusion
		- Respiratory depression (<2y)
		- Sedation ++
  * **Prochlorperazine**: Similar mechanism to Chlorpromazine
	  * **Pharmacokinetics**
		- PO bioavailability erratic
		- Very large hepatic first pass metabolism
		- PO/IV/IM/PR formulation available
	- **Side effects**:
		- EPS: acute dystonia, akathisia (children and young adults prone)
		- As per Chlorpromazine
  * **Perphenazine**: Similar mechanism to Chlorpromazine
	  * Higher incidence of EPS and post op sedation than Prochlorperazine
* **Butyrophenones** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Droperidol**:
	* **Mechanism**: D2 antagonism in CTZ, peripheral alpha ADR blockade, mild anti-histaminergic and anti-choinergic effect
	* **Pharmacokinetics**:
		* IM/IV 
		* Protein bound 90%
		* Hepatic metabolism
		* Renal excretion 99% as metabolites, 1% unchanged
		* Duration of action: 4H
	* **Side effects**:
	  * CVS: Ventricular arrhythmias, QT prolongation, Hypotension from peripheral alpha ADR blockade
	  * CNS: Sedation++, EPS (dose dependent, may develop >12h after administration), Anxiety (25% may experience anxiety up to 48H after administration), Neurolepsis
	  * Hyperprolactinemia
  * **Domperidone**: 
	  * **Mechanism**: Selective peripheral D2 antagonism, does not cross blood-brain barrier
	  * **Pharmacokinetics**:
		  * PO/PR/IM formulation (IV formulation causes arrhythmias)
		  * PO bioavailability 15%
		  * High first pass metabolism
		  * Protein bound 92%
		  * Excreted via urine / bile
	  * **Side Effects**:
		  * Increased prolactin levels > galactorrhea, gynaecomastia
		  * Increased lower esophageal sphincter tone
* **Benzamides** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Metoclopramide**:
	* **Mechanism**:
	  * D2 receptor antagonism at CTZ
	  * 5HT3 receptor antagonism
	  * Prokinetic effect on stomach (via antagonism of peripheral D2 receptors and selective stimulation of gastric muscarinic receptors)
	- **Pharmcokinetics**:
	 - PO bioavailability 30-90% (high variance due to variation in first pass hepatic metabolism)
	 - Hepatic conjugation
	 - Excreted partially unchanged in urine
	* **Side effects**:
	  * CNS: Crosses blood-brain barrier
		  * Extrapyramidal side effects
			  * Restlessnes
			  * Dystonia (more common in young women and elderly)
			  * Parkinsonian symptoms
			  * Tardive dyskinesia (develops on long term treatment)
			  * Oculogyric crisis
			  * Akinesia - common in elderly, young, renal impairment, high dose
		  * Neuroleptic malignant syndrome
		  * Sedation with long term administration
		  * Increased prolactin secretion in hypothalamus: galactorrhea, gynaecomastia, impotence, menstrual disorders
	  * CVS: may cause acute HTN in pheochromocytoma
	  * GIT: increased lower esophageal sphincter tone, pylorus relaxation

### 2.5.4. ANTIMUSCARINICS
**These agents work mainly in the vestibular system and vomiting center.**

* **Example: Hyoscine (Scopolamine)** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Structure**: Only L-hyoscine is pharmacologically active (but exists as a racemic mixture)
  * **Mechanism**: Antimuscarinic effects in vomiting center and vestibular system (M1; decreases signal)
  * **Formulation**: Transdermal patch (1.5 mg) placed behind ear
	  * Variable absorption
	  * PO availability 10-50%
  * DOA shorter than atropine
  * Metabolized by liver esterases, small % excreted unchanged in urine. 
  * **Side effects**: Anticholinergic side effects
	* Sedation (more sedative than atropine)
	* Central anticholinergic syndrome (can present as postoperative agitation)
	* Very dry mouth
	* Visual disturbances - mydriasis (BOV)

- **Example: Atropine**:
  - **Structure**: Only 1-atropine is pharmacologically active (but exists as a reacemic micture)
  - **Mechanism**: Competitive antagonist at muscarinic receptors (minimal action at nicotinic receptors). Has LA properties.
  - **Formulation**: PO/IV formulation available
	  - Rapid GIT absorption but unpredictable
	  - PO bioavailability: 10-20%
	  - Protei bound: 50%
  - Metabolized by liver esterases, small % excreted unchanged in urine
  - Elimination T1/2: 2.5h
  - **Side effects**: Anticholinergic side effects
	  - CNS (less likely): sedation, central anticholinergic crisis, decreased ADH secretion
	  - CVS: initial bradycardia (central effect on vagal nucleus / partial agonist effect on cardiac muscarinic recpetors), tachycardia
	  - Respi: less effective anti-sialogogue than hyoscine, decreased tone of LES, decreased gastric acid secretion
	  - Others: inhibits sweating (pyrexia), increased IOP, decreased ureteric tone

- **Example: Glycopyrrolate**:
  - **Structure**: Charged quatenary structure (does not cross BBB, but CAN cross placenta)
  - **Mechanism**: Competitive antagonist at peripheral muscarinic receptors
  - **Formulation**: IV formulation is the only effective formulation
	  - Negligible GIT absorption, PO bioavailability <5%
  - DOA for vagolytic effects 2-3H
  - Elimination T1/2: 0.6-1.1H
  - **Side effects**:
	  - Tachycardia at high doses
	  - Bronchodilator
	  - Other anticholinergic side effects
	  
⚠️ **Warning:** Warn patients not to touch the scopolamine patch and then touch their eyes, as this can cause pupillary dilation. Use caution in elderly patients due to increased risk of confusion and delirium.

### 2.5.5. NEUROKININ-1 ANTAGONISTS
**These agents block the action of substance P in the vomiting center.**

* **Example: Aprepitant** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Mechanism**: Central action on neurokinin-1 receptors, inhibiting substance P effects > decreased signals to CTZ; also potentiates effects of other anti-emetics
  * **Pharmokinetics**:
	  * PO administered, bioavailability 60%
	  * IV (fosaprepitant) prodrug converted to aprepitant within 30min
	  * Protein bound 97%
	  * Metabolized by CYP3A4 (CYP3A4 inhibitors will increase plasma levels of Aprepitant)> weakly active metabolites > urinary and faecal excretion
  * Usually given together with 5-HT3 antagonsits and corticosteroids to prevent acute and delayed nausea and vomiting on highly emetogenic chemotherapy regimens
  * **Side effects**:
	* Fatigue
	* Hiccups
	* Dizziness
	* Diarrhea
	* Loss of appetite
	* Alopecia
  * **Usage**: Often reserved for PONV refractory to other treatments or high-risk cases

### 2.5.6. OTHER ANTIEMETICS
* **Dexamethasone** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Structure**: Synthetic glucocorticoid
  * **Mechanism**: Acts on nuclear glucocorticoid receptors; may reduce serotonin release from GI tract
  * **Dosing**: 4-8 mg (adults), 0.1 mg/kg in children
	  * Given together with 5-HT3 antagonist to prevent acute + delayed nausea and vomiting in patients with highly emetogenic chemotherapy regimens
  * **Side effects**: Deranged blood glucose in diabetic patients, iatrogenic Cushing's syndrome
  * **Pharmacokinetics**: Renal excretion
  
* **Propofol**: May act via CTZ, provides antiemetic effect even at sub-hypnotic doses [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

* **Cannabinoids**: Nabilone is a synthetic cannabinoid with antiemetic properties [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

- **Benzodiazepines**: Lorazepam, Diazepam
  - **Mechanism**: Binds to allosteric site on GABA receptors > increasing Cl- conductance
	  - Anxiolytic - reducing anticipatory vomiting caused by anxiety
  - **Side effects**: 
	  - Sedative, hynoptic, amnesiac, causes respiratory depression
	  - Fetotoxic (risk of cleft palate if administered during 1st trimester) - avoid in pregnancy

- **Acupuncture**: Point between tendons of flexor carpi radialis and palmaris longus; 4cm from distal skin crease
	- Accubands may be worn by pregnant patients

# 3. GASTROINTESTINAL DIGESTION AND ABSORPTION

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|Part A: Digestion & Absorption]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C]]

## 3.2. SMALL INTESTINE STRUCTURE AND FUNCTION
**The small intestine is the primary site of nutrient digestion and absorption.**

### 3.2.1. ANATOMICAL AND HISTOLOGICAL ARRANGEMENT

* **Divisions of the small intestine** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Duodenum
  * Jejunum
  * Ileum
* **Absorption sites for key nutrients** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Most dietary nutrients - jejunum
  * Vitamin B12 and bile salts - terminal ileum
  * Iron - duodenum
  * Dietary fat and water - throughout small intestine
* **Layers of the small intestine** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **Adventitia** - outermost layer of loose connective tissue
  * **Muscularis externa** - outer longitudinal and inner circular smooth muscle layers
	* Myenteric plexus lies between these layers to coordinate smooth muscle contraction
  * **Submucosa** - contains Meissner's plexus (secondary enteric nervous system plexus), blood vessels, lymphatic vessels, and elastic connective tissue
  * **Mucosa** - innermost layer divided into:
	* Muscularis mucosae - smooth muscle for agitation of mucosa
	* Lamina propria - contains blood vessels and immune cells (Peyer's patches in ileum)
	* Epithelium - absorptive enterocytes

### 3.2.2. ABSORPTIVE SURFACE FEATURES

* **Surface area enhancement structures** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **Valvulae conniventes** - mucosal folds projecting into lumen
  * **Villi** - finger-like projections with goblet cells and intestinal crypts between them
  * **Microvilli** - microscopic projections forming brush border
  * Total absorptive surface area exceeds 250 m²
* **Vascular supply to villi** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **Arteriole** - single vessel giving rise to capillary network
  * **Venule** - drains capillary network, returns blood to portal vein
  * **Lacteal** - lymphatic capillary transporting absorbed fats as chylomicrons to thoracic duct

**Bottom Line:** The small intestine has structural specializations that massively increase its surface area, optimizing it for nutrient absorption. The complex arrangement of blood and lymphatic vessels enables efficient transport of absorbed nutrients into the circulation.

## 3.3. CARBOHYDRATE DIGESTION AND ABSORPTION
**Digestible carbohydrates must be broken down to monosaccharides before absorption.**

* **Digestion process** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **In the mouth** - salivary amylase breaks down complex carbohydrates into smaller polymers and monosaccharides
  * **In the duodenum** - pancreatic amylase continues breakdown of complex carbohydrates
  * **At the brush border** - specific enzymes (sucrase, maltase, lactase) hydrolyze smaller carbohydrate polymers into constituent monosaccharides
	* Enzyme deficiencies (e.g., lactase deficiency causing lactose intolerance) prevent digestion of specific carbohydrates
	* Some carbohydrates like cellulose pass through unchanged due to lack of necessary digestive enzymes
* **Absorption mechanisms** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **At the enterocyte**:
	* **Glucose and galactose**:
	  * Absorbed via secondary active transport through sodium-glucose linked transporter (SGLT1)
	  * Requires low intracellular Na+ maintained by basolateral Na+/K+-ATPase pump
	* **Fructose**:
	  * Absorbed by facilitated diffusion (not Na+ co-transport)
	* **Pentose sugars**:
	  * Absorbed by simple diffusion
  * **Within the enterocyte**:
	* Monosaccharides travel down concentration gradients through the basolateral membrane via GLUT-2 transporter
	* Being osmotically active, monosaccharide absorption drives water absorption
* **Clinical relevance: oral rehydration therapy** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Exploits intestinal Na+-glucose co-transport system
  * Na+ and glucose absorption drives water absorption
  * Replaces lost Na+ and prevents dehydration in diarrheal illness
  * May not replace potassium lost in prolonged diarrhea

**Bottom Line:** Carbohydrate digestion involves multiple enzymatic processes, with final absorption occurring primarily through active transport mechanisms in the small intestine. Understanding these processes helps explain disorders like lactose intolerance and the principles behind oral rehydration therapy.

## 3.4. PROTEIN DIGESTION AND ABSORPTION
**Proteins must be broken down to amino acids, dipeptides, and tripeptides before absorption.**

* **Digestion process** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **In the stomach**:
	* Pepsinogen released by chief cells
	* Converted to pepsin in acidic environment
	* Pepsin cleaves peptide bonds, creating shorter polypeptides
  * **In the duodenum**:
	* Trypsin and chymotrypsin (pancreatic enzymes) continue protein breakdown
	* Create dipeptides and tripeptides but not single amino acids
  * **At the brush border**:
	* Peptidases cleave dipeptides and tripeptides into single amino acids
* **Absorption mechanisms** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **Single amino acids**:
	* Absorbed via Na+-linked co-transport (similar to glucose)
	* Different co-transporters for neutral, basic, and acidic amino acids
  * **Short peptides**:
	* Absorbed via H+-linked co-transport
	* Broken down into single amino acids inside enterocytes
  * **Exit from enterocyte**:
	* Single amino acids exit via facilitated diffusion across basolateral membrane
	* Being osmotically active, amino acid absorption drives water absorption

**Bottom Line:** Protein digestion is a stepwise process involving gastric, pancreatic, and brush border enzymes, with absorption occurring through specialized transporters. This complex process ensures efficient utilization of dietary proteins.

## 3.5. LIPID DIGESTION AND ABSORPTION
**Dietary lipids require special processing due to their hydrophobic nature.**

* **Digestion process** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **Emulsification in duodenum**:
	* Bile acids (from liver via gallbladder) coat lipid droplets
	* Break large droplets into smaller ones, increasing surface area
  * **Enzymatic breakdown**:
	* Pancreatic lipase hydrolyzes triglyceride into free fatty acids and 2-monoglyceride
	* Acts only on droplet surfaces, requiring bile salt emulsification
  * **Micelle formation**:
	* Free fatty acids and monoglycerides combine with bile salts
	* Form small balls of mixed lipids and bile salts (micelles)
* **Absorption mechanisms** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **At the enterocyte**:
	* Micelles contact enterocytes, lipid components absorbed by simple diffusion
	* Bile salts remain in gut lumen for absorption in terminal ileum and recycling
  * **Within the enterocyte**:
	* Monoglycerides and fatty acids travel to endoplasmic reticulum
	* Recombined to form triglyceride
	* Packaged with cholesterol, phospholipid, and apolipoprotein to form chylomicrons
	* Chylomicrons released into lacteals (lymphatic capillaries)
	* Travel through lymphatic system to thoracic duct and systemic circulation
* **Clinical relevance: lipase inhibitors** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Orlistat inhibits lingual and pancreatic lipase
  * Prevents triglyceride hydrolysis, reducing fat absorption
  * Causes steatorrhea (fatty stool)
  * Similar presentation seen in pancreatic insufficiency

**Bottom Line:** Lipid digestion and absorption involve unique processes including emulsification, micelle formation, and lymphatic transport. Understanding these mechanisms is important for appreciating conditions like steatorrhea and the action of drugs like lipase inhibitors.

## 3.6. INTESTINAL MOTILITY
**Intestinal motility patterns ensure proper mixing and propulsion of contents.**

### 3.6.1. FED AND FASTED STATES

* **Fed state motility patterns** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **Segmental contractions**:
	* Contraction of circular smooth muscle creates compartments
	* Longitudinal muscle contraction and relaxation mixes content with digestive enzymes
	* Brings chyme into contact with brush border, promoting absorption
	* Does not significantly propel content forward
  * **Propulsive contractions**:
	* Coordinated contraction of circular muscle behind food bolus
	* Simultaneous longitudinal muscle contraction
	* Propels chyme along GI tract
	* Each peristaltic wave lasts seconds, traveling few centimeters per second
* **Fasted state motility patterns** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Infrequent, irregular contractions
  * **Migrating motor complex (MMC)**:
	* Occurs every 90 minutes during fasting
	* Intense, coordinated contraction spreading from duodenum to ileocecal valve
	* Sweeps undigested chyme toward colon
	* Stimulated by motilin release

### 3.6.2. CONTROL MECHANISMS

* **Myoelectric basis of motility** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Smooth muscle cells have negative resting membrane potential (-40 to -70 mV)
  * Membrane potential fluctuates 20-30 times per minute (slow waves)
  * Food bolus stretches intestinal wall, causing neurotransmitter release
  * Creates small depolarization, exceeding threshold during next slow wave
  * Generates spike potential triggering smooth muscle contraction
* **Control systems** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **Enteric nervous system**:
	* Semi-autonomous with afferent and efferent neurons
	* Myenteric (Auerbach's) and submucosal (Meissner's) plexuses
	* **Autonomic inputs**:
	  * Sympathetic system inhibits enteric activity, reduces motility and secretions
	  * Parasympathetic system (vagus nerve) increases motility and secretions
  * **Endocrine control**:
	* **Motilin** - released every 90 minutes during fasting, stimulates MMC
	* **Vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP)** - increases secretion and motility

**Bottom Line:** Intestinal motility shows distinct patterns in fed and fasted states, controlled by complex interactions between intrinsic neural networks, autonomic input, and hormonal factors. This coordination ensures effective digestion, absorption, and propulsion of intestinal contents.

# 4. METABOLISM AND NUTRITION

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|Part A: Metabolism]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C]]

## 4.2. GLUCOSE METABOLISM
**Glucose is a primary energy source regulated through complex metabolic pathways.**

### 4.2.1. GLUCOSE ABSORPTION

* **Mechanisms of glucose absorption from GI tract** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * **Passive diffusion** - accounts for 80% of absorption
  * **Active transport** - accounts for 20% of absorption
	* Sodium-glucose co-transport mechanism

### 4.2.2. BLOOD SUGAR CONTROL

* **Endocrine control of the liver** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * **Insulin**:
	* Reduces blood glucose by:
	  * Increasing glucose uptake into cells
	  * Increasing glycogen synthesis
	  * Reducing glycogenolysis
	  * Reducing gluconeogenesis
  * **Glucagon**:
	* Increases plasma glucose by:
	  * Reducing glycolysis
	  * Increasing gluconeogenesis
	  * Increasing glycogenolysis
	  * Stimulating lipase in fatty tissue (breaking down fat to fatty acids and glycerol for gluconeogenesis)
	* Additional effects:
	  * Stimulates insulin release
	  * Stimulates growth hormone and somatostatin release
	  * Positive chronotropic and inotropic effects (this is why it is used in beta-blocker toxicity)
* **Other factors affecting blood glucose** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * **Glucocorticoids** - reduce glucose uptake and usage
  * **Growth hormone** - inhibits insulin
  * **Catecholamines** - stimulate glycogenolysis and reduce glucose uptake
  * **Thyroid hormone** - increases glucose uptake, glycolysis, gluconeogenesis, and absorption from gut
  * **Estrogen and progesterone** - can lead to insulin resistance

### 4.2.3. ENERGY PRODUCTION

* **Cellular energy production pathways** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * **Glycolysis**:
	* Does not require oxygen or produce CO2
	* Occurs in cytoplasm
	* Produces 2 molecules of pyruvate per glucose
	* Yields 2 ATP and 2 NADH
	* In hypoxic conditions, pyruvate converts to lactate to recycle NAD
  * **Krebs Cycle**:
	* Occurs within mitochondria
	* Can use pyruvate, ketones, and acetyl-CoA
	* No direct oxygen consumption but requires functioning electron transport chain
	* Produces CO2, NADH, and FADH2
  * **Oxidative phosphorylation**:
	* Electron transport chain in mitochondria
	* Up to 38 ATP per glucose molecule

**Bottom Line:** Glucose metabolism is tightly regulated by multiple hormones, primarily insulin and glucagon, with energy production occurring through glycolysis, the Krebs cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation.

## 4.3. INSULIN
**Insulin is the primary hormone responsible for glucose uptake and anabolic processes.**

### 4.3.1. PRODUCTION AND MECHANISM

* **Insulin production** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Polypeptide anabolic hormone produced by beta cells in pancreatic islets of Langerhans
  * Secreted continuously, with increases in response to:
	* Raised blood sugar levels
	* Amino acids
	* Glucagon
	* Gut hormones
	* Autonomic stimulation
* **Mechanism of action** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Acts on tyrosine kinase receptors in muscle and fat cells
  * Leads to GLUT-4 glucose transport proteins being recruited into the cell membrane
  * In the liver, does not alter membrane permeability to glucose but stimulates intracellular metabolic processes

### 4.3.2. EFFECTS ON METABOLISM

* **Multiple downstream effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Increases glucose (and potassium) uptake from the blood
  * Increases glycogen synthesis
  * Reduces gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis
  * Reduces hepatic release of glucose
  * Promotes anabolism - enhanced protein storage and construction
  * Increases fat synthesis
  * Reduces ketone production

### 4.3.3. FACTORS AFFECTING SECRETION

* **Factors that stimulate insulin release** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Glucose
  * Amino acids
  * Beta-adrenergic stimulation (e.g., salbutamol)
  * Acetylcholine
  * Glucagon
* **Factors that inhibit insulin release** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Hypoglycemia
  * Beta-blockers
  * Alpha-adrenergic stimulation
  * Thiazide diuretics
  * Somatostatin

**Bottom Line:** Insulin is a key anabolic hormone that reduces blood glucose by multiple mechanisms and is affected by numerous physiological factors, medications, and other hormones.

## 4.4. GLUCAGON AND COUNTERREGULATORY HORMONES
**Glucagon and other counterregulatory hormones oppose insulin's effects to maintain glucose homeostasis.**

### 4.4.1. GLUCAGON

* **Structure and production** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Catabolic 29-amino acid peptide hormone
  * Made by α-cells in pancreatic islets
  * Acts via cAMP
* **Regulation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * **Stimulated by**:
	* Hypoglycemia
	* Amino acids
	* Beta-adrenergic stimulation
	* Acetylcholine
	* Stress
  * **Inhibited by**:
	* Hyperglycemia
	* Fatty acids
	* Insulin
	* Somatostatin
	* Alpha-adrenergic agonists
* **Effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Glycogenolysis
  * Gluconeogenesis
  * β-oxidation of fatty acids and lipolysis
  * Increased catecholamine release
  * Positive inotropy

### 4.4.2. OTHER COUNTERREGULATORY HORMONES

* **Epinephrine** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Increases glycogenolysis
  * Inhibits insulin release
  * Stimulates lipolysis
* **Cortisol** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Increases gluconeogenesis
  * Induces insulin resistance
  * Long-acting effect (hours)
* **Growth hormone** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Antagonizes insulin actions
  * Promotes lipolysis
  * Slow-acting effect (hours)

**Bottom Line:** Glucagon and other counterregulatory hormones act to increase blood glucose through multiple mechanisms, providing balance to insulin's glucose-lowering effects and preventing hypoglycemia.

## 4.5. HYPOGLYCEMIA
**Hypoglycemia triggers a coordinated hormonal response to restore normal blood glucose levels.**

* **Definition** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Blood glucose <3.9 mmol/L (70 mg/dL)
  * Symptomatic hypoglycemia generally occurs at <3.0 mmol/L (54 mg/dL)
* **Hormonal response to hypoglycemia** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Pancreatic beta cells detect low blood sugar and reduce insulin secretion
  * Pancreatic alpha cells increase glucagon secretion
  * Adrenal glands release epinephrine and cortisol
  * Pituitary releases growth hormone
* **Symptoms** - reflect both sympathetic activation and neuroglycopenia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * **Sympathetic**: Tremor, sweating, tachycardia, anxiety, hunger
  * **Neuroglycopenic**: Confusion, drowsiness, speech and visual disturbances, seizures, coma

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Anesthetic agents may mask the sympathetic symptoms of hypoglycemia, making regular glucose monitoring essential during anesthesia, especially in diabetic patients or prolonged procedures.

**Bottom Line:** Hypoglycemia triggers a coordinated counterregulatory hormone response that increases blood glucose through multiple mechanisms, but these responses may be blunted during anesthesia.

## 4.6. NUTRITIONAL REQUIREMENTS
**Proper nutrition involves adequate intake of carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, and minerals.**

* **Basic nutritional components** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * **Carbohydrates** - primary energy source, 4 kcal/g
  * **Proteins** - essential for tissue growth and repair, 4 kcal/g
  * **Fats** - concentrated energy source and essential fatty acids, 9 kcal/g
  * **Vitamins** - essential micronutrients required for various metabolic functions
  * **Minerals** - required for structural and functional roles
* **Energy requirements** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * **Basal metabolic rate (BMR)** - energy required at rest
  * **Physical activity** - increases energy requirements above BMR
  * **Thermal effect of food** - energy required for digestion (10-15% of intake)
  * **Average daily requirements**:
	* Men: 2,500 kcal/day
	* Women: 2,000 kcal/day
	* Can vary significantly based on age, activity level, and health status
* **Body composition assessment** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * **Body mass index (BMI)** - weight(kg)/height(m)²
	* Underweight: <18.5
	* Normal: 18.5-24.9
	* Overweight: 25-29.9
	* Obese: ≥30
  * **Body fat estimation** methods:
	* Skinfold thickness
	* Bioelectrical impedance
	* Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA)
	* Air displacement plethysmography

**Bottom Line:** Understanding nutritional requirements and body composition is important for perioperative assessment and management, particularly in patients with malnutrition, obesity, or metabolic disorders.

# 5. PANCREATIC FUNCTION

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|Part A: Pancreatic Function]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C]]

## 5.2. EXOCRINE PANCREAS
**The exocrine pancreas secretes digestive enzymes essential for nutrient breakdown.**

* **Structure and composition** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Comprises approximately 80% of pancreatic tissue
  * Produces digestive enzymes including:
	* **Lipase** - fat digestion
	* **Amylase** - carbohydrate digestion
	* **Trypsin** - protein digestion
	* **Chymotrypsin** - protein digestion
	* Other digestive enzymes
  * Also secretes bicarbonate to neutralize acidic chyme from stomach
  * Secretes a total of ~1.5L/day of alkaline fluids (pH ~8) consisting of the above content: HCO3-, H20, Na, K, Digestive enzymes.
* **Cellular organization** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **Acinar cells** - produce digestive enzymes
  * **Ductal cells** - secrete HCO₃⁻ and water
* **Enzymatic products** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **Trypsinogen and chymotrypsinogen**:
	* On entering duodenum, trypsinogen is cleaved by enterokinase to form trypsin
	* Trypsin then cleaves chymotrypsinogen to form chymotrypsin
	* Trypsin also activates more trypsinogen (autocatalysis)
  * **Pancreatic α-amylase** - hydrolyzes polysaccharides to smaller carbohydrate polymers
  * **Pancreatic lipase** - hydrolyzes dietary triglyceride to free fatty acids and 2-monoglyceride
* **Bicarbonate secretion mechanism** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Ductal cells use carbonic anhydrase to synthesize HCO₃⁻
	* CO₂ diffuses into ductal cells from blood
	* Combined with water to form H₂CO₃ (catalyzed by carbonic anhydrase)
	* H₂CO₃ dissociates into H⁺ and HCO₃⁻
	* HCO₃⁻ enters duct lumen via Cl⁻ exchange (CFTR channel involved)
	* H⁺ expelled across basolateral membrane via Na⁺ exchange
  * HCO₃⁻ is osmotically active, drawing water into pancreatic duct
  * CFTR channel dysfunction in cystic fibrosis affects this process
* **Secretion regulation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Controlled by neural and hormonal mechanisms
  * **Secretin** stimulates bicarbonate-rich fluid secretion
  * **Cholecystokinin (CCK)** stimulates enzyme secretion
  * **Vagal stimulation** increases secretion
* **Control mechanisms** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Very little secretion between meals
  * Stimulated when food enters stomach and especially when chyme enters duodenum
  * **Neural control**:
	* Vagal stimulation during cephalic phase causes small increase in acinar cell activity
  * **Hormonal control**:
	* **Gastrin** - from G cells in stomach stimulated by gastric distension, stimulates acinar cells to secrete enzymes
	* **CCK** - from duodenal mucosa in response to fat/protein, stimulates acinar cells
	* **Secretin** - from duodenal mucosa in response to acid, stimulates ductal HCO₃⁻ secretion
* **Clinical relevance: acute pancreatitis** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Inappropriate activation of proenzymes within pancreas
  * Trypsinogen activates to trypsin, leading to autocatalysis
  * Activated enzymes cause:
	* Phospholipase A₂ digests cell membranes causing necrosis
	* Elastase digests blood vessel walls causing hemorrhage
	* Inflammatory mediators released triggering systemic inflammatory response
	* Pancreatic lipase digests retroperitoneal fat (causing Grey Turner's sign and Cullen's sign)
* **Clinical significance** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Pancreatic enzyme deficiency leads to maldigestion and malabsorption
  * Pancreatic inflammation can release enzymes into circulation
  * Elevated serum amylase and lipase are important markers of pancreatic inflammation

**Bottom Line:** The exocrine pancreas produces enzymes critical for digestion and bicarbonate for neutralizing gastric acid, with its secretion regulated by gut hormones and neural input. Dysfunction can cause malabsorption and nutritional deficiencies, while enzyme activation within the gland can lead to pancreatitis.

## 5.3. ENDOCRINE PANCREAS
**The endocrine pancreas regulates glucose homeostasis through hormone secretion.**

* **Structure and composition** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Comprises approximately 2% of pancreatic tissue
  * Organized in islets of Langerhans
  * Contains specialized cell types:
	* α-cells - produce glucagon
	* β-cells - produce insulin
	* δ-cells - produce somatostatin
	* PP cells - produce pancreatic polypeptide
	* ε-cells - produce ghrelin

### 5.3.1. INSULIN
* **Structure and production** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Polypeptide anabolic hormone
  * Produced by β-cells in pancreatic islets
  * Secreted continuously with increases in response to:
	* Raised blood glucose levels
	* Amino acids
	* Glucagon
	* Gut hormones
	* Autonomic stimulation
* **Factors influencing secretion** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:

| Factors that **stimulate** insulin release | Factors that **inhibit** insulin release |
| --- | --- |
| Glucose | Hypoglycemia |
| Amino acids | Beta blockers |
| Salbutamol | Alpha blockers |
| Acetylcholine | Thiazide diuretics |
| Glucagon | Somatostatin |

* **Mechanism of action** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Acts on tyrosine kinase receptors
  * In muscle and fat: increases GLUT-4 translocation to cell membrane
  * In liver: stimulates metabolic processes that consume glucose
* **Metabolic effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Increases glucose and potassium uptake from blood
  * Increases glycogen synthesis
  * Reduces gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis
  * Reduces hepatic glucose release
  * Promotes protein synthesis and storage
  * Increases fat synthesis
  * Reduces ketone production

### 5.3.2. GLUCAGON
* **Structure and production** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Catabolic 29-amino acid peptide hormone
  * Produced by α-cells in pancreatic islets
  * Acts via cAMP second messenger system
* **Regulation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * **Stimulated by**:
	* Hypoglycemia
	* Amino acids
	* Salbutamol (β-adrenergic stimulation)
	* Acetylcholine
	* Stress
  * **Inhibited by**:
	* Hyperglycemia
	* Fatty acids
	* Insulin
	* Somatostatin
	* Alpha-adrenergic agonists
* **Effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Glycogenolysis (breakdown of glycogen to glucose)
  * Gluconeogenesis (glucose synthesis from non-carbohydrate sources)
  * β-oxidation of fatty acids and lipolysis
  * Increased catecholamine release
  * Positive inotropic effect on heart

### 5.3.3. SOMATOSTATIN
* **Production and regulation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Produced by δ-cells in pancreas
  * Also produced by hypothalamus
* **Effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Inhibits insulin release
  * Inhibits glucagon release
  * Inhibits stomach acid production
  * Relaxes gallbladder
  * Acts as neurotransmitter in substantia gelatinosa

**Bottom Line:** The endocrine pancreas maintains glucose homeostasis through the coordinated release of insulin, glucagon, and somatostatin, with multiple factors influencing their secretion and action.

## 5.4. PANCREATIC SECRETIONS IN DIGESTION
**Pancreatic secretions are essential for nutrient digestion in the small intestine.**

### 5.4.1. ENZYME PRODUCTION

* **Acinar cell products** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **Proenzymes requiring activation**:
	* Trypsinogen - activated by enterokinase to form trypsin
	* Chymotrypsinogen - activated by trypsin to form chymotrypsin
  * **Enzymes secreted in active form**:
	* Pancreatic α-amylase - breaks down starch to maltose
	* Pancreatic lipase - hydrolyzes dietary triglyceride
* **Activation cascade** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Enterokinase (from duodenal mucosa) → trypsinogen → trypsin
  * Trypsin then activates:
	* More trypsinogen (autocatalysis)
	* Chymotrypsinogen → chymotrypsin
	* Prophospholipase A2 → phospholipase A2
	* Proelastase → elastase

### 5.4.2. BICARBONATE SECRETION

* **Production mechanism** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **Ductal cell process**:
	* CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ (catalyzed by carbonic anhydrase)
	* H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻
	* HCO₃⁻ secreted into duct lumen by Cl⁻ exchange
	* H⁺ extruded across basolateral membrane
  * CFTR channel dysfunction in cystic fibrosis impairs this process
* **Physiological importance** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Neutralizes acidic gastric contents entering duodenum
  * Creates optimal pH for pancreatic enzyme activity
  * Prevents denaturation of enzymes by gastric acid

### 5.4.3. CONTROL OF SECRETIONS

* **Neural regulation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Vagus nerve stimulation during cephalic phase
  * Minimal effect compared to hormonal regulation
* **Hormonal regulation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **CCK** (cholecystokinin):
	* Released from duodenal mucosa in response to fat and protein
	* Stimulates acinar cells to secrete enzymes
	* Also stimulates gallbladder contraction and relaxes sphincter of Oddi
	* Slows gastric emptying
  * **Secretin**:
	* Released from duodenal mucosa in response to acidic chyme
	* Stimulates ductal cells to secrete HCO₃⁻ and water
	* Inhibits gastrin release
	* Slows gastric emptying
  * **Gastrin**:
	* Stimulates pancreatic enzyme secretion
	* Less important than CCK
* **Secretory volume and composition** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * 1.5L of pancreatic juice produced daily
  * Composition varies based on stimuli:
	* CCK stimulation → enzyme-rich secretion
	* Secretin stimulation → HCO₃⁻-rich secretion

**Bottom Line:** Pancreatic secretions contain digestive enzymes from acinar cells and bicarbonate from ductal cells, with regulation primarily by CCK and secretin. These secretions are essential for normal digestion and nutrient absorption in the small intestine.

# 6. LIVER AND HEPATOBILIARY SYSTEM

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|Part A: Liver Function]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B: Hepatobiliary Anesthesia]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C]]

## 6.2. LIVER ANATOMY AND BLOOD SUPPLY
**The liver is the largest internal organ with a dual blood supply and complex microarchitecture.**

### 6.2.1. BLOOD SUPPLY

* **Size and structure** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Largest internal organ weighing approximately 1.5 kg
  * Accounts for 2% of total body weight
  * Contained within a fibrous capsule and divided into four main lobes:
	* Left lobe
	* Right lobe (largest)
	* Quadrate lobe
	* Caudate lobe
  * Each lobe is made up of microscopic hexagonal lobules
* **Blood supply** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Receives approximately 25% of cardiac output (approximately 1800 mL/min)
  * Hepatic O2 consumption 6ml/100g/min
  * **Dual afferent blood supply**:
	* **Portal vein** - provides 75% of hepatic blood flow but less oxygen-rich blood
	* **Hepatic artery** - provides 25% of hepatic blood flow but more oxygen-rich blood (98% as per pulse oximetry)
	* Each vessel contributes about 50% of the hepatic oxygen supply due to differences in oxygen content
  * **Hepatic arterial buffer response** - reciprocal relationship between portal and arterial flow; hepatic artery can increase flow by up to 100% to maintain oxygen delivery when portal flow decreases (by adenosine)
* **Additional blood supply details** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Portal vein forms from capillary networks of intestines, stomach, spleen, and pancreas
  * Portal vein contains blood with lower oxygen saturation:
	* Fasting state: ~85% saturation - due to mesenteric arterial shunting through intestinal capillaries
	* Fed state: ~70% saturation - drops once eaten due to metabolism in gut
  * Hepatic artery semi-compensates when portal flow decreases, but portal vein cannot compensate for decreased arterial flow

### 6.2.2. REGULATION OF HEPATIC BLOOD FLOW

* **Intrinsic mechanisms** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **Hepatic artery**:
	* Maintains relatively constant flow through autoregulation
	* Below mean arterial pressure of 60 mmHg, autoregulation fails
	* Flow becomes pressure-dependent
  * **Portal vein**:
	* Contains little smooth muscle
	* Cannot regulate flow like arteries
	* Flow proportional to pressure gradient
* **Extrinsic mechanisms** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Sympathetic nervous system innervation:
	* Causes hepatic artery vasoconstriction
	* Affects portal vein, portal venules, and hepatic veins
	* Can mobilize ~250 mL of blood from capacitance vessels
	* With splanchnic vessels, can mobilize ~1000 mL during physiological stress
  - Hepatic artery has alpha, beta and dopamine receptors; Portal vein has alpha and dopamine receptors only.
	  - Epinephrine thus may cause portal venoconstriction and hepatic artery vasodilation (beta).
	  - Dopamine has minimal effects at physiological concentration
  - Glucagon vasodilates both hepatic artery and portal vein
  - Angiotensin II vasoconstricts both hepatic artery and portal vein
  - Vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP) and Secretin causes hepatic artery dilation
  - Vasopressin causes hepatic artery vasoconstriction
* **Clinical relevance: intraoperative blood flow** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **Anesthetic factors decreasing flow**:
	* Positive-pressure ventilation and PEEP > decreased portal venous flow; conversely, spontaneous ventilation increases systemic venous return and hepatic perfusion and venous return
	* Volatile agents and vasopressors (reduce arterial flow)
		* Halothane decreases hepatic artery flow the most, reducing hepatic arterial buffer mechanism
		* Enflurane decreases portal venous flow more than hepatic artery flow
		* Isoflurane decreases portal venous flow while maintaining arterial buffer mechanism 
		* Sevoflurane and Desflurane maintains or increases hepatic arterial flow
	* IV agents (Thiopentone, Etomidate, Propofol)
		* Dose dependent reduction in flow (decreased cardiac output, obtundation of hepatic arterial buffer mechanism)
	* Hypocapnea (reduces portal flow)
	* Neuraxial blockade (similar to general anesthesia) > decreased MAP > decreased portal venous flow
  * **Surgical factors**:
	* Retraction and packing significantly reduce flow
- **Others**:
  - Feeding > splanchnic vasodilation > increased portal venous flow
  - Exercise > splanchnic vasoconstriction > decreased portal venous flow
  - Hypocapnia > increased portal resistance > decreased portal venous flow by 30% (vice versa with Hypercapnia)
  - Hypoxia > transient decrease in hepatic arterial flow > returns to baseline within 20 minutes
  - Increased CVP / decreased right heart function > hepatic congestion
  - Acute haemorrhage
	  - Decreased portal venous flow > decreased hepatic arterial flow
	  - Sympathetic stimulation > mobilization of 50% of reservoir blood (30ml/100g of liver tissue)
	  - O2 supply maintained by increased O2 extraction
### 6.2.3. EFFECT OF RESPIRATORY CYCLE

* **Spontaneous breathing** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Inspiration (negative intrathoracic pressure) increases hepatic venous blood flow
  * Expiration decreases flow
* **Positive-pressure ventilation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Decreases hepatic venous blood flow due to positive intrathoracic pressure
  * PEEP further reduces flow by increasing intrathoracic pressure
* **Microcirculation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * **Three zones in liver lobule** with varying oxygen content:
	* **Zone 1** (periportal) - receives oxygen-rich blood from portal vein and hepatic artery
	* **Zone 2** (intermediate)
	* **Zone 3** (pericentral) - surrounds the central vein, oxygen-poor, site of anaerobic metabolism and cytochrome P450 enzymes
* **Lymphatic drainage** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Liver generates about half of the body's lymph
  * Fluid from spaces of Disse drains into lymphatic system

**Bottom Line:** The liver's dual blood supply and zonal microarchitecture are important to understand for anesthetic management, as hypoxia and metabolic stressors preferentially affect zone 3 hepatocytes, which are most vulnerable to drug toxicity and ischemia.

## 6.3. MACROSCOPIC ANATOMY OF THE LIVER
**Liver anatomy can be described in terms of either morphological appearance or functional divisions.**

* **Morphological anatomy** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Based on external appearance:
	* **Right anatomical lobe** - much larger
	* **Left anatomical lobe** - smaller
	* Divided by falciform ligament
  * When viewed from beneath:
	* **Caudate lobe** (posterior)
	* **Quadrate lobe** (inferior)
  * This morphological description has limited surgical utility
* **Functional anatomy (Couinaud classification)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Divides liver into eight functionally independent segments:
	* Each segment has its own blood supply and biliary drainage
	* Numbered 1-8 in clockwise direction starting from caudate lobe
  * **Porta hepatis** - point where portal vein, common bile duct, and hepatic artery enter liver
  * **Cantlie's line** - imaginary line between gallbladder fossa and inferior vena cava
	* Separates functional right and left halves
	* Vessels divide at this point (not at falciform ligament)
  * Surgically significant as lobes can function independently when others are resected

**Bottom Line:** Understanding liver anatomy, particularly the functional Couinaud classification, is essential for hepatobiliary surgery and interventions. The functional divisions don't correspond to the external appearance of the lobes.

## 6.4. MICROSCOPIC ANATOMY OF THE LIVER
**The liver's microscopic organization can be viewed using either a classical histological or functional approach.**

* **Histological approach (lobule)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Liver composed of tens of thousands of hexagonal lobules
  * **Central structure** - branch of hepatic vein at center
  * **Radiating structure** - columns of hepatocytes and sinusoids extending outward
  * **Peripheral structure** - hepatic triad at each corner of hexagon containing:
	* Portal venule
	* Hepatic arteriole
	* Bile duct
  * Limited utility in humans as derived from pig liver studies
  * Human liver has more disorganized architecture with more connective tissue
* **Functional approach (acinus)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Elliptical acinus is considered the true functional unit
  * Terminal hepatic veins at each end
  * Two portal triads at midpoint of flattened sides
  * Blood flows from portal triad toward terminal vein
  * **Three zones based on oxygen gradient**:
	* **Zone 1** (periportal) - closest to portal triads, best oxygenated
	  * Site of most energy-consuming processes (gluconeogenesis, β-oxidation)
	* **Zone 2** - intermediate area with characteristics of both zone 1 and 3
	* **Zone 3** (pericentral) - closest to terminal vein, lowest oxygen tension
	  * Site of least energy-consuming processes (glycolysis, drug metabolism)

## 6.5. CELL TYPES WITHIN THE LIVER
**The liver contains several specialized cell types with distinct functions.**

**![](https://lh7-rt.googleusercontent.com/docsz/AD_4nXdyAljSv5sBGd30YhLW0Q5C_oeSZc8XhSEWCu1mgQkLuEFRGmN5Av8bfLv5gV4NGPHSAM0Sv9FqbCjoeyxUcBMYmm7D-9kgQjg-jkWT-YUTvx9gEtTImiF7cU97meZL5AetzFnONWh75LMUP0UHbMPRMtk?key=-qqMwH0uWJGQ6I53QnMhWQ)**

* **Hepatocytes** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Main parenchymal cells (60% by mass, 80% by volume)
  * Arranged in columns
  * Perform wide range of metabolic, synthetic, and exocrine functions
  * Form bile canaliculi between adjacent cells
  * Secrete bile into canaliculi which flows to bile ductules and ducts
* **Kupffer cells** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Specialized macrophages (10% by mass)
  * Line walls of hepatic sinusoids
  * Primary function: destroy bacteria and foreign material from GI tract
  * Remove worn-out red blood cells and leukocytes
  * Part of reticuloendothelial system
* **Other non-parenchymal cells** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **Sinusoidal endothelial cells**:
	* Form highly permeable lining of blood-filled sinusoids
	* Lack tight junctions and have large fenestrations
	* Allow substances to filter through to perisinusoidal space
	* Synthesizes F8
  * **Perisinusoidal cells** (Ito cells, stellate cells):
	* Store vitamin A
	* Produce collagen in liver injury (key in fibrosis)
  * **Biliary epithelial cells**:
	* Line bile ducts
* **Clinical relevance: centrilobular necrosis** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Zone 3 (centrilobular) necrosis occurs in several situations:
	* **Hypoxic injury** - zone 3 most susceptible due to distance from oxygen supply
	* **Drug metabolism** - zone 3 is main site of drug metabolism via cytochrome P450
	  * Toxic metabolites (e.g., NAPQI from paracetamol) accumulate here
	* **Halothane hepatitis**:
	  * Type I - mild, transient enzyme elevation from reductive metabolism
	  * Type II - severe autoimmune reaction to trifluoroacetyl-protein adducts

**Bottom Line:** The liver's cellular organization includes hepatocytes for metabolic and synthetic functions, Kupffer cells for immune defense, and supporting cells that maintain structure and function. This organization explains the zonal pattern of certain liver injuries.

## 6.6. LIVER FUNCTIONS
**The liver performs over 500 different functions critical to metabolism, immunity, and homeostasis.**

### 6.6.1. SYNTHETIC FUNCTIONS

* **Protein synthesis** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Produces all plasma proteins except γ-globulins (formed in plasma cells)
  * Can synthesize 15-50 g of protein per day
  * **Albumin**:
	* Major protein synthesized by liver
	* 120-300mg/kg/day
	* Primary determinant of plasma oncotic pressure
	* Half-life of approximately 21 days (therefore, poor marker of acute liver damage)
  * **Coagulation factors**:
	* Synthesizes all clotting factors except factors III (tissue thromboplastin), IV (calcium), and VIII (von Willebrand factor)
	* Vitamin K required for synthesis of factors II, VII, IX, and X
  * **Non-essential amino acids**:
	* Synthesized from keto acids
  - **Complements, alpha and beta globulins, haptoglobin**
* **Bile production** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Produces approximately 500 mL of bile daily
  * Bile contains water, bile salts, bile pigment, bilirubin, cholesterol, other electrolytes, proteins, metabolites for excretion; isotonic aqueous solution (~500ml-1L/day)
  * Bile salts act as detergents for fat solubilization (because of its amphipathic properties, emulsifying fat to form micelles, increasing its surface area for digestive enzymes to work) and absorption
	  * Bile acids are steroid compounds produced in liver from cholesterol (~0.5g/day)
		  * Primary: synthesized in liver (cholic acid, chenodeoxycholic acid)
		  * Secondary: produced in intestines via action of gut bacteria (ileum, colon) on primary bile acids (deoxycholic acid, lithocholic acid)
	  * Conjugation of bile acids with Taurine / Glycine forms bile salts with sodium and potassium
  * Concentrated in gallbladder to 1/5th of its volume
  * 95% of bile acids are reabsorbed in the enterohepatic circulation at the terminal ileum via apical-Na-dependent transporter, returning to the liver in the portal vein
	  * By recycling, only low amounts of bile acids needs to be produced

### 6.6.2. METABOLIC FUNCTIONS

* **Carbohydrate metabolism** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * **Glucose regulation**:
	* Stores glucose as glycogen
		* Dietary carbohydrates are broken into mono and disaccharide by intestinal enzymatic cleavage. These enter the portal venous system by an active-energy dependent process but enter the hepatic cells by passive diffusion.
		* Glucokinase in hepatocytes convert glucose into glucose-6-phosphate to maintain low intracellular glucose concentration.
	* Converts other carbohydrates to glucose
	* Synthesizes glucose from amino acids and triglycerides (gluconeogenesis)
	* Also responds to insulin and glucagon
  * Acts as glucose buffer for blood
* **Lipid metabolism** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  - Dietary triglycerides
	  - 50% hydrolyzed to glycerol + fatty acids
	  - 40% partially hydrolyzed to monoglycerides
	  - Short chain FA (<12 carbon atoms) are directly transported to liver via portal vein without re-esterification
	  - Longer chain FA are re-esterified to form chylomicrons. Chylomicrons are then hydrolyzed by lipoprotein lipases to form free FA (taken up by adipocytes for storage or metabolized for energy)
  * Synthesizes fat, cholesterol, and phospholipids
  * Generates acetyl-CoA for energy production
  * Packages excess acetyl-CoA as acetoacetic acid (ketone bodies) for use elsewhere in the body (ketogenesis)
  * Cholesterol synthesis - majority (80%) converted to bile salts
* **Protein metabolism** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Deamination of amino acids for energy production or conversion to carbohydrates/fats
	  * Protein turnover in liver: 10 days (vs 180 days for muscle protein)
	  * Transamination, Deamination, Decarboxylation
	  * Amino acids degraded mainly in liver: Arginine, Lysine, Methionine, Phenylalanine, Tryptophan
	  * Amino acids degraded in liver and muscle: Glycine, Alanine, Proline, Glutamic acid, Aspartic acid
  * Removal of ammonia through urea formation
	  * 100g dietary protein > 30g urea 
	  * Urea cycle is a energy dependent process (requires 3 ATP / urea molecule)
	  ![[Pasted image 20250602170834.png]]
  * Ammonia is toxic (>1mcg/ml); intestinal bacteria are an additional source
* **Drug metabolism** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Biotransformation of lipophilic drugs to more water-soluble compounds for excretion
  * Phase I reactions (oxidation (CYP450), reduction (reductase), hydrolysis (hydrolase))
	  * Increases water solubility
	  * CYP450 located in SER, Reductase and Hydrolase are located in cytoplasm
  * Phase II reactions (conjugation - glucuronidation, sulphation, acetylation)
	  * Gluronidation mainly in periportal hepatocytes
  * Clearance dependent on: hepatic blood flow, plasma protein binding, hepatic enzyme activity
- **Acid-Base Metabolism**:
  - CO2 production form substrate oxidation
	  - Liver utilizes 20% of body O2 requirements, produces 20% of body's CO2 > diffuses out of liver, excreted via lungs
  - Metabolism of acid anions
	  - Endogenous (lactate, ketoacids), Exogenous (citrate from transfusion, acetate/gluconate from IV fluids)
	  - Metabolism of exogenous anions (if not administered with H+) > net HCO3- production
	  - Metabolism of endogenous anions > no net production of H+/HCO3-
  - Metabolism of amino acids
	  - Net production of 50mmol/day of fixed acids (acid produced by source other than CO2 and not excreted by lungs)
  - Metabolism of NH4+ (ammonium)
	  - NH4+ > Urea + H+
### 6.6.3. STORAGE FUNCTIONS

* **Blood storage** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Can store up to 1 L of blood
  * Serves as a reservoir during hypovolemia or hypervolemia
* **Vitamin storage** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Vitamin A (10-month supply)
  * Vitamin D (3-month supply)
  * Vitamin E
  * Vitamin K
  * Vitamin B12 (1-year supply)
  * Folate
* **Mineral storage** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Iron stored as ferritin (transported via apoferritin), released when circulating iron levels are low
  * Copper

### 6.6.4. IMMUNE AND HEMATOLOGICAL FUNCTIONS

* **Kupffer cells** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Reticuloendothelial cells lining the venous sinusoids
  * Phagocytize bacteria entering from intestines
  * Less than 1% of bacteria pass through to systemic circulation
* **Hematopoiesis** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Site of hematopoiesis in the fetus
* **Regeneration capability** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Can restore itself after injury or partial hepatectomy
  * Up to two-thirds of the liver can be removed with complete regeneration within weeks
  * Process involves hepatocyte growth factor, epidermal growth factor, cytokines, and other factors
* **Additional functions** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **Immunological functions**:
	* Phagocytosis of microorganisms from portal vein
	* Initiation of inflammatory response via Kupffer cells
	* Synthesis of complement proteins and C-reactive protein
  * **Endocrine functions**:
	* Secretion of hormones (angiotensinogen, thrombopoietin, hepcidin, IGF-1)
	* Synthesis of hormone binding proteins (thyroxine-binding globulin, sex hormone-binding globulin)
	* Activation of hormones (thyroxine to T3, vitamin D hydroxylation)
	* Inactivation of hormones (aldosterone, ADH, insulin, estrogen)
  * **Haematological functions**:
	* RBC removal (shared with spleen)
	* Blood reservoir (can release 250mL with sympathetic stimulation)

**Bottom Line:** The liver's diverse functions in synthesis, metabolism, storage, and immunity make it a critical organ for maintaining homeostasis. Liver dysfunction can have wide-ranging effects on multiple organ systems.

## 6.7. ASSESSMENT OF HEPATIC FUNCTION 


**Various laboratory tests and imaging modalities help assess hepatic function and structure.**

### 6.7.1. LIVER FUNCTION TESTS

* **Markers of hepatocellular injury** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Aspartate aminotransferase (AST)**:
	* Previously called serum glutamic oxaloacetic transaminase (SGOT)
	* Found in liver and other tissues (heart, skeletal muscle, kidney, brain)
	* Elevations not specific for hepatic disease
  * **Alanine aminotransferase (ALT)**:
	* Previously called serum glutamic pyruvic transaminase (SGPT)
	* Primarily localized to the liver
	* More specific for liver disease than AST
  * **Patterns of elevation**:
	* Mild elevations (several fold) - fatty liver, chronic infections
	* Large elevations (>50 times normal) - acute hepatic necrosis
	* AST/ALT ratio >2 suggests alcoholic liver disease
	* AST/ALT ratio <1 suggests viral hepatitis, NAFLD
* **Markers of bile flow obstruction** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Alkaline phosphatase (AP)**:
	* Found in multiple organs (liver, bone, kidney, intestines, placenta)
	* Elevations disproportionate to AST/ALT suggest bile duct obstruction (cholestasis)
  * **5′-nucleotidase (5′-NT)**:
	* Highly specific for hepatobiliary obstruction
	* Useful to determine if elevated AP is of hepatic origin
  * **γ-glutamyl transferase (GGT)**:
	* Sensitive but less specific indicator of biliary tract disease
  * **Bilirubin**:
	* Primarily from breakdown of hemoglobin from senescent red blood cells
	* Two fractions:
	  * **Unconjugated (indirect)** - lipid-soluble
	  * **Conjugated (direct)** - water-soluble
	* Elevated unconjugated bilirubin suggests hemolysis or decreased hepatic uptake/conjugation
	* Elevated conjugated bilirubin suggests impaired intrahepatic excretion or extrahepatic obstruction
* **Tests of hepatic synthetic function** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Serum albumin**:
	* Reflects liver's synthetic capacity
	* 3-week half-life makes it less reliable for acute liver disease
	* Can be low due to other causes (protein loss, malnutrition)
  * **Prothrombin time (PT) and international normalized ratio (INR)**:
	* Sensitive indicators of hepatic disease due to short half-life of factor VII
	* Can be prolonged in vitamin K deficiency despite preserved hepatic function
	* Other conditions affecting PT/INR: congenital coagulation factor deficiencies, DIC, warfarin therapy
* **Other specialized tests** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Indocyanine green (ICG) elimination** - estimates hepatic blood flow and hepatocellular function
  * **MEGX test** - measures conversion of lidocaine to monoethylglycinexylidide via hepatic demethylation
  * **Serologic tests** for specific diagnoses:
	* Viral hepatitis serologies
	* Autoantibodies (for primary biliary cirrhosis)
	* Ceruloplasmin (Wilson disease)
	* Ferritin (hemochromatosis)
	* α1-antitrypsin (α1-antitrypsin deficiency)
	* α-fetoprotein (hepatocellular carcinoma)
  * **Serum ammonia** - useful for monitoring hepatic encephalopathy
* **Additional liver function tests** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **Tests of liver synthetic function**:
	* **Albumin** - half-life of 30 days, poor marker in acute disease
	* **PT/INR** - short half-life (<24h) makes it effective in acute dysfunction
  * **Tests of hepatic clearance**:
	* **Bilirubin** - breakdown product of hemoglobin
	* **Ammonia** - normally converted to urea, rises in severe dysfunction
  * **Serum hepatic enzyme tests**:
	* **ALP** - in biliary canalicular membrane, elevated in biliary obstruction
	* **γ-GT** - also elevated in biliary obstruction, helps distinguish bone vs. liver ALP elevation
	* **ALT** - cytosolic enzyme, elevated in generalized hepatocellular injury
* **Clinical relevance: King's College Criteria** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Used to predict patients needing liver transplant after paracetamol overdose
  * Transplantation considered if either:
	* Arterial pH <7.3
	* Or all three of: PT >100s (INR >6.5), creatinine >300µmol/L, and grade III/IV encephalopathy

### 6.7.2. HEPATOBILIARY IMAGING

* **Plain radiography** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Limited role in liver disease evaluation
  * Can detect calcified gallstones, chronic calcific pancreatitis, gas-containing abscesses
* **Ultrasonography** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Primary screening test for hepatic parenchymal and extrahepatic biliary disease
  * Best for detecting gallstones, ascites, portal/hepatic vein thrombosis
  * Limitations: operator-dependent, cannot penetrate bone or air
* **Computed tomography (CT)** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Supplements ultrasonography
  * Provides information on liver texture, gallbladder disease, bile duct dilation, mass lesions
  * Less operator-dependent than ultrasound
  * Allows for CT-guided biopsy
  * Disadvantages: radiation exposure, cost
* **Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Superior to CT for malignant focal liver lesions and diffuse liver disease
  * Useful for biliary disease evaluation
  * Advantages: no radiation, no contrast-induced nephropathy risk
  * Disadvantages: requires breath-hold sequences, may need sedation/anesthesia
<!-- EPA-8:K1:biliary_disease_ercp EPA-8:K2:ercp_interventions EPA-8:K3:endoscopy_centre -->
* **Cholangiography** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Percutaneous transhepatic cholangiography (THC)**:
	* Percutaneous injection of contrast into bile ducts under fluoroscopy
	* Determines site/cause of biliary obstruction
	* Allows for therapeutic interventions (dilatation, stent placement)
  * **Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP)**:
	* Uses endoscopy to visualize ampulla of Vater and inject contrast
	* Permits sphincterotomy, stone extraction, biopsy, stent insertion
	* Does not require dilated biliary tree
* **Radioisotope scanning** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Primarily used for suspected acute cholecystitis
  * Visualized isotope in gallbladder rules out cystic duct obstruction

### 6.7.3. LIVER BIOPSY

* **Indications** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Determine whether liver damage is due to necrosis, inflammation, steatosis, or fibrosis
  * Diagnosis and staging of liver disease
* **Contraindications** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Coagulopathy
  * Thrombocytopenia
  * (Transjugular approach may be used in these conditions)

**Bottom Line:** A comprehensive assessment of liver function requires integration of laboratory testing, imaging, and sometimes biopsy. Understanding the significance and limitations of each test helps in accurate diagnosis and perioperative risk stratification.

### 6.7.4. MEASUREMENT OF HEPATIC BLOOD FLOW

- **Direct**: (not done in real life)
	- Electromagnetic flowmeters applied directly around hepatic artery / portal vein.
	- Requires surgery to implant device which may decrease total hepatic blood flow 
- **Indirect**:
	- Clearance (as a surrogate): ICG, Sulphobromophthalein (SBP), iodine-131-labelled albumin
		- Assumes substance is solely extracted by liver.
	- Reticuloendothelial cell uptake
		- Gamma camera detects uptake of radiolabelled colloidal substances by Kupffer cells
## 6.8. HEPATIC AND HEPATOBILIARY DISEASES

**Liver disease has multiple etiologies and can be classified based on the primary anatomy affected and chronicity.**

* **Classification by anatomy affected** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Hepatocellular (parenchymal)** - primary liver cell damage
  * **Biliary** - primary damage to bile ducts
	* Note: Cholestasis precedes hepatocellular dysfunction in biliary diseases, while in hepatocellular diseases, cholestasis and synthetic dysfunction appear simultaneously
* **Classification by chronicity** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Acute liver disease** - duration <26 weeks
  * **Chronic liver disease** - duration >26 weeks
* **Epidemiology** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * 11th leading cause of death in the United States (42,000 deaths in 2017)
  * Overall prevalence of chronic liver disease is almost 36%
  * Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is increasingly prevalent, paralleling increases in obesity and type 2 diabetes

### 6.8.1. ACUTE LIVER FAILURE

* **Definition** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Encephalopathy plus coagulopathy (INR ≥1.5) in a patient without pre-existing liver disease
  * Illness duration <26 weeks
* **Epidemiology** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Rare, approximately 2,000 cases per year in the United States
  * Drug-related toxicity accounts for >50% of cases (>80% of these due to acetaminophen)
  * Other causes: idiopathic, acute viral hepatitis, autoimmune, ischemic
  * Natural history: 45% spontaneous recovery, 25% liver transplantation, 30% death without transplantation
* **Clinical manifestations** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Encephalopathy** - required for diagnosis
	* Graded I-IV (see Table 46-3)
	* Cerebral edema risk correlates with encephalopathy depth
	  * Stage I-II: negligible risk
	  * Stage III: 25-35% risk
	  * Stage IV: 75% risk
  * **Coagulopathy** - INR ≥1.5
  * **Other organ system effects**:
	* Circulatory dysfunction and hypotension
	* Acute kidney injury
	* Metabolic derangements
* **Management** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Cerebral edema management**:
	* 30-degree head-up position with head in neutral position
	* Muscle relaxants after intubation to prevent coughing/bucking
	* Mannitol for osmotic diuresis (if renal function adequate)
	* Hypertonic saline (target Na+ 145-155 mEq/L)
	* Maintain normocarbia (reserve hyperventilation for acute ICP rises)
	* Cooling to core temperature of 33-34°C
	* Pentobarbital for refractory intracranial hypertension
  * **Coagulopathy management**:
	* Correction generally reserved for active bleeding or invasive procedures
	* Target platelets >50,000/mm³ and INR <1.5 for procedures
	* Prophylactic therapy generally not recommended except for platelets <10,000/mm³
  * **Hemodynamic management**:
	* Volume assessment and cardiac function evaluation
	* Vasopressors (norepinephrine or dopamine) for hypotension or inadequate CPP
* **Prognosis** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **King's College Hospital criteria** (see Table 46-5):
	* Separate criteria for acetaminophen and non-acetaminophen ALF
	* Good positive predictive value but lower negative predictive value for non-acetaminophen ALF
  * **Clichy criteria**:
	* Based on age and factor V levels
	* Transplantation threshold: factor V <20% (age <30 years) or <30% (age >30 years)

### 6.8.2. ACUTE HEPATITIS

* **Viral hepatitis** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Hepatitis A (HAV)**:
	* Picornavirus spread by fecal-oral route
	* Incubation period: weeks to months
	* Wide range of manifestations from asymptomatic to ALF
	* ALF rare (<1%) with high spontaneous recovery rate (69%)
	* No chronic disease state
  * **Hepatitis B (HBV)**:
	* DNA virus spread via parenteral, cutaneous, or mucosal exposure
	* HBsAg is hallmark of active infection
	* ALF in <1% of cases with only 20% spontaneous recovery
	* Chronic infection in 2-5% of infected adults
  * **Hepatitis C (HCV)**:
	* Transmission primarily parenteral
	* High rate of progression to chronic disease (50-85%)
	* Risk of cirrhosis: 5-25% over 25-30 years
  * **Hepatitis D (HDV or delta-virus)**:
	* Requires HBV for virion assembly
	* Present in 5% of chronic HBV patients
	* Can cause severe illness and ALF
  * **Hepatitis E (HEV)**:
	* Small RNA virus
	* Responsible for epidemics in areas with poor sanitation
* **Common symptoms** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Fatigue, poor appetite, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain
  * Many infections are subclinical
  * Some present with jaundice or serum-sickness-like symptoms (fever, arthralgia, rash)

### 6.8.3. ALCOHOLIC LIVER DISEASE

* **Alcoholic hepatitis** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Characterized by jaundice and liver dysfunction in setting of heavy alcohol use
  * Laboratory findings:
	* Moderate aminotransferase elevations (<300 IU/mL)
	* AST > ALT (AST:ALT ratio >2 in about 70% of patients)
	* Elevated creatinine ominous (may indicate impending HRS)
  * Up to 20% may have coexisting cause of liver disease
  * Key treatment: abstinence
  * Severe cases may benefit from nutritional therapy and medical management

### 6.8.4. DRUG-INDUCED LIVER INJURY

* **General characteristics** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Common cause of morbidity and mortality
  * Leading reason for regulatory actions on pharmaceuticals
  * Nonacetaminophen DILI: 11-13% of ALF cases with 20% spontaneous recovery
* **Mechanisms** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Direct hepatotoxicity
  * Toxic metabolites (often from cytochrome P450 metabolism)
  * Cell injury via:
	* Cell stress (glutathione depletion, protein/lipid binding)
	* Mitochondrial injury (uncoupling respiration, ATP depletion, ROS accumulation)
	* Immune-mediated mechanisms (antibody formation against cell structures)
* **Halothane hepatitis** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Recognized shortly after halothane's introduction in 1956
  * Two forms:
	* Mild, self-limited form: elevated liver enzymes without failure in up to 20%
	* Severe, fulminant form: immune-mediated process with antibodies against liver proteins modified by trifluoroacetyl (TFA) metabolite
  * Other halogenated anesthetics (enflurane, isoflurane, desflurane) associated with rare hepatitis
  * Risk correlates with metabolism rate: halothane (20%) > enflurane (2%) > isoflurane (0.2%) > desflurane (0.01%)

### 6.8.5. PREGNANCY-RELATED LIVER DISEASES

* **Prevalence and classification** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Abnormal liver studies in 3-5% of pregnancies
  * Five acute pregnancy-related conditions:
	* Hyperemesis gravidarum
	* Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy
	* Preeclampsia
	* HELLP syndrome (hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, low platelets)
	* Acute fatty liver of pregnancy (AFLP)
* **Hyperemesis gravidarum** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * First trimester condition characterized by severe vomiting requiring IV hydration
  * Risk factors: hyperthyroidism, molar pregnancy, multiple pregnancies
  * Liver enzymes increased in ~50% of patients (up to 20-fold)
  * Bilirubin usually normal or minimally elevated
  * Treatment: supportive; usually resolves by second trimester
* **Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Second to third trimester presentation
  * Etiology: interference with bile acid transport
  * Findings: pruritus, modest bilirubin increase (<5 mg/dL), aminotransferase elevation (up to 20-fold), serum bile acids increased up to 100-fold
  * Associated with chronic placental insufficiency, premature labor, sudden fetal death
  * Considered a high-risk pregnancy
* **HELLP syndrome** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Occurs in 20% of severely preeclamptic patients
  * Due to vascular endothelial injury with fibrin deposition and platelet consumption
  * Can lead to hepatic infarction, hemorrhage, hematomas, and capsular rupture
  * Laboratory findings:
	* Elevated aminotransferases (10-20 fold)
	* Modest bilirubin increase
	* Schistocytes and burr cells on peripheral smear
	* Platelet count used to classify severity:
	  * Mild: 100,000-150,000/mm³
	  * Moderate: 50,000-100,000/mm³
	  * Severe: <50,000/mm³
  * Imaging: Abdominal CT preferred for major hepatic complications
  * Management:
	* Delivery is definitive therapy
	* Contained hemorrhage: conservative management
	* Capsular rupture: emergency laparotomy
	* Rarely, transplantation for uncontrolled bleeding
* **Acute Fatty Liver of Pregnancy (AFLP)** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Rapid microvesicular fatty infiltration of liver causing portal hypertension and encephalopathy
  * Associated with abnormalities in β-oxidation of fatty acids
  * Similar symptoms to severe preeclampsia/HELLP but may have additional features of liver failure:
	* Hypoglycemia
	* Elevated ammonia
	* Asterixis
	* Encephalopathy
  * Management: rapid delivery
  * Recovery may be prolonged in severely ill patients
  * Role for transplantation if progression to ALF continues after delivery

**Bottom Line:** Acute liver diseases have diverse etiologies but share common pathways of liver injury. Early recognition and appropriate management, including consideration for transplantation in severe cases, are essential for optimal outcomes.

### 6.8.6. CHRONIC LIVER DISEASE

* **Epidemiology and trends** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Prevalence increased from 25% (1988-1994) to 36% (2013-2016)
  * Major causes and prevalence (2013-2016):
	* Hepatitis B: 0.35%
	* Hepatitis C: 2.7%
	* Alcoholic liver disease: 1.0%
	* NAFLD: 32.0%
  * Increase in CLD almost entirely attributable to NAFLD
* **Chronic viral hepatitis** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Hepatitis B**:
	* DNA virus with potential for integration into host genome
	* High risk for hepatocellular carcinoma
	* Chronic infection in 2-5% of infected adults
  * **Hepatitis C**:
	* RNA virus with high mutation rate
	* Chronic infection in 50-85% of cases
	* 5-25% risk of cirrhosis over 25-30 years
	* Can be cured with direct-acting antivirals
* **Alcoholic liver disease**:
  * Spectrum from fatty liver to alcoholic hepatitis to cirrhosis
  * Liver damage correlates with amount and duration of alcohol consumption
  * Abstinence can lead to significant improvement

**Bottom Line:** Chronic liver disease has multiple etiologies with nonalcoholic fatty liver disease becoming increasingly prevalent. The disease process can range from mild inflammation to cirrhosis and end-stage liver disease.

### 6.8.7. CIRRHOSIS AND PORTAL HYPERTENSION

* **Definition and pathophysiology** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * End product of CLD with parenchymal inflammation, necrosis, and disruption of normal architecture
  * Fibrosis and regenerative nodules replace normal hepatic lobules
  * Disrupted blood flow leads to portal hypertension (HVPG >5 mmHg)
  * Severe portal hypertension defined as HVPG >10-12 mmHg
* **Systemic effects** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Affects multiple organ systems
  * Complications include:
	* Hemostatic abnormalities
	* Cardiac dysfunction
	* Renal dysfunction
	* Pulmonary complications
	* Hepatic encephalopathy
	* Ascites
	* Variceal bleeding
* **Physiological changes in cirrhosis** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **Liver function alterations**:
	* Disturbed carbohydrate metabolism (hyper/hypoglycemia)
	* Decreased protein synthesis (clotting abnormalities, hypoalbuminemia)
	* Altered drug metabolism
	* Inadequate ammonia clearance leading to encephalopathy
  * **Anatomical alterations**:
	* Increased resistance to blood flow from fibrosis
	* Portal hypertension (>12 mmHg)
	* Results in:
	  * Ascites - high venous pressure causes fluid filtration into peritoneum
	  * Splenomegaly - impaired venous drainage leading to thrombocytopenia and anemia
	  * Portocaval anastomoses - collateral vessels between portal and systemic circulation (esophageal varices, rectal varices, caput medusae)

**Bottom Line:** Cirrhosis represents the end stage of chronic liver injury, characterized by fibrosis, regenerative nodules, and portal hypertension that lead to multiple systemic complications.

### 6.8.8. HEPATOCELLULAR CARCINOMA

* **Epidemiology** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Second leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide
  * Accounts for 75% of primary liver cancers
  * Risk factors: chronic viral hepatitis, hemochromatosis, cirrhosis
  * 80-90% of HCC patients have underlying cirrhosis
* **Treatment options** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Surgical resection**:
	* Optimal treatment but requires sufficient hepatic reserve
	* <5% of U.S. patients are candidates
  * **Liver transplantation**:
	* Option for non-resection candidates
	* Eligibility criteria include Milan criteria (one tumor ≤5 cm or ≤3 tumors ≤3 cm each)
  * **Locoregional therapies**:
	* Radiofrequency ablation
	* Chemoembolization
	* Can be used for patients ineligible for surgery/transplant
	* Sometimes used to maintain transplant eligibility while on waiting list

**Bottom Line:** Hepatocellular carcinoma is a common complication of cirrhosis with limited treatment options that depend on the extent of underlying liver dysfunction and tumor burden.

### 6.8.9. NONALCOHOLIC FATTY LIVER DISEASE

* **Definition and spectrum** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Range of conditions characterized by excessive hepatic fat deposition
  * Spectrum from simple steatosis to nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH)
  * NASH includes inflammation and hepatocellular necrosis
* **Epidemiology** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Most common cause of elevated liver enzymes in adults
  * Considered the hepatic manifestation of metabolic syndrome
  * Prevalence of NASH in U.S.: 3-5%
* **Associations** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome components:
	* Obesity, especially central adiposity
	* Type 2 diabetes
	* Arterial hypertension
	* Hypertriglyceridemia
  * Can coexist with and exacerbate other liver diseases:
	* Chronic hepatitis C
	* Hemochromatosis
	* Alcoholic liver disease
* **Diagnosis** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Gold standard: liver biopsy
  * Key features: macrovesicular steatosis, lobular inflammation, hepatocyte ballooning, perisinusoidal fibrosis

**Bottom Line:** NAFLD represents a spectrum of liver disease strongly associated with metabolic syndrome, with NASH being the aggressive form that can progress to cirrhosis and HCC.

### 6.8.10. CHOLESTATIC LIVER DISEASE

* **Pathophysiology** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Biliary obstruction increases pressure in bile ducts
  * Bile refluxes into liver sinusoids and may enter vascular system
  * Serum levels of bilirubin, bile salts, and alkaline phosphatase rise
  * Loss of intestinal bile salts can lead to:
	* Bacterial overgrowth
	* Endotoxemia
	* Acute kidney injury
* **Primary biliary cirrhosis (PBC)** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Progressive destruction of small intrahepatic bile ducts with portal inflammation
  * Laboratory hallmark: antimitochondrial antibody (present in 95% of patients)
  * Disease course: steady progression over 10-20 years
  * Treatment: ursodeoxycholic acid (immunomodulatory effects)
  * Definitive therapy: liver transplantation (10-35% recurrence)
* **Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC)** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Progressive inflammatory disease of medium and large intra/extrahepatic bile ducts
  * Diagnostic studies: ERCP and MRCP show characteristic beaded pattern
  * Strong association with inflammatory bowel disease (primarily ulcerative colitis)
  * Complications:
	* Recurrent bacterial cholangitis
	* Cholangiocarcinoma (15-30% over disease course)
	* Colon carcinoma (in those with IBD)
  * Definitive therapy: liver transplantation (with disease recurrence)

**Bottom Line:** Cholestatic liver diseases involve bile duct inflammation and destruction, leading to progressive fibrosis, cirrhosis, and malignancy risk. They have distinct diagnostic features and therapeutic approaches compared to hepatocellular diseases.

## 6.9. COMPLICATIONS OF CIRRHOSIS

### 6.9.1. HEMOSTATIC ABNORMALITIES

* **Coagulation balance** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Cirrhotic patients have traditionally been considered to have a bleeding diathesis based on abnormal PT/PTT
  * However, both procoagulant and anticoagulant factors are reduced
  * Balance of these factors often results in normal thrombin generation in vivo
  * Recent evidence suggests cirrhotic patients may have a procoagulant imbalance due to:
	* Reduced levels of protein C and antithrombin III
	* Increased factor VIII and von Willebrand factor
  * Studies show cirrhotic patients have an increased risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE)
* **Platelet abnormalities** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Thrombocytopenia occurs in 30-64% of cirrhotics
  * Primary cause: splenic sequestration due to portal hypertension
  * Other factors:
	* Decreased thrombopoietin production
	* Immunologic mechanisms
	* Bone marrow suppression
	* Consumptive processes
  * Minimum platelet count for normal thrombin generation: approximately 56,000/mm³
* **Fibrinolysis abnormalities** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Accelerated fibrinolysis: 30-46% incidence in end-stage liver disease
  * Elevated tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) levels (reduced clearance)
  * Reduced plasmin inhibitors (PAI-1, TAFI)
  * Assessment: euglobulin clot lysis time (ECLT), viscoelastic testing
* **Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Overt DIC probably not a feature of stable CLD
  * "Accelerated intravascular coagulation and fibrinolysis (AICF)" occurs in <30% of cirrhotics
  * More common in severe, decompensated disease
  * Increases risk of progression to DIC with stimuli like sepsis or SBP

**Bottom Line:** The hemostatic system in cirrhosis is rebalanced rather than simply deranged toward bleeding. Understanding this concept helps guide appropriate blood product administration and thromboprophylaxis.

### 6.9.2. CARDIAC MANIFESTATIONS

* **Cirrhotic cardiomyopathy** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Four key components:
	* Hyperdynamic circulation (high cardiac output, low SVR)
	* Systolic and diastolic dysfunction
	* Cardiac resistance to β-adrenergic stimulation
	* Electrophysiologic abnormalities
  * Systolic dysfunction revealed only under stress
  * Diastolic dysfunction makes patients sensitive to changes in cardiac filling
  * Autonomic dysfunction in 43% of cirrhotic patients
  * Prolonged QTc interval (30% in Child's A to 60% in Child's C)
* **Coronary artery disease** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Risk factors similar to general population
  * NASH associated with increased cardiac risk
  * Diagnostic challenges:
	* Many patients cannot exercise
	* Dobutamine stress echocardiography has poor sensitivity
	* Consider coronary angiography for transplant candidates with high CAD likelihood

**Bottom Line:** Cardiovascular complications in cirrhosis include hyperdynamic circulation, subtle cardiomyopathy that may decompensate with stress, and increased risk of coronary disease, especially with NASH.

### 6.9.3. RENAL DYSFUNCTION

* **Pathophysiology** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Hallmarks: inappropriate sodium and water retention, renal hypoperfusion, decreased GFR
  * Hepatorenal syndrome (HRS): functional prerenal abnormality due to circulatory changes
  * Renal function is a key predictor of mortality (included in MELD score)
* **Causes of renal dysfunction in cirrhosis** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Hepatorenal syndrome
  * Parenchymal renal disease
  * Sepsis
  * Nephrotoxicity
  * Hypovolemia
  * Comorbidities:
	* Glomerulonephritis
	* Diabetic nephropathy
	* Immune complex nephropathies (with hepatitis C)
	* Systemic diseases affecting both liver and kidney
* **Nephrotoxic medications** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * NSAIDs (inhibit renal prostaglandins)
  * Aminoglycosides
  * ACE inhibitors and ARBs
* **Hepatorenal syndrome (HRS)** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Functional derangement (kidneys from HRS patients can be successfully transplanted)
  * Accounts for 23% of acute kidney injury cases in hospitalized cirrhotics
  * Incidence in cirrhotic patients with ascites: 18% at 1 year, 39% at 5 years
  * Current classification:
	* **HRS-AKI** (formerly Type 1): Acute kidney injury, potentially reversible
	* **HRS-CKD** (formerly Type 2): Chronic kidney disease, reduced GFR <60 mL/1.73 m² for ≥3 months
* **Management of HRS** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Vasoconstrictors plus volume expansion**:
	* Vasopressin or analogues (terlipressin)
	* Somatostatin or analogues
	* α-agonists (norepinephrine, midodrine)
	* Terlipressin effective in 40-60% of HRS-AKI
  * **TIPS**: Limited application due to exclusionary criteria
  * **Liver transplantation**: Definitive therapy
  * **Renal replacement therapy**: Bridge to transplantation
  * Consider combined liver-kidney transplantation for patients on dialysis >6 weeks

**Bottom Line:** Renal dysfunction in cirrhosis has multiple etiologies with hepatorenal syndrome being a unique functional disorder requiring specific management strategies and carrying significant prognostic implications.

### 6.9.4. PULMONARY COMPLICATIONS

* **Overview** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * 50-70% of patients with CLD complain of shortness of breath
  * Differential diagnosis includes:
	* Ventilation-perfusion abnormalities from underlying lung disease
	* Fluid retention
	* Pleural effusion
	* Decreased lung capacities from ascites
  * Two unique vascular abnormalities:
	* Hepatopulmonary syndrome (HPS)
	* Portopulmonary hypertension (POPH)
* **Hepatopulmonary syndrome (HPS)** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Definition**: Liver dysfunction + unexplained hypoxemia + intrapulmonary vascular dilation (IPVD)
  * **Diagnostic criteria**:
	* PaO₂ <80 mmHg or A-a gradient ≥15 mmHg on room air
	* Positive findings on contrast-enhanced echocardiography or abnormal radioactive lung-perfusion scan
	* Liver disease (usually portal hypertension)
  * **Pathophysiology**:
	* Type I lesions: Precapillary dilations at alveolar level (more common)
	* Type II lesions: Larger, centrally located dilations behaving as anatomic shunts
	* Capillary diameter increases from 8-15 μm to 50-500 μm
	* Results in functional shunting of poorly oxygenated blood
  * **Clinical features**:
	* Orthodeoxia (worsening hypoxemia when standing)
	* Type I lesions responsive to supplemental oxygen
	* Type II lesions (true anatomic shunts) minimally responsive to oxygen
  * **Treatment**:
	* Liver transplantation is definitive therapy
	* Can take up to a year for complete correction of hypoxemia
* **Portopulmonary hypertension (POPH)** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Definition**: Pulmonary hypertension in a patient with portal hypertension without other known cause
  * **Diagnostic criteria** (Table 46-7):
	* Portal hypertension with or without hepatic disease
	* Mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) >20 mmHg
	* Mean pulmonary artery occlusion pressure (mPAOP) <15 mmHg
	* Pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) >240 dynes/sec/cm⁵ or >3 Wood units
  * **Severity grading**:
	* Mild: mPAP <35 mmHg
	* Moderate: mPAP 35-50 mmHg
	* Severe: mPAP >50 mmHg
  * **Epidemiology**:
	* 2% prevalence in patients with portal hypertension
	* 4-6% prevalence among liver transplant candidates
	* Unrelated to severity of underlying liver disease
	* Higher risk: females, autoimmune hepatitis
	* Lower risk: chronic hepatitis C
  * **Diagnosis**:
	* Screening: 2D transthoracic echocardiography (97% sensitivity, 77% specificity)
	* Confirmation: right heart catheterization
  * **Treatment**:
	* Conventional management (diuresis)
	* Vasodilator therapy:
	  * Calcium channel blockers contraindicated (worsen portal hypertension)
	  * Prostanoids (epoprostenol)
	  * Phosphodiesterase inhibitors (sildenafil)
	  * Endothelin antagonists (bosentan, ambrisentan)
  * **Liver transplantation**:
	* Unpredictable outcomes
	* Patients with mPAP <35 mmHg can generally tolerate transplant
	* Those with mPAP ≥35 mmHg may be considered if therapy reduces mPAP <35 mmHg and PVR <240 dynes/sec/cm⁵

**Bottom Line:** Pulmonary vascular complications in liver disease include hepatopulmonary syndrome (characterized by hypoxemia) and portopulmonary hypertension (characterized by pulmonary arterial hypertension), both affecting candidacy and outcomes for liver transplantation.

### 6.9.5. HEPATIC ENCEPHALOPATHY

* **Definition and classification** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Reversible neuropsychiatric complication of liver disease
  * Spectrum from minimal (detected only by formal testing) to overt HE
  * Graded I-IV (Table 46-3)
  * Associated with advanced hepatocellular disease and portal-systemic shunting
* **Pathophysiology** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Result of failure to metabolize neurotoxic substances
  * Multiple contributors:
	* Hyperammonemia (central role)
	* Other gut-derived neurotoxins
	* GABA and endogenous GABA receptor agonists
	* Oxidative stress
	* Inflammatory mediators
	* Hyponatremia
	* Abnormal serotonin and histamine neurotransmission
  * Low-grade cerebral edema (unlike ALF, intracranial hypertension is rare)
* **Evaluation** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Rule out other causes:
	* Other metabolic encephalopathies (uremia, sepsis, glucose/electrolyte abnormalities)
	* Structural/vascular CNS lesions
	* CNS infections
	* Drug-related encephalopathy (cirrhotic patients extremely sensitive to sedatives)
  * Search for precipitating factors (Table 46-8):
	* Constipation
	* Dehydration
	* GI bleeding
	* Infection (especially SBP)
	* Excessive dietary protein
	* Electrolyte disturbances
	* Medications
* **Treatment** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Address underlying cause
  * Reduce ammonia production/absorption:
	* **Lactulose**: First-line therapy
	  * Acidifies colon, converting ammonia to ammonium (poorly absorbed)
	  * Cathartic effect reduces absorption
	* **Protein management**: Normal protein intake (1-1.5 g/kg/d) recommended
	  * Plant-based protein preferred over animal protein
	* **Zinc supplementation**: Consider for chronic HE (cofactor in urea cycle)

**Bottom Line:** Hepatic encephalopathy is a reversible neuropsychiatric complication that requires identification and treatment of precipitating factors, along with specific therapies to reduce ammonia levels.

### 6.9.6. ASCITES

* **Significance and etiology** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Most common complication of cirrhosis leading to hospitalization
  * Associated with 70% mortality rate within 5 years
  * 85% of ascites is due to portal hypertension; 15% has nonhepatic etiology
  * Nonhepatic causes:
	* Malignancy
	* Cardiac failure
	* Renal disease
	* Pancreatitis
	* Tuberculosis
* **Diagnosis** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Paracentesis: Most expeditious diagnostic study
  * **Serum-ascites albumin gradient (SAAG)**:
	* Difference between simultaneous serum and ascites albumin levels
	* SAAG ≥1.1 g/dL indicates portal hypertension with 97% accuracy
* **Management** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Initial therapy**:
	* Salt restriction (2 g/day)
	* Diuretics
  * **Hyponatremia management**:
	* Common in cirrhotic patients with ascites
	* Generally does not warrant fluid restriction unless Na⁺ <120-125 mEq/L
	* Cirrhotic patients at high risk for osmotic demyelination syndrome
	* Recommendations: increase of 4-6 mEq/L per day, not exceeding 8 mEq/L in 24 hours
	* Consider using prothrombin complex instead of FFP and 25% albumin instead of 5% albumin to reduce sodium load
* **Refractory ascites** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Definition: Ascites unresponsive to sodium restriction, maximum diuretics, and paracentesis
  * Indicates increased disease severity (6-month mortality: 21%)
  * Treatment options:
	* Serial paracentesis
	* Liver transplantation
	* TIPS placement
	* Peritoneovenous shunt (rarely used)
  * Albumin replacement for large volume paracentesis:
	* Not needed for drainage <5 L
	* 6-8 g albumin/L drainage for larger volumes
* **Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP)** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Diagnosed when ascitic fluid PMN count ≥250 cells/mm³ without other intra-abdominal source
  * Common sources:
	* Bacterial translocation from bowel
	* Nosocomial infection from procedures
  * Treatment:
	* Immediate broad-spectrum antibiotics
	* Treat even if PMN count below diagnostic threshold but clinical suspicion high
	* SBP is a recognized precipitating factor for type I HRS

**Bottom Line:** Ascites is a major complication of cirrhosis with significant mortality implications. Management includes salt restriction, diuretics, and paracentesis, with TIPS reserved for refractory cases. Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis requires prompt recognition and treatment.

### 6.9.7. VARICES

* **Pathophysiology** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Result of portal hypertension
  * Increased portal pressure from hepatic architectural distortion
  * Formation of portosystemic collaterals, particularly in gastroesophageal venous system
  * Increased local nitric oxide production → massive splanchnic vasodilation
  * Rupture of high-pressure collaterals is a lethal complication
* **Diagnosis and epidemiology** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Portal hypertension diagnosed by measuring wedged hepatic venous pressure (WHVP)
  * Hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG) calculated by subtracting free hepatic venous pressure from WHVP
  * Normal HVPG: 3-5 mmHg
  * Patients with esophageal varices typically have HVPG ≥10-12 mmHg
  * Esophagogastroduodenoscopy is gold standard for diagnosing varices
  * Incidence increases with severity of liver disease:
	* Child's A: 40%
	* Child's C: 85%
* **Management** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Prevention**:
	* Nonselective β-blockers reduce portal pressure via:
	  * Decreased cardiac output (β₁)
	  * Splanchnic vasoconstriction (β₂)
  * **Acute variceal bleeding**:
	* Volume resuscitation (target Hb ~8 g/dL)
	* Correction of significant coagulopathy
	* Pharmacologic portal pressure reduction:
	  * Vasopressin and analogues
	  * Somatostatin and analogues
	  * (β-blockers avoided due to systemic effects)
	* Early endoscopic variceal ligation combined with pharmacotherapy

**Bottom Line:** Varices are a life-threatening complication of portal hypertension. Management focuses on portal pressure reduction, and acute bleeding requires a coordinated approach with volume resuscitation, pharmacotherapy, and endoscopic intervention.

## 6.10. BILE PRODUCTION AND EXCRETION 




### 6.10.1. COMPOSITION OF BILE

* **Production and components** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Approximately 500 mL produced daily
  * Contains:
	* Bile salts (derived from cholesterol)
	* Bilirubin
	* Cholesterol
	* Phospholipids
	* Water and electrolytes
* **Role in digestion** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]]:
  * Bile salts act as detergents for fat solubilization
  * Essential for dietary lipid absorption
  * Absorption of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K)

### 6.10.2. BILIRUBIN METABOLISM

* **Formation and transport** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * **Origin**:
	* RBCs taken up by spleen when aged or damaged
	* Hemoglobin broken down, releasing:
	  * Globin chain (recycled to amino acids)
	  * Iron (recycled via transferrin for erythropoiesis)
	  * Porphyrin ring (converted to bilirubin)
  * **Initial processing**:
	* Porphyrin ring oxidized to biliverdin then reduced to bilirubin in splenic macrophages
	* Unconjugated bilirubin is not water soluble, bound to albumin during transport to liver
  * **Hepatic processing**:
	* Hepatocytes take up bilirubin and conjugate it with glucuronic acid
	* Enzyme: glucuronosyltransferase
	* Conjugated bilirubin becomes water soluble
  * **Excretion**:
	* Conjugated bilirubin excreted with bile into small intestine
	* Some reabsorbed (enterohepatic circulation)
	* Remainder passes to large intestine
  * **Intestinal processing**:
	* Bacteria convert conjugated bilirubin to urobilinogen
	* Urobilinogen either:
	  * Further oxidized to urobilin/stercobilin and excreted in feces (giving brown color)
	  * Reabsorbed and transported to liver or excreted in urine (giving yellow color)

![Bilirubin metabolism pathway](GASTRO_AND_HPB_GASTRO_AND_HPB_Untitled_7.png_fe2b9e.png_0e318a.png)

### 6.10.3. JAUNDICE

* **Definition and classifications** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.5 Liver, Nutrition, Gastrointestinal|ⓘ]] 
  * Jaundice: plasma bilirubin >30 μmol/L
  * **Pre-hepatic jaundice**:
	* Increased RBC breakdown (e.g., hemolytic anemia, transfusion, sickle cell)
	* Exceeds liver's conjugation capacity
	* High unconjugated bilirubin
	* No urinary bilirubin (unconjugated not water-soluble)
	* Also includes conjugation disorders (neonatal jaundice, Gilbert's syndrome)
  * **Post-hepatic jaundice**:
	* Failure of conjugated bilirubin excretion (e.g., gallstone in common bile duct)
	* High conjugated bilirubin in plasma
	* Conjugated bilirubin in urine (water-soluble)
	* Pale stools (no urobilinogen/stercobilin formation)
  * **Hepatocellular jaundice**:
	* Hepatocyte necrosis and biliary tree disruption
	* Mixed picture depending on relative damage
	* Causes: cirrhosis, hepatitis, paracetamol toxicity

**Bottom Line:** Bile production and bilirubin metabolism are important liver functions. Understanding the bilirubin pathway helps diagnose different types of jaundice based on the pattern of abnormalities.

# 7. PHARMACOLOGY OF GI DRUGS

> **Syllabus**: [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B: GI Pharmacology]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C]]

## 7.2. DRUGS AFFECTING GASTRIC ACIDITY
**Multiple drug classes are available to reduce or neutralize gastric acid.**

### 7.2.1. ANTACIDS
* **Mechanism of action** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * Direct chemical neutralization of stomach acid
  * Increase gastric pH
* **Types and characteristics** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * **Aluminum compounds**:
	* Work more slowly
	* Cause constipation
	* Can interact with other medications (e.g., tetracyclines)
  * **Magnesium compounds**:
	* Can induce diarrhea
  * **Sodium bicarbonate**:
	* Rapid acting
	* Systemic absorption can affect acid-base balance
  * **Sodium citrate**:
	* Frequently used in emergency obstetric cases
	* Works rapidly
	* Typically given as 30 mL "shot" immediately prior to induction
	* Poor palatability
* **Classification by composition** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Particulate antacids**:
	* Aluminum hydroxide, magnesium hydroxide, calcium carbonate
	* Non-absorbable aluminum, magnesium, and calcium salts
	* Despite raising pH, pulmonary aspiration of these salts still causes damage
  * **Non-particulate antacids**:
	* Sodium citrate
	* Commonly used before cesarean section due to speed of action and non-particulate nature
* **Clinical uses** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Symptomatic relief of heartburn and indigestion
  * Aspiration prophylaxis before anesthesia
  * Treatment of peptic ulcer disease (as adjunct)

### 7.2.2. PROTON PUMP INHIBITORS
* **Examples** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Omeprazole
  * Lansoprazole
  * Pantoprazole
  * Esomeprazole
* **Mechanism of action** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Irreversibly block the H⁺/K⁺-ATPase (proton pump) on parietal cell luminal membrane
  * Inhibit final step in acid production
* **Detailed pharmacology** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * Omeprazole has a chiral center and is administered as a racemic mixture of Rand S-enantiomers
  * S-enantiomer available as esomeprazole
  * Classified as prodrugs, becoming active within the parietal cell
  * Achieve complete achlorhydria
  * More effective than H₂ antagonists at increasing gastric pH
  * Long-term risks include C. difficile infection, osteoporosis, B₁₂ deficiency, iron-deficiency anemia
* **Pharmacokinetics of omeprazole** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * Degraded in gastric acid - prepared as enteric-coated granules
  * Complete hepatic metabolism by CYP2C19 (primarily) and CYP3A4
  * Metabolites excreted in urine (80%) and bile (20%)
  * CYP2C19 subject to genetic polymorphism:
	* Ultrarapid metabolizers (CYP2C19*17) - ineffective treatment
	* Poor metabolizers (CYP2C19*3) - up to 20% of Asians
  * Omeprazole potently inhibits CYP2C19, potentially reducing clopidogrel efficacy
  * Pantoprazole and esomeprazole may be better alternatives with clopidogrel
* **Pharmacology of omeprazole** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Dosing**: 40 mg PO or IV
  * **Adverse effects**:
	* Hyponatremia
	* Drug interactions via CYP450
	  * Notable interaction with clopidogrel (inhibits CYP2C19, reducing activation of clopidogrel)
* **Clinical uses** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * GERD
  * Peptic ulcer disease
  * Stress ulcer prophylaxis
  * Zollinger-Ellison syndrome
* **Effects** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * **GI** - reduced acidity and volume of gastric secretions
  * **No change** in lower esophageal sphincter tone or gastric emptying
  * **Metabolic** - inhibition of CYP450 (less than cimetidine); potential interactions with warfarin, phenytoin, diazepam
  * **Miscellaneous** - rare rashes and gastrointestinal upset

### 7.2.3. H2 RECEPTOR ANTAGONISTS
* **Examples** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Cimetidine**:
	* Selective H₂ receptor competitive antagonist
	* Acts on parietal cells to reduce acid secretion
	* **Side effects**:
	  * Can trigger bradycardia
	  * Anti-androgen effects
	  * CYP450 inhibitor (many drug interactions)
  * **Ranitidine**:
	* More potent than cimetidine
	* No significant inhibition of hepatic enzymes
	* No longer widely used due to association with stomach and esophageal cancer
* **Detailed pharmacology of cimetidine** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * Only H₂ antagonist with imidazole structure
  * **Effects**:
	* **GI** - raises gastric pH, reduces secretion volume; no effect on gastric emptying or LES tone
	* **Cardiovascular** - bradycardia and hypotension with rapid IV administration
	* **CNS** - confusion, hallucinations, seizures (usually with renal impairment)
	* **Respiratory** - increased risk of nosocomial pneumonia in ventilated patients (due to loss of acid's antibacterial effect)
	* **Endocrine** - gynaecomastia, impotence, reduced sperm count (anti-androgenic effects)
	* **Metabolic** - inhibits cytochrome P450, slowing metabolism of many drugs
  * **Pharmacokinetics**:
	* 60% oral bioavailability
	* 20% protein binding
	* Up to 60% hepatic metabolism
	* Approximately 50% excreted unchanged in urine
* **Detailed pharmacology of ranitidine** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * More potent than cimetidine
  * **Effects**:
	* **GI** - similar to cimetidine
	* **Cardiovascular** - potential arrhythmias with rapid IV administration
	* **Metabolic** - should be avoided in porphyria (inconclusive reports)
	* No anti-androgenic effects
	* **Miscellaneous** - rare thrombocytopenia, leucopenia, liver function abnormalities, anaphylaxis
  * **Pharmacokinetics**:
	* Good oral absorption
	* 15% protein binding
	* Greater first-pass metabolism than cimetidine (50% oral bioavailability)
	* 50% excreted unchanged in urine
* **Clinical uses** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Peptic ulcer disease
  * GERD
  * Aspiration prophylaxis
* **Newer H₂ antagonists** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Nizatidine and famotidine - increased potency
  * Like ranitidine, do not inhibit hepatic cytochrome P450

### 7.2.4. MUCOSAL PROTECTANTS
* **Sucralfate** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * Forms a barrier over the gut lumen
  * Specifically protects ulcerated regions
  * Does not alter gastric pH, motility, or LES tone
  * Has bacteriostatic effects
  * **Mechanism**: Local effects with minimal absorption
  * **Effects**:
	* Minor gastric disturbances
	* Enhanced aluminum absorption in renal dysfunction
	* May reduce absorption of certain drugs (ciprofloxacin, warfarin, phenytoin, H₂ antagonists) through direct binding

### 7.2.5. PROSTAGLANDIN ANALOGUES
* **Misoprostol** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * Synthetic PGE₁ analogue
  * **Uses**: Prevention and treatment of NSAID-induced ulcers
  * **Mechanism**: Inhibits gastric acid secretion and increases mucous secretion
  * **Effects**:
	* **Endocrine** - increases uterine tone, may cause miscarriage, menorrhagia
	* **GI** - severe diarrhea and gastrointestinal upset
	* **Cardiovascular** - potential hypotension at normal doses (caution in cerebrovascular/cardiovascular disease)
  * **Pharmacokinetics**:
	* Rapid gut absorption
	* Metabolized by fatty-acid-oxidizing systems throughout body
	* No dose adjustment needed in renal/hepatic impairment
* **Clinical relevance: neutralisation of gastric acid** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] 
  * Key in management of GERD, peptic ulcers, and aspiration risk
  * Used preoperatively in high-risk patients (acute abdomen, pregnancy)
  * Approaches include:
	* **Antacids** - direct neutralization
	* **Acid inhibitors** - prevent secretion via:
	  * H₂ antagonists - block histamine effect on parietal cells
	  * PPIs - irreversibly inhibit H⁺/K⁺-ATPase (more effective than H₂ antagonists)

**Bottom Line:** Drugs affecting gastric acidity act through different mechanisms - direct neutralization (antacids), inhibition of acid secretion (PPIs, H₂ antagonists), or mucosal protection. Understanding their properties helps in selecting appropriate agents for aspiration prophylaxis and managing GI conditions.

## 7.3. DRUGS AFFECTING GI MOTILITY
**These agents alter GI tract movement and can be used to treat motility disorders.**

* **Prokinetic agents** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Metoclopramide**:
	* **Mechanism**: Dopamine antagonist with both antiemetic and prokinetic effects
	* **Detailed structure and pharmacology** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] 
	  * Structurally similar to procainamide but no local anesthetic properties
	  * Peripheral mechanism: D₂ receptor antagonism and selective stimulation of gastric muscarinic receptors
	  * CNS mechanism: D₂ receptor antagonism in chemoreceptor trigger zone
	* **Side effects**:
	  * Crosses blood-brain barrier → extrapyramidal effects
		* Oculogyric crisis
		* Akinesia
		* Dystonia (more common in young women and elderly)
	  * Can trigger porphyric crisis
	  * Hyperprolactinemia (dopamine is a natural prolactin inhibitor)
	* **Additional effects** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] 
	  * **CNS** - extrapyramidal effects (high doses, renal impairment, elderly, young), sedation, enhanced antidepressant actions, neuroleptic malignant syndrome
	  * **GI** - increased LES tone, pyloric relaxation, no effect on gastric secretion
	  * **Cardiovascular** - conduction abnormalities with rapid IV administration, hypertension in pheochromocytoma
	  * **Metabolic** - hyperprolactinemia, galactorrhoea, avoided in porphyria, inhibits plasma cholinesterase (may prolong effects of drugs metabolized by this enzyme)
	* **Pharmacokinetics** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] 
	  * Well absorbed orally but variable first-pass metabolism (30-90% bioavailability)
	  * Available IV
	  * Hepatic conjugation and urinary excretion
	* **Contraindications**: GI obstruction, pheochromocytoma
  * **Domperidone**:
	* Similar to metoclopramide but does not cross blood-brain barrier
	* Less risk of extrapyramidal effects
	* Can cause gynaecomastia and QT prolongation
	* **Administration routes** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] 
	  * IV preparation withdrawn due to serious arrhythmias with large doses
	  * Only available as tablets or suppositories
	  * Limited use in children (only for nausea/vomiting after chemotherapy or radiotherapy)
* **Anticholinergics** (decrease motility) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Examples**: Atropine, glycopyrrolate
  * **Effects**: Reduce GI motility and secretions
  * **Uses**: Premedication, treatment of irritable bowel syndrome
* **Opioids** (decrease motility) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Mechanism**: Act on μ-receptors in gut
  * **Effects**: Decrease propulsive contractions, increase sphincter tone
  * **Clinical significance**: Can cause constipation, delayed gastric emptying

**Bottom Line:** Drugs affecting GI motility can either increase (prokinetics) or decrease (anticholinergics, opioids) gut movement. Their effects are important to consider for aspiration risk, postoperative ileus, and perioperative medication selection.

## 7.4. ANTIEMETIC MEDICATIONS
**See Section 2.4 for detailed coverage of antiemetic medications.**

* **Major classes** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * 5HT3 receptor antagonists (ondansetron, granisetron)
  * Dopamine antagonists (metoclopramide, droperidol, prochlorperazine)
  * Histamine antagonists (cyclizine)
  * Anticholinergics (scopolamine)
  * Neurokinin-1 antagonists (aprepitant)
  * Corticosteroids (dexamethasone)
  * Others (propofol, cannabinoids)

**Bottom Line:** Multiple classes of antiemetics target different pathways in the complex neurophysiology of nausea and vomiting. Combination therapy with agents from different classes provides synergistic effects for PONV management.

## 8.2. PERIOPERATIVE RISK ASSESSMENT IN LIVER DISEASE


**Various scoring systems help assess perioperative risk in patients with liver disease.**

* **Considerations for acute hepatitis** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Consensus opinion: elective surgery should be postponed in patients with acute hepatitis
  * Historical data: high mortality rates (up to 30%) in patients with undiagnosed hepatitis undergoing laparotomy
  * Modern diagnostic testing reduces likelihood of unsuspected hepatitis

### 8.2.1. CHILD-PUGH SCORE
* **Components and scoring** (Table 46-9) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Five factors**:
	* Albumin
	* Prothrombin time/INR
	* Bilirubin
	* Ascites
	* Encephalopathy
  * **Score calculation**:
	* Class A: 5-6 points
	* Class B: 7-9 points
	* Class C: 10-15 points
  * **Adjustment for cholestatic diseases**: Modified bilirubin scoring since cholestatic diseases can have disproportionate bilirubin elevations
* **Predictive value for surgery** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Child A**: 10% perioperative mortality
  * **Child B**: 17-30% perioperative mortality
  * **Child C**: 60-80% perioperative mortality
  * For comparison, 3-month non-surgical mortality:
	* Child A: 4%
	* Child B: 14%
	* Child C: 51%

### 8.2.2. MELD SCORE
* **Formula and components** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * MELD score = 9.57 × log₍ₑ₎(creatinine mg/dL) + 3.78 × log₍ₑ₎(bilirubin mg/dL) + 11.2 × log₍ₑ₎(INR) + 6.43
  * Uses objective laboratory values (creatinine, bilirubin, INR)
  * Advantages over Child-Pugh: eliminates subjective elements (ascites, encephalopathy), uses continuous variables instead of categories
* **Predictive value for surgery** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * c-statistic for predicting 30-day mortality: 0.72 overall, 0.80 for abdominal surgery
  * MELD 25-30: associated with 50% 30-day mortality after abdominal surgery
  * Each MELD point up to score of 20: ~1% additional mortality
  * Each MELD point over 20: ~2% additional mortality
  * Additional risk factors:
	* Age >70 years (equivalent to +3 MELD points)
	* ASA physical status >IV (equivalent to +5 MELD points)
* **Clinical application** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * MELD <11: low postoperative mortality, acceptable surgical risk
  * MELD ≥20: high mortality risk, elective procedures contraindicated until after liver transplantation
  * End-stage liver disease patients with risk of postoperative liver failure: consider surgery at institutions with liver transplant programs
* **VOCAL-Penn score** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Newer risk assessment tool derived from >4,700 surgeries in 3,700 patients
  * Includes nine variables (including surgery category but only bilirubin from MELD score)
  * c-statistic of 0.86 (superior to previous scoring systems)
  * Available online (<www.vocalpennscore.com>)

**Bottom Line:** Risk assessment is critical for patients with liver disease undergoing surgery. The MELD score provides an objective measure with specific thresholds for surgical decision-making, while the newer VOCAL-Penn score may offer improved prediction.

## 8.3. PREOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT 


**Optimization of cirrhotic patients requires a multisystem approach.**

* **Preoperative optimization** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Treat active infection** - particularly SBP
  * **Minimize vasoactive infusions**
  * **Optimize volume status** - balance central blood volume and renal function
  * **Minimize ascites**
  * **Improve encephalopathy**
  * **Correct coagulopathy** when appropriate
* **Surgical considerations** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Consider less invasive surgical approaches
  * Laparoscopic surgery appears safe in Child-Pugh A and B cirrhosis
	* Lower mortality and shorter hospital stay compared to open procedures
	* Child's C patients may benefit from percutaneous drainage rather than laparoscopic approach
  * Preoperative TIPS placement may improve outcomes in severe portal hypertension
* **Preoperative assessment** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **History**: Fatigue, anorexia, weight loss, nausea, vomiting, easy bruising, pruritus, dark urine, biliary colic, abdominal distention, GI bleeding
  * **Physical examination**: Icterus, palmar erythema, spider angiomas, gynecomastia, hepatosplenomegaly, ascites, testicular atrophy, petechiae, ecchymoses, asterixis
  * **Laboratory testing**: Generally not warranted in asymptomatic patients without risk factors
	* Minor elevations (<3× normal) may not be clinically significant
	* Approach to asymptomatic patients with abnormal results: repeat testing; if <3× normal, reasonable to proceed with surgery

**Bottom Line:** Preoperative management of patients with liver disease should focus on optimizing multiple organ systems. When possible, less invasive surgical approaches should be considered, especially for patients with advanced disease.

## 8.4. INTRAOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT 


### 8.4.1. MONITORING AND VASCULAR ACCESS
* **Arterial pressure monitoring** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Consider for end-stage liver disease based on:
	* Preoperative systemic hypotension due to vasodilation
	* Anticipated blood loss
	* Need for intraoperative laboratory studies
	* Coexisting disease
	* Age
  * Indicated for patients undergoing liver resection
* **Central venous pressure monitoring** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Utility for predicting fluid responsiveness questioned
  * Some experts have abandoned CVP monitoring for liver resection
  * Recommended approach: do not place CVP catheter exclusively for monitoring
* **Pulmonary artery catheterization** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Consider for patients with known/suspected pulmonary hypertension
  * Consider for patients with low cardiac ejection fraction
* **Transesophageal echocardiography** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Sensitive for assessing preload, contractility, ejection fraction, regional wall motion, emboli
  * Safe in patients with esophageal varices (multiple studies confirm safety)
  * Recommendation: avoid transgastric views to minimize esophageal manipulation
* **Coagulation monitoring** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Viscoelastic testing (TEG, thromboelastometry) useful if available
  * Reflects overall effects of altered proand anticoagulant factors
  * Standard PT/INR may overestimate bleeding risk as they only reflect procoagulant factors

### 8.4.2. ANESTHETIC TECHNIQUE SELECTION
* **Neuraxial vs. general anesthesia** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Hepatic blood flow reduced by epidural anesthesia, further reduced by norepinephrine
  * Vasopressors (ephedrine, dopamine) may restore hepatic blood flow
  * High (T-5) neuraxial blocks reduce hepatic blood flow; effect may persist despite correction of hypotension
  * Recommendation: avoid high neuraxial block and hypotension in advanced liver disease
  * Standard contraindications to neuraxial techniques apply (coagulopathy, thrombocytopenia)
  * Consider alternative regional techniques (TAP, QL blocks) when neuraxial contraindicated
* **Volatile anesthetics** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Effects on hepatic blood flow**:
	* Halothane: greatest reduction in hepatic blood flow
	* Isoflurane/sevoflurane at 1 MAC: minimal reduction
	* Desflurane at 1 MAC: 30% reduction
	* Sevoflurane: no dose-dependent reduction in flow beyond 1 MAC
	* Isoflurane/sevoflurane: maintain hepatic arterial buffer response
  * **Metabolism and potential hepatotoxicity**:
	* Halothane: 20% metabolized, associated with immune-mediated hepatitis
	* Enflurane: 2.5% metabolized
	* Isoflurane: 0.2% metabolized
	* Desflurane: 0.02% metabolized
	* Sevoflurane: more extensively metabolized than isoflurane/desflurane but doesn't produce TFA metabolites
  * **Clinical implications**:
	* Little evidence other than halothane causes significant hepatic injury
	* Several reports of hepatic dysfunction after repeated exposure to halogenated agents
	* Recommendation: patients sensitized to one volatile anesthetic should not receive any other fluorinated anesthetic
* **Nitrous oxide** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * No hepatocellular injury in absence of hepatic hypoxemia
  * No biochemical worsening in patients with mild alcoholic hepatitis
  * Can decrease hepatic blood flow due to sympathomimetic effects
  * Inhibits methionine synthase (clinical significance unclear)
* **Intravenous anesthetics** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Propofol, etomidate, midazolam: no apparent alteration in hepatic function with short-duration use
  * Propofol infusion syndrome: rare complication with prolonged (>48h), high-dose (>5 mg/kg/h) infusions
	* Monitor for worsening lactic acidosis and escalating vasopressor requirements
	* Discontinue propofol and consider hemofiltration if suspected
* **Opioids** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * No independent effect on hepatic function beyond effects on hepatic blood flow
  * All opioids increase sphincter of Oddi pressure
  * No differential effect between morphine and meperidine on sphincter of Oddi

### 8.4.3. PHARMACOKINETIC AND PHARMACODYNAMIC ALTERATIONS
* **Factors affecting drug handling** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Decreased functional hepatocyte mass
  * Reduced hepatic blood flow due to portocaval shunts
  * Altered protein binding
  * Altered volume of distribution
* **Low vs. high extraction ratio drugs** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Low extraction ratio drugs** (<0.3):
	* Clearance affected by protein binding, enzyme induction/inhibition, age, hepatic pathology
	* Not significantly affected by hepatic blood flow
	* Often have long elimination half-lives (e.g., diazepam t₁/₂ = 43 hours)
  * **High extraction ratio drugs** (>0.7):
	* Extensive first-pass metabolism
	* Significantly affected by hepatic blood flow changes
	* Typically have shorter half-lives (e.g., propranolol t₁/₂ = 3.9 hours)
* **Specific drug classes** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Benzodiazepines**:
	* Low extraction ratio
	* Conflicting effects of cirrhosis on midazolam metabolism
	* Free fraction may increase due to decreased protein binding
	* Pharmacodynamic effects more pronounced in patients with encephalopathy
  * **Opioids**:
	* Reduced metabolism in liver disease
	* Dosing intervals should be increased to avoid accumulation
	* Prolonged elimination more prominent with morphine and meperidine
	* Single bolus less affected than continuous infusion due to redistribution
	* Remifentanil: exception due to esterase metabolism
	* Pharmacodynamic effects altered by liver disease; dose reduction recommended
  * **Induction agents**:
	* Most are highly lipophilic with high extraction ratios
	* Clearance in cirrhotics similar to normal patients
	* Pharmacodynamic effects more pronounced
	* Some agents have prolonged duration of action
  * **Neuromuscular blocking agents**:
	* Vecuronium, rocuronium: prolonged duration in liver disease
	* Pancuronium: prolonged action
	* Initial resistance to neuromuscular blockers due to elevated γ-globulin and increased volume of distribution
	* Atracurium, cisatracurium: organ-independent elimination; duration unaffected
	* Succinylcholine: altered metabolism due to reduced plasma cholinesterase activity (rarely clinically significant)

### 8.4.4. HEMODYNAMIC MANAGEMENT
* **Vasopressors** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Cirrhotic patients show reduced response to endogenous vasoconstrictors (angiotensin II, vasopressin, norepinephrine)
  * Hyporesponsiveness modulated by nitric oxide, prostacyclin, and other endothelial-derived factors
* **Volume resuscitation** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Selection of fluid and blood products generally similar to non-liver disease patients
  * Albumin has specific indications in cirrhotic patients:
	* After large volume paracentesis (4-5 L)
	* SBP with bilirubin >4 mg/dL or creatinine >1 mg/dL
	* Hepatorenal syndrome-AKI (with splanchnic vasoconstrictors)

**Bottom Line:** Anesthetic management of patients with liver disease requires careful consideration of monitoring requirements, anesthetic technique selection, and altered drug pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics to minimize hepatic injury and optimize outcomes.

## 8.5. HEPATIC RESECTION

**Hepatic resection has evolved to become a relatively safe procedure with modern techniques.**

* **Historical perspective** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Once considered extremely dangerous
  * Pringle technique (digital compression of hepatic hilar vessels) described in 1908
  * First right hepatectomy performed in 1952
  * Segmental liver anatomy described by Couinaud in 1957
  * Mortality rates >20% in 1980s, now 5% or less
  * Mortality for partial hepatectomy in normal livers: 1-2%
* **Surgical techniques** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Improved approaches (avoiding thoracoabdominal approach)
  * Various transection techniques:
	* "Smooth fracture" with bipolar coagulation
	* Ultrasonic dissectors
	* High-pressure water jets
	* Harmonic scalpels
	* Conventional clamp crush technique remains effective
* **Bleeding management** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Transfusion necessary in <20% of patients
  * Hepatic veins remain significant source of blood loss
  * Low CVP technique historically recommended but controversial:
	* Some question necessity given reduced blood loss with modern techniques
	* Uncertain clinical relevance of potential renal effects
	* Reliability as fluid responsiveness monitor questioned
	* Meta-analysis: optimal methods to lower CVP uncertain, low CVP does not necessarily reduce morbidity
  * Vasopressors reduce splanchnic pressure and may decrease blood loss
  * Vascular control techniques:
	* Portal triad clamping (afferent vessels)
	* Total vascular occlusion (afferent vessels plus supra/infrahepatic vena cava)
	* Ischemic preconditioning may be as effective as intermittent clamping with less blood loss
* **Complications** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Air embolism:
	* More common with large hepatectomy, tumors near vena cava, portal vessel involvement
	* Low CVP may theoretically increase risk (not substantiated)
  * Coagulation abnormalities:
	* INR and platelet count often abnormal after resection
	* Severity correlates with extent of resection
	* Peaks 1-2 days postop, resolves over 5+ days
	* Implications for neuraxial analgesia (catheter placement vs. removal)
	* Brief hypercoagulable period possible despite prolonged PT
* **Analgesia options** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Epidural analgesia**:
	* Controversial due to postoperative coagulopathy
	* Some advise against preoperative placement
	* Others recommend correcting coagulopathy before catheter removal
	* Large database review showed no effect on mortality/morbidity but 0.5 day increase in length of stay
  * **Alternatives**:
	* Intrathecal opioid with IV analgesics
	* Local anesthetic infusion systems
	* Erector spinae plane blocks
	* Posterior quadratus lumborum blocks
* **Postoperative complications** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Common after hemihepatectomy (52% incidence):
	* Pleural effusions
	* Biliary leakage
	* Wound dehiscence
	* Ascites
	* Intra-abdominal abscess
  * Higher preoperative MELD score associated with complications

**Bottom Line:** Hepatic resection involves complex considerations for anesthetic management, including vascular control techniques, fluid management, monitoring strategies, and planning for postoperative analgesia in the setting of potential coagulopathy.

## 8.6. TIPS PROCEDURE

**TIPS creates a connection between portal and systemic circulations to decompress portal hypertension.**

* **Indications** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Decompress portal hypertension for:
	* Esophageal varices
	* Intractable ascites
* **Anesthetic considerations** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Sedation vs. general anesthesia**:
	* Sedation commonly used
	* Some prefer GA to limit patient movement, control diaphragmatic excursion, reduce aspiration risk
  * **Preparation**:
	* Volume resuscitation may be necessary for recent variceal bleeding
	* Coagulopathy may require clotting factors/platelets
  * **Potential complications**:
	* Pneumothorax or vascular injury during jugular access
	* Dysrhythmias from catheter stimulation of endocardium
	* Hemorrhage from extrahepatic artery or portal vein puncture
	* Increased venous return can unmask cardiac dysfunction or pulmonary hypertension
* **Hemodynamic effects** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Increases venous return
  * Can unmask previously undiagnosed:
	* Cardiac dysfunction
	* Pulmonary hypertension

**Bottom Line:** The TIPS procedure requires careful anesthetic planning due to potential hemodynamic changes and risks of coagulopathy, bleeding, and cardiac complications in patients with portal hypertension.

## 8.7. POSTOPERATIVE LIVER DYSFUNCTION 


**Postoperative liver dysfunction ranges from mild enzyme elevations to fulminant hepatic failure.**

* **Clinical presentation** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Asymptomatic enzyme elevations
  * Jaundice
  * Symptoms of liver failure (encephalopathy)
* **Mild transient elevations** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Common within hours after surgery
  * Usually resolve within 2 days
  * More common with halothane than newer agents
  * Rarely seen with current anesthetics in patients without pre-existing liver disease
* **Causes of significant postoperative hepatic dysfunction** (Table 46-10) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Hepatocellular causes**:
	* Drug toxicity (anesthetics, other medications)
	* Ischemia (shock, hypotension, iatrogenic injury)
	* Viral hepatitis
  * **Cholestatic causes**:
	* Benign postoperative cholestasis
	* Sepsis
	* Bile duct injury
	* Drug effects (antibiotics, antiemetics)
	* Choledocholithiasis or pancreatitis
	* Cholecystitis
	* Gilbert syndrome
* **Causes of hyperbilirubinemia** (Table 46-11) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Unconjugated (indirect)**:
	* Excessive bilirubin production (hemolysis)
	* Immature enzyme systems
	* Inherited defects (Gilbert syndrome, Crigler-Najjar syndrome)
	* Drug effects
  * **Conjugated (direct)**:
	* Hepatocellular disease (hepatitis, cirrhosis, drugs)
	* Intrahepatic cholestasis (drugs, pregnancy)
	* Benign postoperative jaundice, sepsis
	* Congenital conditions (Dubin-Johnson syndrome, Rotor syndrome)
	* Obstructive jaundice (calculus, stricture, neoplasm)
* **Risk factors for postoperative liver failure** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Nature and site of surgical procedure (abdominal surgery reduces hepatic blood flow)
  * Hepatic resection (especially in underlying liver disease)
  * Obstructive jaundice (21% postoperative mortality vs. 8.7% in cirrhosis and 1% in normal liver)
  * Renal failure with hyperbilirubinemia (>50% mortality)
  * Cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (31% overall mortality in cirrhotics; 80% in Child's B)
  * 1-year mortality after cardiac surgery: 27% (Child A), 66% (Child B), 79% (Child C)

**Bottom Line:** Postoperative liver dysfunction has multiple potential causes ranging from transient enzyme elevations to life-threatening failure. The risk is particularly high with specific surgeries (hepatic resection, cardiac surgery) and in patients with pre-existing liver disease.

## 8.8. MANAGEMENT OF PONV 


**See Section 2.3 for detailed coverage of PONV management.**

* **Risk assessment and prophylaxis** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Risk stratification using Apfel score
  * Multimodal prophylaxis based on risk level
  * Combination therapy with agents from different classes
* **Treatment strategies** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Avoid repeating prophylactic agents for treatment
  * Consider agent from different class than those used for prophylaxis
  * Rescue with small-dose propofol (10-20 mg) for urgent relief

**Bottom Line:** PONV management requires risk assessment, appropriate prophylaxis, and prompt treatment with consideration of the mechanism of action of available agents.

**Conclusions** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:

* Patients with new-onset liver disease: postpone elective surgery until disease course is known
* Patients with pre-existing liver disease: characterize severity to assess risk
* Patients with MELD ≥20 or Child Class C: defer elective high-risk procedures until after liver transplantation
* Patients with MELD <11: acceptable risk
* End-stage liver disease patients at risk for postoperative liver failure: have elective abdominal surgery at institutions with liver transplant programs
* No universally preferred anesthetic technique, though coagulopathy may contraindicate neuraxial techniques
* Maintain splanchnic, hepatic, and renal perfusion
* Consider transfer to liver transplant center for unavoidable surgery in advanced liver disease
