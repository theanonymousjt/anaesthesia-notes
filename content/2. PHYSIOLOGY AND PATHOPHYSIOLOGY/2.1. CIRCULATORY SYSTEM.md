# 1. INTRODUCTION TO THE CIRCULATORY SYSTEM

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|Part A: Cardiovascular Physiology]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#2.1 Clinical Decision-Making and Procedures|Part C]]

**The heart is a dual-component, self-actuated hydraulic pump that adjusts to changing physiologic conditions.**

* **Heart**: Phasic, electrically self-actuated, variable speed, hydraulic pump composed of two dual-component, elastic muscular chambers, each consisting of an atrium and a ventricle [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].
* **Primary functions** of the heart [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]] :
  * The right side generates flow around the pulmonary circulation, moving deoxygenated blood from the heart to the lungs
  * The left side generates pressure in the arterial circulation, moving oxygenated blood from the heart to the other organs of the body
  * Flow regulation according to tissue demand
* **Myocardium** responds to:
  * Stimulation rate
  * Muscle stretch before contraction (**preload**) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * Resistance to contraction (**afterload**) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* Heart has its own energy supply and adapts by:
  * Altering intrinsic mechanical behavior (Frank-Starling Principle) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * Responding to neurohormonal signaling [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* Cardiac performance is determined by:
  * Contractile performance of atria and ventricles
  * Ability to collect blood at normal filling pressures [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* **Endocrine function**: The heart is also an endocrine organ with a role in the regulation of plasma volume :
  * Stretch receptors in cardiac atria and ventricles sense increases in plasma volume
  * Secretion of atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP) and brain natriuretic peptide (BNP)
  * Both ANP and BNP act on the kidney to produce a natriuresis, restoring plasma volume to normal

**Bottom Line:** The heart functions as a sophisticated pump that adjusts to physiological demands through intrinsic mechanisms (Frank-Starling) and external regulation (neurohormonal), with performance determined by both contractility and effective filling.

# 2. CARDIAC ANATOMY

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|Part A: Cardiac Anatomy]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#2.1 Clinical Decision-Making and Procedures|Part C]]

## 2.1. THE PERICARDIUM

**The heart and roots of the great vessels are enclosed within the fibrous pericardium, a conical sac.**

* **Anatomical relationships** of the pericardium:
  * Apex fused with adventitia of great vessels at the level of the angle of Louis
  * Anterior surface attached to posterior sternum by loose fibrous tissue (sternopericardial ligament)
  * Base blends with the central tendon of the diaphragm
  * Heart position depends on diaphragm position (lower with deep inspiration, higher in recumbency)
* **External relationships** of the pericardium:
  * Anteriorly: Sternum, 3rd-6th costal cartilages, anterior borders of both lungs
  * Laterally: Mediastinal pleura and phrenic nerve
  * Posteriorly: Esophagus, descending aorta, bronchi, bodies of 5th-8th thoracic vertebrae
* **Serous pericardium**:
  * Parietal layer: Lines the fibrous pericardium
  * Visceral layer (epicardium): Adheres to the heart
  * Pericardial cavity between these layers normally contains no contents
  * Lines of reflection marked on the posterior heart aspect:
	* Transverse sinus: Between SVC/left atrium posteriorly and pulmonary trunk/aorta anteriorly
	* Oblique sinus: Bordered by the pulmonary veins and IVC, forming a recess between left atrium and pericardium

## 2.2. STRUCTURE OF THE HEART

**The heart is located in the thorax, enclosed within a fibrous sac called the pericardium**.

* **Heart Tissue Layers**:
  * **Epicardium**: The outer connective tissue layer. A small amount of pericardial fluid separates the epicardium from the pericardium, reducing friction as the heart moves.
  * **Myocardium**: The middle layer, composed of cardiac muscle.
  * **Endocardium**: A layer of epithelial cells lining the inner surface of the heart. In contact with blood and continuous with the endothelial layer of blood vessels.
* **Cardiac Chambers**:
  * **Right Atrium (RA)**: Receives deoxygenated blood from the superior and inferior venae cavae
  * **Right Ventricle (RV)**:
	* Has a complex shape: crescent-shaped in transverse plane, triangular in longitudinal plane
	* Difficult to model mathematically, making echocardiographic volume estimation challenging
  * **Left Atrium (LA)**: Receives oxygenated blood from the lungs through four pulmonary veins
  * **Left Ventricle (LV)**:
	* Circular transverse section and conical longitudinal section
	* More amenable to accurate echocardiographic volume estimation
* **Functional Syncytium**:
  * Myocardium arranged in networks of striated cardiac muscle cells joined by intercalated discs
  * Intercalated discs contain three types of cell-cell interactions:
	* **Gap junction complexes**: Allow direct passage of ions and larger molecules between cells, forming electrical synapses
	* **Fascia adherens**: Anchor actin filaments within the sarcomere to the cell membrane
	* **Macular adherens (desmosomes)**: Anchor cardiac cells to one another
  * Cardiac muscle is electrically, chemically, and mechanically coupled to function as a single coordinated unit

## 2.3. GROSS ANATOMY

**The cardiac skeleton provides structural support for the heart's muscular and valvular components.**

* **Skeleton**: Composed of [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]:
  * Annuli of valves
  * Aortic and pulmonary arterial roots
  * Central fibrous body
  * Left and right fibrous trigones
* Functions of cardiac skeleton:
  * Supports translucent, macroscopically avascular valves [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * Resists forces of developed pressure and blood flow [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * Provides site of insertion for superficial subepicardial muscle [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* **Myocardium** is supported by an interstitial collagen fiber network with elastin interwoven within the matrix [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* Myocardial organization:
  * Atrial and ventricular myocardium arises from and inserts within the skeleton [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * Cannot be separated into distinct layers using an "unwinding" dissection technique [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * **Left and right atria (LA and RA)**: Two relatively thin sheets of myocardium oriented perpendicular to each other [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * **Right and left ventricles (RV and LV)**: Three muscle layers [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]:
	* Interdigitating deep sinospiral
	* Superficial sinospiral
	* Superficial bulbospiral

**Left ventricular dynamics:**
* Contraction of obliquely arranged subepicardial and subendocardial fibers shortens the LV chamber along its longitudinal axis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* Causes a torsional twisting motion (similar to wringing a wash cloth) that substantially increases force [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* Loss of this torsional twist reduces ejection fraction (EF) and is a characteristic finding in patients with LV systolic dysfunction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* LV contraction shortens its long axis, decreases chamber diameter, and rotates its apex in an anterior-right direction toward the chest wall [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* Elastic recoil of systolic twist during LV relaxation is the major determinant of the diastolic suction mechanism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * Enhances early LV filling during hypovolemia and exercise [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]

**Right ventricular characteristics:**
* Located anterior and to the right of the LV within the mediastinum [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* Thinner-walled, crescent-shaped (vs. thicker-walled, ellipsoidal-shaped LV) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* Pumps deoxygenated blood into a lower pressure, more compliant pulmonary arterial tree [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* Contracts in a more peristaltic manner (vs. LV's temporally uniform activation) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* Composed of embryologically distinct inflow and outflow tracts [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* Moves toward the septum with a "bellows-like" action [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* Septum and LV provide a "splint" against which the RV free wall shortens during contraction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]

**Ventricular structural and functional differences:**
* Thinner RV is more vulnerable to acute increases in afterload [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* LV can generate pressure–volume work that is five to seven-fold greater than RV [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* RV is more compliant and can accommodate excess volume more easily than LV [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* RV compliance is limited by pericardial restraint [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** The heart's anatomical organization, with its skeleton and complex myocardial fiber arrangement, enables specialized mechanical function of each chamber. The LV and RV have distinct structural and functional characteristics that impact their responses to physiological and pathological conditions.

## 2.4. VALVES

**Cardiac valves ensure unidirectional blood flow through the heart chambers.**

### 2.4.1. TRILEAFLET VALVES

* **Pulmonic valve**: Right, left and anterior leaflets
* **Aortic valve**: Named based on presence or absence of an adjacent coronary ostium
  * **Sinuses of Valsalva**: Dilated segments above each leaflet that [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]:
	* Prevent aortic valve leaflets from adhering to the aortic wall by facilitating hydraulic flow vortices (eddy currents) during ejection
	* Ensure right and left coronary leaflets do not occlude their coronary ostia
	* Assist with aortic valve closure when ejection ceases by maintaining leaflet mobility during early diastole
  * Effective orifice area during peak ejection is nearly equal to the cross-sectional area of the respective annulus [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]

### 2.4.2. BILEAFLET VALVES

* **Mitral valve**:
  * Anterior (oval shaped) and posterior (crescent shaped) leaflets
  * Leaflets approximate in a central curve with the anterior mitral leaflet forming the convex border [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * **Chordae tendinae**: Thin fibrous bands attached to the papillary muscles [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
	* Primary and secondary chordae tendinae insert into the edges and bodies of the leaflets, respectively
	* Tertiary chordae tendinae extend from the posterior medial papillary muscle and insert into the posterior mitral leaflet or adjacent myocardium near the annulus
  * Papillary muscle contraction tightens the chordae tendinae, inhibiting excessive leaflet motion past the coaptation zone and preventing leaflet inversion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * Mitral annular circumference decreases modestly during LV contraction through a sphincter-like action [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * 💡 Removal of chordae tendinae–papillary muscle attachments during mitral valve replacement surgery is associated with a reduction in LV ejection fraction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* **Tricuspid valve**:
  * Anterior, posterior, and septal leaflets [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * Identification of a septal papillary muscle can often be used to distinguish the morphologic RV from the LV in patients with complex congenital heart disease [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * No clear cartilaginous annulus like the mitral valve; tricuspid valve originates from the AV groove [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
	* ⚠️ The right coronary artery lies within this groove and must be avoided during tricuspid valve surgery [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 2.4.3. VENTRICULAR INTERNAL STRUCTURE

* RV has coarse trabeculae carnae throughout the chamber [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* LV has finer trabeculations [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Cardiac valves have specialized structures that optimize flow dynamics and ensure unidirectional blood flow. The mitral and tricuspid valves have supporting structures (chordae tendinae and papillary muscles) that prevent regurgitation, while the semilunar valves utilize flow dynamics to ensure efficient closure.

## 2.5. CONDUCTION SYSTEM

**The cardiac conduction system ensures coordinated electrical activation of the heart chambers.**

* **Sinoatrial (SA) node** - primary cardiac pacemaker [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]] unless:
  * Marked decrease in firing rate
  * Conduction delays or blockade
  * Accelerated firing of secondary pacemakers
* **Internodal pathways**:
  * Anterior, middle (Wenckebach), and posterior (Thorel) pathways rapidly transmit the initial SA node depolarization through the RA myocardium to the AV node [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * **Bachmann's bundle** (anterior internodal pathway branch) transmits the SA node depolarization from the RA to the LA across the atrial septum [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* **Atrioventricular conduction**:
  * Cartilaginous skeleton of the heart isolates the atria from the ventricles by acting as an electrical insulator [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * Electrical isolation between atrial and ventricular chambers and normal transmission delay through the slowly conducting AV node establish sequential atrial followed by ventricular contraction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * **His bundle** pierces the connective tissue of the cartilaginous skeleton and transmits the AV depolarization signal along the right and left bundle branches to the RV and LV myocardium [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * Conduction occurs through the **Purkinje network** located within the inner one-third of the ventricular walls [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * This electrical configuration facilitates synchronous biventricular contraction and coordinated ejection [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]

**Conducting system of the heart (Supplementary Information)**:
* The heart beat is initiated in the sinoatrial node (the 'pacemaker of the heart'), located in the upper part of the crista terminalis just to the right of the opening of the superior vena cava into the right atrium
* From the SA node, the cardiac impulse spreads throughout the atrial musculature to reach the atrioventricular node lying in the atrial septum immediately above the opening of the coronary sinus
* The impulse is then conducted to the ventricles by the specialized tissue of the atrioventricular bundle (of His)
* The bundle divides at the junction of the membranous and muscular parts of the interventricular septum into its right and left branches, which run immediately beneath the endocardium to activate all parts of the ventricular musculature
* The sinoatrial node is supplied by the right coronary artery in approximately 65% of cases. In the remainder, the supply is usually from the circumflex branch of the left coronary artery
* The atrioventricular node is perfused by the right coronary artery in 80% of subjects

**Bottom Line:** The cardiac conduction system ensures coordinated electrical activation, with the SA node as the primary pacemaker and the AV node providing critical delay to allow atrial contraction before ventricular activation. The specialized conduction pathway facilitates synchronized ventricular contraction for efficient ejection.

## 2.6. CORONARY ANATOMY

**The coronary vasculature provides the heart's blood supply through a complex network of vessels.**

### 2.6.1. MAJOR CORONARY ARTERIES

* **Left anterior descending (LAD)**, **Left circumflex (LCx)** and **Right coronary arteries (RCA)** supply blood to the heart
* **LAD** supplies:
  * Medial half of the LV anterior wall
  * Apex
  * Anterior 2/3 of the interventricular septum
  * Anterior wall of RV
* **LCx** supplies:
  * Anterior and inferior aspect of the lateral wall
* **RCA** supplies:
  * Medial portions of inferior wall
  * Posterior 1/3 of the interventricular septum
  * Most of RV
* Substantial variations in blood supply to the LV are observed between individuals [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* **Left coronary artery (left main stem)** arises from the left posterior aortic sinus, just above the left cusp of the aortic valve
  * Travels a short distance in the left atrioventricular (AV) groove (less than 2.5 cm) before bifurcating into:
	* **Left anterior descending (LAD) artery**: Descends in the anterior interventricular groove, giving off septal and diagonal branches
	* **Left circumflex artery**: Continues in the left AV groove, giving off one or more obtuse marginal branches
* **Right coronary artery** originates from the anterior aortic sinus
  * Travels along the right AV groove before dividing into:
	* **SA branch**: Present in 60% of individuals, supplies the SA node
	* **Right marginal artery**: Travels down the right margin of the heart toward the apex, supplies the RV
	* **Posterior descending artery (PDA)**: Supplies the posterior part of the septum and the AV node

### 2.6.2. CORONARY DOMINANCE

* Defined by which artery supplies the **posterior descending coronary artery (PDA)**
* **Right dominance** (RCA supply): Observed in approximately 80% of patients
* **Left dominance** (LCCA supply): Occurs in the remainder (about 15% of population)

### 2.6.3. COLLATERAL CIRCULATION

* Direct connections between distal coronary arteries or collateral blood vessels between major coronary arteries may be present
* These vessels provide an alternative route of blood flow to myocardium, but their presence is variable

### 2.6.4. PAPILLARY MUSCLE BLOOD SUPPLY

* Posterior-medial papillary muscle:
  * Supplied by either RCA or LCx (ratio of RCA to LCCA of 2:1)
  * Renders it vulnerable to ischemia
  * Approximately one-third of individuals have a dual blood supply (RCA and LCCA), making them less susceptible to posterior-medial papillary muscle ischemia
* Anterior-lateral papillary muscle:
  * Usually has dual blood supply from LAD and LCx
  * Ischemic dysfunction of this papillary muscle is unusual

### 2.6.5. VENTRICULAR BLOOD SUPPLY

* RV receives blood supply from RCA, but anterior wall also receives blood from LAD
  * RV dysfunction may occur because of either RCA or LAD occlusion
* LCx supplies coronary blood flow to the LA
  * Enhanced LA contractility from Frank–Starling principle may occur with LAD ischemia/infarction
  * This compensatory response may not occur with LCCA compromise due to LA ischemia
* RCA and LCx supply coronary blood flow to the RA

### 2.6.6. CONDUCTION SYSTEM BLOOD SUPPLY

* SA node: Supplied by a nodal artery from the RCA (55% of patients) or the LCx (45% of patients)
* AV node (follows coronary dominance): Supplied by PDA which most of the time arises from RCA, or less commonly branches from the LCx

### 2.6.7. CORONARY VASCULAR STRUCTURE

* RCA, LCx and LAD and proximal branches located on epicardial surface of the heart
* Give rise to intramural vessels penetrating deep into myocardium
* Three functionally distinct compartments:
  * **Large conductive arteries (proximal)**:
	* Serve as conduits
	* Exhibit flow-mediated vasodilation
	* No meaningful resistance to blood flow unless atherosclerosis is present
  * **Prearterioles (intermediate)**:
	* Maintain pressure within narrow range at arteriole origin when changes in coronary perfusion pressure and blood flow occur
	* Diameter regulated through myogenic mechanism (vascular smooth muscle contracts or dilates in response to pressure changes)
  * **Intramural arterioles (distal)**:
	* Largest contribution to coronary vascular resistance
	* Significant vasodilator reserve
	* Very sensitive to vasoactive metabolites produced by myocardium due to metabolic demand
* **Capillary density**:
  * High density in myocardium due to exceptional metabolic demand [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * Ratio of capillaries to muscle fibers is approximately one [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * Capillary recruitment maintains myocardial oxygen tension when demand increases [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * Distribution is uniform throughout atria and ventricles (3,000-4,000/mm²) except in AV node and interventricular septum, where capillary volume is less dense [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * 💡 Less dense capillary volume helps explain why the proximal portion of the RV and LV conduction system seems more vulnerable to ischemia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]

### 2.6.8. COLLATERAL CIRCULATION

* Blood flow through coronary collaterals is usually minimal because driving pressure across vessels is relatively equal
* If blood flow supplying one branch of a collateral vessel is restricted by stenosis or occlusion:
  * Pressure gradient develops
  * Diverts flow from patent artery through collateral vessel into distribution of artery with inadequate flow
* 💡 Presence and extent of coronary collaterals often determines whether a patient with coronary artery disease will develop anginal symptoms in response to increased myocardial oxygen demand [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 2.6.9. VENOUS DRAINAGE

* Coronary venous drainage retraces the course of major coronary arteries along AV and interventricular grooves
* Major veins:
  * **Great cardiac vein**: Courses along the AV groove and LAD
  * **Anterior cardiac vein**: Located adjacent to RCA
  * **Middle cardiac vein**: Associated with PDA
  * **Small cardiac vein**: accompanies RCA in posterior AV groove
  * **Oblique vein**: Traverses the back of the LA
* Two coronary veins lie along either side of each major coronary artery branch
* Great, middle, small, and oblique Veins converge into the **coronary sinus** which empties into posterior aspect of RA immediately above tricuspid valve.
* Anterior cardiac vein drain directly into the RA
* Venous blood flow into coronary sinus peaks during late systole as contracting LV compresses major venous drainage channels
* Approximately 85% of total coronary blood flow returning from LV empties into coronary sinus
* Remaining flow drains directly into atrial and ventricular cavities through the **thebesian veins** (RA > LA and LV)
* RV veins drain into anterior cardiac veins which empty individually into RA

**Bottom Line:** The coronary circulation features a complex network of arteries, collaterals, and veins that ensure myocardial perfusion. Variability in dominance patterns and collateral formation significantly impacts individual susceptibility to ischemic injury. The specialized vascular compartments (conductive arteries, prearterioles, and arterioles) serve distinct functions in regulating coronary blood flow.

# 3. CORONARY PHYSIOLOGY





> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|Part A: Coronary Physiology]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#2.1 Clinical Decision-Making and Procedures|Part C]]

**Coronary blood flow is regulated by complex mechanisms to match myocardial oxygen demand.**

### 3.1.1. CORONARY FLOW DYNAMICS

* **Poiseuille's Law** is *not strictly applicable* due to:
  * Arterial pressure being **phasic**
  * Blood viscosity varying with **shear rate**
  * Vessels being **compliant**
* **Coronary blood flow determinants**:
  * Proportional to **arterial-venous pressure gradient**
  * Inversely related to **vascular resistance**
* **Coronary perfusion pressure** = Aortic diastolic pressure − LV end-diastolic pressure (coronary venous pressure negligible) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * **Arteriolar smooth muscle** controls vascular resistance
  * Changes in **vessel caliber** cause large changes in resistance (∝ fourth power of vessel radius, per Poiseuille's law) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* **Resting coronary blood flow** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]:
  * 250 ml/min (1 ml/min/g)
  * Represents **5% of cardiac output (CO)**
  * The myocardium has high O₂ requirement even in inactive subjects:
	* O₂ extraction is greater in the heart (around 70% at rest) than in any other organ
	* This requirement for high blood flow and efficient O₂ extraction makes the heart very susceptible to ischemia

### 3.1.2. LEFT VENTRICLE (LV) BLOOD FLOW

* Occurs **almost entirely during diastole**
  * **Systole**: Extravascular compressive forces restrict subendocardial intramural vessels
  * **Diastole**: Reduced arteriolar vasomotor tone allows **uniform transmural LV perfusion** (subepicardial : subendocardial blood flow ratio = 1:1)
* **Increased heart rate** shortens diastole
  * Normally compensated by **coronary flow reserve**
  * ⚠️ **Flow-limiting coronary artery stenosis** or **pressure-overload hypertrophy** may result in **subendocardial ischemia** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 3.1.3. RIGHT VENTRICLE (RV), LEFT ATRIUM (LA), AND RIGHT ATRIUM (RA)

* Pressures remain **lower than aortic pressure**
* **Coronary blood flow** generally unaffected by compressive forces

### 3.1.4. MYOCARDIAL METABOLISM

* Dependent on **aerobic metabolism** (mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation of **fatty acids**, **glucose**, and **lactate**)
* **Anaerobic metabolism** (glucose → lactate) is limited
  * Contraction ceases **within 10-15 seconds** after **acute coronary artery occlusion** unless sufficient **collateral blood flow** exists [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* **Flow-metabolism coupling** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]:
  * Mediated by intrinsic regulators:
	* **CO₂**, **ROS** → **NO** (muscle relaxant)
	* **KATP channels** → hyperpolarization, ↓ Ca²⁺ entry
  * Impaired by **ATHEROSCLEROSIS**, **DIABETES MELLITUS (DM)**, **HYPERLIPIDEMIA (HLD)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 3.1.5. OXYGEN EXTRACTION

* ~75% of **O₂** extracted from capillary blood
* Coronary sinus **O₂ tension** ≈ 20 mmHg
* **LV** depends on increased **blood flow** (limited O₂ extraction reserve) to meet higher **O₂ demand**

### 3.1.6. CORONARY FLOW RESERVE

* **Ratio of maximum to baseline coronary blood flow**
* Measured by analysis of transient increase in flow after:
  * Brief coronary artery occlusion (reactive hyperemia)
  * Administration of potent vasodilator
* In normal coronary arteries: Flow reserve is very large (500-600%)
* 💡 Reduced by coronary stenosis, pressure-overload hypertrophy, and microvascular dysfunction → more susceptible to ischemia during increased O₂ demand [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

![image.png](CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_image_5.png_be4b19.png_a52a7e.png_09e17_.png)

### 3.1.7. CORONARY PRESSURE-FLOW AUTOREGULATION

* Ability of coronary circulation to maintain relatively constant blood flow across wide range of perfusion pressure
* Not dependent on ANS or metabolic factors, but by intrinsic myogenic phenomenon
  * Lower coronary perfusion pressure = vasodilation
  * Higher coronary perfusion pressure = vasoconstriction

### 3.1.8. ADRENERGIC REGULATION

* **A1 adrenoceptors**: Found in large and intermediate-sized coronary vessels
* **A2 adrenoceptors**: Found in arterioles
  * Larger vessels less affected by vasoconstrictive effect
  * Smaller arterioles greatly affected, especially when coronary atherosclerosis is present → reduced perfusion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **B1 adrenoceptors**: Found in arterioles, causes vasodilation
* **Adrenaline** → increased myocardial O₂ demand → metabolic vasodilation → overwhelms A2 adrenoceptor vasoconstriction → increases LV coronary blood flow [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* **M3 receptors** (parasympathetic muscarinic): Found in coronary endothelium and smooth muscle
  * Acetylcholine activation → endothelium-dependent vasodilation
  * ⚠️ Vasoconstriction occurs when endothelial dysfunction is present [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 3.1.9. FACTORS INFLUENCING CORONARY BLOOD FLOW 

* **O₂ demand**: Coronary blood flow is tightly coupled to O₂ demand
  * Possible mechanism: Increased cardiac work → reduction in myocardial cytosolic ATP and increase in AMP → adenosine release → vasodilation
  * Other possible vasodilatory mediators: K⁺, CO₂, H⁺, and NO
* **Autoregulation**: Coronary arterioles maintain constant blood flow between perfusion pressures of 60-180 mmHg
  * Below 60 mmHg (arterioles maximally dilated), blood flow falls with further pressure decrease
* **Heart rate**: Increased HR encroaches more on diastole than systole, decreasing left coronary blood flow
  * Right coronary blood flow relatively unaffected
* **Patency of coronary vessels**:
  * Atheroma prevents vasodilation in response to increased O₂ demand
  * Acute obstruction (thrombosis, embolus, vasospasm) can cause ischemia even without increased O₂ demand
* **Autonomic control**: Minor direct effect
  * Parasympathetic: Weak vasodilatory effect
  * Sympathetic: Increases coronary blood flow mainly through increased O₂ demand secondary to increased inotropy and HR
* **Medications**:
  * **Nitrates**: Cause coronary vasodilation and reduce cardiac preload/afterload, decreasing O₂ demand
  * **β-blockers**: Reduce HR, prolonging diastole and increasing coronary blood flow to LV
  * **Ca²⁺ channel blockers**: Cause coronary arteriolar vasodilation and reduce afterload
  * **K⁺ channel openers** (e.g., nicorandil): Increase K⁺ efflux from smooth muscle cells, causing hyperpolarization and vasodilation
  * **Ranolazine**: Inhibits late Na⁺ current, reduces intracellular Ca²⁺, decreasing O₂ demand
  * **Antiplatelet agents**: Prevent occlusive thrombus formation
  * **Statins**: Reduce cholesterol and exert plaque stabilization effects

**Bottom Line:** Coronary blood flow is tightly regulated to match myocardial oxygen demand, occurring primarily during diastole for the LV due to systolic compression of vessels. Coronary flow reserve provides substantial capacity to increase flow during increased demand, but can be compromised by stenosis, hypertrophy, or microvascular dysfunction, increasing vulnerability to ischemia.

# 4. CARDIAC MYOCYTE ANATOMY AND FUNCTION





> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|Part A: Cardiac Myocyte Function]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#2.1 Clinical Decision-Making and Procedures|Part C]]

**Cardiac myocytes have specialized structures that enable coordinated contraction and relaxation.**

![image.png](CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_image_6.png_8bfd16.png_cfe4bb.png_e5b1f_.png)

### 4.1.1. SARCOLEMMA

* External membrane of the cardiac muscle cell
* This bilayer lipid membrane contains:
  * **Ion channels** (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Cl⁻) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * **Active and passive ion transporters** (Na⁺-K⁺ ATPase, Ca²⁺-ATPase, Na⁺-Ca²⁺ and Na⁺-H⁺ exchangers) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * **Receptors** (beta1-adrenergic, muscarinic cholinergic, adenosine, opioid) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * **Transport enzymes** (e.g., glucose) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* These components modulate intracellular ion concentrations, regulate homeostasis of contraction and electrophysiology, mediate signal transduction, and provide substrates for metabolism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* **Transverse tubules** penetrate deep into the myoplasm to facilitate rapid transmission of cellular depolarization [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]

### 4.1.2. SARCOPLASMIC RETICULUM (SR)

* Primary Ca²⁺ reservoir (bound to calsequestrin and calreticulin)
* Enables homogeneous dispersal and reuptake of Ca²⁺ throughout the myofilaments
* When sarcolemma depolarizes → subsarcolemmal cisternae within the SR have ryanodine receptors → Ca²⁺ release [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* **SERCA** (sarcoendoplasmic reticulum Ca²⁺-ATPase):
  * Energy-dependent ion pump in SR membrane
  * Removes vast majority of Ca²⁺ ions from myofilaments and myoplasm when sarcolemma repolarizes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * **Phospholamban** in SR membrane partially inhibits SERCA [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * Phosphorylation of phospholamban by protein kinase A antagonizes this inhibition → enhanced SERCA-mediated Ca²⁺ uptake [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * 💡 **Dobutamine** and **milrinone** modify protein kinase A-mediated signal transduction → enhanced rate and extent of relaxation by facilitating Ca²⁺ reuptake (lusitropic) while simultaneously increasing Ca²⁺ available for contraction (inotropic) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Another Ca²⁺-ATPase and the Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger, each located in the sarcolemmal membrane, also play roles in Ca²⁺ removal after repolarization [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]

### 4.1.3. MITOCHONDRIA AND CONTRACTILE APPARATUS

* Each myocyte is densely packed with mitochondria
* Mitochondria + contractile apparatus comprise 80% of the myocyte's total volume [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* **Sarcomere** is the contractile unit of the cardiac myocyte [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * Myofilaments arranged in parallel cross-striated bundles:
	* **Thin fibers** (containing actin, tropomyosin, and the troponin complex)
	* **Thick fibers** (primarily composed of myosin and supporting proteins)
  * Sarcomeres are connected end-to-end in series [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]

The long and short axes of each myocyte shorten and thicken, respectively, during contraction.

Thick and thin fibers interact in an area known as the "A" band that becomes wider (indicating more pronounced overlap) as the sarcomere shortens.

The sarcomere region containing thin filaments alone is termed the "I" band, which is reduced during contraction.

A "Z" line ("zuckung," which means "twitch" in German) bisects each "I" band and is the border of two sarcomeres.

Thus, the "A" band and two split "I" bands (between the "Z" lines) define the length of each sarcomere. The "A" band also contains a central "M" band composed of thick filaments that are oriented in a cross-sectional hexagonal arrangement by myosin-binding protein C.

Intercalated discs mechanically join adjacent myocytes through the fascia adherens (which links actin molecules at each "Z" line) and desmosomes. They also create electrical continuity between myocytes through gap junctions that allow diffusion of ions and small molecules [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Cardiac myocytes contain specialized structures including the sarcolemma with ion channels, sarcoplasmic reticulum for calcium handling, abundant mitochondria for energy production, and sarcomeres as the basic contractile units. These components work together to enable efficient excitation-contraction coupling and relaxation.

## 4.2. CONTRACTILE APPARATUS PROTEINS

**The cardiac contractile apparatus consists of specialized proteins that enable coordinated contraction and relaxation.**

### 4.2.1. CONTRACTILE PROTEINS

* Six major proteins: myosin, actin, tropomyosin, three-protein troponin complex

#### 4.2.1.1. MYOSIN

* Contains a pair of intertwined alpha-helical proteins (tails), each with a globular head that binds the actin molecule, and two adjoining pairs of light chains
* Enzymatic digestion reveals:
  * **Light meromyosin** (composed of tail sections)
  * **Heavy meromyosin** (containing globular heads and light chains)
* Binding of myosin head to actin molecule → cascade → activation of myosin ATPase → hinge rotation and actin release during contraction and relaxation respectively
* Myosin ATPase activity is a major determinant of maximum velocity of sarcomere shortening → actin pulled towards center [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* Direct relationship between maximal velocity of unloaded muscle shortening (Vmax) and myosin ATPase activity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* Extent of sarcomere shortening during contraction is dependent on sarcomere length before sarcolemmal depolarization
* **Length-dependent activation** of the sarcomere:
  * Forms basis of Frank–Starling principle in intact heart
  * May relate to increased myofilament Ca²⁺ sensitivity, more optimal spacing between actin and myosin, or titin-induced elastic recoil [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * **Anrep effect**: Abrupt increases in load during shortening cause transient rise in contractile force through length-dependent activation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * **Woodworth phenomenon**: Extended pause between contractions causes transient rise in contractile force [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* Myosin is oriented in series along length of thick filament and joined "tail to tail" in filament's center at M line [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]

#### 4.2.1.2. THICK FILAMENTS

* Composed of myosin + binding protein + Titin
* **Titin**: Long elastic molecule attaching myosin to "Z" lines
  * Essential contributor to myocardial elasticity
  * Acts as a length sensor (like a bidirectional spring)
  * Creates progressively larger restoring forces as sarcomere approaches maximum or minimum length
  * Important regulator of stress–strain behavior of cardiac muscle [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]

#### 4.2.1.3. ACTIN

* Major component of thin filament
* Polymerized filamentous configuration wound in double-stranded helical chains of monomers
* Single helix of actin contains 14 monomers and is 77 nm in length [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* Activator of myosin ATPase through its reversible binding with myosin
* Actin-myosin complex hydrolyzes ATP → energy needed during contraction and relaxation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]

#### 4.2.1.4. TROPOMYOSIN

* Inhibitor of interaction between actin and myosin
* Rigid double-stranded alpha-helix protein linked by single disulfide bond
* Human tropomyosin has alpha and beta isoforms [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]

#### 4.2.1.5. TROPONIN COMPLEX

* Composed of Troponin C, I, and T
* **Troponin C**:
  * Responds to intracellular Ca²⁺ concentration
  * Ca²⁺-troponin C binding produces conformational changes in troponin–tropomyosin complex that expose myosin-binding site on actin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * During diastole, intracellular Ca²⁺ low → small amounts of Ca²⁺ bind to Troponin C → complex confines tropomyosin to outer region of groove between actin filaments and prevents cross-bridge formation with myosin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * This is reversed when intracellular Ca²⁺ concentration increases >100-fold from:
	* Sarcolemmal depolarization → Land T-type Ca²⁺ channels open
	* Ca²⁺ influx from extracellular space + ryanodine receptor-mediated Ca²⁺-induced Ca²⁺ release from SR
	* Ca²⁺ binds to troponin C → allosteric modifications in complex → troponin I and actin interaction weakens
	* Inhibition of actin-myosin complex by tropomyosin minimized (exposed binding site of myosin)
	* Dissociation of Ca²⁺ reverses this process [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
* **Troponin I**:
  * Inhibitor of actin-myosin binding when combined with tropomyosin
  * Contains serine residue susceptible to protein kinase A-mediated phosphorylation through cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) → reduces ability of troponin C to bind Ca²⁺ → relaxation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]
  * 💡 This pathway occurs during administration of beta-adrenoreceptor agonist or phosphodiesterase fraction III inhibitor [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Troponin T**:
  * Anchor for other troponin molecules [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]

![image.png](CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_image_9.png_81ed59.png_d9c42a.png_daa68_.png)

**Bottom Line:** The contractile apparatus of cardiac myocytes consists of precisely organized proteins including myosin (thick filaments), actin (thin filaments), tropomyosin, and the troponin complex. These proteins interact through calcium-regulated processes to enable coordinated contraction and relaxation, with length-dependent activation serving as the basis for the Frank-Starling mechanism.

## 4.3. MYOSIN-ACTIN INTERACTION

**The interaction between myosin and actin generates force for cardiac contraction through a coordinated cycle of events.**

### 4.3.1. CROSS-BRIDGE CYCLE

1. **ATP binding and hydrolysis**:
   * ATP binds to catalytic domain of myosin
   * Myosin ATPase hydrolyzes ATP to ADP and phosphate (active complex with potential energy released from hydrolysis reaction)

2. **Cross-bridge formation**:
   * Myosin-ADP-PO₄ complex binds to actin
   * PO₄ anion released → tension-induced conformation within the cross-bridge

3. **Power stroke**:
   * Release of ADP and potential energy from this activated orientation combine to rotate the cross-bridge at the hinge point separating the helical tail from the globular head of myosin
   * Each cross-bridge rotation generates 3.5 × 10^-12 newtons of force and moves myosin 11 nm along the actin molecule

4. **Rigor state and dissociation**:
   * The myosin–actin complex does not immediately dissociate after rotation but remains in a low-energy bound (rigor) state
   * Dissociation of myosin and actin molecules occurs when a new ATP molecule binds to myosin

**Bottom Line:** The myosin-actin interaction follows a precise cycle of ATP binding, hydrolysis, cross-bridge formation, power stroke, and dissociation that converts chemical energy into mechanical force. This cycle enables cardiac contraction and is regulated by calcium availability and ATP.

## 4.4. CARDIAC ACTION POTENTIAL

**The cardiac action potential has distinct phases that regulate myocyte depolarization and repolarization.**

![image.png](CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_image_10.png_974e44.png_1e1837.png_1a07_.png)

* **Phase 0** - Depolarization:
  * Transmembrane potential rises from -85mV to +20mV
  * Rapid upstroke driven by fast sodium channels
* **Phase 1** - Early rapid repolarization:
  * Brief partial repolarization
  * Sodium channels inactivate
  * Transient outward potassium current
* **Phase 2** - Plateau phase:
  * Depolarization for about 0.2 seconds
  * Allows longer contraction than skeletal muscle cells due to:
	* Slow calcium channels
	* Decreased permeability of potassium channels after initiation of action potential
  * During this plateau phase, the cardiomyocyte cannot be restimulated for about 0.25 to 0.3 second, called the **refractory period**
  * Additional 0.05 second **relative refractory period** follows, when myocardium can only be stimulated by a strong excitatory signal
* **Phase 3** - Rapid repolarization:
  * Potassium channels open
  * Calcium channels close
  * Membrane potential returns toward resting level
* **Phase 4** - Resting potential:
  * Stable negative potential maintained by Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase

**Bottom Line:** The cardiac action potential features a rapid depolarization (phase 0), early rapid repolarization (phase 1), prolonged plateau (phase 2), rapid repolarization (phase 3), and resting state (phase 4). The plateau phase creates a long refractory period that prevents tetanic contraction and allows coordinated myocardial contraction and relaxation.

## 4.5. PACEMAKER ACTION POTENTIAL

**The pacemaker action potential has unique characteristics that enable spontaneous depolarization.**

![image.png](CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_image_11.png_c189a1.png_65787e.png_1007_.png)

* **Ionic basis**: Calcium, sodium, and potassium are all responsible for generating an action potential in the sinoatrial node
* **Unique feature**: The sinoatrial and atrioventricular nodes depolarize during phase 0 of the **action potential** via **influx of calcium** into the cell instead of sodium (which occurs in most other electrically active tissues)

### 4.5.1. PACEMAKER CELL CHARACTERISTICS

* Sinoatrial node contains cells that act as the primary pacemaker site for the heart
* Unlike traditional myocytes, pacemaker cells do not have a defined resting potential
* Depolarization mediated by calcium influx through slow calcium channels instead of sodium through fast sodium channels

### 4.5.2. PACEMAKER ACTION POTENTIAL PHASES

* No phase 1 or 2 due to absence of fast sodium channels
* Phases 0, 3, and 4 are relatively analogous to their counterparts in traditional myocytes
* **Phase 4 - Spontaneous depolarization**:
  * **Funny currents (If)** allow spontaneous inward flow of sodium ions, resulting in slow depolarization
  * As membrane potential reaches around −50 mV, **T-type (transient) calcium channels** open, allowing calcium influx and accelerating depolarization
  * When membrane potential reaches −40 mV, **L-type (long-acting) calcium channels** open, allowing more calcium into the cell and causing depolarization threshold to be met
* **Phase 0 - Depolarization**:
  * Driven by L-type calcium channels
  * Funny currents (If) and T-type calcium currents begin to cease
* **Phase 3 - Repolarization**:
  * Potassium channels open, allowing potassium efflux from the pacemaker cell
  * Causes repolarization, eventually resulting in hyperpolarization
  * Resets all voltage-gated ion channels in the myocyte pacemaker

### 4.5.3. EFFECTS OF ALTERED CALCIUM LEVELS AND MEDICATIONS

* **Increased extracellular calcium** during phase 0:
  * Creates larger concentration gradient
  * More rapid depolarization
  * 💡 This effect partly explains increased chronotropy seen with excess calcium administration [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Beta-adrenergic blocking agents**:
  * Inhibit beta-1 adrenergic receptor
  * Decrease automatic calcium influx into cell during phase 4
  * Decrease potassium efflux from cell during phase 3
  * Result in clinical bradycardia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Pacemaker cells have distinctive action potentials characterized by spontaneous depolarization during phase 4, driven by funny currents and calcium channels rather than sodium channels. This unique mechanism enables automatic rhythmicity and is affected by calcium levels and autonomic influences.

# 5. LAPLACE'S LAW





> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|Part A: Laplace's Law]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#2.1 Clinical Decision-Making and Procedures|Part C]]

**Laplace's law relates wall stress to pressure and chamber geometry in the heart.**

### 5.1.1. PRINCIPLES OF LAPLACE'S LAW

* Chamber volume is modeled as a pressurized spherical shell: V = 4πr³/3
* Laplace's law relates wall stress to pressure and chamber geometry based on three fundamental assumptions:
  * The chamber is spherical with a uniform wall thickness and an internal radius (r)
  * The stress (σ) throughout the thickness of the chamber wall is constant
  * The chamber is in static equilibrium (not actively contracting)

### 5.1.2. MECHANICAL PRINCIPLES

* Tension development within each sarcomere causes increase in wall stress that is translated into pressure (P) generation within the chamber
* Pressure is defined as perpendicular distending force exerted against chamber walls
* Wall stress is the force exerted around circumference of chamber that resists distention
* Product of pressure and wall cross-sectional area (πr²) represents total force pushing chamber hemispheres apart
* Force within chamber walls resisting this distracting force equals σ times cross-sectional wall area

### 5.1.3. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION

* For equilibrium: Pπr² = σ[π(r + h)² – πr²]
* Simplified to: Pr = σh(2 + h/r)
* Since chamber wall is thin relative to internal radius, h/r term may be neglected
* Final expression: σ = Pr/2h

### 5.1.4. CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS

* Wall stress varies directly with internal pressure and radius, and inversely with wall thickness
* **Hypertrophy** is an important compensatory adaptation to chronically altered chamber load:
  * Increase in wall thickness reduces wall stress and tension developed by individual sarcomeres
  * 💡 Reduces myocardial oxygen demand [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Wall stress distribution:
  * Not uniformly distributed across LV thickness in intact heart
  * Greatest in subendocardium, progressively declining toward epicardial surface
  * 💡 Important in LV pressure-overload hypertrophy, where subendocardium is exposed to marked increases in LV pressure and myocardial oxygen demand, rendering it more susceptible to ischemia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

![image.png](CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_image_12.png_35ca18.png_d56197.png_a517_.png)

**Bottom Line:** Laplace's law demonstrates how wall stress is directly proportional to chamber pressure and radius, and inversely proportional to wall thickness. This relationship explains why ventricular hypertrophy develops as a compensatory mechanism to reduce wall stress in conditions of pressure or volume overload, though the subendocardium remains vulnerable to ischemia due to higher stress distribution.

# 6. CARDIAC CYCLE





> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|Part A: Cardiac Cycle]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#2.1 Clinical Decision-Making and Procedures|Part C]]

**The cardiac cycle comprises a coordinated sequence of electrical, mechanical, and hemodynamic events.**

### 6.1.1. OVERVIEW OF CARDIAC CYCLE 

The cardiac cycle refers to the complete sequence of physiological events that occur in the heart, from the beginning of one heartbeat to the beginning of the next.

**The cardiac cycle consists of two phases**:
* **The diastolic phase**, during which the ventricles are relaxed and filling with blood
* **The systolic phase**, during which the ventricles contract and eject blood into the aorta and pulmonary artery

### 6.1.2. SYSTOLE

* **Synchronous depolarization** of RV and LV = systole → increase in RV and LV pressures
* **Atrioventricular valve closure**:
  * Tricuspid and mitral valves close when RV/LV pressures exceed RA/LA pressures
  * Produces first heart sound (S1)
  * Mitral valve normally closes slightly earlier than the tricuspid valve, resulting in a 'split' S1
* **Isovolumic contraction**:
  * First phase of LV systole between mitral valve closure and aortic valve opening
  * LV volume remains constant
  * LV transforms from ellipsoidal shape to more spherical geometry as longitudinal axis shortens and wall thickness increases
  * Maximum rate of increase of LV pressure (dP/dtmax) occurs during this phase
  * Serves as an index of myocardial contractility
* **Ejection phase**:
  * Begins when LV pressure exceeds aortic pressure
  * Approximately 2/3 of LV end-diastolic volume (EDV) is ejected
  * Compliance of proximal arterial vessels (elasticity) stores potential energy that is released to distal vascular bed during diastole
* **Right ventricular contraction**:
  * True isovolumic contraction does not occur in RV due to sequential nature of contraction of inflow and outflow tracts
  * Similar events occur in RV and pulmonary artery during systole
* **End of systole**:
  * Ejection stops when LV begins to repolarize and arterial force resisting ejection exceeds ventricular forward force
  * Aortic and pulmonary pressures exceed LV and RV pressures → semilunar valves close (S2 heart sound) → beginning of diastole
  * 💡 Aortic valve closes slightly earlier than pulmonic valve during inspiration due to RV ejection being modestly prolonged by augmented venous return → 'split' S2 heart sound
* **Normal volumes**:
  * End-diastolic volume (Ved): ~120 mL
  * End-systolic volume (Ves): ~40 mL
  * Stroke volume (SV): 80 mL (Ved - Ves)
  * Ejection fraction (EF): 67% (SV/Ved ratio)

### 6.1.3. DIASTOLE

#### 6.1.3.1. FOUR PHASES OF LV DIASTOLE

1. **Isovolumic relaxation**:
   * Period between aortic valve closure and mitral valve opening
   * LV volume remains constant
   * RV diastole has similar characteristics except true isovolumic relaxation does not occur

2. **Early ventricular filling**:
   * When LV pressure < LA pressure, mitral valve opens, blood enters LV via pressure gradient
   * LV pressure continues to fall below LA pressure after mitral valve opening as sarcomere relaxation completes and myocardial elastic components recoil
   * Creates diastolic suction mechanism
   * Sustained pressure gradient between LA and LV extending from base to apex
   * Rate and extent of LV pressure decline plus LA pressure at mitral valve opening determine magnitude and duration of pressure gradient
   * 💡 Early LV filling is rapid, exceeding flow rate across aortic valve during LV ejection
   * ⚠️ Delayed LV relaxation may occur due to age or disease (e.g., myocardial ischemia, hypertrophy) → blunted early LV filling due to reduced initial LA–LV pressure gradient [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
   * Vortex formation created by primary mitral blood flow jet facilitates selective filling of LV outflow tract
   * Most of LV volume increase during early filling occurs while LV pressure continues to fall
   * Early filling phase normally provides 70-75% of total SV ejected during next LV contraction
   * 💡 Diastolic suction preserves early LV filling during profound hypovolemia and strenuous exercise

3. **Diastasis**:
   * When LA and LV pressures equalize, mitral valve remains open
   * Pulmonary venous blood flows directly through LA into LV
   * LA acts as conduit during diastasis (adds 5% of LV stroke volume)
   * Progressive increases in heart rate shorten and eventually eliminate diastasis

4. **Atrial systole**:
   * LA contraction creates second positive pressure gradient
   * Peristaltic pattern of LA contraction and unique pulmonary venous–LA junction anatomy largely prevent retrograde blood flow into pulmonary veins during atrial systole when LA pressures are not elevated
   * Contributes 15-25% of total LV stroke volume
   * Becomes more important for LV filling in pathologic states with delayed LV relaxation or reduced LV compliance
   * ⚠️ Improperly timed LA contraction or atrial tachyarrhythmias may cause profound hemodynamic compromise in patients dependent on atrial systole for LV filling (e.g., acute myocardial ischemia, pressure-overload hypertrophy) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
   * At rest, this atrial 'kick' accounts for only 10% of ventricular filling, but during exercise when diastole is shortened, atrial contraction contributes up to 40% of ventricular filling

### 6.1.4. ATRIAL PRESSURE WAVEFORMS

* **LA waveforms**:
  * "a" wave: LA contracts immediately after P wave of atrial depolarization
  * "c" wave: LV contraction at onset of systole transmits pressure wave to LA retrograde by mitral valve closure
  * "v" wave: During late LV isovolumic contraction, LV ejection, and majority of LV isovolumic relaxation, pulmonary venous blood progressively fills LA and gradually increases LA pressure
	* Enhanced in presence of mitral regurgitation or reduced LA compliance
  * LA volume waveform is monophasic (LA not filled during systole) unlike biphasic LA pressure waveform
* **RA waveforms**:
  * Similar "a-c-v" pattern as LA
  * Transmitted to jugular veins and may be observed in neck during physical examination in supine position

### 6.1.5. HEART SOUNDS 

* **First heart sound (S1)**: Produced by AV valve closure (mitral and tricuspid)
* **Second heart sound (S2)**: Produced by semilunar valve closure (aortic and pulmonary)
  * Physiological splitting may occur during inspiration
* **Third heart sound (S3)**: Not usually audible in normal adults
  * May be heard in conditions with increased ventricular filling (mitral regurgitation) or poor ventricular compliance (dilated cardiomyopathy)
* **Fourth heart sound (S4)**: Not usually audible in normal adults
  * May be heard in patients with reduced ventricular compliance requiring forceful atrial contraction

### 6.1.6. CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS 

* **Cardiac output effects**:
  * Cardiac output is directly dependent on heart rate and stroke volume
  * At high heart rates (>150 bpm), diastolic filling time decreases, reducing stroke volume
  * At low heart rates (<40 bpm), CO decreases despite modest increase in SV
  * Optimal sinus heart rate for most people is between 50-150 bpm
* **Age-related changes**:
  * Reduced aortic compliance increases afterload
  * LV hypertrophy and decreased compliance develop
  * LV relaxation becomes less efficient
  * Atrial contraction contributes increasingly to ventricular filling (up to 40% of LVEDV)
  * Atrial fibrillation may cause significant hemodynamic compromise in elderly patients
  * Maximum achievable heart rate decreases with age: HRmax = 208 - (0.7 × age)
* **Valvular disease management**:
  * **Aortic stenosis**: ("Full, slow/sinus, tight") Maintain sinus rhythm, low/normal HR (50-70 bpm), avoid SVR reduction (i.e. also maintain hematocrit as low hematocrit results in reduced viscosity and hence reduced SVR by HP law)
  * **Mitral stenosis**: Low/normal HR to allow ventricular filling, maintain sinus rhythm, avoid conditions exacerbating pulmonary hypertension
  * **Aortic regurgitation**: High/normal HR (90-110 bpm) to reduce regurgitant fraction, maintain contractility, low SVR
  * **Mitral regurgitation**: High/normal HR, low SVR, avoid conditions exacerbating pulmonary hypertension

**Bottom Line:** The cardiac cycle consists of coordinated mechanical events in systole (isovolumic contraction and ejection) and diastole (isovolumic relaxation, early filling, diastasis, and atrial systole). Proper timing of these events and the pressure gradients they create are essential for efficient cardiac performance. Atrial contribution becomes increasingly important in conditions with impaired ventricular relaxation.

**Pressure-volume loop diagrams provide a comprehensive visualization of cardiac function during the cardiac cycle.**

### 7.1.1. BASIC PRESSURE-VOLUME LOOP

* PV Loop Diagrams: Temporal changes in LV pressure with respect to volume are projected in a counterclockwise fashion
* **Key points in the PV loop**:
  * **Point A - End diastole**:
	* LV undergoes isovolumic contraction (increase in LV pressure at constant LV volume)
	* Aortic valve opens when LV pressure exceeds aortic pressure (Point B)
  
  * **Point B to Point C - LV ejection**:
	* LV volume decreases as ejection occurs into aorta and proximal great vessels
	* LV pressure gradually falls until below aortic pressure at end ejection
	* Aortic valve closes (Point C)
  
  * **Point C to Point D - LV isovolumic relaxation**:
	* Begins when aortic valve closes
	* Rapid decline in LV pressure without change in LV volume
	* When LV pressure falls below LA pressure, mitral valve opens and LV filling begins (Point D)
  
  * **Point D to Point A - LV Filling**:
	* LV pressure gradually increases after early filling, diastasis, and LA systole
	* Cycle repeats

### 7.1.2. CLINICAL UTILITY OF PV DIAGRAMS

* The steady-state LV pressure-volume diagram provides advantages over individual plots of LV pressure and volume waveforms:
  * Allows identification of mechanical cardiac events without ECG correlation
  * Facilitates evaluation of acute alterations in LV loading conditions
  * Point A represents Ved; Point C represents Ves (allows calculation of SV and EF)
  * Rightward movement of right side indicates increased preload and greater SV
  * Narrowed and taller diagram indicates increased afterload (narrow due to decreased SV; taller due to greater LV pressure)
  * Area of the diagram defines the LV pressure–volume work (stroke work) for a single cardiac cycle

**Bottom Line:** Pressure-volume loops graphically depict the cardiac cycle as a counterclockwise path through four key phases: isovolumic contraction, ejection, isovolumic relaxation, and filling. This representation allows assessment of preload, afterload, contractility, and stroke work, making it a valuable tool for evaluating cardiac function in various physiologic and pathologic conditions.

## 7.2. PVR DIFFERENCES BETWEEN LV AND RV

**Right and left ventricular pressure-volume relationships differ significantly due to their unique structures and functions.**

![image.png](CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_image_19.png_4a2199.png_9152a0.png_4d11_.png)

### 7.2.1. RIGHT VENTRICULAR PV CHARACTERISTICS

* Sequential pattern of right ventricular free wall contraction leads to:
  * Peak pressure within RV occurring very early in systole
  * Blood continuing to leave via pulmonic valve for an extended period due to inertia
  * More triangular shape of RV pressure-volume loop
  * Only brief period of isovolumic relaxation
* RV is very sensitive to changes in afterload due to prolonged low-pressure emptying
* RV lacks defined end-systolic point, making Ees analysis less applicable
* Preload recruitable stroke work (PRSW) considered superior for RV contractility assessment

### 7.2.2. RV UNDER PATHOLOGICAL CONDITIONS

* With pulmonary hypertension, RV pressure-volume loop resembles LV loop
* Change occurs due to altered magnitude and timing of peak RV pressure

**Bottom Line:** The right ventricular pressure-volume loop has a distinctive triangular shape compared to the left ventricle's more rectangular pattern, reflecting differences in loading conditions and contractile patterns. The RV is particularly sensitive to afterload changes, and its contractility is better assessed using preload recruitable stroke work rather than end-systolic elastance.

### 7.3.2. EFFECT OF VALVULAR PATHOLOGY

## 7.4. ESPVR AND CONTRACTILITY

**The end-systolic pressure-volume relationship (ESPVR) provides a load-independent measure of myocardial contractility.**

### 7.4.1. ESPVR AND CONTRACTILITY ASSESSMENT

* A decrease in the ESPVR slope indicates reduced myocardial contractility
* In LV systolic dysfunction or Heart Failure with reduced Ejection Fraction (HFrEF):
  * ESPVR slope decreases
  * Accompanied by compensatory LV dilation (movement of pressure-volume diagram along a normal EDPVR)
  * Increase in preload preserves stroke volume and cardiac output
  * At the cost of higher LV filling and pulmonary venous pressures [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

![image.png](CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_image_24.png_3fdeba.png_2c79a4.png_d6b4_.png)

**Bottom Line:** The slope of the end-systolic pressure-volume relationship (ESPVR) is a relatively load-independent measure of myocardial contractility. In systolic heart failure, the ESPVR slope decreases, and the heart compensates by operating at a higher preload to maintain stroke volume, though at the cost of elevated filling pressures.

## 7.5. EDPVR AND COMPLIANCE

**The end-diastolic pressure-volume relationship (EDPVR) characterizes ventricular compliance and filling properties.**

### 7.5.1. EDPVR AND VENTRICULAR COMPLIANCE

* The family of pressure–volume diagrams describes the end-diastolic pressure–volume relationship (EDPVR) that characterizes LV compliance
* LV compliance is a major determinant of LV filling
* Increase in EDPVR slope implies reduced LV compliance:
  * LV cannot fill without higher LV end-diastolic pressure
  * Found in LV diastolic dysfunction and Heart Failure with preserved Ejection Fraction (HFpEF)
  * Myocardial contractility is preserved but LV filling pressures are elevated
  * May present with pulmonary venous congestion and clinical symptoms [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 7.5.2. VENTRICULAR PROPERTIES AND LOADING CONDITIONS

* ESPVR and EDPVR are determined by intrinsic properties of the LV
* Relative positions of end-diastolic and end-systolic points along these lines are established by:
  * Venous return (preload)
  * Arterial tone (afterload)
* Simultaneous depression of ESPVR and elevation of EDPVR indicates presence of both LV systolic and diastolic dysfunction - usually in HFrEF [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 7.5.3. VENTRICULAR ENERGETICS

* Triangular-shaped area to the left of the steady-state LV pressure–volume diagram between ESPVR and EDPVR represents remaining potential energy of the system
* Important factor in determining LV energetics and mechanical efficiency

**Bottom Line:** The end-diastolic pressure-volume relationship (EDPVR) reflects ventricular compliance. An increased EDPVR slope indicates reduced compliance, requiring higher filling pressures for adequate ventricular filling, as seen in diastolic heart failure. The combination of ESPVR and EDPVR abnormalities comprehensively characterizes ventricular dysfunction patterns.

## 7.6. PV DIAGRAMS BY ULTRASOUND TRANSDUCERS

**Regional myocardial function can be assessed using pressure-length or pressure-segment relationships.**

* Pressure-volume can also be studied by ultrasonic transducers within the LV wall - measuring changes in segment length, LV diameter, or wall thickness in a single region or dimension of the LV wall
* Segment length or LV diameter normally increases during diastole and shortens during systole (analogous to changes in continuous LV volume), with reciprocal changes in wall thickness
* Clinical applications:
  * Acute coronary artery occlusion causes time-dependent collapse of steady-state LV pressure–length diagram in central ischemic zone
  * Consistent with rapid decline (hypokinesis) and eventual absence of regional stroke work (akinesis)
  * LV pressure–segment length diagram tilts right in border zone surrounding central ischemic region
  * Three regions identified in the diagram:
	* Systolic lengthening (paroxysmal systolic aneurysmal bulging of ischemic zone)
	* Postsystolic shortening (shortening in ischemic zone after ejection due to tethering to adjacent normal myocardium)
	* Variable area between the two contributing to regional stroke work

**Bottom Line:** Ultrasound transducers can assess regional myocardial function through pressure-segment relationships, particularly valuable for detecting and quantifying regional wall motion abnormalities during ischemia. Characteristic changes include systolic lengthening in the ischemic zone and post-systolic shortening due to tethering effects from adjacent normal myocardium.

## 7.7. PV DIAGRAM ON LA FUNCTION

**Left atrial function can be characterized using pressure-volume analysis, revealing its reservoir, conduit, and contractile properties.**

* Pressure-volume analysis can be applied to LA function
* Steady-state LA pressure-volume diagram shows "infinity" pattern with active "A" and passive "V" loops
* LA cycle:
  * LA end diastole (ED) → LA ejection (pressure increases while volume decreases) until end systole (ES, minimum LA volume) - graph proceeds in anticlockwise fashion
  * ED and ES determine LA emptying fraction (EF)
  * Following MV closure, LA fills throughout LV systole and isovolumic relaxation
  * LA pressure and volume gradually increase during reservoir phase (bottom part of "A" loop and upper part of "V" loop, clockwise direction)
  * Area of "A" loop represents active LA stroke work
  * "V loop" represents passive energy stored during reservoir phase - index of reservoir function
  * Slope between minimum LA pressure of "A" loop and maximum LA pressure in "V" loop indexes LA compliance
  * Can be affected by regional myocardial ischemia or severe LV dysfunction increasing slope
  * After MV opens, LA empties causing rapid decline in LA volume and pressure (bottom part of "V" loop)
  * Pulmonary venous return enters LA during LV diastasis without altering LA volume (mitral valve open)
* LA volume measurements:
  * Total LA reservoir volume = difference between minimum and maximum LA volumes
  * LA conduit phase between MV opening and LA end diastole
  * LA conduit volume = difference between maximum and end-diastolic volumes
  * Interrelationship among LA loading conditions, contractile state, relaxation rate/extent, elastic properties, and pulmonary venous blood flow determine relative areas of "A" and "V" loops and their intersection point

**Bottom Line:** Left atrial pressure-volume analysis reveals an "infinity" pattern with distinct active ("A" loop) and passive ("V" loop) components reflecting contractile and reservoir functions, respectively. # 8. DETERMINANTS OF MYOCARDIAL O2 CONSUMPTION

**Myocardial oxygen consumption is determined by key hemodynamic parameters and cardiac work.**

### 8.1.1. COMPONENTS OF MYOCARDIAL WORK

* Myocardial O₂ Consumption (MVO₂) is determined by total cardiac work
* Total cardiac work comprises:
  * **External work**: Work of ejecting blood from the heart (also known as "Stroke Work")
  * **Internal work**: Everything outside of systolic ejection (i.e., maintaining wall tension) (PE)

### 8.1.2. PRIMARY DETERMINANTS OF MVO₂

* **Heart rate**: Most important determinant
* **Preload**: Minor contributor
* **Afterload**: Significant contributor, especially in hypertension
* **Contractility**: Increases with inotropic stimulation
* **Basal metabolic rate**: Relatively constant

### 8.1.3. CLINICAL SURROGATES FOR MVO₂

* **Stroke Work** = SV × MAP
  * Direct measure of external cardiac work
* **Rate Pressure Product (RPP)** = HR × SBP
  * Most commonly used clinical indicator
  * Strong correlation with actual measured MVO₂
  * 💡 Useful for estimating cardiac stress during exercise testing and evaluating risk in patients with coronary artery disease [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Myocardial oxygen consumption is primarily determined by heart rate, afterload, and contractility, with preload contributing to a lesser extent. The rate-pressure product (HR × SBP) serves as a useful clinical surrogate for estimating myocardial oxygen demand, especially in patients with coronary artery disease where balancing oxygen supply and demand is critical.

# 9. DETERMINANTS OF CARDIAC PERFORMANCE





> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|Part A: Cardiac Performance]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#2.1 Clinical Decision-Making and Procedures|Part C]]

**Cardiac performance depends on multiple interrelated physiological determinants.**

### 9.1.1. OVERVIEW OF CARDIAC PERFORMANCE DETERMINANTS

* **Systolic function** depends on:
  * Intrinsic contractile properties of the LV myocardium
  * Preload
  * Afterload
  * Heart rate and rhythm determine the volume of blood pumped per minute (assuming unimpeded venous return)
  * LV structural integrity affects effective forward flow (valvular disease/abnormal intracardiac flow i.e., VSD)
* **Diastolic function** depends on:
  * Pulmonary venous blood flow
  * LA function
  * Mitral valve dynamics
  * Pericardial restraint
  * Active (relaxation) and passive (compliance) properties of the LV
  * 💡 LV diastolic function is normal when these factors provide sufficient CO to satisfy cellular metabolism without elevated LV end-diastolic pressure (approximately 10 mmHg)
  * ⚠️ LA or MV dysfunction, delayed LV relaxation, reduced compliance, or increased pericardial pressure will restrict LV filling unless LV filling pressures increase
  * LV diastolic dysfunction causes higher pulmonary venous, LA, and LV end-diastolic pressures

![image.png](CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_image_29.png_b6ed64.png_97a4a6.png_0e90_.png)

**Bottom Line:** Cardiac performance depends on a complex interplay of systolic and diastolic determinants. Systolic function is determined by contractility, preload, afterload, and heart rate, while diastolic function depends on ventricular relaxation, compliance, atrial function, and pericardial restraint. Either systolic or diastolic dysfunction can lead to clinical heart failure.

## 9.2. HEART RATE

**Heart rate significantly impacts myocardial contractility and cardiac output.**

### 9.2.1. FORCE-FREQUENCY RELATIONSHIP

* Increase in stimulation frequency of isolated cardiac muscles produces parallel increase in myocardial contractility
* Known as "treppe"/"Bowditch"/"staircase" phenomenon or "force-frequency" relationship
* Maximum contractile force occurs at 150-180 stimulations per minute during isometric contraction of isolated cardiac muscle
* Above this rate, intracellular processes for diastolic Ca²⁺ removal from contractile apparatus have insufficient time to occur

### 9.2.2. CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE

* "Treppe" phenomenon is an important compensatory mechanism that maintains CO when LV filling is restricted (i.e., cardiac tamponade)
* Also seen with prolonged delay between beats (AV conduction abnormality) or after LV extrasystole - termed "interval-strength" effect
* Temporal increase in available Ca²⁺ for contractile activation and increased preload from greater diastolic filling are responsible
* 💡 Pressure or volume overload hypertrophy decreases the stimulation rate at which peak "treppe" effect occurs
* This effect is abolished in failing myocardium [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bowditch effect (Treppe effect)**:
* Intrinsic autoregulatory phenomenon where tachycardia leads to increased myocardial contractility
* Mechanism:
  * As HR increases, cardiac cycle shortens (diastolic interval reduced more than systolic)
  * Increased systolic Ca²⁺ influx through L-type Ca²⁺ channels
  * Na⁺ efflux via Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase cannot keep pace with systolic Na⁺ influx
  * Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger cannot effectively reduce intracellular Ca²⁺ due to elevated Na⁺
  * Accumulation of intracellular Ca²⁺ leads to positive inotropic effect
  * Similar mechanism seen with digoxin therapy (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase blockade)

**Bottom Line:** Heart rate directly affects myocardial contractility through the force-frequency relationship (Bowditch phenomenon), with increasing heart rate enhancing contractility up to 150-180 beats/minute. This serves as an important compensatory mechanism when ventricular filling is compromised, though this effect is diminished in hypertrophied hearts and abolished in heart failure.

## 9.3. PRELOAD

**Preload refers to the initial myocardial fiber length before contraction and is a primary determinant of stroke volume.**

### 9.3.1. DEFINITION AND MEASUREMENT

* **Preload** (conventional definition): Sarcomere length immediately before onset of myocyte contraction
* Alternative: Degree of muscle stretch applied to isolated cardiac muscle before stimulation
* Clinical definition: Volume of blood contained within each chamber at end diastole

### 9.3.2. MEASUREMENT CHALLENGES

* Precise real-time measurement of continuous LV volume throughout cardiac cycle (including LV end-diastolic volume, LV Ved) remains technically challenging
* **Experimental methods**:
  * Sonomicrometry, conductance catheter (very precise but invasive)
* **Noninvasive methods**:
  * Radionuclide angiography
  * Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)
  * Limited utility in operating room or intensive care unit
  * 2D/3D TEE can be applied (transgastric LV midpapillary short axis view to measure LV end-diastolic area or diameter as surrogate marker)

### 9.3.3. PRESSURE ESTIMATES OF PRELOAD

* LV preload may be estimated by LV end-diastolic pressure (EDP) measurement:
  * Invasive measurement in cardiac catheterization laboratory or during surgery
  * Fluid-filled or pressure transducer–tipped catheter advanced from aorta across aortic valve or through LA across mitral valve
  * LV EDP related to Ved based on exponential EDPVR
* Indirect estimates from "upstream" measurements:
  * Mean LA pressure
  * Pulmonary capillary occlusion pressure
  * Pulmonary arterial diastolic pressure
  * RA (central venous) pressure
  * ⚠️ These estimates are affected by functional integrity of structures separating each measurement location from LV [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * A correlation between RA and LV EDP assumes unimpeded fluid column between RA and LV (reasonable in healthy subjects but not in patients with significant pulmonary/cardiac disease)

**Bottom Line:** Preload describes the initial stretch of cardiac myocytes before contraction, clinically assessed as end-diastolic volume or pressure. While direct measurement is challenging, various indirect measures like pulmonary capillary wedge pressure or central venous pressure are used clinically, though their accuracy depends on the integrity of the cardiovascular structures between the measurement site and the ventricle of interest.

## 9.4. AFTERLOAD

**Afterload refers to the force opposing ventricular ejection and significantly impacts cardiac performance.**

### 9.4.1. DEFINITION AND CONCEPTUALIZATION

* The definition of afterload is clear in isolated cardiac muscle but more difficult to conceptualize and measure in intact cardiovascular system
* Several methods used to quantify afterload

![image.png](CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_image_31.png_20a360.png_564133.png_5e7c_.png)

### 9.4.2. AORTIC INPUT IMPEDANCE

* **Aortic input impedance [Zin(ω)]**: Ratio of aortic pressure (forces acting on blood) to blood flow (resultant motion)
* Derived from power spectral or Fourier series analysis of high-fidelity aortic pressure and flow measurements
* Incorporates arterial viscoelasticity, frequency dependence, and wave reflection
* Characterized by modulus and phase angle spectra in frequency domain
* Interpreted using electrical three-element Windkessel model - useful but limited practical applicability
  * Three components:
	* **Characteristic aortic impedance (Zc)**: Resistance to LV ejection from aorta and great vessels
	* **Total arterial compliance (C)**: Determined by compliance of proximal large arterial vasculature (energy storage component)
	* **Total arterial resistance (R)**: Combined resistances of remaining arterial circulation
  * Magnitude of Zin(ω) largely determined by total arterial resistance (property of terminal arterioles)
  * Approximated by systemic vascular resistance (SVR): (MAP-RAP)80/CO
	* MAP and RAP: Mean arterial and right atrial pressures
	* CO: Cardiac output
	* 80: Constant converting mmHg/min/L to dynes·sec·cm⁻⁵

![image.png](CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_image_32.png_fbb306.png_b4573a.png_f375_.png)

### 9.4.3. WALL STRESS APPROACH

* Forces to which LV is subjected during ejection can define LV afterload
* During systole:
  * LV pressure and wall thickness increase during isovolumic contraction
  * Large decrease in LV volume after aortic valve opens
  * Dramatic increase in LV systolic wall stress, maximizing during early LV ejection then declining
  * ⚠️ Peak LV systolic wall stress is major stimulus for pressure-overload hypertrophy in poorly controlled hypertension and aortic valve stenosis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * At end systole, forces driving further ejection and those resisting it are equal
  * LV end-systolic wall stress is a determinant of stroke volume
  * Assumptions about LV geometry, nonlinear force distribution between subendocardium and subepicardium, and nonuniform wall thickness complicate use of LV end-systolic wall stress as quantitative afterload index

![image.png](CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_image_33.png_049897.png_e2c931.png_61cb_.png)

### 9.4.4. VENTRICULAR-ARTERIAL COUPLING

* From mechanical systems perspective:
  * Optimal energy transfer from LV to arterial circulation during ejection requires coupled elastic chambers
  * LV-arterial coupling described with series elastic chamber model:
	* LV elastance (Ees) and effective arterial elastance (Ea) in pressure–volume plane
	* Using slopes of LV ESPVR (LV Ees) and aortic end-systolic pressure–stroke volume relation (Ea)
  * Ratio of Ees to Ea defines coupling between LV and arterial circulation
  * Specifies SV that may be transferred between them
  * Provides foundation for studying myocardial energetics and mechanical efficiency

![image.png](CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_image_34.png_1ea8d3.png_2cf05a.png_52b5_.png)

### 9.4.5. LIMITATIONS OF SVR AS AFTERLOAD MEASURE

* SVR is not a quantitative measure of LV afterload because it:
  * Ignores mechanical characteristics of blood (viscosity, density) and arterial walls (compliance)
  * Fails to incorporate arterial wave reflection
  * Does not consider frequency-dependent, phasic nature of arterial pressure and flow (assumes constant flow)
  * Phasic contributions to arterial load especially important in elderly patients or with peripheral vascular atherosclerosis
  * SVR should be used as nonparametric estimate, not to quantify LV afterload changes from vasoactive drugs or cardiovascular disease

### 9.4.6. COMPONENTS OF AFTERLOAD IN INTACT SYSTEM

Four components mediate LV afterload in intact cardiovascular system:

1. Physical properties of arterial blood vessels (diameter, length, elasticity, branches)
2. LV wall stress (determined by LV pressure development and geometric changes)
3. Total arterial resistance (determined primarily by arteriolar smooth muscle tone)
4. Volume and physical properties of blood (rheology, viscosity, density)

### 9.4.7. CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS

* Acute increase in LV afterload well tolerated in healthy individuals
* In heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF):
  * LV systolic dysfunction → Sympathetic nervous system response → increase in LV afterload → further decrease in CO
  * ⚠️ Decreasing LV afterload by reducing inciting stress is the most important objective in clinical management of HFrEF [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* RV afterload similar to LV but with two major differences:
  * Pulmonary arterial vasculature more compliant than systemic arterial counterpart
  * RV more sensitive to acute afterload changes than LV

**Bottom Line:** Afterload represents the forces opposing ventricular ejection and can be conceptualized through multiple approaches including aortic impedance, ventricular wall stress, and ventricular-arterial coupling. While SVR is commonly used clinically, it has significant limitations as a true measure of afterload. Afterload reduction is particularly important in the management of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction.

## 9.5. MYOCARDIAL CONTRACTILITY

**Myocardial contractility refers to the intrinsic ability of heart muscle to generate force and is a key determinant of cardiac function.**

### 9.5.1. CONTRACTILITY ASSESSMENT

* A "gold standard" of myocardial contractility has not been developed
* All contractile indices have substantial limitations as inotropic state and loading conditions are inseparably related at sarcomere level
* Indices classified into three categories based on:
  * Pressure–volume relations
  * Isovolumic contraction
  * Ejection phase

![image.png](CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_image_35.png_64672e.png_52fd9a.png_717b_.png)

### 9.5.2. PRESSURE-VOLUME ANALYSIS

* **End-systolic elastance (Ees)** and **preload recruitable stroke work (Msw)**:
  * Not routinely used clinically (require invasive LV pressure-volume measurements and extensive offline analysis)
  * Useful conceptual tools for understanding contractility in intact heart
* **Load independence assessment**:
  * Intervention that acutely changes loading conditions without affecting contractility (e.g., transient caval occlusion to reduce preload or phenylephrine to increase afterload) generates family of loops
  * End-systolic points in loop series conform to linear pressure-volume relationship defining ESPVR
  * End-systolic elastance (Ees) defines ESPVR slope - load-independent contractility index
  * End-diastolic points define nonlinear end-diastolic relationship (EDPVR)
  * With constant contractile state and afterload, progressive preload reduction shifts loops toward lower volumes at end-systole and end-diastole, decreasing stroke volume
  * Selective afterload increase causes narrowing and elongation of loops, decreasing stroke volume
  * ESPVR responds to acute myocardial contractility changes:
	* Increased Ees slope indicates positive inotropic intervention
	* Decreased Ees indicates negative inotropic intervention

![image.png](CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_image_37.png_703f91.png_2a4c4d.png_d80a_.png)

* **Time-varying elastance**:
  * Ratio of continuous LV pressure-to-volume during cardiac cycle
  * [E(t)], such that E(t) = P(t)/[V(t) − V₀], where:
	* P(t) and V(t) are time-dependent changes in LV pressure and volume
	* V₀ is LV volume at 0 mmHg of LV pressure
  * Maximum LV elastance (Emax) occurs at or very close to upper left corner of steady-state LV pressure–volume diagram
  * Nested set of LV pressure–volume diagrams generated by acute loading changes; each loop has distinct Emax
  * These Emax values are linearly related and establish ESPVR
  * ESPVR slope ("end-systolic elastance"; Ees) is afterload-insensitive contractility index as analysis is performed at end systole
  * 💡 Alterations in contractile state reflected in Ees changes - e.g., dobutamine increases Ees, quantifying positive inotropic effect [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

![image.png](CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_image_38.png_e0bdce.png_ad884b.png_f296_.png)

### 9.5.3. ISOVOLUMIC CONTRACTION INDEX

* **dP/dt max**:
  * Maximal rate of change of ventricular pressure pulse
  * Can be determined noninvasively by echocardiography
  * Normal LV values ~1000 mmHg/sec
  * RV values ~400 mmHg/sec
  * Difference attributed to peak dP/dt occurring when semilunar valves open and developed pressure substantially higher in LV than RV (not due to contractile state difference)
  * Disadvantages: Distortion by wall properties and valvular dysfunction, dependence on loading conditions and heart rate
  * To correct for load dependency, ratio of dP/dt to developed pressure or standard pressure has been used

### 9.5.4. EJECTION PHASE INDEX

* **Ejection Fraction**:
  * Determined by invasive/noninvasive techniques:
	* Image-based volume measurements (echocardiography, angiography, MRI, PET)
	* Indicator dilution techniques
  * Provides useful systolic pump performance information
  * Heavily influenced by afterload [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Preload Recruitable Stroke Work (PRSW)**:
  * Derived from LV pressure–volume diagrams based on Frank–Starling principle relating preload to CO
  * Area of each LV pressure–volume diagram (stroke work; SW) and corresponding Ved are linearly related:
	* SW = Msw(Ved − Vsw)
	* Msw and Vsw are slope and volume intercept of relation
  * Slope of "preload recruitable stroke work" relation quantifies contractility changes in relatively load-independent manner

![image.png](CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_image_39.png_efe30f.png_032286.png_227c_.png)

* **PRSW Measurement**:
  * Venous return decreased by inferior vena cava occlusion → progressive reduction in ventricular end-diastolic volume
  * Pressure-volume loop area (external work) plotted against end-diastolic volume for each beat
  * Slope defines work ventricle can perform for given preload - reflects contractility
  * Increased slope indicates increased contractility; decreased slope indicates decreased contractility
  * Advantage: Provides overall ventricular performance index combining systolic and diastolic components
  * Disadvantage: Not practical in clinical environment

**Bottom Line:** Myocardial contractility can be assessed through various methods including pressure-volume analysis (end-systolic elastance, preload recruitable stroke work), isovolumic contraction indices (dP/dt max), and ejection phase measurements (ejection fraction). While each method has limitations, pressure-volume based indices provide the most load-independent assessment of contractility but require specialized invasive measurements rarely used in routine clinical practice.

# 10. CARDIAC OUTPUT AND ITS MEASUREMENT





> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|Part A: Cardiac Output Measurement]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#2.1 Clinical Decision-Making and Procedures|Part C]]

**Various techniques are available for measuring cardiac output in clinical practice.**

### 10.1.1. DETERMINANTS OF CARDIAC OUTPUT 

**The cardiac output (CO) is the volume of blood ejected by the left ventricle (LV) or right ventricle (RV) per minute.**

* CO is directly dependent on two factors:
  * Heart rate (HR)
  * Stroke volume (SV)
  * CO = SV × HR
  * Typical resting CO: 70 mL × 75 bpm = 5.25 L/min
  * CO may increase fivefold during maximal exercise
* SV is determined by three factors:
  * Preload: The intraluminal pressure that stretches the ventricle to its end-diastolic dimensions
  * Myocardial contractility: The intrinsic ability of cardiac myocytes to generate mechanical power
  * Afterload: The stress developed in the ventricular wall during ejection

### 10.1.2. REGULATION OF CARDIAC OUTPUT 

* CO varies with changing body requirements:
  * Age: Children have proportionately greater CO relative to body surface area
  * Exercise: Up to fivefold increase in normal individuals
  * Pregnancy: Up to 50% increase at term
  * Eating: ~25% increase
* Heart rate regulation:
  * Denervated heart: Basal HR of 100-120 bpm
  * Parasympathetic tone: Reduces resting HR to 60-70 bpm via vagal acetylcholine release
  * Sympathetic stimulation: Increases HR through norepinephrine release and β1-adrenoceptor activation
* Preload regulation:
  * Venous return as main determinant
  * Starling's law ensuring matched output between RV and LV
* Contractility regulation:
  * Sympathetic nervous system (main regulator)
  * Bowditch effect (HR-dependent contractility increase)
  * Pharmacological agents (positive inotropes: dobutamine, negative inotropes: β-blockers)
  * Disease states (reduced contractility in sepsis, ischemia, electrolyte disturbances)
* Afterload regulation:
  * Governed by systemic vascular resistance
  * Compensatory mechanisms include Anrep effect (intrinsic increase in inotropy with increased afterload)

### 10.1.3. CARDIAC INDEX AND OTHER STANDARDIZED MEASUREMENTS 

* **Cardiac index (CI)**: CO standardized based on body surface area (BSA)
  * CI = CO/BSA
  * Normal resting CI: 3.0-3.5 L·min⁻¹·m⁻²
* **Stroke volume index (SVI)**: SV standardized based on BSA
  * SVI = SV/BSA
  * Normal resting SVI: 33-47 mL·m⁻²·beat⁻¹

### 10.1.4. RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN MAP AND CO 

* Mean arterial pressure (MAP) related to CO by:
  * MAP ≈ CO × SVR
  * SVR dependent primarily on arteriolar radius and (to lesser extent) blood viscosity
* Factors affecting MAP at constant CO:
  * Increased by vasoconstriction or increased blood viscosity
  * Decreased by vasodilation or decreased blood viscosity

### 10.1.5. METHODS OF CO MEASUREMENT 

**Classifications of CO measurement methods**:

* **Invasive methods**:
  * Using pulmonary artery catheter (PAC)
  * Using central venous catheter
  * Using arterial line
  
* **Minimally invasive methods**:
  * Ultrasound techniques utilizing proximity of esophagus to heart and great vessels
  
* **Non-invasive methods**:
  * Trans-thoracic Doppler echocardiography
  * Trans-thoracic electrical bioimpedance
  * Magnetic resonance imaging

## 10.2. THERMODILUTION METHOD

**The thermodilution method involves detecting temperature changes from a cold indicator to calculate cardiac output.**

### 10.2.1. HISTORICAL DEVELOPMENT

* First described by George Neil Stewart in late 19th century:
  * Injected sodium chloride solution into central vein
  * Detected in contralateral femoral artery
  * Change in blood concentration proportional to cardiac output
* Later modified by Hamilton:
  * Promoted explicit time-concentration curve concept
  * Cardiac output calculated as amount of indicator injected divided by area of dilution curve
* Indocyanine green was conventional indicator until 1970s

**Bottom Line:** The thermodilution method has evolved from early indicator dilution techniques to become a standard approach for cardiac output measurement in clinical practice, utilizing temperature change rather than dye concentration for measurement.

## 10.3. PULMONARY ARTERY CATHETER AND CARDIAC FUNCTION CURVES

**The pulmonary artery catheter enables both cardiac output measurement and assessment of filling pressures.**

### 10.3.1. TECHNIQUE AND PRINCIPLES

* **Pulmonary Artery Catheter (PAC)** allows:
  * Measurement of CO using Stewart-Hamilton thermodilution curve
  * Measurement of pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (LV preload surrogate)
* **Measurement procedure**:
  * Injection of indicator (usually 10 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride) with known temperature into central vein
  * Detection of blood temperature change downstream at PAC tip

### 10.3.2. VALIDITY REQUIREMENTS

1. **Pressure transmission**:
   * Pressure from LA (average equal to LV EDP) must be reflected back through pulmonary circulation
   * PAC tip must be wedged in small pulmonary vessel preventing blood flow beyond it
   * ⚠️ Mitral valve should not have significant stenosis or regurgitation

2. **Compliance considerations**:
   * Changes in ventricular diastolic pressure-volume relationship (compliance) must be considered
   * Stiff/noncompliant ventricle shows impaired filling and exaggerated pressure increase
   * May erroneously imply increased preload when muscle stretch is unaltered or reduced
   * Stiff ventricle characteristic of various pathologies:
	 * Myocardial ischemia
	 * Healing/healed myocardial infarction
	 * Myocardial hypertrophy
	 * Constrictive pericarditis

3. **Constant conditions**:
   * Afterload and heart rate must be constant when using multiple cardiac function curves to assess contractility changes via stroke volume at given preload

4. **Valve competence**:
   * ⚠️ Tricuspid valve cannot have significant regurgitation
   * Causes variation in indicator transit time
   * Leads to both underestimates and overestimates of cardiac output

**Bottom Line:** Pulmonary artery catheters provide valuable hemodynamic data including cardiac output and filling pressures, but interpretation depends on several factors including proper catheter positioning, consideration of ventricular compliance, and valvular competence. Stiff ventricles or valvular pathology can significantly affect the accuracy of measurements.

## 10.4. TRANSPULMONARY THERMODILUTION METHOD

**Transpulmonary thermodilution measures global cardiac parameters using a central venous and arterial catheter system.**

### 10.4.1. TECHNIQUE

* Cold saline injected into central vein
* Temperature change detected with thermistor-tipped catheter in central artery (commonly femoral or axillary artery)
* Measures LV rather than RV cardiac output

### 10.4.2. DERIVED PARAMETERS

* **Global end-diastolic volume (GEDV)**:
  * Volume of blood present in four heart chambers
  * Better preload estimate than pulmonary artery occlusion pressure
  * Actual volume rather than pressure surrogate
  * Avoids erroneous conclusions from high LV filling pressure in stiff noncompliant ventricle
* **Extravascular lung water**:
  * Calculated from pulmonary blood volume
  * Provides early index of pulmonary edema and fluid overload in critically ill patients

![image.png](CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_image_40.png_384d67.png_a31bf6.png_d3b0_.png)

* Upper row: Intrathoracic thermal volume (ITTV) is complete volume of distribution of thermal indicator:
  * Encompasses right atrium (RAEDV), right ventricle (RVEDV), left atrium (LAEDV), left ventricle end-diastolic (LVEDV) volumes, pulmonary blood volume (PBV), and extravascular lung water (EVLW)
  * Calculated by multiplying cardiac output (FT) with mean transit time (MTT) of indicator
* Middle row: Pulmonary thermal volume (PTV):
  * Largest mixing chamber of system
  * Includes PBV and EVLW
  * Calculated by multiplying cardiac output (FT) with exponential decay time (EDT) of indicator
* Bottom row: Derived calculations:
  * GEDV = ITTV - PTV
  * Intrathoracic blood volume (ITBV) = GEDV × 1.25
  * EVLW = ITTV - ITBV

**Bottom Line:** Transpulmonary thermodilution provides valuable hemodynamic data beyond simple cardiac output measurement, including global end-diastolic volume (a volume-based preload measure) and extravascular lung water (an early indicator of pulmonary edema), making it particularly useful for fluid management in critically ill patients.

## 10.5. FICK METHOD

**The Fick method calculates cardiac output based on oxygen consumption and arteriovenous oxygen difference.**

### 10.5.1. PRINCIPLE AND FORMULA

* **Fick CO (L/min)** = (oxygen consumption VO₂)/[(arteriovenous O₂ difference) × 10]
* **VO₂ estimation options**:
  * Based on nomogram accounting for patient age, sex, height, and weight
  * Directly measured by exhaled breath analysis
  * Standard reference value of 250 mL oxygen per minute often used
* **Arteriovenous O₂ difference calculation**:

![image.png](CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_image_41.png_71e530.png_80e2da.png_eb7e_.png)

* Where:
  * SaO₂ is arterial oxygen saturation
  * SvO₂ is mixed venous oxygen saturation (pulmonary arterial blood)

**Bottom Line:** The Fick method calculates cardiac output by measuring oxygen consumption and the arteriovenous oxygen difference, based on the principle that the amount of oxygen consumed equals the product of cardiac output and the difference in oxygen content between arterial and venous blood. This technique can be valuable when thermodilution methods are unavailable or unreliable.

## 10.6. ECHOCARDIOGRAPHY

**Echocardiography provides non-invasive assessment of cardiac output using Doppler measurements.**

### 10.6.1. TECHNIQUE

* **Pulsed wave Doppler method**:
  * Record velocity profile of flow in the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT)
  * Trace profile to obtain velocity time integral (VTI) in centimeters
  * Multiply LVOT VTI by LVOT area to obtain stroke volume in milliliters:
	* LVOT assumed circular
	* Area = πr² (LVOT diameter measured with 2D echo and divided by two for radius)
  * **SV = LVOT VTI × LVOT area**
  * **CO = SV × HR**

### 10.6.2. DERIVED RESISTANCE CALCULATIONS

* Once CO is measured, SVR and PVR can be derived:

![image.png](CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_image_43.png_907f18.png_4fea58.png_4896_.png)

* SVR and PVR multiplied by 80 to convert to dynes·s·cm⁻⁵
* Pulmonary artery occlusion pressure (PAOP) serves as surrogate for left atrial pressure [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Echocardiography provides a non-invasive method for cardiac output measurement using Doppler assessment of flow through the left ventricular outflow tract. This approach is particularly valuable when invasive monitoring is unavailable or contraindicated, though accuracy depends on proper measurement technique and alignment of the Doppler beam with blood flow.

### 10.6.3. ADDITIONAL CO MEASUREMENT METHODS 

* **Methods based on Fick principle**:
  * **Direct Fick method**: Uses O₂ as the marker substance, measuring O₂ consumption and arteriovenous O₂ difference
  * **Dye dilution method**: Known amount of indicator dye injected and concentration sampled at arterial line
  * **Thermodilution method**: Cold saline injected through PAC, temperature change measured by thermistor
* **Methods based on pulse contour analysis**:
  * Analyze morphology of arterial pressure waveform to derive SV and CO
  * **Calibrated systems**:
	* PiCCO: Uses central line and thermistor-tipped arterial line
	* LiDCO: Uses arterial line calibrated with lithium dilution
  * **Uncalibrated systems**:
	* FloTrac/Vigileo: Uses specialized pressure sensor with demographic-based calibration
	* LiDCOrapid: Uses pulse power analysis algorithm with nomogram calibration
* **Minimally invasive methods**:
  * **Oesophageal Doppler**: Measures descending aortic blood flow using ultrasound
  * **Trans-oesophageal echocardiography (TOE)**: Provides detailed cardiac imaging and Doppler-based flow measurement

**Bottom Line:** Various methods exist for measuring cardiac output, each with advantages and limitations. Invasive methods offer greater accuracy but carry risk of complications, while less invasive techniques may provide reasonable estimates with fewer risks. The choice of monitoring technique should consider the clinical context, available resources, and risk-benefit profile for the individual patient.

**Diastolic function encompasses a complex sequence of events that determine ventricular filling.**

### 11.1.1. OVERVIEW

* LV diastole involves a complicated sequence of temporally related, heterogeneous events

![image.png](CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_image_44.png_bcbec5.png_33647d.png_dac3_.png)

**Bottom Line:** Diastolic function encompasses a complex sequence of events including myocardial relaxation, passive ventricular filling properties, atrial contraction, and pericardial constraints. These components must function in concert to achieve optimal ventricular filling at normal pressures.

## 11.2. RELAXATION

**Myocardial relaxation is an energy-dependent process crucial for early ventricular filling.**

### 11.2.1. CHARACTERISTICS AND MECHANISMS

* Relaxation is an energy-dependent process requiring active removal of Ca²⁺ from myoplasm
* "Active elasticity" occurs when actin–myosin cross bridges fail to dissociate due to:
  * Inadequate energy supply
  * Abnormal intracellular Ca²⁺ homeostasis

### 11.2.2. CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS

* **Delayed relaxation effects**:
  * Blunts early LV filling
  * Makes LV filling more dependent on LA contraction
  * ⚠️ If LA contraction is lost (e.g., atrial tachyarrhythmia) → acute heart failure [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * May reduce LV compliance (upward-leftward shift of EDPVR) when caused by hypoxemia or regional myocardial ischemia in large perfusion territory
  * Compromises coronary blood flow by prolonging extravascular compression of subendocardial arterioles

### 11.2.3. QUANTIFICATION METHODS

* Using rate of LV pressure decrease to quantify relaxation is unreliable as this parameter highly depends on LV end-systolic pressure magnitude
* **Time constant (τ) method**:
  * LV pressure decline during relaxation follows exponential time course
  * Quantified using equation: P(t) = P(0)^(e⁻ᵗ/τ)
	* P(t) is time-dependent LV pressure
	* P₀ is LV pressure at end systole
	* e is natural exponent
	* t is time (ms) after LV end systole
  * **Model limitations**:
	* Assumes LV pressure falls to 0mmHg
	* In reality, LV pressure may drop to sub-atmospheric pressure during marked hypovolemia or exercise
  * **Clinical applications**:
	* Increases in τ signify delayed LV relaxation in:
	  * Acute myocardial ischemia
	  * Pressure-overload hypertrophy
	  * Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
	  * Negative inotropic drugs (e.g., volatile anesthetics) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Reductions in τ indicate more rapid LV relaxation during:
	  * Tachycardia
	  * Sympathetic nervous system stimulation
	  * Positive inotropic medications (e.g., dobutamine, milrinone) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Isovolumic relaxation time (IVRT)**:
  * Most commonly used noninvasive index of LV relaxation
  * Duration between aortic valve closure and mitral valve opening
  * Usually measured with Doppler echo - interval between cessation of aortic blood flow and onset of transmitral blood flow
  * Without MV or AV disease, rate of LV relaxation and difference between LV ESP and LA pressure at MV opening are major IVRT determinants

**Bottom Line:** Myocardial relaxation is an active, energy-dependent process crucial for early ventricular filling. Delayed relaxation impairs early filling, increases dependence on atrial contraction, and compromises coronary perfusion. It can be quantified using the relaxation time constant (τ) or isovolumic relaxation time (IVRT), with changes reflecting alterations in myocardial function or loading conditions.

## 11.3. LV FILLING

**Left ventricular filling patterns reflect the complex interaction between relaxation, compliance, and atrial function.**

### 11.3.1. ASSESSMENT METHODS

* Regardless of method used to obtain continuous LV volume signal, its first derivative with respect to time (dV/dt) produces a biphasic waveform
* Characterized by peaks corresponding to early LV filling and LA systole ("E" and "A" waves, respectively)
* dV/dt waveform is the basis of transmitral blood flow profile obtained using pulse wave Doppler echocardiography and mitral annulus motion pattern in tissue Doppler imaging

### 11.3.2. PARAMETERS DERIVED

From the dV/dt waveform, other filling parameters may be determined:

* E and A wave peak filling rates
* Deceleration rate and half-time of E wave
* E/A ratio
* Areas obtained by integration of E and A waves (corresponding to early LV filling and LA systole blood volumes)
* Ratio of early LV filling to total LV end-diastolic volumes (percentage of early LV filling)
* Time intervals of these events

**Bottom Line:** Left ventricular filling can be characterized by the early filling (E) and atrial contraction (A) waves. The relationship between these waves provides valuable information about diastolic function, with alterations in their magnitude, timing, and ratio reflecting changes in relaxation, compliance, and atrial function.

## 11.4. COMPLIANCE

**Ventricular compliance determines the pressure-volume relationship during filling and significantly impacts diastolic function.**

### 11.4.1. ASSESSMENT METHODS

* From a series of differentially loaded LV pressure-volume diagrams, the EDPVR describes LV compliance
* Relationship between end-diastolic pressure (Ped) and Ved is exponential: Ped = Ae^(KVed) + B
  * K is modulus of chamber stiffness (end-diastolic elastance)
  * Increase in K (e.g., pressure overload hypertrophy) → LV chamber stiffer → higher LV Ped required for normal Ved [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * A and B are curve-fitting constants

### 11.4.2. MATERIAL PROPERTIES ASSESSMENT

* **Hooke's law** describes elastic behavior of myocardium:
  * Develops resisting force (stress; σ) as length (strain; ε) increases
  * Strain defined as percent change in muscle length (L) from unstressed muscle length (L₀; estimated at LV pressure of 0 mmHg)
  * Lagrangian [ε = (L−L₀)/L₀] or natural (ε = L/L₀) strain used to normalize muscle length
  * Stress–strain relation is exponential: σ = α(eᵝᵉ−1)
	* α is coefficient of gain
	* β is modulus of myocardial stiffness
  * Shift of stress–strain relationship up and left consistent with increased β
  * Known to occur during:
	* Acute myocardial ischemia
	* Pressure-overload hypertrophy
	* Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Ventricular compliance describes the pressure-volume relationship during filling and is determined by both chamber geometry and myocardial material properties. Reduced compliance (increased stiffness) requires higher filling pressures for adequate ventricular filling and is a key component of diastolic dysfunction. Compliance assessment requires analysis of the end-diastolic pressure-volume relationship or myocardial stress-strain characteristics.

# 12. PERICARDIAL RESTRAINT





> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|Part A: Pericardial Physiology]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#2.1 Clinical Decision-Making and Procedures|Part C]]

**The pericardium provides external constraint to cardiac filling and influences chamber interactions.**

### 12.1.1. PERICARDIAL STRUCTURE AND FUNCTION

* Smooth surface of visceral pericardium, surfactant phospholipids, and pericardial fluid (15-35 mL) facilitate cardiac movement
* Pericardial fluid composition:
  * Plasma ultrafiltrate
  * Myocardial interstitial fluid
  * Small quantity of lymph
* Fibrous layer of parietal pericardium determines "J"-shaped pericardial pressure–volume relationship
  * Much less compliant than LV myocardium - very limited volume reserve without large pressure increase
* Pericardial pressure:
  * Normally subatmospheric (between −5 and 0 mmHg)
  * Varies with changes in intrathoracic pressure
  * Negligible effect in normal heart under euvolemic conditions

### 12.1.2. CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE

* **Restraining force**:
  * Most apparent in thinner-walled atria and RV - major determinant of diastolic pressure-volume relationship of these chambers
  * Affects LV filling - acute increase in pericardial pressure → upward shift of LV EDPVR + ventricular interdependence → severely restricted LV filling
  * Essential role in ventricular interdependence (influence of pressure and volume of one ventricle on the other)
	* Restrains both LV and RV equally
	* Increase in RV size → increases pericardial pressure → reduces LV compliance → restricts LV filling
	* LV distention encroaches on RV, shifts its EDPVR up and left, and limits RV filling
* **Respiratory Effects**:
  * Inspiration decreases intrathoracic pressure, enhances venous return, and causes modest RV dilation
  * These actions mildly reduce LV filling by decreasing compliance, resulting in small declines in SV, CO, and mean arterial pressure
  * During expiration, RV filling is attenuated, and LV filling is augmented
  * In positive pressure ventilation - opposite effect occurs (because inspiration is positive pressure)
  * ⚠️ Compression of ventricular chambers (i.e., tamponade or constrictive pericarditis) → exaggerates respiratory changes in RV and LV filling [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * 💡 Maintenance of spontaneous ventilation is important - positive pressure ventilation markedly limits venous return and may cause cardiovascular collapse [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Post-pericardiectomy Effects**:
  * Atrial volume, RV and LV end-diastolic volumes, stroke volume, and CO increase because pericardial restraining forces are no longer present
  * Myocardium alone determines chamber compliance
* **Chronic Adaptations**:
  * Chronic pericardial effusion or chamber enlargement gradually stretches the pericardium
  * Increases pericardial compliance and attenuates or abolishes restraining characteristics

**Bottom Line:** The pericardium provides external constraint to the heart, influencing ventricular filling and facilitating ventricular interdependence. While normally having minimal effect, acute increases in pericardial pressure significantly restrict cardiac filling. The pericardium plays a crucial role in modulating cardiovascular responses to respiratory changes, with particularly important implications in pathological conditions like tamponade or constrictive pericarditis.

# 13. DETERMINANTS OF ATRIAL FUNCTION





> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|Part A: Atrial Function]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#2.1 Clinical Decision-Making and Procedures|Part C]]

**Atrial function encompasses contractile, reservoir, and conduit properties that significantly impact ventricular filling.**

### 13.1.1. LA CONTRACTILITY

* LA contractility resembles LV with similar characteristics
* Emptying fraction depends on preload and contractile state
* When dilated beyond optimal myofilament length, emptying fraction decreases with no meaningful contribution to LV Ved
* Autonomic nervous system effects:
  * Sympathetic nervous system increases emptying fraction and LV end-diastolic contribution
  * Parasympathetic nervous system exerts negative inotropic effect
* ⚠️ **Volatile anesthetics** cause LA myocardial depression [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 13.1.2. LA AFTERLOAD

* Determined by LV compliance and pressure during late diastasis
* LV diastolic dysfunction increases LA afterload
  * LA myocardium upregulates beta-myosin isoform to preserve emptying fraction
  * LA more susceptible to afterload mismatch than LV due to less muscle mass
  * 💡 Vasodilator treatment of hypertension reduces LA/LV afterload and improves LA contribution to LV filling [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 13.1.3. LA RESERVOIR AND CONDUIT FUNCTION

* LA relaxation after contraction produces forward flow from pulmonary veins during early LV isovolumic contraction
* LA reservoir function impaired by:
  * LA ischemia, hypertrophy or dilation
  * Severe LV systolic dysfunction (reduces pulmonary venous return)
  * RV systolic dysfunction (affects late reservoir phase)
* LA compliance critical for reservoir and conduit function:
  * Decreased compliance impairs LA filling
  * LA appendage more compliant than main body
  * LA appendage exclusion/clipping/resection reduces overall LA compliance, attenuates reservoir function, and blunts early LV filling
* Pericardium limits LA filling

### 13.1.4. PHYSIOLOGICAL ADAPTATIONS

* **Exercise Effects**:
  * Increases LA contractility and reservoir function
  * Creates larger LA-LV pressure gradient during early LV filling
  * Results in greater SV and CO
  * Increased conduit function observed in endurance athletes compared to normal subjects
* **Aging Effects**:
  * Causes LA dilation
  * Decline in passive emptying
  * Compensatory increase in LA ejection force (increased LA contribution to LV Ved)
  * LA dilation increases storage fraction (ratio of LA reservoir to LV SV)
  * May contribute to further increase in LA wall stress and contractile dysfunction

**Bottom Line:** Atrial function encompasses contractile, reservoir, and conduit properties that are dynamically regulated by loading conditions, autonomic influences, and underlying cardiac disease. Atrial contribution to ventricular filling becomes increasingly important with aging and in conditions of impaired ventricular relaxation. Volatile anesthetics can depress atrial contractility, while vasodilator therapy can improve atrial function by reducing afterload.

# 14. STARLING'S LAW AND CARDIAC DYSFUNCTION




> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|Part A: Frank-Starling Mechanism]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#2.1 Clinical Decision-Making and Procedures|Part C]]

### 14.1.1. FRANK-STARLING LAW 

**The Frank-Starling law states that the strength of ventricular contraction is dependent on the length of the resting fibers.**

* When all other factors remain constant, increased left ventricular preload causes increased stroke volume
* Left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV) is difficult to measure, so left ventricular end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP) often serves as surrogate
* The relationship between stroke volume and LVEDP is nonlinear
* **Length-Tension Relationship**:
  * The degree of overlap between actin and myosin myofilaments in diastole determines the extent of crossbridge formation
  * Maximal force of contraction occurs at sarcomere length of approximately 2.2 μm
  * At shorter lengths (<2.2 μm), overlapping thin filaments reduces tension generation
  * At longer lengths (>2.2 μm), fewer actin-myosin crossbridges form, reducing contractile force
  * At 3.6 μm, no overlap exists and active tension development is zero
* **Key consequence**: Frank-Starling mechanism matches stroke volume between right and left sides of the heart
  * Increased venous return to right ventricle → increased RV stroke volume → greater pulmonary blood flow → greater LVEDV → greater LV stroke volume

![Frank-Starling Curves: Normal vs Failure](frank_starling_curves.png)


### 14.1.2. CARDIAC FAILURE 

**Cardiac failure occurs when the heart is unable to provide sufficient cardiac output to meet tissue demands.**

* **Classification**:
  * **High-output heart failure**: CO is normal, but tissue O₂ demand is high (thyrotoxicosis, pregnancy)
  * **Low-output heart failure**: Tissue O₂ demand is normal, but CO is insufficient
* **Types of heart failure**:
  * **Systolic heart failure** (ejection fraction <45%):
	* Impaired pump function
	* Causes include myocyte dysfunction from ischemia/infarction, inflammation, or cardiomyopathy
	* Chronically increased afterload (hypertension, aortic stenosis)
	* Results in heart dilation (cardiomegaly)
	* Annual mortality of approximately 20%
  * **Diastolic heart failure**:
	* Reduced ventricular compliance from impaired relaxation or pathological hypertrophy
	* Impaired ventricular filling leads to reduced stroke volume
	* Atrial contraction particularly important (fourth heart sound may be present)
	* Atrial fibrillation poorly tolerated
	* Annual mortality of approximately 8%

### 14.1.3. COMPENSATED VS. DECOMPENSATED HEART FAILURE 

* **Compensated heart failure**:
  * Ventricular function impaired but CO maintained through compensatory mechanisms:
	* Sympathetic stimulation increases contractility and heart rate
	* Blood volume expansion (RAA axis activation) increases preload
* **Decompensated heart failure**:
  * Compensatory mechanisms no longer maintain adequate CO
  * Cardiac sarcomeres stretched beyond optimal length
  * Tension generation reduced (descending limb of Frank-Starling curve)
  * Increased ventricular radius creates mechanical disadvantage (Laplace's law)
  * Increasing preload further decreases stroke volume

### 14.1.4. CLINICAL CONSEQUENCES 

* **Forward heart failure**:
  * Insufficient blood pumped to meet metabolic demands
  * Consequences include renal failure, exercise intolerance, cardiogenic shock
* **Backward heart failure**:
  * Increased LVEDV causes abnormally high atrial pressure
  * Left heart: Pulmonary edema, dyspnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea
  * Right heart: Peripheral edema, ascites, hepatomegaly, intestinal edema

**Bottom Line:** The Frank-Starling law describes how increased preload enhances ventricular contraction up to an optimal sarcomere length. Heart failure occurs when the ventricle cannot meet metabolic demands due to impaired systolic function (reduced contractility) or diastolic function (impaired filling). Compensatory mechanisms initially maintain cardiac output, but eventually fail in decompensated heart failure, leading to both forward and backward clinical manifestations.

# 15. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF HEART FAILURE





> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|Part A: Heart Failure Pathophysiology]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|Part B]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#2.1 Clinical Decision-Making and Procedures|Part C]]

**Heart failure occurs when the heart cannot meet metabolic demands due to impaired filling or ejection.**

### 15.1.1. DEFINITION AND CLASSIFICATION

* Heart failure occurs when the LV cannot eject blood at a sufficient rate to meet metabolic demands or cannot completely fill without elevated pressure
* Abnormal LV diastolic function contributing to or independently causing heart failure was recognized decades ago
* Heart failure classification:
  * **Heart Failure with preserved Ejection Fraction (HFpEF)** - EF ≥50%, previously termed "diastolic heart failure" (approximately 50% of all heart failure patients)
  * **Heart Failure with reduced Ejection Fraction (HFrEF)** - EF <40%, formerly known as "systolic heart failure" (approximately 33%)
  * Heart failure with modestly depressed LV EF (EF 40-49%) (approximately 16%)
  * 💡 Classification based on EF 50% cutoff seems arbitrary but is firmly established in literature

### 15.1.2. EPIDEMIOLOGY

* Proportion of patients with HFpEF and hospitalization rates steadily increasing
* Rising prevalence of conditions associated with HFpEF:
  * Hypertension
  * Diabetes mellitus
  * Obesity
  * Metabolic syndrome
  * Chronic kidney disease
  * Atrial fibrillation
  * Sedentary lifestyle
  * Generalized deconditioning
* Aging population likely accounts for disproportionate increase in HFpEF compared to HFrEF

**Bottom Line:** Heart failure occurs when the ventricle cannot adequately fill or eject blood, leading to insufficient cardiac output to meet metabolic demands. Classification into HFpEF (preserved ejection fraction) and HFrEF (reduced ejection fraction) has important implications for pathophysiology, presentation, and management. The prevalence of HFpEF is increasing due to rising rates of associated conditions like hypertension, diabetes, and obesity in an aging population.

## 15.2. HEART FAILURE WITH REDUCED VS PRESERVED EJECTION FRACTION

**HFrEF and HFpEF represent distinct clinical entities with different pathophysiology, demographics, and treatment approaches.**

### 15.2.1. CLINICAL FEATURES COMPARISON

| Feature | HFrEF | HFpEF |
|---------|-------|-------|
| Age | Younger | Older |
| Sex | Men > women | Women > men |
| Medical comorbidities | CAD, CABG/PCI, Tobacco abuse, HLD, PVD | CAD, HTN, Atrial fibrillation, DM, Obesity, Metabolic syndrome, COPD, CKD, Anemia, Deconditioning |
| Physical findings | S3 gallop | PND, Peripheral edema, JVD, Elevated PCWP |

### 15.2.2. PATHOPHYSIOLOGICAL DIFFERENCES

| Feature | HFrEF | HFpEF |
|---------|-------|-------|
| Myocardial contractility | Markedly depressed | Depressed |
| LV ESPVR | Reduced Ees | Elevated Ees (systolic stiffening) |
| LV end-diastolic pressure | Increased | Increased |
| LV relaxation | Delayed | Delayed |
| Myocardial stiffness | Normal to increased | Markedly increased |
| Extracellular matrix | Less interstitial fibrosis | Greater interstitial fibrosis |
| LV afterload | Increased secondary to neurohormonal activation | Prominent arterial stiffening (increased Ea) |
| LV–arterial coupling | Ees/Ea < 1 | Ees/Ea > 1 |
| PAH/RV dysfunction | Common | Common |
| Chronotropic incompetence | Less common | More common |

**Bottom Line:** HFrEF and HFpEF demonstrate distinct demographic patterns, comorbidities, and pathophysiological mechanisms. HFpEF is more common in older women with multiple comorbidities and features predominantly diastolic dysfunction with preserved contractility, while HFrEF typically affects younger men with previous coronary events and features predominantly systolic dysfunction.

## 15.3. PRESENTATION

**HFrEF and HFpEF present with different clinical features and pathophysiological manifestations.**

### 15.3.1. DISTINCT CLINICAL PHENOTYPES

* HFrEF and HFpEF are distinct clinical phenotypes
  * HFpEF rarely becomes HFrEF in long-term survivors
  * Risk factors for HFpEF (obesity, metabolic syndrome, physical inactivity, deconditioning) often not associated with HFrEF

### 15.3.2. HFPEF CHARACTERISTICS

* Heterogeneous clinical syndrome where comorbid conditions conspire to produce sustained low-grade proinflammatory state
* Exacerbates preexisting LV and arterial stiffening
* Causes coronary microvascular dysregulation
* Impairs skeletal muscle oxygen utilization
* Accelerates disease progression beyond what is anticipated based on comorbidities alone

### 15.3.3. HFREF CHARACTERISTICS

* Higher prevalence of:
  * Coronary atherosclerosis
  * Myocardial ischemia or infarction
  * Previous percutaneous coronary intervention or coronary artery bypass graft surgery
  * Peripheral vascular disease
* Characteristic features not found in HFpEF:
  * Myocardial necrosis
  * Apoptosis
  * Eccentric LV remodeling
  * Compensatory neurohormonal activation for depressed myocardial contractility and reduced CO

### 15.3.4. DIAGNOSTIC CHALLENGES

* HFrEF diagnosis generally easy based on clinical presentation alone
* HFpEF more difficult to recognize:
  * Patients may initially be asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic at rest
  * May only display symptoms during exercise
  * Provocative testing may be required to definitively establish diagnosis in early stages

### 15.3.5. TREATMENT RESPONSE

* HFrEF responds positively to:
  * Renin-angiotensin-aldosterone axis inhibitors
  * Beta-adrenoceptor antagonists
  * Neprilysin inhibitors
  * Nitrates
  * Digitalis glycosides
* These approaches ineffective in HFpEF based on large-scale randomized controlled trials
* 💡 Sodium-glucose cotransport protein 2 inhibitors (SGLT2) show efficacy in HFpEF:
  * Reduce risk of cardiovascular-related death and hospitalization
  * Work independent of diabetes
* HFpEF treatment (except SGLT2 inhibitors) restricted to:
  * Symptom mitigation
  * Lifestyle modifications (exercise training, weight loss)
  * Management of chronic comorbid conditions
* Heterogeneous pathophysiology of HFpEF likely explains poor response to treatments effective for HFrEF

### 15.3.6. DEMOGRAPHIC PATTERNS

* **Gender differences**:
  * HFrEF: Male predominance
  * HFpEF: Women more affected than men, gender difference widens with age
* **Racial disparities**:
  * Black women disproportionately affected by HFpEF
  * Black men at greater risk of HFrEF

### 15.3.7. MORTALITY AND HOSPITALIZATION PATTERNS

* Death from primary cardiovascular cause more common in HFrEF
* Hospital admission and readmission for noncardiac causes more frequent in HFpEF

**Bottom Line:** HFrEF and HFpEF represent distinct clinical entities with different presentations, underlying pathophysiology, demographics, and treatment responses. HFrEF is characterized by impaired contractility and responds to established heart failure medications, while HFpEF features preserved contractility but impaired filling and responds poorly to traditional heart failure therapies except SGLT2 inhibitors. HFpEF patients tend to be older, female, and have multiple comorbidities, with diagnostic confirmation often requiring provocative testing.

## 15.4. DIAGNOSIS

**Heart failure diagnosis relies on clinical features, ejection fraction, and evidence of diastolic dysfunction.**

### 15.4.1. HFREF DIAGNOSIS

* Usually straightforward based on clinical presentation alone
* Common symptoms and signs:
  * Fatigue
  * Tachypnea
  * Dyspnea at rest or on exertion
  * Paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea
  * Orthopnea
  * S3 gallop
  * Jugular venous distention
  * Peripheral edema
  * Evidence of reduced tissue perfusion
* When LV ejection fraction is decreased, these symptoms allow HFrEF diagnosis using established guidelines, provided other causes (e.g., pericardial or valve disease) are excluded

### 15.4.2. HFPEF DIAGNOSIS

* Often more difficult to recognize:
  * Patients may be asymptomatic or symptomatic only during exercise
  * Provocative testing may be required for diagnosis
* **ACCF/AHA guidelines** focus on three diagnostic features:
  * Heart failure symptoms
  * EF ≥50%
  * Evidence of LV diastolic dysfunction detected using transthoracic echocardiography
  * Useful for patients with clear symptomatology but less reliable for subclinical HFpEF
* **ESC criteria** are more detailed:
  * Two-dimensional echocardiographic measurements (LV size, wall thickness, and mass; LA size)
  * Transmitral blood flow velocity (early LV filling peak velocity [E])
  * Tissue Doppler imaging (lateral mitral annulus velocity [e'], E/e' ratio)
  * Plasma natriuretic peptide concentrations
  * Both ACCF/AHA and ESC guidelines rely on resting assessment and don't incorporate provocative testing
* **Cardiopulmonary exercise testing**:
  * Important for identifying HFpEF in patients with minimal symptoms at rest
  * These individuals develop severe symptoms from exaggerated increases in LV filling pressures during exercise
  * Evaluates symptoms, continuous systemic and pulmonary arterial pressures, and peak oxygen consumption during step-wise increases in workload
  * HFpEF patients show:
	* Greater dyspnea earlier during graded exercise
	* Lower maximum workloads
	* Marked increases in respiratory rate, mean pulmonary artery pressure, and pulmonary capillary wedge pressure compared to healthy individuals

**Bottom Line:** HFrEF diagnosis is typically straightforward based on symptoms, signs, and reduced ejection fraction. HFpEF diagnosis is more challenging, requiring assessment of symptoms, preserved ejection fraction, and evidence of diastolic dysfunction. While resting diagnostic criteria are established by ACCF/AHA and ESC guidelines, cardiopulmonary exercise testing is increasingly recognized as important for identifying HFpEF in patients with minimal resting symptoms but exertional limitations.

## 15.5. FUNCTIONAL CHARACTERISTICS

**The functional manifestations of heart failure reflect distinct underlying pathophysiological mechanisms.**

### 15.5.1. HFREF PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

* Defining characteristic is reduced myocardial contractility, often from ischemia, infarction, or cardiomyopathy
* Pressure–volume analysis shows:
  * Shallow slope of LV end-systolic pressure–volume relationship (Ees) indicating reduced contractility
  * Compensatory LV dilation (rightward shift along EDPVR)
  * This preserves SV and CO but increases LV filling pressures and causes pulmonary venous congestion
* Neurohormonal activation (sympathetic tone, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone axis) mitigates decreases in CO and mean arterial pressure
* This causes deleterious increases in heart rate, LV afterload, and myocardial oxygen demand that further compromise forward flow

### 15.5.2. HFPEF PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

* LV diastolic dysfunction is primary determinant of elevated LV filling pressures
* LV EDPVR shows increased LV filling pressures required for normal LV Ved
* Steeper EDPVR curve from:
  * Delayed LV relaxation
  * Increased myocardial stiffness
* These abnormalities restrict LV filling, increase LV diastolic pressure, predisposing to:
  * LA hypertension
  * LA systolic and diastolic dysfunction
  * Pulmonary venous congestion
  * Dyspnea
  * Pulmonary hypertension
  * RV dilation and dysfunction
* Afterload sensitivity of LV relaxation prominent in HFpEF due to hypertension and arterial stiffening
* These abnormalities further delay LV relaxation, compromising early LV filling by:
  * Attenuating LA–LV pressure gradient
  * Blunting torsional recoil
  * Inhibiting diastolic suction mechanism
  * Raising LV diastolic pressure
* In patients with HFpEF, LV relaxation does not shorten in response to tachycardia, contributing to exercise intolerance

### 15.5.3. INFLAMMATORY AND STRUCTURAL CHANGES

* Chronic proinflammatory state with elevated nitrosative–oxidative stress contributes to:
  * Interstitial fibrosis
  * Cardiac myocyte stiffening
* Inflammatory markers predict HFpEF development in elderly patients
* Intensity and pattern of inflammatory response distinguishes HFpEF from HFrEF
* Endomyocardial biopsies from HFpEF patients show:
  * Greater cardiac myocyte diameter
  * Higher myofibrillar density
  * Increased collagen volume compared to HFrEF
* Coronary microvascular density reduced and endothelial dysfunction present in HFpEF:
  * Correlates with myocardial fibrosis extent
  * Results in limited coronary blood flow reserve
* Dysregulation of NO metabolism adversely modifies specific titin isoforms linked to increased myocardial stiffness in HFpEF

### 15.5.4. DIAGNOSTIC ASSESSMENT

* Cardiac catheterization required to measure EDPVR in HFpEF, but impractical for most patients
* Echocardiography used to estimate LV compliance with established algorithms:
  * Two-dimensional imaging
  * Doppler measurement of blood flow
  * Mitral annular velocities
* Common echocardiographic findings in HFpEF:
  * Impaired LV filling
  * Attenuated systolic pulmonary venous blood flow
  * Reduced tissue Doppler velocities
  * Enlarged LA size
  * Increased LV wall thickness with relatively normal chamber dimensions
* Without clinical symptoms, LV diastolic dysfunction alone does not establish HFpEF diagnosis
  * Approximately 70% of patients over age 75 have some echocardiographic evidence of LV diastolic dysfunction
  * Moderate or severe LV diastolic dysfunction without heart failure often predicts subsequent HFpEF development

### 15.5.5. VENTRICULAR-ARTERIAL COUPLING

* **HFpEF**:
  * Steep slope of ESPVR (systolic stiffening)
  * Smaller LV chamber size
  * Despite increased baseline Ees, myocardial contractility is abnormal
  * Reduction of contractility substantially less than in HFrEF
  * Ability to augment contractility and raise CO during exercise/stress is attenuated
  * This limitation of systolic reserve causes exercise intolerance
  * Systolic stiffening affects vasodilator therapy response:
	* Afterload reduction causes substantial hypotension with only modest SV increase
	* Therefore, vasodilator therapy is not a mainstay in HFpEF treatment (unlike HFrEF)

![image from source](CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_CIRCULATORY_SYSTEM_image_46.png_5549a0.png_155d51.png_c892_.png)

* **Arterial Stiffness in HFpEF**:
  * Reduced aorta and proximal great vessels compliance
  * Increases arterial pulse pressure, LV afterload, and myocardial oxygen demand
  * Degree of arterial stiffening exceeds that predicted from hypertension or advanced age alone
  * Important factor in arterial pressure lability and afterload mismatch common in HFpEF
  * Endothelial dysfunction from impaired NO metabolism likely plays role (flow-mediated vasodilation markedly attenuated)
  * Series elastic chamber model illustrates interaction between arterial and LV systolic stiffening:
	* Both LV end-systolic elastance (Ees) and effective arterial elastance (Ea) increased
	* Reflects LV systolic and arterial stiffening respectively
	* Assures near-optimal SV transfer from LV to great vessels at rest
	* Steep slopes of Ees and Ea mean even modest loading reductions (diuretic, vasodilator, exercise) may cause large arterial pressure decline
  * In contrast, LV–arterial coupling is abnormal (Ees/Ea < 1) at rest in HFrEF:
	* Primarily because myocardial contractility is depressed (reduced Ees)
	* Arterial stiffness less affected
	* Decreased LV afterload (Ea) from vasodilator beneficial as it improves LV-arterial matching
	* Results in greater forward flow without pronounced hypotension

### 15.5.6. ATRIAL, PULMONARY, AND RIGHT VENTRICULAR EFFECTS

* **Left Atrial Changes**:
  * Progressive LA dilation in response to reduced LV compliance and increased LV diastolic pressures
  * Initially increases chamber's systolic contribution to LV filling
  * Buffers retrograde transmission of elevated LV diastolic pressures to pulmonary circulation and RV
  * Compensatory LA contractility augmentation declines as HFpEF worsens:
	* LA afterload continues to increase
	* LA dilates further
	* Attenuates LA pumping ability
	* Reduces contribution to LV preload during atrial systole
  * LA dilation from chronic pressure increases reduces compliance and ability to collect pulmonary venous blood
  * Remodeling and reduced compliance restrict pulmonary venous blood flow into LA
  * LA dysfunction present in both heart failure forms but especially prominent in HFpEF
  * Limits exercise tolerance, associated with pulmonary hypertension, RV dysfunction, and mortality
  * LA dilation primary cause of atrial fibrillation, present in up to two-thirds of HFpEF patients and major morbidity cause
* **Pulmonary Hypertension**:
  * Common in both HFrEF and HFpEF due to elevated LV filling pressures transmitting through LA to pulmonary venous circulation
  * Chronic pulmonary venous hypertension plus temporal remodeling of pulmonary venules and arterioles increases pulmonary vascular resistance and reduces pulmonary arterial compliance
  * Observed in >75% of HFpEF patients and linked to LV diastolic dysfunction severity
  * Directly associated with mortality
  * Pulmonary vasodilation during exercise attenuated in HFpEF regardless of resting pulmonary vascular resistance
  * Peak pulmonary vascular resistance during exercise predicts heart failure–related hospitalization and mortality
  * Pulmonary hypertension contributes to RV dysfunction in approximately one-third of HFpEF patients
  * Patients with HFpEF and RV dysfunction more likely to develop:
	* Tricuspid regurgitation
	* Atrial fibrillation
	* Renal insufficiency
	* LV dysfunction
  * RV dysfunction can occur in HFpEF independent of pulmonary hypertension
  * RV distention reduces LV filling through ventricular interdependence despite increased LV filling pressures
  * Like pulmonary hypertension, RV dysfunction associated with substantially increased mortality in HFpEF
* **Chronotropic Response**:
  * Heart rate is major determinant of increased CO during exercise
  * CO reserve doesn't reach predicted maximum in HFpEF because peak heart rate during exercise is limited
  * "Chronotropic incompetence" probably results from beta-adrenoceptor desensitization rather than dysfunctional sympathetic nervous system outflow
  * New chronotropic incompetence may predict symptom onset or indicate further decompensation in HFpEF
  * Ability to enhance SV during exercise blunted in HFpEF:
	* LV diastolic stiffening and chronically elevated LV end-diastolic pressures limit further LV filling
	* Effectively eliminates Frank-Starling mechanism

**Bottom Line:** HFrEF features reduced contractility with compensatory dilation, while HFpEF demonstrates preserved but stiff ventricular and arterial properties with impaired filling. Both conditions lead to elevated filling pressures, but through different mechanisms, explaining their distinct responses to therapy. HFpEF is characterized by a proinflammatory state, myocardial fibrosis, impaired relaxation, and left atrial dysfunction, with associated pulmonary hypertension and right ventricular dysfunction that compound morbidity and mortality.

# 16. VASCULAR SYSTEM





## 16.1. CAPILLARIES AND ENDOTHELIUM





**Competency Focus:** 

---

**Capillaries are the primary site of exchange between blood and tissues.**

* **Capillary structure**:
  * Single layer of endothelial cells connected by tight junctions
  * Surrounded by basement membrane
  * Diameter of 5-10 μm, allowing single-file passage of red blood cells
  * Exchange occurs via diffusion, filtration, and reabsorption
* **Starling forces** govern fluid movement across capillary walls:
  * Hydrostatic pressure gradient: Blood pressure (Pc) minus interstitial pressure (Pi)
  * Oncotic pressure gradient: Plasma oncotic pressure (πc) minus interstitial oncotic pressure (πi)
  * Net fluid movement = k[(Pc-Pi) - σ(πc-πi)]
	* k is filtration coefficient (capillary permeability)
	* σ is reflection coefficient (effectiveness of vessel wall in preventing protein passage)
* **Clinical relevance: haemorrhage**:
  * Loss of intravascular blood volume risks organ hypoperfusion
  * Body responds with massive sympathetic outflow
  * Causes arteriolar vasoconstriction that:
	* Increases systemic vascular resistance to maintain MAP
	* Reduces capillary hydrostatic pressure
  * Lower capillary pressure shifts Starling forces toward net absorption
  * Allows "autotransfusion" of ~500 mL fluid from interstitium within 30 minutes
* **Capillary blood flow regulation**:
  * At rest, only ~25% of capillaries are patent
  * Blood can bypass capillary networks via metarterioles
  * Pre-capillary sphincters control blood flow into each capillary
  * Metabolically active tissues have more open sphincters
  * Control mechanisms include:
	* Neural stimulation
	* Low O₂ tension
	* High metabolite concentrations (H+, CO₂)

### 16.1.1. WHAT ARE THE FUNCTIONS OF THE ENDOTHELIUM? 

**The endothelium is a metabolically active tissue with numerous important functions.**

* **Synthesis of vasoactive substances**:
  * **Nitric oxide (NO)**: Vasodilator produced from L-arginine by nitric oxide synthase (NOS)
	* Inhaled NO used in neonatal/adult pulmonary hypertension and ARDS
	* NO release makes veins easier to cannulate when tapped
	* NO donors (glyceryl trinitrate) used as anti-anginal drugs
  * **Prostacyclin (PGI₂)**: Vasodilator
  * **Endothelin (ET)**: Potent vasoconstrictor
* **Haemostasis**:
  * **Anticoagulant properties**:
	* Thrombomodulin binds thrombin and activates protein C
	* Heparan sulphate activates antithrombin III
	* NO and PGI₂ inhibit platelet aggregation
  * **Procoagulant properties**:
	* Synthesizes von Willebrand factor (vWF)
	* vWF binds exposed collagen to platelets when vessels are damaged
* **Inflammatory system**:
  * Expresses adhesion molecules in response to inflammatory cytokines
  * Facilitates neutrophil and lymphocyte migration across the endothelium

**Bottom Line:** The endothelium forms the inner lining of blood vessels and is far more than a passive barrier, playing critical roles in regulating vascular tone, blood fluidity, inflammation, and exchange between blood and tissues. Its functions are mediated through the release of numerous bioactive substances that maintain vascular homeostasis.

## 16.2. VENOUS SYSTEM





**Competency Focus:** 

---

### 16.2.1. ROLES OF THE VENOUS SYSTEM 

**The venous system has several important functions beyond simply returning blood to the heart.**

* **Primary functions**:
  * Transport of deoxygenated blood from capillaries to right heart
  * Storage of blood (contains 65% of circulating blood volume)
  * Venous return to the heart (determines cardiac preload)
  * Thermoregulation (arteriovenous anastomoses in skin)
* **Exceptions to deoxygenated blood transport**:
  * Pulmonary veins carry oxygenated blood to left heart
  * Umbilical vein carries oxygenated blood from placenta to fetus
  * Portal veins connect two capillary networks:
	* Hepatic portal vein (gut to liver)
	* Hypophyseal portal veins (hypothalamus to anterior pituitary)

### 16.2.2. STRUCTURE DIFFERENCES FROM ARTERIES 

**Veins have structural differences from arteries that suit their functional requirements.**

* **Anatomical characteristics**:
  * Thinner walls and larger lumens than equivalent-sized arteries
  * Less smooth muscle and more elastin in tunica media
  * Tunica externa (containing elastin and collagen) is thickest layer
  * More distensible and often collapsed when empty
  * Contain valves formed by tunica intima folds that prevent backflow
  * No valves in venae cavae, portal veins or cerebral veins

### 16.2.3. COMPLIANCE 

**Venous compliance is much higher than arterial compliance, allowing storage of large blood volumes.**

* **Compliance definition**: Change in volume caused by unit change in distending pressure
* **Venous vs arterial compliance**:
  * Veins ~30 times more compliant than arteries
  * Veins hold 65% of circulating blood volume vs 15% in arteries
  * Critical for cardiovascular function: left ventricle moves blood from high-compliance veins to low-compliance arteries, generating pressure (ΔP = ΔV/C)

### 16.2.4. FACTORS DETERMINING VENOUS RETURN 

**Multiple mechanisms ensure blood returns to the heart against gravity.**

* **Key mechanisms**:
  * **Valves**: One-way valves every few centimeters ensure unidirectional flow
  * **Venous pressure** (mean systemic filling pressure): As veins fill, pressure increases, enhancing return
  * **Respiratory (abdominothoracic) pump**: Negative intrathoracic pressure during inspiration pulls open venae cavae by radial traction; increased intra-abdominal pressure propels blood toward heart
  * **Cardiac pump**: During ventricular contraction, atrioventricular rings pull downward, increasing atrial volume and decreasing pressure
  * **Skeletal muscle pump**: Rhythmical contraction of skeletal muscles, particularly calf, propels blood through deep veins
  * **Body position**: Supine position increases venous pressure and return; Trendelenburg position further increases venous return
* **Clinical relevance: venous return and anaesthesia**:
  * Several anaesthetic factors reduce venous return:
	* Positive-pressure ventilation increases intrathoracic pressure, reducing venous return
	* Positive end-expiratory pressure worsens this effect
	* Inferior vena cava compression in pregnancy can cause hypotension when supine

### 16.2.5. VENOUS PRESSURE WAVEFORMS 

**Central venous pressure waveforms provide important clinical information about right heart function.**

* **Key features of normal CVP waveform**:
  * **a-wave**: Corresponds to atrial contraction, occurs after P-wave on ECG
  * **c-wave**: Caused by tricuspid valve bulging into RA in early systole
  * **x-descent**: Corresponds to atrial relaxation and downward movement of RA during RV contraction
  * **v-wave**: Represents continued venous return to RA during ventricular systole with closed tricuspid valve
  * **y-descent**: Occurs after tricuspid valve opens when blood flows from RA to RV
* **Diagnostic value in arrhythmias**:
  * **Atrial fibrillation**: Absence of a-waves
  * **Third-degree heart block**: Occasional "cannon a-waves" when atria contract against closed tricuspid valve
  * **Tricuspid regurgitation**: "Giant v-wave" replacing c-wave, x-descent, and v-wave
* **Normal CVP measurement**:
  * Normal mean CVP: 2-8 mmHg measured at RA level
  * Elevated CVP (>8 mmHg) causes:
	* Transducer below RA level
	* Positive-pressure ventilation/PEEP
	* Congestive cardiac failure
	* Isolated right ventricular failure
	* Hypervolemia
	* Pulmonary embolus
	* Constrictive pericarditis
  * Low CVP (<2 mmHg) causes:
	* Transducer above RA level
	* Acute hypovolemia
* **Using CVP for fluid management**:
  * CVP trends more useful than absolute values
  * Transient rise with fluid bolus indicates RV on ascending Starling curve
  * Sustained rise suggests RV at maximal preload
  * Clinical deterioration with marked rise suggests RV on descending Starling curve (overfilled)

**Bottom Line:** The venous system plays critical roles in cardiovascular function beyond simply returning blood to the heart. Its high compliance allows it to serve as a blood reservoir, while multiple mechanisms including valves, respiratory and muscle pumps ensure venous return against gravity. Central venous pressure measurement can provide valuable information about right heart function and guide fluid management.

## 16.3. THE GREAT VEINS OF THE NECK





**Competency Focus:** , 

---

**The great veins of the neck are common sites for central venous access and have important anatomical relationships.**

* **Internal jugular vein (IJV)**:
  * Course: From jugular foramen in skull to junction with subclavian vein behind sternal end of clavicle
  * Position: Lateral to internal/common carotid artery within carotid sheath
  * Vagus nerve lies between and behind artery and vein
  * Tributaries: Pharyngeal venous plexus, common facial vein, lingual vein, superior/middle thyroid veins
* **External jugular vein (EJV)**:
  * Formation: From posterior division of retromandibular vein
  * Course: Crosses sternocleidomastoid in superficial fascia, traverses posterior triangle
  * Termination: Pierces deep fascia 2.5 cm above clavicle to drain into subclavian vein
  * ⚠️ When lacerated can allow air embolism as it is splinted open by deep fascia
* **Anterior jugular vein**:
  * Location: Runs on either side of midline of neck
  * Crosses thyroid isthmus
  * Communicates with fellow vein above sternum
  * Passes deep to sternocleidomastoid to enter external jugular vein
* **Subclavian vein**:
  * Continuation of axillary vein from outer border of 1st rib
  * Courses over 1st rib to join internal jugular vein at medial border of scalenus anterior
  * Forms brachiocephalic vein behind sternoclavicular joint
  * On left side, receives thoracic duct
* **Brachiocephalic (innominate) veins**:
  * Formation: Behind sternoclavicular joints by junction of internal jugular and subclavian veins
  * Right brachiocephalic vein: ~3 cm long, descends vertically
  * Left brachiocephalic vein: ~6 cm long, descends obliquely
  * Junction: Form superior vena cava at lower border of 1st costal cartilage
  * ⚠️ Close proximity to pleural dome (2.5 cm above medial third of clavicle)
* **Clinical relevance: central venous catheterization**:
  * Common routes include internal jugular and subclavian veins
  * IJV approach: Mid-point of sternocleidomastoid or between sternal/clavicular heads
  * Subclavian approach: Junction of middle and medial thirds of clavicle
  * Potential complications:
	* Large hematomas (especially after anticoagulation)
	* Pneumothorax (3% with blind technique)
	* Air embolism
	* Catheter embolism
	* Carotid arterial puncture (9.4% in blind techniques)
	* Thoracic duct damage (left-sided approaches)
	* Catheter-related sepsis (up to 10%)
  * Ultrasound guidance recommended for all insertions
  * Catheter tip should be placed in SVC just at entry to right atrium

**Bottom Line:** The great veins of the neck have important anatomical relationships that must be understood when performing central venous catheterization. Ultrasound guidance has improved the safety of these procedures, though complications including pneumothorax, arterial puncture, and catheter-related sepsis remain significant concerns. The choice of approach (internal jugular vs. subclavian) should consider the specific clinical situation and potential risks.

## 16.4. LYMPHATICS





**Competency Focus:** 

---

### 16.4.1. ANATOMY OF THE LYMPHATIC SYSTEM 

**The lymphatic system consists of a conducting system and lymphoid tissue.**

* **Key anatomical components**:
  * Small lymphatic capillaries drain into larger lymphatic vessels
  * These converge on right lymphatic duct and thoracic duct
  * **Right lymphatic duct**: Short (~1.5 cm), drains into right subclavian vein
  * **Thoracic duct** (left lymphatic duct): Larger, drains into left brachiocephalic vein
	* Collects lymph from majority of body (except right arm, right chest, right side of head/neck)
  * Lymphatic vessels are pulled open by radial traction from surrounding connective tissue
  * Larger lymph vessels have valves ensuring unidirectional flow
* **Lymphoid tissue**:
  * **Primary lymphoid organs**: Thymus and bone marrow (produce lymphocytes)
  * **Secondary lymphoid organs**: Lymph nodes, lymphoid follicles in tonsils, spleen, Peyer's patches, and mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue
* **Clinical relevance: central venous cannulation**:
  * Left-sided central venous cannulation risks damage to thoracic duct
  * Junction of internal jugular and subclavian veins in close proximity to thoracic duct
  * Puncture can lead to chylothorax
  * Can be prevented by:
	* Using right-sided approaches
	* Using high-neck approach to internal jugular vein
	* Using ultrasound guidance

### 16.4.2. FUNCTIONS OF THE LYMPHATIC SYSTEM 

**The lymphatic system has three main functions.**

* **Fluid balance**:
  * Returns excess interstitial fluid to circulation
  * Approximately 4 L of interstitial fluid daily must be returned to circulation
  * This fluid is called lymph
* **Immune function**:
  * Lymph capillaries are the only route for filtered proteins, lymphocytes, and other debris to leave interstitium
  * Lymph passes through lymph nodes before returning to circulation
  * Lymph nodes contain lymphocytes that detect foreign substances and pathogens
  * Major pathway for carcinoma metastasis
* **Digestion**:
  * Dietary triglycerides form chylomicrons
  * These enter lacteals (lymphatic capillaries of intestine)
  * Combined with lymph to form chyle
  * Conducted through lymphatic vessels to systemic circulation via thoracic duct
* **Clinical relevance: lymphoedema**:
  * Accumulation of excess lymphatic fluid in interstitium
  * Acute lymphoedema: Occurs during transplant (loss of lymphatic supply) or thoracic surgery
  * Chronic lymphoedema: Common after cancer surgery with lymph node resection (especially breast cancer)
  * Initially presents as pitting oedema; becomes non-pitting with time due to inflammation
  * May preclude non-invasive blood pressure monitoring
  * Cannulation of affected limb should be avoided (can worsen lymphoedema)
* **Clinical relevance: subcutaneous drug administration**:
  * Protein-based drugs (e.g., insulin, low-molecular-weight heparins) often delivered subcutaneously
  * Large, negatively charged proteins cannot cross capillary membrane
  * Absorbed by lymphatic system and enter systemic circulation via thoracic duct

**Bottom Line:** The lymphatic system plays essential roles in fluid balance, immune function, and digestion. It returns excess interstitial fluid to the circulation, facilitates immune surveillance, and provides a route for fat absorption from the intestine. Disruption of lymphatic drainage can lead to lymphoedema, while the system also provides a route for drug absorption and cancer metastasis.

# 17. INTEGRATED CARDIOVASCULAR RESPONSES





**Competency Focus:** , 

---

## 17.1. CARDIOVASCULAR REFLEXES





**Competency Focus:** , 

---

**Cardiovascular reflexes are essential for maintaining relatively constant mean arterial pressure despite changing physiological conditions.**

* **Importance of MAP stability**:
  * Organs require stable perfusion pressure
  * Despite autoregulation in key organs (brain, heart, kidneys), significant MAP changes can compromise perfusion
* **Classification of cardiovascular reflexes**:
  * **Reflexes originating within cardiovascular system**:
	* Arterial baroreceptor reflex
	* Bainbridge reflex
	* Chemoreceptor reflex
	* CNS ischemic response
  * **Reflexes triggered by external stimuli**:
	* Pain, emotion, temperature

### 17.1.1. ARTERIAL BARORECEPTOR REFLEX 

**The arterial baroreceptor reflex is the primary mechanism for minimizing short-term MAP fluctuations.**

* **Baroreceptor location and function**:
  * Mechanoreceptors in carotid sinus and aortic arch
  * Sense arterial wall distension:
	* Increased MAP → increased distension → increased afferent firing
	* Decreased MAP → reduced distension → decreased afferent firing
* **Neural pathway**:
  * Afferent signals via glossopharyngeal nerve (carotid) and vagus nerve (aortic)
  * Processing in vasomotor center in medulla oblongata
  * Two functional areas:
	* Vasoconstrictor area: Triggers sympathetic outflow
	* Vasodilator area: Inhibits sympathetic outflow
* **Response mechanism**:
  * Increased MAP → increased baroreceptor firing → vasodilator area activated → decreased sympathetic outflow → peripheral vasodilation, decreased HR and contractility → MAP normalized
  * Decreased MAP → reduced baroreceptor firing → vasoconstrictor area activated → increased sympathetic outflow → peripheral vasoconstriction, increased HR and contractility → MAP normalized
* **Important features**:
  * Influences plasma volume via renin-angiotensin-aldosterone axis
  * Functions via two neuron types (A and C fibers) for wide pressure sensitivity (80-150 mmHg)
  * Resets in chronic hypertension
  * Affected by arterial compliance (reduced with aging/atherosclerosis)

### 17.1.2. BAINBRIDGE REFLEX 

**The Bainbridge reflex causes isolated tachycardia in response to increased central venous pressure.**

* **Mechanism**:
  * Low-pressure mechanoreceptors in great veins and right atrium
  * Activated by increased wall distension from elevated CVP
  * Afferent signals via vagus nerve to vasomotor center
  * Increased sympathetic outflow to sinoatrial node only
  * Results in isolated tachycardia
* **Physiological manifestations**:
  * Respiratory sinus arrhythmia: HR increases during inspiration due to increased venous return
  * Uterine autotransfusion: Tachycardia after delivery from uterine contraction returning ~500 mL to maternal circulation

### 17.1.3. CHEMORECEPTOR ACTIVATION 

**Peripheral chemoreceptors affect cardiovascular function in response to blood gas abnormalities.**

* **Peripheral chemoreceptors**:
  * Carotid and aortic bodies activated by:
	* Low PaO₂
	* High PaCO₂
	* Low arterial pH (carotid bodies)
	* Severe hypotension (MAP <60 mmHg)
* **Cardiovascular effects**:
  * Afferent signals to respiratory center in medulla/pons
  * Connection to nearby vasomotor center
  * Increased sympathetic outflow
  * Hypoxia, hypercapnea, and acidosis trigger increased sympathetic activity
* **Clinical relevance: Cushing's reflex**:
  * Triad of hypertension, bradycardia, irregular respiratory pattern
  * Response to brainstem ischemia (e.g., raised intracranial pressure)
  * Massive sympathetic outflow causing widespread vasoconstriction
  * Increased MAP detected by baroreceptors, triggering compensatory bradycardia
  * Purpose: Maintain cerebral perfusion pressure

### 17.1.4. RESPONSE TO HEMORRHAGE 

**Hemorrhage triggers complex compensatory cardiovascular and neuroendocrine responses.**

* **Physiological response phases**:
  * **Immediate response** (seconds):
	* Detection by low-pressure mechanoreceptors and arterial baroreceptors
	* Increased sympathetic outflow causing:
	  * Tachycardia and increased contractility
	  * Generalized arteriolar vasoconstriction (preserved flow to brain, heart, kidneys)
	  * Venoconstriction of capacitance veins
  
  * **Early response** (minutes to hours):
	* Transcapillary refill/autotransfusion: ~500 mL in 30 minutes from reduced capillary hydrostatic pressure
	* Increased plasma glucose from sympathetic-mediated glycogenolysis
	* Renal conservation of sodium and water through:
	  * Reduced renal blood flow and GFR
	  * Activation of renin-angiotensin-aldosterone axis
	  * Decreased natriuretic peptides
	  * Increased ADH release
	  * Additional cortisol release
  
  * **Late response** (days):
	* Plasma volume restoration from oral intake and renal conservation
	* Reduced hematocrit and plasma protein initially
	* Liver synthesizes albumin within 4-6 days
	* Hemoglobin returns to normal within 8 weeks if iron available
  
  * **Decompensated shock** (>30% blood loss):
	* Compensatory mechanisms overwhelmed
	* Paradoxical vasodilation and decrease in tachycardia (endogenous opioid signaling)
	* Myocardial ischemia, cardiac depression, rapid MAP fall
* **Clinical relevance: classes of hemorrhagic shock**:
  * **Class 1** (<15% volume loss): Normal vital signs
  * **Class 2** (15-30% loss): Tachycardia, normal SBP, raised DBP
  * **Class 3** (30-40% loss): Tachycardia, reduced BP, tachypnea
  * **Class 4** (>40% loss): Very low BP, marked tachycardia, minimal urine output, altered consciousness

![Hemorrhagic Shock Classification Table](hemorrhagic_shock_table.png)


**Bottom Line:** Cardiovascular reflexes are essential for maintaining arterial pressure and organ perfusion despite changing physiological conditions. The arterial baroreceptor reflex is the primary short-term regulator, while the Bainbridge reflex, chemoreceptor activation, and responses to hemorrhage provide additional control mechanisms. Understanding these reflexes is crucial for interpreting cardiovascular changes in clinical scenarios such as blood loss, anesthesia, and critical illness.

## 17.2. VALSALVA MANEUVER





**Competency Focus:** , 

---

**The Valsalva maneuver involves forced expiration against a closed glottis, causing significant cardiovascular changes.**

### 17.2.1. CARDIOVASCULAR CHANGES DURING VALSALVA 

**The Valsalva maneuver produces characteristic sequential hemodynamic changes.**

* **Technique**:
  * Forced expiration against closed glottis
  * Intrathoracic pressure increased to ~40 cmH₂O for 10 seconds
  * Can be performed awake (exhale against closed mouth/nose) or under anesthesia (closing adjustable pressure-limiting valve)
* **Four phases of Valsalva**:
  * **Phase 1**: Initial increase in MAP and transient bradycardia
	* Increased intrathoracic pressure compresses pulmonary vessels
	* Blood squeezed into left heart, increasing stroke volume and MAP
	* Baroreceptors respond with transient decrease in HR
  
  * **Phase 2**: Steady fall in MAP followed by compensatory tachycardia
	* High intrathoracic pressure prevents venous return
	* Reduced stroke volume leads to falling MAP
	* Baroreceptor reflex triggered: HR increases to maintain MAP
  
  * **Phase 3**: Further MAP decrease with continued HR increase
	* Release of intrathoracic pressure
	* Venous return fills empty intrathoracic vessels
	* Stroke volume temporarily decreased further
	* MAP falls more, HR increases further
  
  * **Phase 4**: MAP overshoot with reflex bradycardia
	* Left ventricular preload restored
	* With still-elevated HR, MAP overshoots baseline
	* Baroreceptor reflex causes rapid vagally-mediated bradycardia
	* Both MAP and HR return to normal

![Valsalva Maneuver Phases Chart](valsalva_phases.png)


### 17.2.2. USES OF VALSALVA MANEUVER 

**The Valsalva maneuver has several clinical and diagnostic applications.**

* **Original use**: Test of Eustachian tube patency and expelling pus from middle ear
* **Current applications**:
  * Termination of supraventricular tachycardia (via Phase 4 vagal stimulation)
  * Diagnosis of heart murmurs:
	* Increases intensity of hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy murmur
	* Decreases intensity of most other murmurs (including aortic stenosis)
  * Diving: Helps equalize middle ear pressure during descent
  * Increasing venous pressure to check hemostasis intraoperatively

### 17.2.3. TESTING AUTONOMIC FUNCTION 

**The Valsalva maneuver can be used to assess autonomic nervous system integrity.**

* **Normal response**: Four typical phases described above
* **Abnormal autonomic response**:
  * No compensatory reflex tachycardia in Phase 2 (MAP continues to fall)
  * No MAP overshoot or compensatory bradycardia in Phase 4
  * Seen in conditions affecting autonomic nervous system:
	* Diabetic autonomic neuropathy
	* Spinal cord injury

### 17.2.4. ABNORMAL RESPONSES 

**Certain pathological conditions produce distinctive responses to the Valsalva maneuver.**

* **Square-wave response**:
  * Elevated MAP throughout raised intrathoracic pressure
  * Little change in HR
  * No MAP overshoot in Phase 4
  * Seen in high venous pressure conditions:
	* Congestive cardiac failure
	* Constrictive pericarditis
  * Indicates patients operating on plateau of Starling curve with near-maximal vasoconstriction

**Bottom Line:** The Valsalva maneuver produces characteristic cardiovascular changes that can be used to assess autonomic function and aid diagnosis of certain cardiac conditions. The four phases reflect complex interactions between intrathoracic pressure, venous return, stroke volume, and baroreceptor-mediated responses. Abnormal responses provide valuable information about underlying cardiovascular and autonomic pathology.

## 17.3. EXERCISE PHYSIOLOGY





**Competency Focus:** , 

---

**Exercise represents a major physiological challenge requiring integrated responses from multiple body systems.**

### 17.3.1. TYPES OF EXERCISE 

**Exercise can be categorized as dynamic or static, each with different physiological effects.**

* **Dynamic (isotonic) exercise**:
  * Rhythmical contraction and relaxation of muscles with joint movement (e.g., running)
  * Marked muscle capillary vasodilation from aerobic metabolism
  * Decreased systemic vascular resistance
  * Increased heart rate compensates for reduced diastolic blood pressure
* **Static (isometric) exercise**:
  * Sustained muscle contraction without lengthening/shortening (e.g., weight lifting)
  * Muscle capillaries compressed by contraction, promoting anaerobic metabolism
  * Increased systemic vascular resistance and diastolic blood pressure
  * Heart rate increases but less than with dynamic exercise

### 17.3.2. SKELETAL MUSCLE FIBERS 

**Skeletal muscles contain different fiber types with distinct metabolic and contractile properties.**

* **Type I (slow-twitch) fibers**:
  * Use oxidative metabolism of triglycerides
  * Rich in myoglobin (red appearance), mitochondria, capillaries
  * Found in postural muscles requiring sustained contraction
  * Contract more slowly but resistant to fatigue
* **Type II (fast-twitch) fibers**:
  * **Type IIa** (fast-twitch oxidative): Intermediate properties
  * **Type IIb** (fast-twitch glycolytic): Rely on anaerobic metabolism
	* Larger glycogen stores, less myoglobin (white appearance)
	* Found in muscles for rapid, powerful movements (e.g., sprinting)
	* More susceptible to fatigue due to limited ATP generation capacity
* **Distribution variations**:
  * Individual muscles contain varying mixtures of fiber types
  * Proportion varies widely between individuals
  * Genetic fiber type distribution influences athletic performance (sprinters vs. endurance athletes)

### 17.3.3. ENERGY SUBSTRATES 

**Skeletal muscles use multiple energy sources with different temporal patterns during exercise.**

* **ATP**: Direct energy source, limited stores (1-2 seconds of contraction)
* **Phosphocreatine**: Rapidly regenerates ATP from ADP, sufficient for ~7 seconds of intense exercise
* **Glycolysis**: Utilizes glucose or muscle glycogen, activated within seconds
  * Two ATP molecules from glucose, three from glycogen
  * Produces lactic acid byproduct that can accumulate during strenuous exercise
* **Oxidative phosphorylation**: Most efficient mechanism (36 ATP from complete glucose breakdown)
* **Fat metabolism**: Generates more ATP per unit weight than carbohydrates but takes longer to activate
  * Becomes important during sustained exercise as glycogen depletes
  * Transition to predominant fat metabolism causes fatigue in marathon runners ("hitting the wall")

### 17.3.4. MUSCLE FATIGUE 

**Muscle fatigue is a protective mechanism preventing complete ATP depletion.**

* **Definition**: Decline in ability to generate force, including reduced peak tension and contraction velocity
* **Contributing factors**:
  * Increased ADP and phosphate from ATP breakdown:
	* May reduce calcium reuptake into sarcoplasmic reticulum
	* May trigger ATP-sensitive K⁺ channel opening, reducing membrane excitability
  * Increased extracellular K⁺ (up to 8 mmol/L arterial, 12 mmol/L interstitial)
  * Lactate accumulation
  * Inhibition of aerobic metabolism enzymes
  * Glycogen store depletion in prolonged exercise

### 17.3.5. ANTICIPATORY CHANGES 

**Physiological changes begin before exercise commences through central command and anticipation.**

* **Pre-exercise cardiovascular adaptations**:
  * Increased sympathetic outflow, decreased parasympathetic activity
  * Venoconstriction increases venous return
  * Increased heart rate and contractility
  * Skeletal muscle capillary vasodilation via sympathetic cholinergic fibers
  * Vasoconstriction in gut and skin
* **Feedforward control**:
  * Anticipatory response magnitude varies with expected exercise duration/intensity
  * Greater pre-exercise HR increase before sprint vs. endurance running
  * Allows rapid adaptation to expected physiological demands

### 17.3.6. PHYSIOLOGICAL EFFECTS OF EXERCISE 

**Exercise induces coordinated changes across multiple organ systems.**

* **Skeletal muscle**:
  * Resting blood flow: 2-4 mL/100g/min
  * Exercise blood flow: Up to 50-100 mL/100g/min
  * Increased flow due to local vasodilatory metabolites (H⁺, AMP, K⁺, phosphate)
  * Metabolites produced proportional to O₂ consumption
* **Cardiovascular system**:
  * **Cardiac output**: Increases up to 5-fold (5 L/min to 25 L/min)
	* Preload increased by venoconstriction, skeletal muscle pump, respiratory pump
	* Afterload reduced by skeletal muscle arteriolar vasodilation
	* Heart rate increases proportionally to exercise intensity
	* Myocardial contractility increases via sympathetic stimulation and Bowditch effect
  * **Blood pressure**:
	* Dynamic exercise: SBP increases, DBP stable or decreased, MAP slowly increases
	* Static exercise: Rapid rise in SBP, DBP, and MAP
  * **Regional blood flow**:
	* Skeletal muscle: Increases from 20% to 80% of cardiac output
	* Coronary flow: Increases 5-fold (250 mL/min to 1250 mL/min)
	* Skin blood flow: Substantially increased for heat dissipation
	* Splanchnic blood flow: Substantially decreased
	* Renal blood flow: Decreased but less than splanchnic
	* Cerebral blood flow: Unchanged at any exercise intensity
* **Respiratory system**:
  * Minute ventilation increases up to 20-fold (5 L/min to 100 L/min)
  * O₂ consumption increases from 250 mL/min to 5000 mL/min
  * Ventilation increases initially via:
	* Motor cortex activity
	* Limb joint proprioceptor afferent signals
  * CO₂ production drives proportional ventilatory increase
  * At anaerobic threshold, lactic acid production causes further ventilatory stimulation
  * PaO₂ remains normal due to adequate alveolar-capillary O₂ transfer
  * Oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve shifts right (acidosis, increased temperature)
  * Pulmonary blood flow increases with minimal MPAP increase
  * V̇/Q̇ ratio trends toward 1.0, improving efficiency
* **Thermoregulation**:
  * Only 20-25% of chemical energy converted to mechanical energy, rest dissipated as heat
  * Initial transient fall in core temperature (1°C) as venous return increases
  * Progressive heat generation with continuing exercise
  * Heat loss mechanisms:
	* Sweating (latent heat of evaporation)
	* Cutaneous vasodilation (conduction)
	* Increased minute ventilation (respiratory evaporation)
  * Hot, humid environments impair heat loss:
	* Conduction ineffective when ambient temperature exceeds body temperature
	* Evaporation limited in high humidity
	* Can lead to heat stroke (core temperature >40.6°C)

### 17.3.7. VO₂ MAX 

**VO₂ max represents the maximum capacity for oxygen consumption during incremental exercise.**

* **Definition**: Maximum capacity for O₂ transport and utilization during incremental exercise (mL O₂/kg/min)
* **Characteristics**:
  * Increases linearly with exercise intensity until reaching plateau
  * Used as measure of physical fitness
  * Elite athletes have substantially higher VO₂ max than general population
  * Measured during cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET)
* **Post-exercise oxygen consumption**:
  * Elevated O₂ consumption continues after exercise (EPOC or "oxygen debt")
  * **Alactacid phase**: Rapid, involves replenishment of phosphocreatine, ATP stores, myoglobin O₂, and glycogen
  * **Lactacid phase**: Longer, involves conversion of lactate to pyruvate mostly in liver
  * Time to repay depends on exercise duration/intensity (minutes to >24 hours)

### 17.3.8. ELITE ATHLETES VS NORMAL POPULATION 

**Elite athletes show specific adaptations to training that enhance performance.**

* **Cardiovascular changes**:
  * **Stroke volume**: Increased by up to 40% due to cardiac hypertrophy
  * **Resting heart rate**: Lower due to increased vagal tone
  * **Maximum heart rate**: Unchanged
  * **Maximum cardiac output**: Increased due to higher stroke volume
  * **VO₂ max**: Increased by up to 25% due to improved O₂ delivery from increased muscle vascularization
* **Other adaptations**:
  * **Pulmonary**: Increased maximum breathing rate, lung volume, and pulmonary capillaries
  * **Skeletal muscle**:
	* Endurance training: Increased type I fiber diameter, muscle capillaries, mitochondrial density, oxidative enzyme activity
	* Strength training: Increased type IIb fiber diameter, anaerobic enzyme activity
  * **Bone mineral density**: Increased with weight-bearing exercise

**Bottom Line:** Exercise represents a major physiological challenge requiring coordinated responses from cardiovascular, respiratory, muscular, and thermoregulatory systems. The nature of the response depends on exercise type (dynamic vs. static), intensity, and duration. Different muscle fiber types and energy sources are recruited according to exercise demands, while elite athletes develop specific physiological adaptations that enhance performance capabilities.

**Exercise testing provides objective assessment of a patient's functional capacity and surgical risk.**

### 17.4.1. ASSESSING FITNESS FOR SURGERY 

**Preoperative assessment aims to identify patients at increased risk of perioperative complications.**

* **Factors in surgical risk assessment**:
  * Invasiveness and difficulty of proposed procedure
  * Patient co-morbidities (known/newly diagnosed, well/poorly controlled)
  * Physical fitness (quantified via exercise testing)
* **Goals of assessment**:
  * Inform patient of individual risk to aid decision-making
  * Guide multidisciplinary team decisions (e.g., offering alternative procedures)
  * Plan appropriate post-operative care (e.g., high-dependency bed requirement)
* **Clinical exercise capacity assessment**:
  * Questions about daily activities: Walking distance, stair climbing ability
  * Often subjective with patients overestimating capabilities

### 17.4.2. METHODS OF ASSESSMENT 

**Various methods exist to assess exercise capacity with differing complexity and objectivity.**

* **Questionnaire-based**:
  * Duke Activity Status Index (DASI): 12-question self-assessment about ability to complete physical tasks
  * Each task weighted according to metabolic cost (METs)
  * Patients unable to complete tasks of at least 4 METs at higher perioperative risk
* **Incremental shuttle walk test**:
  * Patients walk between cones 9m apart with progressively decreasing allowed time
  * <250m distance associated with increased perioperative risk
  * Limitations: Not suitable for lower limb amputees, peripheral vascular disease, hip/knee osteoarthritis
* **Cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET)**:
  * Gold standard exercise test
  * Measures expired gases during increasing work on cycle ergometer
  * Collects ~5000 measurements during 10-minute test:
	* Work rate (Watts)
	* Metabolic gas exchange (VO₂, VCO₂, respiratory exchange ratio)
	* Ventilatory measurements (O₂ saturation, VE, VT, respiratory rate, ventilatory equivalents)
	* Cardiovascular parameters (heart rate, ECG, blood pressure)
  * Results displayed as nine-panel plot
  * Advantages: Can provide data even for patients unable to complete other tests

### 17.4.3. CPET AND VO₂ 

**CPET provides comprehensive assessment of cardiopulmonary function and surgical risk.**

* **Key CPET parameters**:
  * **Anaerobic threshold (AT)**: VO₂ above which aerobic metabolism is supplemented by anaerobic metabolism
	* AT <10.2 mL/kg/min associated with greater perioperative risk
  * **Peak oxygen consumption (VO₂ peak)**: Maximum VO₂ achieved during test
	* Normal values: 25-40 mL/kg/min
	* VO₂ peak <15 mL/kg/min associated with higher perioperative risk
* **Exercise limitation factors**:
  * **O₂ delivery to muscles**:
	* O₂-carrying capacity (anemia reduces VO₂ peak)
	* Muscle capillary density (limiting factor in normal exercise and athletes)
	* Athletes train at altitude to increase muscle vascularization and hematocrit
  * **Cardiovascular limitations**:
	* Inability to increase cardiac output in cardiovascular disease
	* Medications limiting cardiac output (e.g., β-blockers) reduce VO₂ peak
  * **Respiratory limitations**:
	* Rarely limiting except in severe lung disease
	* Ventilatory capacity typically exceeds cardiac output increase

### 17.4.4. METS AND VO₂ 

**MET values provide a practical way to express energy cost of physical activities.**

* **Definition**: One MET (metabolic equivalent) equals basal metabolic rate = 3.5 mL O₂/kg/min
* **METs and surgical risk**:
  * Inability to climb one flight of stairs (~3 METs, VO₂ ~9.5 mL/kg/min): Higher perioperative mortality
  * Ability to climb two flights without stopping (~4 METs, VO₂ ~14 mL/kg/min): Lower perioperative risk
  * Climbing five flights (~8 METs, VO₂ >20 mL/kg/min): Minimal perioperative mortality

**Bottom Line:** Exercise testing provides objective assessment of a patient's functional capacity and surgical risk. CPET represents the gold standard with comprehensive measurement of cardiopulmonary function, while simpler tests like shuttle walk or activity questionnaires offer more accessible alternatives. Patients unable to achieve 4 METs of activity face significantly higher perioperative risk, warranting additional consideration in surgical planning and perioperative management.

## 18.1. DEVELOPMENT OF THE HEART 

**The heart develops through a complex process of tube formation, looping, and chamber separation.**

* **Early development**:
  * Primitive heart is a single tube
  * Shows grooves demarcating sinus venosus, atrium, ventricle, and bulbus cordis
  * Tube enlarges and kinks, bringing caudal end behind cephalic end
  * Sinus venosus absorbed into atrium, bulbus incorporated into ventricle
  * Final arrangement: Atria and great veins posterior to ventricles and great arteries
* **Atrioventricular canal division**:
  * Dorsal and ventral endocardial cushions grow and meet in midline
  * Divides common atrioventricular orifice into right (tricuspid) and left (mitral) orifices
* **Atrial septation**:
  * **Septum primum**: Grows from posterior/superior atrial walls toward endocardial cushions
  * Before complete fusion, hole appears in upper septum (**foramen secundum**)
  * **Septum secundum**: Develops to right of primum, never complete, has free lower edge
  * Overlapping defects form valve-like **foramen ovale**
  * After birth, usually fuses completely, leaving **fossa ovalis**
  * Remains probe-patent in ~10% of adults
* **Ventricular septation**:
  * Muscular septum grows from apex toward endocardial cushions
  * Stops short, leaving upper **interventricular foramen**
  * Spiral septum divides truncus arteriosus into aorta and pulmonary trunk
  * Grows down to ventricle, fuses with ventricular septum free border
  * Contributes **pars membranacea septi**, completing ventricular separation
* **Atrial modifications**:
  * **Right atrium**: Sinus venosus absorbed → venae cavae open separately
  * Smooth-walled part from sinus venosus, trabeculated part from primitive atrium
  * **Left atrium**: Single pulmonary venous trunk absorbed → four separate pulmonary veins
  * Smooth-walled part from absorbed pulmonary vein, trabeculated part from primitive atrium

## 18.2. DEVELOPMENT OF THE AORTIC ARCHES AND THEIR DERIVATIVES 

**The great arteries develop from a system of paired aortic arches.**

* **Aortic arch development**:
  * Truncus arteriosus gives rise to six pairs of aortic arches
  * Arches curve around pharynx, join dorsal aortae that fuse into descending aorta
  * 1st and 2nd arches disappear
  * 3rd arches become carotid arteries
  * 4th arch on right: Becomes brachiocephalic and right subclavian artery
  * 4th arch on left: Becomes definitive aortic arch and left subclavian artery
  * 5th arch is rudimentary and disappears
  * 6th arch: Connected to pulmonary trunk after truncus arteriosus splits
	* Forms right and left pulmonary arteries
	* Left side maintains connection to dorsal aorta (ductus arteriosus)
* **Recurrent laryngeal nerve pathway**:
  * Determined by asymmetrical development of aortic arches
  * Vagus nerve lateral to primitive pharynx, separated by aortic arches
  * Recurrent laryngeal branches pass medially, caudal to aortic arches
  * As neck elongates and heart migrates caudally, nerves hooked by descending arches
  * Right nerve: Loops around 4th arch (right subclavian artery)
  * Left nerve: Loops around distal 6th arch (ligamentum arteriosum)

## 18.3. FETAL CIRCULATION 

**Fetal circulation features unique vascular pathways that bypass the non-functioning lungs.**

* **Key features**:
  * Shunts oxygenated blood from placenta to brain and heart
  * Relatively desaturated blood to less essential structures
  * Oxygenated blood from placenta via umbilical vein → inferior vena cava (largely bypassing liver via ductus venosus)
  * Minimal mixing in right atrium:
	* IVC valve directs oxygenated blood through foramen ovale to left atrium
	* Deoxygenated SVC blood directed to right ventricle
  * From left atrium → left ventricle → ascending aorta → brain and heart
  * Deoxygenated blood from right ventricle largely bypasses lungs:
	* Most blood shunted via ductus arteriosus to descending aorta
	* Supplies abdominal viscera, lower limbs, and returns to placenta via umbilical arteries
* **Circulatory changes at birth**:
  * Lung expansion → increased pulmonary blood flow
  * Uncoiling of fetal pulmonary vessels → decreased pulmonary vascular resistance
  * Decreased right atrial pressure, increased left atrial pressure
  * Pressure changes close foramen ovale (septum primum and secundum apposition)
  * Increased oxygen tension causes ductus arteriosus contraction and functional closure
  * Ductus arteriosus complete obliteration takes 2-3 months
  * Umbilical vessels thrombose and obliterate in 3-4 weeks after cord division

## 18.4. CONGENITAL ABNORMALITIES OF THE HEART AND GREAT VESSELS 

**Complex cardiac development leads to numerous potential congenital abnormalities.**

* **Positional anomalies**:
  * **Dextroposition**: Heart and vessels as mirror image to normal
  * May be associated with situs inversus (reversal of all intra-abdominal organs)
* **Septal defects**:
  * **Patent foramen ovale**: Present in ~10% of subjects
  * **Atrial septal defect**:
	* Ostium secundum defect: High in atrial wall, relatively easy surgical closure
	* Ostium primum defect: Above atrioventricular boundary, may be associated with ventricular septal defect
  * **Ventricular septal defect**: May be so large that ventricles form single cavity (trilocular heart)
* **Pulmonary stenosis**:
  * May affect pulmonary trunk, valve, or right ventricular infundibulum
  * If combined with septal defect: Right ventricular hypertrophy creates high pressure
  * Shunts deoxygenated blood through defect into left heart → cyanosis at birth
* **Tetralogy of Fallot**:
  * Most common cyanotic congenital abnormality
  * Results from unequal truncus arteriosus division by spiral septum
  * Four features:
	1. Pulmonary stenosis
	2. Ventricular septal defect
	3. Overriding aorta (positioned over both ventricles)
	4. Right ventricular hypertrophy
  * Cyanosis from shunting desaturated blood from right ventricle to left ventricle and directly to aorta
* **Patent ductus arteriosus**:
  * Relatively common congenital defect
  * If uncorrected: Progressive left heart hypertrophy and pulmonary hypertension
* **Aortic coarctation**:
  * Abnormality in ductus arteriosus obliteration process
  * May involve extensive aortic obstruction from left subclavian to ductus
  * More commonly affects short segment near ligamentum arteriosum
  * Collateral circulation develops:
	* Via scapular arteries to intercostals
	* Via internal thoracic to inferior epigastric arteries
  * Clinically: Enlarged vessels around scapula, rib notching from hypertrophied intercostals
* **Other anomalies**:
  * Abnormal aortic arch development: Right-sided or double arch
  * Abnormal right subclavian arising from dorsal aorta, passing behind esophagus (dysphagia lusoria)
  * Aortopulmonary window: Incomplete truncus division

**Bottom Line:** Cardiovascular development involves complex processes of tube formation, looping, septation, and remodeling. The fetal circulation features unique pathways including the foramen ovale and ductus arteriosus that bypass the non-functioning lungs. Abnormalities in these developmental processes result in a wide spectrum of congenital heart defects ranging from simple septal defects to complex malformations like tetralogy of Fallot, with significant implications for oxygen delivery and hemodynamics.
