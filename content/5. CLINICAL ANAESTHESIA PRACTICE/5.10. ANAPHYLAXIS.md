## 1. DEFINITION AND PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

### 1.1. DEFINITION OF ANAPHYLAXIS

**Anaphylaxis is a life-threatening systemic allergic reaction** characterized by:
* Rapid onset with potential for severe or fatal outcomes
* Involvement of multiple organ systems
* Release of vasoactive mediators from mast cells and basophils
### 1.2. PATHOPHYSIOLOGIC MECHANISMS

**The release of vasoactive mediators causes:*** ↑ Mucous membrane secretions
* ↑ Bronchial smooth muscle tone
* ↑ Capillary permeability
* ↓ Vascular smooth muscle tone (vasodilation)

**Primary mediators include:**
* Histamine
* Tryptase
* Leukotrienes
* Prostaglandins
* Platelet-activating factor
* Various cytokines

### 1.3. ANAPHYLAXIS VS ANAPHYLACTOID REACTIONS

#### 1.3.1. ANAPHYLAXIS (TRUE ANAPHYLAXIS)

* **IgE-mediated type I hypersensitivity reaction**
* Sensitization occurs with prior exposure to an antigen, which produces antigen-specific IgE antibodies that bind to Fc receptors on mast cells and basophils
* Upon re-exposure to the antigen, IgE antibodies cross-link Fc receptors causing degranulation and release of stored mediators
* Reaction is independent of dose
#### 1.3.2. ANAPHYLACTOID REACTIONS

* Direct activation of mast cells and basophils by non-IgE mechanisms, or activation of the complement system
* May occur on first exposure to an antigen
* Reaction is dose-dependent
* Also called non-IgE-mediated anaphylaxis

💡 **Clinical Pearl:*
* Anaphylactic and anaphylactoid reactions present similarly and are treated IDENTICALLY, despite different underlying mechanisms.

**Bottom Line:*
* Anaphylaxis involves IgE-mediated mast cell degranulation requiring prior sensitization, while anaphylactoid reactions activate mast cells directly without requiring prior exposure, but both present clinically the same way and receive identical treatment.

## 2. CLINICAL MANIFESTATIONS

### 2.1. SIGNS AND SYMPTOMS

The classic triad of anaphylaxis includes:
* Cardiovascular collapse
* Bronchospasm
* Angioedema, flushing or rash affecting skin and mucosa

**Specific manifestations by system:**

![Untitled](ANAPHYLAXIS_Untitled.png_efbbc8.png)

**Cardiovascular:**
* Hypotension
* Tachycardia
* Arrhythmias
* Cardiovascular collapse

**Respiratory:**
* Bronchospasm
* Laryngeal edema
* Increased airway pressures
* Hypoxemia

**Cutaneous:**
* Flushing
* Urticaria
* Angioedema

**Gastrointestinal:**
* Nausea
* Vomiting
* Diarrhea
* Abdominal pain

### 2.2. TIMING AND PRESENTATION

* More than 90% of reactions occur within 3 minutes of exposure to the trigger
* Can be delayed by hours in some cases
* Presentation may be variable and incomplete (not all systems may be affected)* ⚠️ Cutaneous signs may be absent in up to 20% of perioperative anaphylaxis cases, especially in severe reactions
### 2.3. DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS

**Conditions that may mimic anaphylaxis include:**
**Causes of tachycardia:**
* Direct causes:
  * Pain or inadequate depth of anesthesia  * Drug-induced (atropine, glycopyrrolate, high partial pressures of desflurane)
* Indirect causes (compensation for hypotension):
  * Hypovolemic shock (hemorrhage)  * Septic shock  * Cardiogenic shock  * Obstructive shock (pulmonary embolism - fat, air, amniotic fluid, bone cement)
**Causes of high airway pressures:**
* Bronchospasm from asthma
* Endobronchial intubation
* Tension pneumothorax
* Pneumoperitoneum causing diaphragmatic splinting
💡 **Clinical Pearl:*
* While there are other explanations for each sign in isolation, when presenting together in quick succession, anaphylaxis is the likely diagnosis.

**Bottom Line:*
* Anaphylaxis typically presents as a constellation of cardiovascular collapse, bronchospasm, and cutaneous manifestations within minutes of trigger exposure, though presentation may be variable and must be differentiated from other causes of acute decompensation.

## 3. PERIOPERATIVE TRIGGERS AND RISK FACTORS

### 3.1. COMMON PERIOPERATIVE TRIGGERS

The sixth national audit project (NAP6) identified the four most common triggers of perioperative anaphylaxis as:
![Untitled](ANAPHYLAXIS_Untitled_1.png_731fbe.png)

* **Antibiotics (47%)**  * Teicoplanin comprised 12% of antibiotic exposures but caused 38% of antibiotic-induced anaphylaxis  
* **Neuromuscular blocking agents (NMBAs) (33%)**  * Suxamethonium-induced anaphylaxis mainly presented with bronchospasm and was twice as likely as with other NMBAs  * Atracurium-anaphylaxis mainly presented with hypotension  * Non-depolarizing NMBAs had similar incidences to each other  
* **Chlorhexidine (9%)*** **Patent Blue dye (4.5%)*** **Others:**
  * Latex (consider this if the reaction develops after the operation has started)  * Intravenous anesthetics such as thiopental  * Colloids  * Opioids
💡 **Clinical Pearl:*
* The timing of reaction can provide clues to the trigger - reactions immediately after induction often implicate induction agents or antibiotics, while delayed reactions may suggest latex or chlorhexidine.

### 3.2. LATEX ALLERGY

**Risk assessment:*
* Obtain a careful history for patients at high risk:
* Healthcare workers (frequent exposures)
* Children with spina bifida (multiple prior medical procedures/exposures)
* Patients with urogenital abnormalities (history of multiple urogenital catheters)
* Patients with food allergies to tropical fruits (mango, kiwi, avocado, passion fruit, bananas) and other cross-reactive foods (fig, chestnuts) due to shared antigens
**Preventive measures for latex-allergic patients:**
* Schedule as first case of the day
* Establish latex-free environment with latex-free equipment and supplies
* Remove tops of multi-dose vials when drawing up drugs for patients with significant latex allergy
* Consider prophylactic steroids and/or H1 blockers
⚠️ **Safety Warning:*
* Always "prepare for the worst, hope for the best" when managing patients with known or suspected latex allergy.

### 3.3. HIGH-RISK PATIENT POPULATIONS

* Patients with previous history of anaphylaxis
* Patients with multiple allergies
* Patients with atopy (asthma, eczema, allergic rhinitis)
* Patients with specific occupational exposures (healthcare workers)
* Patients with multiple previous surgeries (increased exposure to potential allergens)
**Bottom Line:*
* Antibiotics, neuromuscular blocking agents, chlorhexidine, and patent blue dye are the most common perioperative anaphylaxis triggers. Patients with latex allergy require special precautions, including a latex-free environment and consideration as the first case of the day.

## 4. MANAGEMENT

### 4.1. ACUTE PHASE MANAGEMENT

1. **Stop administration of offending antigen** (muscle relaxants, latex, antibiotics, colloids, blood, contrast, etc.)2. **Notify surgeon AND call for help**3. **Increase FiO2 to 100%**4. **Consider discontinuation of agents that may augment hypotension** in hypotensive patients   * Inhaled anesthetics cause vasodilation   * Narcotic infusions suppress sympathetic response   * Give other amnestic agent (e.g., midazolam, ketamine)
2. **Give IV fluid bolus**   * May require many liters, 2-4 L or more to compensate for vasodilation and hypotension
3. **Give Epinephrine (Adrenaline)**   * α-1 effects support BP; β-2 effects cause bronchial smooth muscle relaxation   * Start with 10-100 mcg IV boluses for hypotension; escalate as needed   * Start early epinephrine infusion (0.02-0.3 mcg/kg/min)   * If no IV access, give 0.3-0.5 mg IM in anterolateral thigh, repeat q5-15 min   * ACLS doses (0.1-1 mg) for cardiovascular collapse
4. **Consider vasopressin bolus (IV 2U) or norepinephrine infusion** for refractory hypotension	- If taking beta blockers - IV Glucagon 1mg
5. **Treat bronchospasm with albuterol and epinephrine** (if severe)
⚠️ **Safety Warning:*
* Do not delay epinephrine administration while waiting for cutaneous signs to appear, as these may be absent in severe reactions or in sedated/anesthetized patients.

💡 **Clinical Pearl:*
* Epinephrine works on multiple targets to reverse anaphylaxis:
* α-receptor agonism counteracts peripheral vasodilation and vasoplegic shock
* β-receptor agonism causes bronchodilation, improves cardiac output, and reduces histamine release by acting on mast cell β2 receptors

### 4.2. SECONDARY MANAGEMENT

**Airway management:**
* Consider intubation, especially if signs of angioedema
**Invasive monitoring:**
* Place large-bore IVs, arterial line, central venous catheter, Foley catheter as indicated by severity
**Adjunctive medications** (after stabilization with epinephrine):* **H1-blocker:*
* Diphenhydramine 0.5-1 mg/kg IV; Chlorphenamine 10mg iv
* **H2-blocker:*
* Ranitidine (not a first-line agent, but low risk of harm)* **Steroids:*
* To decrease airway swelling and prevent recurrent symptoms in biphasic anaphylaxis  * Hydrocortisone 0.25-1 g IV (200mg IV), or
  * Methylprednisolone 1-2 g IV
### 4.3. MONITORING AND DISPOSITION

* Continuous monitoring of hemodynamic parameters, oxygenation, and ventilation
* Consider monitoring patient for 24 hours post-recovery due to risk of biphasic anaphylaxis
* Consider keeping intubated and sedated in severe cases
* ICU admission for patients with severe reactions or requiring vasopressors
### 4.4. LABORATORY INVESTIGATIONS

**Send the following labs to confirm diagnosis:*** **Serum tryptase:*
* Peaks < 60 minutes post event (although can be negative in ~35% of patients)  * Samples should be taken:
	
* As soon as the patient is stable	* 1-2 hours after reaction onset (peak level)	* 24 hours after (baseline level)* **Serum histamine:*
* Peaks < 30 minutes post event
**Bottom Line:*
* Management of anaphylaxis requires prompt recognition, immediate discontinuation of suspected triggers, administration of epinephrine and fluids, supportive care, and systematic monitoring for recurrence. Laboratory testing should include tryptase levels at specific time intervals to confirm the diagnosis.

## 5. POST-CRISIS CARE

### 5.1. BIPHASIC REACTIONS

* Recurrence of symptoms after initial resolution
* Can occur in up to 5% of anaphylaxis cases
* May occur more than 24 hours after the first reaction
* Generally less severe than initial reaction* ⚠️ Provides rationale for extended monitoring period
### 5.2. PATIENT REFERRAL AND FOLLOW-UP

* Refer for postoperative allergic testing
* Referral should be made to allergy clinic for formal investigation and identification of the culprit agent
* Provide patients with detailed information about the reaction and suspected triggers
* Consider providing medical alert bracelet or similar identification
### 5.3. DOCUMENTATION AND REPORTING

* Document the reaction in detail, including:
  * Clinical presentation
  * Timing relative to administration of potential triggers
  * Treatment given and response
  * Laboratory results
  * Suspected agents
* Report the reaction via the Yellow Card Scheme to the Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) or relevant national reporting system
* Ensure the reaction is prominently noted in the patient's medical record
* Provide clear communication to primary care and other providers about the suspected allergic triggers
**Bottom Line:
* Post-crisis care involves monitoring for biphasic reactions, arranging formal allergy testing to identify the specific trigger, thorough documentation, and reporting the event to relevant authorities. These measures help protect the patient during future anesthetic exposures and contribute to epidemiological understanding of perioperative anaphylaxis.
