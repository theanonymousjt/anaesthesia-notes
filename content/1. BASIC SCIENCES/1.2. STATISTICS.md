# 1. FOUNDATIONS OF BIOSTATISTICS

## 1.1. STATISTICAL FOUNDATIONS IN SCIENTIFIC MEDICINE

## 1.2. DATA STRUCTURE AND TYPES

### 1.2.1. INTERVAL DATA (CONTINUOUS AND DISCRETE)

* **Interval Data**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Equal distance between successive intervals
  * Example: The difference between 15 and 10 is the same as the difference between 25 and 20
  * Two subtypes:
* **Discrete Interval Data**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Can have only integer values
  * Examples: number of living children, parity, number of teeth
  
* **Continuous Interval Data**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]]:
  * Measured on a continuum and can be a decimal fraction
  * Examples: blood pressure, temperature, height, weight
  * Can be described to any desired level of precision (e.g., 136, 136.1, or 136.14 mm Hg)
  * Same statistical techniques used for both discrete and continuous interval data

### 1.2.2. CATEGORICAL DATA (NOMINAL AND ORDINAL)

* **Categorical Data**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]]:
  * Observations placed into two or more discrete subsets
  * Numeric values assigned as labels to categories for statistical analysis
  
* **Dichotomous (Binary) Data**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]]:
  * Allow only two possible values
  * Examples: mortality (dead/alive), gender (male/female)
  
* **Ordinal Data**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]]:
  * Three or more categories that can be logically ranked or ordered
  * Ranking indicates only relative, not absolute, differences between values
  * Example: American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) Physical Status score
  * The difference between ASA I and II is not necessarily the same as between III and IV
  * Often treated as interval data for statistical analysis, though specific techniques for ordinal data may be preferable
  
* **Nominal Data**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]]:
  * Categories with no logical ordering
  * Example: Eye colors (blue, hazel, brown) might be assigned numbers 1, 2, 3, but these values have no inherent meaning

![Untitled](STATISTICS_Untitled.png_79fd41.png)

**Bottom Line:** Understanding data types is essential for selecting appropriate statistical techniques. Interval data (continuous or discrete) represent measurements on a scale with equal intervals, while categorical data classify observations into groups that are either ordered (ordinal) or unordered (nominal).

## 1.3. DESCRIPTIVE STATISTICS

### 1.3.1. MEASURES OF CENTRAL TENDENCY

* **Arithmetic Mean**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Average of the numbers in the data set
  * Denoted by x̄ where n is the count of objects in the sample
  * If all population values could be obtained, the population mean (μ) could be calculated similarly
  * The sample mean is the unbiased, consistent, minimum variance, sufficient estimator of the population mean
  * Affected by extreme values
* **Median**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * The middlemost number that divides the sample into two equal parts
  * Found by ranking the sample values from lowest to highest and counting halfway
  * The concept of ranking is used in nonparametric statistics
  * A virtue of the median is that it is hardly affected by a few extremely high or low values
  * Equal to the mean in a normal distribution
* **Mode**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * The most popular (frequently occurring) number in a sample
  * A sample may be bior polymodal with more than one most common value
  * These modes may be widely separated or adjacent
  * Raw data should be inspected for this unusual appearance
  * Often mentioned in discussions but rarely used in statistical practice
  * Equal to mean and median in a normal distribution

### 1.3.2. MEASURES OF SPREAD/VARIABILITY

* **Variance**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Describes the spread of a set of numbers by calculating the average squared distance from each number to the center
  * Population variance is a parameter represented by σ²
  * Sample variance is used in place of population variance (since population variance is usually unknown)
  * Sample variance: VAR = SD² = Σ(xi - x̄)²/(n-1)
  * Using (n-1) rather than n as the divisor makes the sample variance an unbiased estimator of the population variance
  * Units are squared units of the original observations
* **Standard Deviation (SD)**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Square root of the variance
  * Has the same units as the original observations
  * Sample SD and population SD (σ) are square roots of respective variances
  * In a normal distribution:
	* Mean ± 1 SD encompasses roughly 68% of the sample and population members
	* Mean ± 2 SD encompasses roughly 95% of values
	* Mean ± 3 SD encompasses roughly 99% of values
* **Standard Error of the Mean (SE)**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]]:
  * Standard deviation of sample means from the same population
  * Calculated as SD/√n
  * Gets smaller as sample size increases
  * Used to specify confidence intervals around the sample mean
  * An inferential statistic unlike other descriptive statistics
* **Degrees of Freedom**    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]]:
  * When calculating a mean value, at least two data points are required
  * The first is a reference point, and the second is the first 'free' variable
  * Therefore, degrees of freedom is always the sample size (n) minus one (n-1)
  * Becomes less relevant as sample size increases but remains good practice

### 1.3.3. NORMAL DISTRIBUTION AND OTHER DISTRIBUTIONS

* **Normal Distribution** characteristics    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]],    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]]:
  * Bell-curve shaped distribution
  * Mode, mean, and median are all equal
  * Equal shaped tails on either side of the middle
  * Examples of normally distributed variables: height, weight, IQ
  * Normal distributions allow **parametric** tests to be used in statistical analysis
* **Skewed Distributions**    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]]:
  * Asymmetrical distribution with a long tail on either the positive (positive skew) or negative (negative skew) side
  * Skew drags the mean away from the median and mode
  * Data transformation techniques:
	* 'Log'-ing the data can sometimes convert a positive skew to a more manageable normal distribution
	* A negative skew can occasionally be 'normalised' by squaring the dataset

![Normal vs Skewed Distributions](statistics_skewed_distributions_k7m2p9_1734159135000.png)

**Bottom Line:** Descriptive statistics summarize key properties of data sets through measures of central tendency (mean, median, mode) and spread (variance, standard deviation). Understanding the normal distribution and its properties is crucial for applying appropriate statistical tests, as most biologic variables follow this pattern.

## 1.4. PROBABILITY CONCEPTS

### 1.4.1. BASIC PROBABILITY THEORY

* Probability is the likelihood of an event occurring, expressed as a value between 0 (impossible) and 1 (certain)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]].
* In medical statistics, probability forms the basis for inferential statistics and hypothesis testing    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]].
* Probability distributions describe how values of a variable are distributed    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Normal distribution (Gaussian) - most common in biological systems
  * Binomial distribution - used for binary outcomes
  * Chi-square distribution - used for categorical data analysis
  * t-distribution - used when sample sizes are small or population standard deviation is unknown
  * F-distribution - used in analysis of variance

### 1.4.2. CONFIDENCE INTERVALS

* A **confidence interval (CI)** is an interval estimate of a population parameter    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]].
* A 95% CI describes the range that has a 95% chance of capturing the true population mean    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]].
* Technical definition: If the experiment were done over and over again, 95 of each 100 CIs would be expected to contain the true value of the mean    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]].
* CIs have the form: summary statistic ± (confidence factor) × (precision factor)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]].
* For interval data from a normally distributed population    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]]:
  * The precision factor is the standard error of the mean (SEM = SD/√n)
  * The confidence factors are obtained from the normal distribution or t-distribution
  * For 95% CI, the confidence factor is approximately 1.96 (often rounded to 2)
  * A 95% CI includes 1.96 standard deviations either side of the sample mean    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]]

![Confidence Intervals Visualization](statistics_confidence_intervals_8t4q2w_1734159135000.png)
* Larger percentage confidence intervals (e.g., 99%) have a wider range between upper and lower values    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]].
* Even when sample size is only five or greater, the coefficients 1, 2, and 3 (for 68%, 95%, and 99% confidence) provide sufficiently accurate approximations for quick mental calculations    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]].

### 1.4.3. STANDARD ERROR

* The **standard error of the mean (SEM)** is the standard deviation of sample means from the same population    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]],    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]].
* Formula: SEM = SD/√n    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]],    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]].
* The SEM is smaller than the SD and decreases as sample size increases    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]].
* SE and SD are often confused in research reports in the form "mean ± number"    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]]:
  * When describing the spread, scatter, or dispersion of the sample, use SD
  * When describing the precision with which the population mean is known, use SE
* The central limit theorem states that the SE can always be used (if the sample size is sufficiently large) to specify CIs around the sample mean    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]].
* This is true even if the population distribution is so different from normal that SD cannot be used to characterize the dispersion of the population members    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]].
* General guidelines for necessary sample size for using SEM based on central limit theorem    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]]:
  * For interval data, 25 and above is large enough
  * 4 and below is too small

**Bottom Line:** Probability concepts underpin statistical inference. Confidence intervals provide a range estimate of population parameters based on sample statistics, with the standard error serving as a measure of precision for these estimates. The 95% CI is commonly used in medical research and indicates the range likely to contain the true population parameter.

# 2. RESEARCH DESIGN AND METHODOLOGY

## 2.1. TYPES OF RESEARCH DESIGN

### 2.1.1. LONGITUDINAL VS. CROSS-SECTIONAL STUDIES

* **Longitudinal Studies**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Study of changes over time
  * Example: Investigating hemodynamic effects of different drugs during anesthesia
* **Cross-sectional Studies**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Describe a phenomenon at a certain point in time
  * Example: Reporting the frequency with which certain drugs are used during anesthesia

### 2.1.2. PROSPECTIVE VS. RETROSPECTIVE STUDIES

* **Prospective (Cohort) Studies**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Assemble groups of subjects by some input characteristic thought to change an output characteristic
  * Example: Dividing patients undergoing neurologic surgery into two groups, giving two different opioids (remifentanil or fentanyl), and following for development of perioperative myocardial infarction
  * Further divided into:
	* Studies of deliberate intervention
	  * Investigator performs a deliberate intervention
	  * Example: Choosing several anesthetic maintenance techniques and comparing incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting
	* Observational studies
	  * Investigator merely observes
	  * Example: Observing patients receiving anesthetics chosen at the discretion of each patient's anesthesiologist and comparing risk of postoperative nausea and vomiting
* **Retrospective (Case-control) Studies**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Gather subjects by an output characteristic (status after an event)
  * Example: Identifying patients who suffered perioperative myocardial infarction from hospital records, then selecting similar patients who did not suffer infarction, and comparing relative use of different opioids
  * Primary tool of epidemiology
  * Can identify association between input and output characteristic, but causal link is more difficult to specify

### 2.1.3. RANDOMIZED CONTROLLED TRIALS

* **Definition:** A longitudinal, prospective study of deliberate intervention with concurrent controls    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]].
* **Types of controls** in studies of deliberate intervention    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]]:
  * **Concurrent controls**
	* Simultaneous parallel control group
	* Self-control study (crossover design)
  * **Historical controls**
	* Previous studies
	* Literature reports
* **Novel Trial Designs**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]]:
  * Adaptive design
  * Platform trials
  * Stepped-wedge trials
  * Noninferiority and equivalence trials
  * Pragmatic trials
* The randomized, controlled clinical trial is the most potent scientific tool for evaluating medical treatment    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]].
* Randomization into treatment groups is relied upon to equally weight the subjects for baseline attributes that might predispose or protect from the outcome of interest    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]].

### 2.1.4. OBSERVATIONAL STUDIES

* In an observational study, the investigator does not control the intervention    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]].
* May reveal differences among treatment groups, but unclear whether differences are due to treatments or other patient differences    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]].
* Comparison of cohorts with interventions not assigned under experimental control is a prominent technique of epidemiology    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]].
* The same principles apply to animal experimentation as to human studies    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]].
* **Hierarchy of Evidence** from strongest to weakest    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]]:
  1. 1a - Systematic review and Meta-analysis
  2. 1b - Randomized controlled trial
  3. 2a - Non-randomized but well-designed controlled trial
  4. 2b - Cohort study
  5. 3 - Case-control study, Cross-sectional study, or Case series
  6. 4 - Expert opinion

**Bottom Line:** Research design significantly impacts the validity and applicability of study findings. Randomized controlled trials provide the strongest evidence for causal relationships, while observational studies may identify associations but cannot definitively establish causation. The hierarchy of evidence reflects the relative strength of different study designs.

## 2.2. MANAGEMENT OF BIAS

### 2.2.1. SAMPLING METHODS

* **Population** in statistical language refers to any target group (animate or inanimate) of interest    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]].
  * Examples: mothers in first stage of labor, head-trauma victims undergoing craniotomy, cell cultures, hospital bills
* **Sample** is a subset of the target population    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]].
  * Samples are taken because it's impossible to observe the entire population
  * Researchers wish to generalize from the small sample to the entire population
* **Strata** are subgroups within a population    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]].
  * Example: Head-injury patients can have open/closed wounds, coexisting diseases, normal/increased intracranial pressure
  * Researchers often restrict the target population to increase homogeneity
  * Must balance restriction against desire to have results applicable to broadest possible population
* **Sampling methods**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]]:
  * **Simple random sampling**: Every subject in population has same chance of being observed (best for representative sample)
  * **Stratified random sampling**: Random sampling from each stratum (appropriate when several strata are important)
  * **Cluster and systematic sampling**: Other specialized methods
  * **Convenience sampling**: Using patients who happen to show up at researchers' hospitals (most common in clinical anesthesia studies)
	* Subject to nuances of surgical schedule, referring physician goodwill, patient willingness
	* At best representative of patients at that institution, with no assurance of similarity to patients elsewhere
	* Also the rule in studying new anesthetic drugs (typically performed on healthy, young volunteers)

### 2.2.2. EXPERIMENTAL CONSTRAINTS

* Researchers must define conditions to which sample members will be exposed    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]].
* Decision between rigid standardization vs. adjusted/individualized conditions    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]]:
  * **Fixed approach**: Standardizing treatment groups by fixed doses simplifies research
  * **Risks of standardization**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]]:
	* Fixed dose may produce excessive side effects in some patients
	* Fixed dose may be therapeutically insufficient in others
	* Standardized treatment may be so artificial it lacks broad clinical relevance, even if superior
  * Researchers should carefully choose and report adjustment/individualization of experimental treatments    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]].

### 2.2.3. RANDOMIZATION TECHNIQUES

* **Random allocation** prevents selection bias and minimizes systematic differences among experimental groups [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]].
* Methods that should **not** be used for group assignment    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Design of Clinical Trial|ⓘ]]:
  * Investigator whim
  * Day of the week
  * Referring physician preference
  * Patient wish
  * Assignment of previous subject
  * Availability of study drug
  * Hospital chart number
  * Other arbitrary criteria
* **Benefits of random allocation**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Prevents selection bias by the researcher
  * Minimizes the possibility of systematic differences between experimental groups
  * Disarms critics' complaints about research methods
* **Implementation of randomization**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Most commonly accomplished using computer-generated random numbers
  * **Concealment of random allocation** is critical    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
	* Research personnel should not know group assignment of next patient to be recruited
	* Failure to conceal leads to biases in clinical study results
	* Well-described techniques (centralized random allocation, opaque sealed envelopes, etc.) prevent this form of selection bias

### 2.2.4. BLINDING AND OTHER BIAS MANAGEMENT

* **Blinding** refers to masking experimental group assignment from patient and experimenters    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]].
* **Concealment of random allocation**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Starts before patient enrollment
  * Prevents referring physicians or research team members from manipulating entrance of specific patients
  * Manipulation often stems from personal opinions about relative merits of treatment groups
* **Patient blinding**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Each subject should remain ignorant of assigned treatment after entering the protocol
  * Prevents placebo effect from biasing outcome based on treatment reputation
* **Investigator/observer blinding**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Researcher's knowledge of treatment assignment can bias protocol administration and data recording
  * True for clinical, animal, and in vitro research
  * Those who observe data cannot trust themselves to record impartially if treatment group is known
* **Terminology for blinding**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Terms "single-blind" and "double-blind" commonly used but inconsistently applied
  * Researchers should carefully plan and report exactly who is blinded
* **Types of bias in research**    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]]:
  * **Selection Bias**: Cohort selected not actually representative of the whole population
  * **Allocation Bias**: People not allocated to groups in genuinely random fashion
  * **Performance Bias**: Different care depending on group allocation (reduced by adequate blinding)
  * **Detection Bias**: Differences due to equipment faults or reporting bias (reduced by standardizing equipment and measurement)
  * **Attrition Bias**: Differing levels of patients dropping out from each group (reduced by 'intention-to-treat' analysis)
  * **Reporting Bias**: Results influence how information is reported (e.g., negative results not published or published in smaller journals)

**Bottom Line:** Bias management is crucial for valid research. Key strategies include proper sampling methods, appropriate experimental constraints, true randomization with allocation concealment, and comprehensive blinding. These techniques work together to minimize selection, performance, detection, attrition, and reporting biases that could otherwise invalidate research findings.

# 3. HYPOTHESIS TESTING AND INFERENTIAL STATISTICS

## 3.1. HYPOTHESIS TESTING FRAMEWORK

### 3.1.1. NULL AND ALTERNATIVE HYPOTHESES

* In a statistical hypothesis, statements are made about the relationship among parameters of one or more populations    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]].
* The typical statistical hypothesis is established in a somewhat rote fashion regardless of methods, materials, or goals [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]].
* The algebraic formulation creates two mutually exclusive statements about population parameters    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * **Null Hypothesis (H₀)**: The hypothesis of no difference between conditions (e.g., H₀: f₁ = f₂)
  * **Alternative Hypothesis (Hₐ)**: Usually nondirectional (e.g., Hₐ: f₁ ≠ f₂), meaning either f₁ < f₂ or f₁ > f₂
  * This is a more conservative approach than assuming the inequality can only be in one direction (two-tailed vs. one-tailed)
* **The null hypothesis** is the baseline assumption in statistical analysis    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]].
  * It assumes there is no difference between the groups being analyzed
  * It assumes they are from the same population
  * The study seeks to see if a difference exists, allowing researchers to disprove or 'reject' the null hypothesis
* **Logic of null hypothesis testing**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Assume the null hypothesis is true (even though the goal is to show a difference)
  * Examine the consequences by analyzing sample values for variables of interest
  * Calculate a sample test statistic with an associated probability (P value)
  * Choose the level of significance (probability level considered too low to warrant support of null hypothesis)
  * If sample values are sufficiently unlikely by chance (P less than significance level), reject null hypothesis
  * Otherwise, do not reject null hypothesis

### 3.1.2. STATISTICAL SIGNIFICANCE

* Statistical significance refers to the likelihood that an observed difference between groups is due to chance    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]].
* A result is considered statistically significant when the P value is less than the pre-specified significance level (commonly 0.05)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]].
* **Important limitations of statistical significance**    [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#5. Practice-Based Learning and Improvement|ⓘ]]:
  * Statistical significance does not always equate to clinical importance
  * Small, clinically unimportant differences can be statistically significant with large sample sizes
  * Even small P values don't guarantee the finding is true or important

### 3.1.3. VALUES AND THEIR INTERPRETATION

* The **P value** is the probability of obtaining results at least as extreme as those observed, assuming the null hypothesis is true    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]].
* Common threshold is P<0.05, meaning there's less than a 5% chance the observed difference is due to chance alone, assuming the null hypothesis is true    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]].
* **Common misinterpretation** of P values    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Most physicians incorrectly believe that if a statistical test is significant at P < 0.05, there is 95% or greater certainty that the null hypothesis is incorrect
  * This is categorically wrong because the P value is calculated on the assumption that the null hypothesis is true
  * P value cannot be a direct measure of the probability that the null hypothesis is false
* The **correct interpretation** of P<0.05    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]]:
  * "*It is too unlikely that the observed result is due to pure chance*"
  * We're accepting that in 1 out of 20 cases, there may be a false-positive result
* The odds that the alternative hypothesis is true depend on    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * The data of the experiment
  * The prior plausibility of the hypothesis
  * An implausible hypothesis (e.g., 19-to-1 odds against) still remains implausible (11% chance of a real effect) even with P = 0.05 in an experiment

**Bottom Line:** Hypothesis testing is a structured approach to determining whether observed differences are likely real or due to chance. It involves formulating null and alternative hypotheses, collecting data, calculating test statistics, and interpreting P values. However, statistical significance (P<0.05) doesn't guarantee clinical importance or absolute certainty about conclusions.

## 3.2. STATISTICAL ERRORS

### 3.2.1. TYPE I (ALPHA) AND TYPE II (BETA) ERRORS

* **Type I Error (Alpha Error)**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Wrongly rejecting the null hypothesis (false-positive)
  * Concluding there is a difference when there isn't one
  * Probability is denoted by alpha (α)
  * Researchers choose alpha before collecting data
  * Most common choice is α = 0.05
  * Consequence of α = 0.05: If there's no difference between conditions and the experiment is repeated 20 times, a mistaken conclusion of difference would occur once (5% of 20)
  * The smaller the chosen alpha, the smaller the risk of Type I error
* **Type II Error (Beta Error)**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Failing to reject a false null hypothesis (false-negative)
  * Concluding there is no difference when there actually is one
  * Probability is denoted by beta (β)
  * Trade-off with Type I error: The smaller the alpha, the greater the chance of a Type II error
  * This prevents researchers from automatically choosing a very small alpha
* **Error scenarios** can be displayed in a two-way truth table [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:

| | Reality: Conditions Equal | Reality: Conditions Not Equal |
|---|---|---|
| **Conclusion: Conditions Equal** | Correct conclusion | False-negative (Type II/beta error) |
| **Conclusion: Conditions Not Equal** | False-positive (Type I/alpha error) | Correct conclusion |

![Type I and II Errors](statistics_type_errors_hypothesis_3w9k5n_1734159135000.png)

### 3.2.2. STATISTICAL POWER

* **Power** = 1 - β    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]],    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]]
* Represents the ability of a test to detect a significant difference between study groups when one truly exists    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]].
* Typically, a power of 80-90% is desirable for clinical studies    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]].
* **Factors affecting Type II error probability** (and thus power) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  1. **Significance level (α)**: Lower α increases Type II error risk
  2. **Population variability**: More variability increases Type II error risk (analogous to listening to a noisy radio broadcast - more static makes word discrimination harder)
  3. **Sample size**: Larger samples decrease Type II error risk
  4. **Effect size**: The magnitude of difference between conditions (most important factor)
	 * Type II error probability varies from very high (small difference) to extremely low (large difference)

### 3.2.3. SAMPLE SIZE CALCULATIONS

* Sample size planning has become an important part of research design for controlled clinical trials    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]].
* Some controlled trials claiming no advantage of new therapies lacked sufficient statistical power to detect important therapeutic improvements    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]].
* **Formula for calculating sample size** when comparing means of two populations    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:

  n = 2(zα + zβ)²σ²/(μ₁ - μ₂)²

  * z values are taken from normal probability distribution representing assumptions about alpha and beta
  * σ is the assumed common standard deviation
  * μ's are the assumed population values
* **Four options for increasing statistical power** (decreasing Type II error)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  1. Raise alpha (increases Type I error risk)
  2. Reduce population variability
  3. Make the sample bigger
  4. Make the difference between conditions greater
  5. Under most circumstances, only sample size can be varied

![Factors Affecting Statistical Power](statistics_power_factors_7h2m4p_1734159135000.png)

* A **power calculation** before the study determines required sample sizes    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]].
* Some published research still fails the test of adequate sample size planning    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Understanding statistical errors is crucial for research design and interpretation. Type I errors (false positives) are controlled by setting an appropriate significance level (typically α=0.05), while Type II errors (false negatives) are minimized by ensuring adequate power through appropriate sample size calculations. These calculations must account for expected effect size, population variability, and desired confidence levels.

# 4. STATISTICAL TESTS AND APPLICATIONS

## 4.1. TESTS FOR INTERVAL DATA

### 4.1.1. T-TESTS (PAIRED AND UNPAIRED)

* **Student's t-test** was a significant advance in statistical inference, allowing hypothesis testing when population variance is unknown    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]].
* Most common use: comparing mean values of two populations    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]].
* **Paired t-test (One-sample t-test)**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Used when each subject has two measurements (e.g., before and after a drug)
  * Each control measurement is paired with a measurement in the same patient
  * Reduces variability and increases statistical power
  * The difference (d𝑖 = x𝑖 − y𝑖) of each pair is calculated, and the average (d̄) is computed
  * Test statistic: t = d̄/(SEd̄), where SEd̄ is the standard error of d̄
  * Degrees of freedom = sample size minus one
* **Unpaired t-test (Two-sample t-test)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Used when measurements are taken on two separate groups
  * Example: One group receives treatment, another receives no treatment
  * No matching of individual measurements between groups
  * Test statistic: t = (x̄ - ȳ)/SE, where SE is a weighted average of the standard deviations
  * Degrees of freedom = sum of subjects in two groups minus two
* All t statistics follow the same structure    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Numerator: difference between two means
  * Denominator: standard error of the means
  * If the difference is large compared to variability, null hypothesis is rejected
  * Critical values from the t probability distribution
  * t distribution is symmetric and bell-shaped but more spread out than normal distribution
* **Complete reporting** of comparison results should include    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Individual mean values with their confidence intervals
  * Results of the t-test
  * Mean difference with its confidence interval (effect size)
  * Effect size facilitates interpretation of clinical importance (vs. statistical significance)

### 4.1.2. ANALYSIS OF VARIANCE (ANOVA)

* **Purpose**: Handle comparisons of means between more than two groups or several measurements in the same group [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]].
* **Problem with multiple t-tests**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Using many t-tests for all possible pairings increases overall error rate
  * With type I error set at 0.05 for each individual comparison, the cumulative error rate is given by α = 1 − 0.95ᵏ
  * For 13 t-tests, real error rate is 49%
  * Will misleadingly identify statistical significance when none exists
* **ANOVA approach**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Creates test statistics on means for more than two groups
  * Test statistics called F ratios (after statistician Ronald Fisher)
  * Critical values from F probability distribution
  * For three groups, simultaneously asks:
	* Is group 1 different from group 2?
	* Is group 2 different from group 3?
	* Is group 1 different from group 3?
* **F statistic structure**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Numerator: expresses variability of mean values across groups
  * Denominator: expresses average variability of each sample value from overall mean
  * Has two degrees of freedom (m and n):
	* m is a function of number of experimental groups
	* n is a function of number of subjects in all groups
* **Post-hoc testing**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * If null hypothesis is rejected (differences exist among groups), multiple comparison techniques determine where differences lie
  * Various techniques available for multiple comparisons after ANOVA

### 4.1.3. PARAMETRIC VS. NON-PARAMETRIC TESTS

* **Assumptions for parametric tests**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Populations follow normal distribution
  * Some data types may not meet this assumption (e.g., proportions, percentages, response times)
* **Options for non-normal data** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  1. **Use parametric tests anyway** (often reasonable):
	 * Parametric statistics are "robust" statistics
	 * Accuracy of decisions remains credible even with moderately severe departures from assumptions
	 * Robustness means type I error magnitude isn't seriously affected by ill-conditioned data

  2. **Use nonparametric tests**:
	 * Don't require assumptions about probability distributions
	 * Tests of choice for ordinal data
	 * Based on ability to rank/order observations (also called order statistics)
	 * Still require theoretical probability distributions (binomial, normal, chi-square)
	 * Examples:
	   * Sign test (analogous to paired t-test)
	   * Mann-Whitney rank sum test (analogous to unpaired t-test)
	   * Kruskal-Wallis one-way analysis of variance (analogous to one-way ANOVA)
* **Limitations of nonparametric tests**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Don't adapt well to complex statistical models
  * Less able to distinguish between null and alternative hypotheses if data are normally distributed
* **Test selection guide based on variable type and study design**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]],    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]]:

**For unpaired data**:
  * Normally distributed, two groups: Student's T test
  * Normally distributed, more than two groups: Analysis of variance (ANOVA)
  * Non-normal, two groups: Mann Whitney U test
  * Non-normal, more than two groups: Kruskal Wallis test

**For paired data**:
  * Normal, two groups: Paired T-test
  * Normal, more than two groups: Paired ANOVA
  * Non-normal, two groups: Wilcoxon signed rank test
  * Non-normal, more than two groups: Friedman's test

![Untitled](STATISTICS_Untitled_3.png_40ad8d.png)

**Bottom Line:** Selecting the appropriate statistical test depends on data type, distribution, and study design. For interval data, t-tests (paired or unpaired) are used for two-group comparisons, while ANOVA handles multiple groups. When data violate normality assumptions, researchers can rely on the robustness of parametric tests for moderate violations or switch to nonparametric alternatives that make fewer assumptions about the underlying distribution.

## 4.2. TESTS FOR CATEGORICAL DATA

### 4.2.1. CHI-SQUARE TEST

* **Purpose**: Used for comparing categorical data from two different groups to detect significant differences from expected values    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]].
* **Application**: Appropriate for nominal data and some applications with ordinal data    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]].
* **Equation**: χ² = Σ(Observed - Expected)²/Expected    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]].
* **Interpretation**:
  * The assumption is no significant difference between groups, so χ² = 0
  * χ² value increases with the magnitude of difference
  * Value must be interpreted with pre-calculated tables incorporating degrees of freedom to determine p-value
  * Null hypothesis: No association between rows and columns in contingency table
  * Alternative hypothesis: An association exists
* **Limitations**:
  * If the expected number of observations is less than five, Fisher's exact test should be used instead    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]].

### 4.2.2. FISHER'S EXACT TEST

* Used when sample sizes are small and expected frequencies fall below five    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]].
* More computationally complex but more accurate for small samples    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]].
* Provides exact p-values rather than approximations    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]].
* Example from study comparing hyperbaric bupivacaine versus hyperbaric lidocaine for spinal anesthesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * 9/35 (26%) patients in lidocaine group had transient neurologic symptoms (TNS)
  * 1/35 (3%) patients in bupivacaine group had TNS
  * Fisher's exact test p-value = 0.0134, indicating statistically significant difference

### 4.2.3. CONFIDENCE INTERVALS FOR PROPORTIONS

* For binary data (some subjects have a certain characteristic), a ratio of responders to total can be calculated as p = x/n    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]].
* In the population, this ratio is a parameter denoted π (not related to 3.14159…)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]].
* Sample proportion p is the best estimate of population proportion π    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]].
* **Confidence Interval Calculation**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * If sample is sufficiently large (n × p ≥ 5; n × (1 − p) ≥ 5), central limit theorem allows derivation of standard error: SE = √[p(1-p)/n]
  * CI calculated similarly to interval data, using this SE
  * Larger samples provide more precise CIs
* **Zero Numerator Problem** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * When a study reports zero events (e.g., no morbidity in 167 patients receiving a new anesthetic)
  * Best estimate of population parameter is 0, but upper bound of 95% CI is relevant
  * "Rule of three": Upper bound ≈ 3/n = 3/167 ≈ 1.8%
  * Provides insight into how high the event rate might be despite observing zero events

### 4.2.4. MEASURES OF BINARY TREATMENT EFFECT

Results of binary outcome studies are often presented as effect sizes    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:

* **Risk Difference (RD)**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]],    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]]:
  * Absolute difference in occurrence between two groups
  * Formula: RD = A/(A+B) - C/(C+D) where A, B, C, D are cells in a 2×2 contingency table
* **Risk Ratio (RR)**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]],    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]]:
  * Ratio comparing reference group to control group incidence
  * Formula: RR = [A/(A+B)]/[C/(C+D)]
  * RR = 1 suggests no difference between treatment and control
  * RR < 1 indicates treatment reduces the measured outcome
  * RR > 1 indicates treatment increases the measured outcome
  * If incidence is low in control group, relative risk reduction can be overestimated
* **Odds Ratio (OR)**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]],    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]]:
  * Ratio of odds of outcome in treatment group compared to control group
  * Formula: OR = (A/B)/(C/D)
  * Widely used in case-control studies and logistic regression
* **Number Needed to Treat (NNT)** or **Number Needed to Harm (NNH)**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]],    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]]:
  * Number of patients that would need to receive treatment for one to see benefit/harm
  * Formula: NNT = 1/RD
  * Provides clinically intuitive measure of treatment effect
* In the spinal anesthesia example    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Patients were much more likely to develop TNS with lidocaine (RR = 9, OR = 11.8)
  * NNH = 4.6, meaning for every 4-5 patients receiving lidocaine, one additional patient would suffer TNS

**Bottom Line:** Statistical analysis of categorical data requires specialized techniques. Chi-square and Fisher's exact tests assess associations in contingency tables, while measures like risk ratio, odds ratio, and number needed to treat quantify the magnitude of treatment effects for binary outcomes. Understanding these measures is essential for interpreting clinical study results and their practical implications.

## 4.3. REGRESSION ANALYSIS

### 4.3.1. LINEAR REGRESSION

* **Purpose**: Find relationships between two variables to predict one characteristic from another    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]].
* **Data collection**: Involves data pairs (xi, yi) from clinical trials or observational studies    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]].
* **Variable terminology**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * y variable: Dependent or response variable
  * x variable: Independent or explanatory variable
* **Linear relationship assumption**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * y is considered a function of x
  * Expressed as linear regression equation: y = a + bx
  * Parameters:
	* b is the slope (for each 1 unit change in x, y changes by b units)
	* a is the intercept (value of y when x equals 0)
* **Parameter estimation**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Uses least squares method
  * Sets slope b value to minimize sum of vertical distances from data pairs to regression line
  * Formula: b = Σ[(xi - x̄)(yi - ȳ)]/Σ[(xi - x̄)²]
* **Significance testing**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Parameter of greatest interest is usually the slope
  * Zero-value slope implies x and y are not linearly related
  * With assumption of bivariate normality, t-test used to check statistical significance of slope
* **Correlation analysis**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Typically performed on same (xi, yi) data pairs
  * Correlation coefficient r measures linear covariation of x and y
  * r ranges from -1 to 1
  * No linear correlation if r equals zero
  * Formula: r = Σ[(xi - x̄)(yi - ȳ)]/√[Σ(xi - x̄)² × Σ(yi - ȳ)²]
  * Test of statistical significance of r is equivalent to test for significance of regression slope b
* **Coefficient of Determination** (r²)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Varies between 0 and 1 (sometimes expressed as percentage)
  * Interpretation: Fraction of variation in y explained by variation in x
  * Example: r² = 0.67 means 67% of biomarker variation is explained by age variation
* **Importance of Data Visualization**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Anscombe's quartet: Four datasets with identical summary statistics, regression parameters, and correlation coefficients but very different relationships
  * Demonstrates that regression statistics alone can be misleading
  * Visual inspection can reveal:
	* Linear relationships (appropriate for linear regression)
	* Curvilinear relationships (requiring different models)
	* No relationships (despite significant statistics due to outliers)
	* Outlier-driven correlations
  * Reports of linear regression should always include scatter plots of raw data

### 4.3.2. LOGISTIC REGRESSION

* Used when the response variable is binary (alive/dead, complication/no complication)    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]].
* Creates a sigmoidal regression curve of binary outcome versus predictor variable    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]].
* Multiple methods available, with logistic regression being most common    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Logistic regression model**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Response variable yi denotes binary outcome in ith individual (typically coded as 0 or 1)
  * π represents probability of response variable having value 1
  * Logit transformation (log of odds ratio) = log[π/(1-π)]
  * Allows π to be expressed as linear combination of covariates
  * Formula: log[π/(1-π)] = β₀ + β₁x₁ + β₂x₂ + … + βₖxₖ
  * Logit transformation is link function relating covariates to probability of binary outcome

### 4.3.3. MULTIVARIABLE REGRESSION

* **Multivariable Linear Regression**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Extends regression to relate continuous response to multiple explanatory variables
  * Creates model relating outcome (e.g., heart rate) to multiple covariates
  * Data from n patients of form (yi, xi,1, xi,2, … xi,k) where:
	* i denotes ith patient
	* yi is response in ith patient
	* xi,j is value of jth covariate in ith patient
  * Linear model equation: yi = β₀ + β₁xi,1 + β₂xi,2 + … + βₖxi,k + εi
  * Expected value μi is linear sum of each covariate value multiplied by its coefficient
  * Difference between observed (yi) and expected (μi) reflects biologic variability and measurement error
* **Multivariable Logistic Regression**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Used to control for confounding in binary outcome analysis
  * Simultaneously fits all explanatory variables
  * **Confounding** occurs when apparent association between covariate and outcome is affected by a third variable related to both
  * Example: In exploring relationship between tobacco and myocardial infarction, male sex, poverty, and sedentary lifestyle could be confounders
  * Thousands of medical studies use stepwise multivariable logistic regression to identify "independent" predictors for clinical outcomes
  * Common for modeling risk factors like postoperative nausea and vomiting
* **Limitations of Predictive Models**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Require validation with new patients at different time/place
  * Many prognostic models (especially for mortality/critical illness) lack validation
  * Often have modest accuracy and unproven clinical utility
  * Example: Widely used sepsis prediction model implemented in hundreds of hospitals showed poor discrimination and calibration when independently evaluated

**Bottom Line:** Regression analysis examines relationships between variables, with linear regression for continuous outcomes and logistic regression for binary outcomes. While these techniques can identify associations and predict outcomes, visual data inspection is essential to verify appropriate model fit, and all models require external validation before clinical application. Multivariable approaches help control for confounding but introduce additional complexity in interpretation.

# 5. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS OF STATISTICS

## 5.1. RISK ASSESSMENT

### 5.1.1. ABSOLUTE VS. RELATIVE RISK

* **Absolute Risk Reduction (ARR)**    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]]:
  * The direct arithmetic difference in outcome occurrence between two groups
  * Example: If complication rate decreases from 10% to 5%, ARR = 5%
  * Provides clear perspective on actual magnitude of benefit
* **Relative Risk Reduction (RRR)**    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]]:
  * The proportional reduction in rates between two groups
  * Example: If complication rate decreases from 10% to 5%, RRR = 50%
  * Can make benefits seem more impressive than ARR
  * If incidence is low in the control group, RRR can be overestimated
  * A relative risk of 1 suggests no difference in outcome between treatment and control groups
  * If relative risk is less than one, the treatment reduces the measured outcome, and vice versa
* **Interpretation challenges**    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]]:
  * RRR can exaggerate perceived benefits for rare outcomes
  * Example: Reducing risk from 0.02% to 0.01% is 50% RRR but only 0.01% ARR
  * Important to consider both measures when evaluating treatments

### 5.1.2. ODDS RATIO

* **Definition**: The ratio of the odds of a given outcome in the treatment group compared to the control group    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]].
* **Formula**: OR = (A/B)/(C/D) where A,B,C,D are cells in a 2×2 contingency table    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]].
* **Interpretation**:
  * OR = 1: No difference between groups
  * OR > 1: Increased odds of outcome in treatment group
  * OR < 1: Decreased odds of outcome in treatment group
* **Applications**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Widely used in case-control studies
  * Standard measure in logistic regression
  * Used when calculating adjusted risk in the presence of confounders

### 5.1.3. NUMBER NEEDED TO TREAT (NNT)

* **Definition**: The number of patients that would need to receive the treatment for one person to see a clinical benefit    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]].
* **Formula**: NNT = 1/ARR    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]].
* **Interpretation**:
  * Lower NNT indicates more effective treatment
  * Provides intuitive clinical perspective on treatment effect
  * Example: NNT = 20 means twenty patients need treatment for one to benefit
* **Number Needed to Harm (NNH)**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Statistical Analysis|ⓘ]]:
  * Similar concept for adverse effects
  * Number of patients who receive treatment before one experiences harm
  * Example from spinal anesthesia study: NNH = 4.6 for lidocaine vs. bupivacaine regarding transient neurologic symptoms

**Bottom Line:** Different measures of risk provide complementary perspectives on treatment effects. Absolute risk reduction quantifies actual difference in outcome rates, relative risk reduction expresses proportional benefit, and number needed to treat translates statistical findings into clinically meaningful terms. Understanding all these measures helps clinicians accurately interpret research and make informed treatment decisions.

## 5.2. DIAGNOSTIC TESTS

### 5.2.1. SENSITIVITY AND SPECIFICITY

* **Sensitivity**    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]]:
  * Definition: "If I have the condition, how likely is it that the test will detect it?"
  * Formula: Number of true positives divided by total number with the condition
  * Highly sensitive tests are good for ruling out disease (when negative)
  * Remembering aid: "SnNOut" - Sensitive test, Negative result, rules Out disease
* **Specificity**    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]]:
  * Definition: "If I don't have the condition, how likely am I to test negative?"
  * Formula: Number of true negatives divided by total number without the condition
  * Highly specific tests are good for confirming disease (when positive)
  * Remembering aid: "SpPIn" - Specific test, Positive result, rules In disease
* **Trade-off**: Tests often balance sensitivity against specificity; increasing one typically decreases the other    [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#5. Practice-Based Learning and Improvement|ⓘ]].

![Diagnostic Matrix: Sensitivity, Specificity, PPV, NPV](statistics_diagnostic_2x2_table_4r8n2k_1734159135000.png)

### 5.2.2. POSITIVE AND NEGATIVE PREDICTIVE VALUES

* **Positive Predictive Value (PPV)**    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]]:
  * Definition: "If I test positive, how likely is it that I actually have the condition?"
  * Formula: Number of true positives divided by total number of positive tests
  * Affected by disease prevalence (higher prevalence → higher PPV)
  * More relevant than sensitivity/specificity for clinical decision-making
* **Negative Predictive Value (NPV)**    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]]:
  * Definition: "If I test negative, how sure can I be that I don't have it?"
  * Formula: Number of true negatives divided by total number of negative tests
  * Also affected by disease prevalence (lower prevalence → higher NPV)
* **Accuracy**    [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Biostatistics and Evidence based Medicine/Practice - paper critic|ⓘ]]:
  * Definition: The proportion of tests that gave the correct outcome
  * Formula: Sum of true positives and true negatives, divided by total number of tests
  * Overall measure of test performance

### 5.2.3. ROC CURVES

* **Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves** plot sensitivity vs. (1-specificity) for different test cutoff values    [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#5. Practice-Based Learning and Improvement|ⓘ]].
* **Area Under the Curve (AUC)** quantifies overall test performance    [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#5. Practice-Based Learning and Improvement|ⓘ]]:
  * AUC = 0.5: Test performs no better than chance
  * AUC = 1.0: Perfect discrimination
  * Generally, AUC > 0.8 indicates good discriminative ability
* ROC analysis helps determine optimal cutoff values balancing sensitivity and specificity    [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#5. Practice-Based Learning and Improvement|ⓘ]].

![ROC Curve Interpretation](statistics_roc_curve_interpretation_5t7q3m_1734159135000.png)

**Bottom Line:** Diagnostic test evaluation requires understanding sensitivity, specificity, and predictive values. Sensitivity measures a test's ability to identify true positives, while specificity measures its ability to identify true negatives. Predictive values, which depend on disease prevalence, directly address the probability of disease given test results. These metrics help clinicians select and interpret appropriate tests for different clinical scenarios.

# 6. ADVANCED STATISTICAL METHODS

## 6.1. EPIDEMIOLOGICAL METHODS

### 6.1.1. PROPENSITY SCORE MATCHING

* **Purpose**: Provides estimation of treatment effect in non-randomized studies (NRSs) by reducing selection bias    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Concept**: Aims to create comparable groups from observational data by accounting for variables that influence treatment assignment    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Problem addressed** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * In NRSs, baseline prognostic covariates (confounders) may be imbalanced between treatment groups
  * Any difference in outcomes may be due to these confounders rather than the treatment itself
  * Example: Pulmonary artery catheterization (PAC) study showed lower survival in PAC patients, but these patients had more multiorgan system failure at baseline
* **Definition**: The propensity score is a subject's probability of receiving a specific treatment, conditional on baseline covariates    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Implementation**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Usually estimated by multivariable logistic regression
  * Unlike regular logistic regression for prediction, all available covariates are included regardless of statistical significance
  * Covariates must be restricted to those known prior to intervention
  * For each patient, a z-score is calculated from regression coefficients and covariate values
  * Taking the antilogit yields the probability of receiving the intervention
  * Matched subsets are created by pairing patients with similar propensity scores from different treatment groups
* **Advantages** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Can balance many covariates simultaneously
  * Example: Study of epidural anesthesia balanced 45 baseline characteristics after matching
  * Allows analysis of cohorts that appear similar in all measured covariates except treatment received
* **Limitations** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Cannot account for unmeasured or unobservable confounders
  * Total of matched patients will always be less than original dataset
  * Cannot replace randomized controlled trials
  * Example: Observational studies using propensity matching suggested blood transfusion increased mortality after cardiac surgery, but RCT showed restrictive transfusion threshold actually increased mortality
* **Proper reporting** should include    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Details of propensity score building
  * Matching method
  * Demonstration of covariate balancing (tabular or graphical)

### 6.1.2. MULTIVARIABLE LOGISTIC REGRESSION

* **Purpose**: Identifies "independent" predictors for various clinical outcomes using observational data    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Application**: Thousands of medical reports use this method annually    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Process**:
  * **Stepwise regression**: Automated procedure for selecting predictors from many potential variables when no underlying theory guides selection    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]].
  * Used when many baseline characteristics might influence outcomes    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Model validation**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Requires acquisition of new patients at different time/place
  * Framework exists for assessing predictive model performance
  * Many models lack proper validation
* **Limitations**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Many prognostic models (especially for mortality/critical illness) have not been validated
  * Often have modest accuracy
  * Typically lack documented clinical utility
  * Example: Widely implemented sepsis prediction model showed poor discrimination and calibration in independent evaluation

**Bottom Line:** Advanced epidemiological methods like propensity score matching and multivariable logistic regression attempt to control for confounding in observational studies. While valuable for analyzing existing data when randomization isn't possible, they can't fully substitute for randomized trials. Understanding their strengths and limitations is essential for critically appraising medical literature where these methods are increasingly common.

## 6.2. SYSTEMATIC REVIEWS AND META-ANALYSIS

### 6.2.1. PRINCIPLES OF EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

* **Systematic Review Process**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  1. Choice of study inclusion/exclusion criteria
  2. Explicitly defined literature searching
  3. Abstraction of data from included studies
  4. Appraisal of risk of bias within each study
  5. Systematic pooling of data
  6. Discussion of inferences

* This structured protocol minimizes bias in the review process    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Bias Assessment in Included Studies**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * **Selection bias**: Systematic differences between patients in intervention groups
  * **Performance bias**: Systematic differences in care beyond the planned interventions
  * **Attrition bias**: Systematic differences in withdrawal of patients from groups
  * **Detection bias**: Systematic differences in outcome ascertainment and recording
* **Focus of bias detection** in trials incorporated into SRs    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  1. Randomization process
  2. Concealment of random allocation
  3. Use of blinding
  4. Reporting/analysis of dropouts

### 6.2.2. META-ANALYSIS METHODS

* **Definition**: Statistical techniques for combining results from different studies    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Requirements**: Sufficient clinical similarity among included studies    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]].
* **Outcome measures** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Binary outcomes: Typically compared by risk ratio (RR) or odds ratio (OR)
  * Continuous variables: Effect size is the mean difference
* **Forest Plot**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Standard way to present MA results
  * Layout:
	* Far left column: Included studies
	* Center-left columns: Observed data
	* Center-right columns: Effect sizes (e.g., RRs with 95% CIs) for individual studies and summary
	* Far right column: Graphical representation (horizontal lines for individual studies, diamond for summary)
  * Also includes statistics on heterogeneity and significance of summary statistics

![Reading a Forest Plot](statistics_forest_plot_anatomy_2k9w7p_1734159135000.png)
* **Interpretation example**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * MA of transient neurologic symptoms (TNS) after spinal anesthesia
  * 11 of 15 individual comparisons had wide, non-significant CIs
  * Overall consistency of effect was clear (lidocaine "favored" in only one study)
  * Combined RR = 7.31 (95% CI [4.16, 12.86])
  * About every seventh patient (92/637) with lidocaine spinal block had TNS
  * Risk approximately seven times higher compared to other local anesthetics
* **Strengths of MA**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Combines evidence across studies to increase statistical power
  * Can resolve inconsistencies in reported literature
  * Provides more precise estimates of effect sizes
* **Evolution of MA methods**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Multiple treatment comparisons
  * Diagnostic test evaluation
  * Random effects models
  * Trial sequential analysis to maintain Type I error rate with updating
  * Various approaches to statistical heterogeneity
* **Sources of systematic reviews**    [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Evidence Based Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * Individual researchers publishing in journals
  * American Society of Anesthesiologists practice parameters
  * Cochrane Collaboration (international network of volunteers producing ~9,000 SRs)
  * Cochrane reviews are regularly updated as new evidence emerges

**Bottom Line:** Systematic reviews and meta-analyses represent the highest level of evidence by systematically synthesizing findings across multiple studies. The rigorous methodology includes comprehensive literature searches, bias assessment, and statistical synthesis of results using forest plots. These techniques increase statistical power and provide more precise effect estimates, though the quality of conclusions depends on the quality of included studies.
