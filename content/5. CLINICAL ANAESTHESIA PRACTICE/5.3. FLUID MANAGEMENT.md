## 1.1. MOLARITY, MOLALITY, AND EQUIVALENCY

* **Mole (mol)** represents 6.02 × 10^23 molecules [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* **Molarity** is the standard SI unit defined as moles of solute per liter of solution (mol/L or M) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* **Molality** is defined as moles of solute per kilogram of solvent [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* **Equivalency** refers to the number of equivalents of an ion in solution, calculated as the number of moles multiplied by its charge (valence) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Example: A 1 M solution of MgCl₂ yields 2 equivalents of magnesium per liter and 2 equivalents of chloride per liter [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]

## 1.2. OSMOLARITY AND OSMOLALITY

* **Osmosis** is the net movement of water across a semipermeable membrane resulting from a difference in nondiffusible solute concentrations between two sides [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* **Osmotic pressure** is the pressure that must be applied to the side with more solute to prevent net movement of water across the membrane [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Depends primarily on the number of nondiffusible solute particles, not their identity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* **Osmole (Osm)** equals 1 mol of nondissociable substances [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * For substances that ionize, each mole results in n Osm, where n is the number of ionic species produced [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Ionic interaction between cation and anion reduces the effective activity (e.g., NaCl behaves as if only 75% ionized) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* **Osmolarity** equals the number of osmoles per liter of solution [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* **Osmolality** equals the number of osmoles per kilogram of solvent [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]

## 1.3. TONICITY

* **Tonicity** refers to the effect a solution has on cell volume but is often used interchangeably with osmolarity and osmolality [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* An **isotonic solution** has no effect on cell volume [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* A **hypotonic solution** increases cell volume [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* A **hypertonic solution** decreases cell volume [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* **Plasma tonicity** reflects the concentration of solutes that do not easily cross cell membranes (rather than total solutes) and therefore affect water distribution between cells and extracellular volume (ECV) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* Urea distributes throughout total body water (TBW) and does not cause hypertonicity despite increasing blood urea nitrogen (BUN) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* Tonicity is primarily driven by sodium [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]

## 1.4. STARLING FORCES AND GLYCOCALYX

* **Starling forces** determine the filtration rate of fluid from capillaries into interstitial space, representing the net result of oncotic and hydrostatic pressure gradients between intravascular and interstitial spaces [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]

![Q = fluid filtration, k = capillary filtration coefficient (conductivity of water), A = area of the capillary membrane, Pc = capillary hydrostatic pressure, Pi = interstitial hydrostatic pressure, σ = the reflection coefficient for albumin, πi = interstitial oncotic pressure, and πc = capillary oncotic pressure.](FLUIDS_FLUIDS_image.png_0641c2.png_737d00.png)

* Traditional Starling's law is oversimplified and does not account for the important role of capillary glycocalyx in fluid filtration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* **Revised Starling equation**: Reflects the discovery of the endothelial glycocalyx layer, an endoluminal web of membrane-bound proteoglycans, glycoproteins, and glycosaminoglycans present in capillaries, arterioles, and venules [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 1.4.1. ENDOTHELIAL GLYCOCALYX LAYER (EGL) STRUCTURE AND FUNCTION

* **Composition**: Web of membrane-bound glycoproteins and proteoglycans on the luminal side of endothelial cells, associated with glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) including heparan sulfate, chondroitin sulfate, and hyaluronic acid [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Volume and dimensions**:
  * Estimated volume in humans: ~700 mL (from indocyanine green dilution studies) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * May be as large as ~1700 mL when measured using erythrocyte and Dextran 40 exclusion methods [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Thickness varies by location: 0.2 μm in microcirculation to 8 μm in larger vessels [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Average thickness ~2 μm with endothelial surface area of 350 m² [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Key features of the glycocalyx**:
  * **Barrier function**: Separates plasma from a 'protected' region of the sub-glycocalyx space which is almost protein-free [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Non-circulating volume**: The sub-glycocalyx space contains non-circulating plasma volume and has a colloid oncotic pressure of approximately 70% of the capillary lumen [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Revised pressure gradient**: The sub-glycocalyx colloid oncotic pressure (πsg) replaces the interstitial colloid oncotic pressure (πi) as a major determinant of transcapillary flow along oncotic gradients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Filtration relationship**: Fluid filtration (Jv) is therefore proportional to πc – πsg [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Semi-permeability**: Acts as a molecular sieve with refection coefficient (σ) for albumin varying from 0.6 to 0.9 in various capillary beds [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Red blood cell exclusion**: RBCs are excluded from the EGL, creating a non-circulating intravascular volume [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Molecular size filtration**: Impermeable to Dextran 70 (70 kDa) and larger molecules in healthy state [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* The albumin attached to the endothelial glycocalyx results in the oncotic pressure gradient being the difference between πc and the oncotic pressure in the space between the endothelial glycocalyx and capillary wall [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]

### 1.4.2. "NO ABSORPTION RULE" AND FLUID RETURN

* **"No absorption rule"**: According to the revised model, no fluid reabsorption takes place within the capillary, even at the venous end [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] `[W:385]`
  * Fluid moves out of the capillary along its entire length (continuous filtration) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] `[W:385]`
  * πc opposes but does not reverse filtration rate [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] `[W:385]`
  * Even at the venous end, the oncotic pressure difference is insufficient to reverse net filtration [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Mechanism explanation** `[W:385]`:
	* Subglycocalyx colloid oncotic pressure (πsg) replaces πis as determinant of Jv `[W:385]`
	* Oncotic pressure difference across EGL opposes but does not reverse filtration `[W:385]`
	* Proteins diffuse into subglycocalyx space from ISF, diminishing COP difference `[W:385]`
  * **Transient absorption exception** `[W:390]`:
	* Acute reduction in transendothelial pressure difference (pre-capillary vasoconstriction, post-capillary vasodilation, hypovolemia) can cause transient absorption `[W:390]`
	* Equivalent to ~500 mL "autotransfusion" in humans `[W:390]`
	* Effect lasts only few minutes as proteins diffuse into subglycocalyx space `[W:390]`
	* This is NOT the same as the traditional Starling concept of continuous absorption at venous end `[W:390]`
* **Lymphatic return**: Fluid is returned to circulation via the lymphatics, not directly reabsorbed into capillaries [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] `[W:385]` `[H:93]`
  * Filtered fluid accounting for 2-10% of cardiac output returns as lymph [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] `[H:93]`
  * Most filtered fluid must return as lymph since no significant absorption occurs `[H:93]` `[W:385]`
  * Lymphatic system capacity is critical - when overwhelmed, edema develops [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Lymph production by tissue** `[W:387]`:
	* Liver accounts for ~50% of total body lymph production `[W:387]`
	* Hepatic lymph has higher than average protein concentration `[W:387]`
	* Liver is major site of transcapillary escape of plasma proteins when capillary function unimpaired `[W:387]`
	* In resuscitated hyperdynamic septic shock: hepatic blood flow increases to ~50% of cardiac output `[W:387]`
  * **Lymphatic importance highlighted by revised model** `[W:393]`:
	* With no venular absorption, lymphatic return is the primary route for fluid return `[W:393]`
	* Preservation of lymphatic flow is critical for fluid homeostasis `[W:393]`
  * 💡 **Clinical implication**: This explains why colloids cannot "pull fluid back" from interstitium to treat edema as previously believed [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] `[W:388]`

### 1.4.3. GLYCOCALYX DAMAGE AND SHEDDING

* **Mechanisms of glycocalyx injury**:
  * **Volume loading**: Rapid crystalloid infusion (>25 mL/kg over 30 min) can damage glycocalyx integrity [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Hyperglycemia**: Acute or chronic hyperglycemia reduces EGL volume by ≥1 liter in diabetic patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Surgical stress**: Surgery, particularly major procedures, causes glycocalyx shedding [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Trauma**: Severe trauma associated with glycocalyx degradation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Sepsis and inflammation**: Systemic inflammatory response causes glycocalyx breakdown [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Inflammatory mediators implicated in glycocalyx damage**:
  * C-reactive protein [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * A3 adenosine receptor stimulation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Tumor necrosis factor [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Bradykinin [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Mast cell tryptase [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Markers of glycocalyx shedding** (elevated in plasma/urine):
  * Syndecan-1 (major glycocalyx component) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Heparan sulfate [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Hyaluronic acid [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Chondroitin sulfate [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Consequences of glycocalyx shedding**:
  * Increased transcapillary protein flux (increased "large pore" permeability) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Loss of plasma volume-expanding effects of colloids [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Increased edema formation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Reduced antibacterial properties of plasma [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Compaction of remaining glycocalyx structure [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 1.4.4. GLYCOCALYX PROTECTION AND RESTORATION STRATEGIES

* **Potential protective agents**:
  * **N-acetyl cysteine**: May prevent glycocalyx damage in hyperglycemia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Antithrombin III**: Reduces shedding following ischemia/reperfusion [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Hydrocortisone**: Preserves vascular barrier by protecting glycocalyx; prevents TNF-α induced shedding [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] `[L:1]`
  * **Sevoflurane anesthesia**: Preserves glycocalyx against ischemia-reperfusion injury; reduces leukocyte and platelet adhesion [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Restoration strategies**:
  * **GAG replacement**: Infusion of chondroitin sulfate and hyaluronic acid can restore compacted EGL volume [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ **Clinical limitation**: These strategies remain largely experimental; routine clinical application not yet established [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Established therapies with potential glycocalyx benefits** `[L:1]`:
  * Glucose control (avoiding hyperglycemia) `[L:1]`
  * Avoidance of hypervolemia `[L:1]`
  * Hydrocortisone in septic shock `[L:1]`
* **Experimental therapies under investigation** `[L:1]`:
  * TNF-α inhibition `[L:1]`
  * Albumin infusion (interacts with glycocalyx, promotes vasodilation and nutrient blood flow) `[L:1]`
  * Avoidance of natriuretic peptide release `[L:1]`
  * Statins `[L:1]`
  * Sevoflurane `[L:1]`
* **Unavailable/future therapeutic targets** `[L:1]`:
  * Increasing synthesis of glycocalyx components `[L:1]`
  * Refurbishing the glycocalyx `[L:1]`
  * Selectively preventing protease degradation `[L:1]`
* 💡 **Emerging therapeutic goal**: Protection or restoration of the EGL might be an important therapeutic target in critical illness [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 1.4.5. ASSESSMENT OF GLYCOCALYX INTEGRITY

* **Optical methods** `[L:1]`:
  * Can assess EGL thickness indirectly in capillaries in vivo `[L:1]`
  * Demonstrates EGL reduction in acute and chronic inflammation `[L:1]`
* **Biomarkers of glycocalyx disruption** (elevated in plasma/urine) `[L:1]`:
  * **Syndecan-1**: Major glycocalyx component; elevated in septic shock `[L:1]`
  * **Glycosaminoglycans (GAGs)**: Including heparan sulfate, hyaluronic acid, chondroitin sulfate `[L:1]`
  * **Clinical correlations in sepsis** `[L:1]`:
	* Associated with septic shock `[L:1]`
	* Predict mortality from sepsis `[L:1]`
	* Correlate with higher SOFA scores `[L:1]`
* **Clinical effects of glycocalyx damage** `[L:1]`:
  * Capillary leak `[L:1]`
  * Edema `[L:1]`
  * Hypercoagulability `[L:1]`
  * Inflammation `[L:1]`
  * Loss of vascular responsiveness `[L:1]`
  * Platelet aggregation `[L:1]`
* ⚠️ **Recovery timeline**: Once disrupted, the endothelial glycocalyx takes days to recover `[L:1]`

### 1.4.6. CAPILLARY HETEROGENEITY

* **Sinusoidal capillaries** (liver, spleen, bone marrow) `[W:386]`:
  * Phago-endocytic phenotype with no effective glycocalyx `[W:386]`
  * Express hyaluronic acid uptake receptors, preventing EGL development `[W:386]`
  * Open fenestrations allow passage of macromolecules as large as chylomicrons `[W:386]`
  * Upper effective pore size ~180 nm `[W:387]`
  * No COP mechanism to oppose filtration `[W:387]`
  * Filtration dependent on hydrostatic pressure gradients alone `[W:387]`
  * Return to circulation via lymphatics `[W:387]`
  * Liver accounts for ~50% of total body lymph production with higher than average protein concentration `[W:387]`
  * Major site of transcapillary escape of plasma proteins when capillary function unimpaired `[W:387]`
  * 💡 Sinusoidal tissues have little/no compliance due to fibrous capsules - limited ability to accommodate ISF expansion `[W:387]`
* **Fenestrated capillaries with continuous basement membrane**:
  * **Renal glomeruli** `[W:387]`:
	* Full basement membrane and EGL `[W:387]`
	* Generously fenestrated (65 nm anatomically) `[W:387]`
	* Effective pore size only ~15 nm due to glycocalyx flanking over open fenestrations `[W:387]`
	* Additional filtration barrier at podocyte foot processes (slit diaphragms limit to ~6 nm) `[W:387]`
	* Albumin and larger molecules normally not filtered into tubular fluid `[W:387]`
	* **Albuminuria**: Previously presumed index of capillary permeability, but mechanism likely more complex `[W:387]`
	* **Protein filter function**: Impaired by hyperglycemia and probably other kidney injuries `[W:387]`
  * **Other fenestrated beds** (kidney cortex/medulla, GI mucosa, lymph nodes) `[W:387]`:
	* Continuous basement membrane `[W:387]`
	* Diaphragmed fenestrations induced by vascular endothelial growth factors `[W:387]`
	* Upper pore size 6–12 nm `[W:387]`
	* Notable exceptions to "no absorption rule" - capable of reabsorbing fluid `[W:387]`
* **Non-fenestrated capillaries** (muscle, connective tissue, lung, nervous system) `[W:387]`:
  * Continuous basement membrane and EGL `[W:387]`
  * **Blood-brain barrier** `[W:387]`:
	* Endothelial cell membranes tightly opposed by zona occludens tight junctions with few breaks `[W:387]`
	* Very small effective pore size (~1 nm) `[W:387]`
	* Only permeable to smallest non-lipid soluble molecules `[W:387]`
  * **Other non-fenestrated beds** (muscles, connective tissues, lungs) `[W:387]`:
	* Macula occludens loose junctions to intercellular clefts `[W:387]`
	* Effective pore size up to 5 nm `[W:387]`
	* Permeable to molecules as large as myoglobin `[W:387]`
	* Breaks within inter-endothelial cell junctions constitute primary pathways for filtration `[W:387]`
	* Increased porosity in inflammation may be due to increase in these normally infrequent discontinuities `[W:387]`
  * **Glycocalyx-junction-break model**: Alternative interpretation where `[W:387]`:
	* Pore size (small or large) is function of spaces between EGL matrix fibers `[W:387]`
	* Area for fluid exchange is function of length of junction breaks between adjacent endothelial cells `[W:387]`
  * **Aquaporins**: Present in endothelial cell membranes, particularly muscles `[W:388]`:
	* Very small effective pore size `[W:388]`
	* Believed to contribute little to transvascular filtration `[W:388]`
  * **Large-pore systems**: Controversy about significance of transcellular large-pore systems for protein transport in systemic inflammation `[W:388]`:
	* If they exist, effective pore size >50 nm `[W:388]`
	* Increase in large pore numbers is important component of inflammatory increase in Jv `[W:388]`
* **Clinical implications of capillary heterogeneity** `[W:387]`:
  * Tissues that can accumulate substantial ISF after trauma and sepsis (compliant tissues): loose connective tissues, muscles, lungs, GI mesentery and mucosa `[W:388]`
  * Example: Extravascular lung water can increase from ~500 mL to 2.5 L in pulmonary edema `[W:388]`
  * Loose connective tissues and muscles can expand to many liters of peripheral edema `[W:388]`
  * **Volume kinetics implications**: Expandable ECF volume (~6-8 L) excludes spaces limited by rigid structures (bone/brain) or fibrous capsules (liver/spleen/kidney) `[W:387]`
  * 💡 **Systemic capillary leak syndrome**: Fluid goes to soft tissues of limbs, can cause compartment syndromes `[W:388]`

### 1.4.7. CAPILLARY PRESSURE AND FLUID MOVEMENT

* **Capillary hydrostatic pressure (Pc)** is the most powerful factor promoting fluid movement into the interstitial fluid volume (IFV) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Determined by capillary flow, arterial resistance, venous resistance, and venous pressure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * If Pc overwhelms oncotic pressure and lymphatic drainage, edema results [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * In a euvolemic patient, infusion of any fluid (colloid or crystalloid) increases Pc and fluid moves out of the capillary despite the potential increase in πc seen with colloid [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Context-dependent fluid distribution** (see section 1.3.5.2 for full discussion):
  * At low capillary pressures (hypotension, hypovolemia): Crystalloid distribution temporarily arrests, behaving similarly to colloids [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * At normal capillary pressures: Crystalloids distribute across ECF as expected [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * At elevated capillary pressures: Increased Jv proportional to transendothelial pressure difference [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Colloid osmotic pressure** (or oncotic pressure) is determined by the concentration differential across a capillary bed and the permeability of capillary membranes to protein, known as its **reflection coefficient (σ)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * σ of 0 represents free permeability and 1 represents complete impermeability [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * The σ for albumin ranges from 0.6 to 0.9 in various capillary beds [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Altered in states of acute inflammation, trauma, surgery, or illness, resulting in loss of barrier function and increased transcapillary flux [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

## 1.5. THE EXTRACELLULAR MATRIX AND BASEMENT MEMBRANE

* **Three resistances in series**: Glycocalyx is first and major fiber matrix resistor; basement membrane and extracellular matrix are second and third resistances `[W:387]`
* **Basement membrane** (where it exists) `[W:387]`:
  * Specialized part of extracellular matrix `[W:387]`
  * 60–100 nm thick `[W:387]`
  * Composed of type IV collagen and laminin `[W:387]`
  * Closely adherent to cell membrane `[W:387]`
* **Extracellular matrix** `[W:387]`:
  * Web of collagen fibrils within interstitial space `[W:387]`
  * Upon which glycoproteins (e.g., fibronectin) and proteoglycans (protein molecules with GAG side chains) are arranged `[W:388]`
  * Contains free GAGs `[W:388]`
  * **Toll-like receptors**: Found within ECM; believed to have pivotal role in early development of systemic inflammatory response and ventilator-induced lung injury `[W:388]`
  * **Integrins and their receptors**: Modulate cell locomotion through ECM `[W:388]`
	* Can modulate Pis by bringing about conformational changes to collagen allowing GAGs to become hydrated `[W:388]`
	* **Acute reduction in Pis** occurs in inflammatory conditions `[W:388]`
	* Increases transendothelial pressure difference `[W:388]`
	* Can increase Jv by as much as 20-fold independently of other causes of capillary "leak" `[W:388]`

## 1.6. PLASMA PROTEINS

### 1.6.1. CAPILLARY HETEROGENEITY

* **Sinusoidal capillaries** (liver, spleen, bone marrow):
  * Phago-endocytic phenotype with no effective glycocalyx [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Express hyaluronic acid uptake receptors, preventing EGL development [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Open fenestrations allow passage of macromolecules as large as chylomicrons [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Upper effective pore size ~180 nm [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * No COP mechanism to oppose filtration [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Liver accounts for ~50% of total body lymph production [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Fenestrated capillaries with continuous basement membrane**:
  * **Renal glomeruli**: Generously fenestrated (65 nm anatomically, 15 nm effective due to glycocalyx flanking), with additional filtration by podocyte slit diaphragms (6 nm) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Other fenestrated beds** (kidney cortex/medulla, GI mucosa, lymph nodes): Upper pore size 6-12 nm; notable exceptions to "no absorption rule" - can reabsorb fluid [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Non-fenestrated capillaries** (muscle, connective tissue, lung, nervous system):
  * Continuous basement membrane and glycocalyx [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Blood-brain barrier**: Very small effective pore size (~1 nm) due to tight zona occludens junctions [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Other non-fenestrated beds**: Loose macula occludens junctions with effective pore size up to 5 nm [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Primary pathways for filtration are breaks in inter-endothelial cell junctions [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Can accumulate substantial edema in compliant tissues (skin, lungs, GI tract, muscle) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 1.6.2. PLASMA PROTEINS

* **Oncotic role**: Proteins have oncotic role across endothelial glycocalyx; COP difference opposes but does not reverse Jv `[W:388]`
* **EGL interaction**: The EGL is semi-permeable to albumin molecules; presence of albumin within EGL is important determinant of its filter function `[W:388]`
  * **Endothelial surface layer**: Functional unit of EGL with its contained albumin `[W:388]`
  * Plasma albumin concentration is major determinant of plasma COP in health `[W:388]`
  * In congenital analbuminaemia or acquired hypoalbuminaemia, other proteins become more important `[W:388]`
* **Distribution** `[W:388]`:
  * In health, ~40% of total body albumin is intravascular `[W:388]`
  * In inflammation, intravascular proportion decreases and extravascular proportion increases `[W:388]`
* **Transcapillary escape rate of albumin (TCERA)** `[W:388]`:
  * Index of "vascular permeability" `[W:388]`
  * Normal TCERA: ~5% of plasma albumin per hour `[W:388]`
  * Can double during surgery `[W:388]`
  * May increase to 20% or more in septic shock `[W:388]`
* **Pulmonary permeability assessment** `[W:388]`:
  * Gallium-transferrin pulmonary leak index used as index of pulmonary permeability `[W:388]`
  * Inversely related to plasma albumin and transferrin concentrations in both septic and non-septic ICU patients with acute lung injury `[W:388]`
* **Clinical use of albumin/plasma for resuscitation - evidence conflicts with traditional beliefs** `[W:388]`:
  * **Traditional belief**: Based on original Starling principle - colloids selectively resuscitate intravascular volume; transendothelial protein concentration difference regulates Jv `[W:388]`
  * **Revised model predictions**: No absorption rule precludes significant intravascular benefit from raising plasma oncotic pressure `[W:388]`
  * **Clinical observations supporting revised model** `[W:388]`:
	* Hypoalbuminaemia is marker of disease severity and predictor of complications in surgical patients, but treatment provides no clinical benefit `[W:388]`
	* ARDS patients have low plasma albumin and transferrin, but hyperoncotic albumin ± diuretic produces no improvement in pulmonary edema `[W:388]`
	* Negative fluid balance (rather than COP difference) improves alveolar-arterial oxygen tension ratio in ARDS `[W:388]`
	* In septic and non-septic patients, fluid loading against CVP produces greater cardiac output increases with albumin than ISS, but no benefits for pulmonary edema or lung injury score `[W:388]`
* **Red cell dilution studies - interpretation considerations** `[W:389]`:
  * Studies of hyperoncotic albumin transfusion interpreted as showing osmotic absorption from extravascular to intravascular compartment `[W:389]`
  * Without Dextran 40 indicator of whole intravascular volume, such conclusion not justified `[W:389]`
  * Acute increase in circulating plasma COP would draw water from non-circulating intravascular volume within EGL `[W:389]`
  * ⚠️ Red cell dilution studies not accounting for EGL should be interpreted with caution `[W:389]`

### 1.6.3. PLASMA SUBSTITUTES

* **Purpose**: Used to maintain or raise plasma COP `[W:388]`
* **Effects on albumin** `[W:388]`:
  * Displace albumin from circulation (like transfused albumin) `[W:388]`
  * Suppress hepatic albumin synthesis by elevating COP `[W:388]`
  * Little known about effect on EGL `[W:388]`
  * Would not be expected to support EGL filter function as albumin does `[W:388]`
* **Volume kinetics in normovolaemic volunteers** `[W:388]`:
  * Modified fluid gelatin or HES solutions distributed to ISF more slowly than ISS (as explained by revised Starling equation) `[W:388]`
  * No difference in arterial pressure, urine output, or renal hormone concentrations compared to ISS `[W:388]`
* **Common expectation**: Plasma substitutes more effective than ISS at inducing hyperdynamic circulation for haemodynamic goal-directed therapy `[W:388]`
  * Volume kinetic experiments show ISS clearance from intravascular compartment substantially slower in anaesthetized vs. unanaesthetized patients `[W:388]`
  * This is context sensitivity phenomenon `[W:388]`
  * ISS can achieve hyperdynamic goals `[W:388]`
* **Context sensitivity comparison** `[W:388]`:
  * Volume kinetics in volunteers: Elimination rate constant from central to peripheral compartments for HES NOT reduced (unlike ISS) but INCREASED during anaesthesia vs. awake `[W:388]`
  * Duration of resuscitation after blood removal shorter in anaesthetized subjects `[W:390]`
* **Clinical implications**: Extent to which context sensitivity attributable to reduced transendothelial pressure difference unclear, but reduced filtration response to crystalloid loading in hypovolaemic subjects demonstrated `[W:390]`
  * Crystalloids during vasodilation from spinal anaesthesia ('co-loading') more effective than 'preload' crystalloids `[W:390]`
  * Mean arterial pressure is important determinant of ISS distribution from intravascular space in patients under general or regional anaesthesia `[W:390]`
  * Lower pressure → slower clearance from circulation `[W:390]`
* **Anti-inflammatory properties**: Albumin may have potential benefit through anti-inflammatory or immune regulatory properties `[W:389]`
  * Analysis of published data does not show plasma substitutes equivalent to albumin in this respect or better than ISS `[W:389]`

### 1.6.4. COLLOID OSMOTIC PRESSURE IN CLINICAL PRACTICE

* **Traditional paradigm**: Great importance attached to COP of plasma in clinical practice based on standard Starling principle `[W:389]`
* **Clinical observations conflicting with traditional view** `[W:389]`:
  * No difference in COP between septic and non-septic patients `[W:389]`
  * COP does not influence pulmonary transcapillary filtration in patients with pulmonary oedema `[W:389]`
  * COP not determinant of outcome in intensive care practice `[W:389]`
  * Albumin transiently raises plasma COP vs. HES or ISS, but fluid balance and development of peripheral or pulmonary oedema not different between groups `[W:389]`
  * In post-surgical patients with acute lung injury, plasma substitute resuscitation worsened total thoracic compliance vs. normal saline; type of fluid did not affect pulmonary permeability or oedema `[W:389]`
* **"COP paradox"** `[W:389]`:
  * Properties other than COP effect contribute to capillary "sealing" effect of albumin or plasma substitutes `[W:389]`
  * Traditional COP-based explanations inadequate `[W:389]`

**Bottom Line:** Fluid movement between vascular and interstitial compartments is governed by a complex interplay of hydrostatic and oncotic pressures, with the glycocalyx playing a critical role that traditional Starling equations don't fully account for. Understanding these forces is essential for predicting fluid distribution and managing edema clinically. The revised Starling model highlights that volume status and capillary hydrostatic pressure are more critical determinants of fluid movement than oncotic pressure differences.

### 1.7.1. TOTAL BODY WATER DISTRIBUTION

* The human body at birth is approximately 75% water by weight [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* By 1 month, this value decreases to 65%, and by adulthood to approximately 60% for males and 50% for females [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* Total body water (TBW) represents approximately 60% of body weight (45 liters in a 75 kg male) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Water content is decreased in:
  * Obese individuals [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Women [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Advanced age [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * This is primarily because adipose tissue has decreased water content [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* Normal daily water intake in an adult is approximately 2.5L [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * 1.5L is excreted in urine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * 100ml in sweat [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * 100ml in feces [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * 300-400ml through inhaled air that is saturated with water vapor when exhaled [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * 400ml through skin (not perceived as sweat) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* 💡 Water loss through lungs is greater in cold environments than warm because the water content of inhaled air decreases with decreased ambient temperature, requiring more endogenous water to achieve saturation at body temperature [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* The impermeable cornified layer of skin normally limits water loss, but this protection is destroyed in burn injuries, leading to increased fluid losses [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
- Measurement of fluid compartments
	- TBW: heavy water (deuterium oxide) (freely distributed across all compartments)
	- ECF: indicator that can cross capillary endothelium but not cellular phospholipid bilayer
		- lonic tracers (82Br, 35SO4, chloride isotopes)
		- Crystalloid tracers (inulin, mannitol)
	- Plasma volume: radio-labelled albumin (indicator that cannot cross capillary endothelium)
	- RBC volume: radio-labelled RBCs
	- ICF & interstitial fluid: indirectly calculated
		- ICF volume = TBW - ECF volume
		- Interstitial fluid = ECF volume - plasma volume
	- Total blood volume: indirectly calculated
		- Blood volume = plasma volume / (1HCT)

### 1.7.2. INTRACELLULAR FLUID

* Comprises approximately 2/3 of total body water (40% of body weight; 55-65% of TBW) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* The composition of intracellular volume is maintained by a metabolically active cellular membrane [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Has a low sodium concentration (10 mmol/L) and a high potassium concentration (150 mmol/L) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Cellular fluid balance is maintained by:
  * A membrane-bound adenosine triphosphate (ATP)-dependent pump that exchanges Na⁺ for K⁺ in a 3:2 ratio [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Cell membranes are relatively impermeable to sodium and, to a lesser extent, potassium ions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Potassium is concentrated intracellularly, whereas sodium is concentrated extracellularly [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Potassium is the most important determinant of intracellular osmotic pressure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Impermeability of cell membranes to most proteins results in a high intracellular protein concentration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Proteins act as nondiffusable solutes (anions) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * The unequal exchange of 3 Na⁺ for 2 K⁺ prevents relative intracellular hyperosmolality [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* ⚠️ Interference with the ATPase activity results in progressive swelling of cells [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 1.7.3. EXTRACELLULAR FLUID

* Comprises approximately 1/3 of total body water (20% of body weight; 35-45% of TBW) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Contains two primary components:
  * **Interstitial fluid (ISF)** (3/4 of ECF; 15% of body weight) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Held in the gel structure of the interstitial space [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
	* Acts as a reservoir from which water and electrolytes can be mobilized into circulation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
	* Includes cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), gastrointestinal tract fluid, and fluid in potential spaces (pleural, pericardial, peritoneal, synovial) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
	* Excess manifests as peripheral edema [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
	* Very little ISF exists as free fluid; most is in chemical association with extracellular proteoglycans (as a gel) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
	* ISF pressure is generally thought to be negative; however, with increased ECF volume, ISF volume will rise proportionately, and ISF pressure will rise and eventually become positive [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
	* The interstitial compartment acts as an "overflow" reservoir for the intravascular compartment (tissue edema) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
	* Has an electrolyte composition similar to plasma with a high sodium concentration (140 mmol/L) and a low potassium concentration (4 mmol/L) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Contains less protein than plasma and therefore has a lower oncotic pressure [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Plasma** (1/4 of ECF; 5% of body weight) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Noncellular portion of blood [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
	* Average volume 3L (a little over half of the blood volume of 5L) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Loss of plasma volume from intravascular space is minimized by colloid osmotic proteins [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
	* Has a composition similar to interstitial space, with high sodium and low potassium concentrations [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Has key functions: providing fluid volume to suspend red cells, clotting, and immunological functions [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Plasma and ISF are in dynamic equilibrium through pores in capillaries [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* The constituents in the ECF are carefully regulated by the kidneys, creating an osmotically neutral fluid containing proper concentrations of electrolytes and nutrients [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* **Sodium** is quantitatively the most important extracellular cation and the major determinant of extracellular osmotic pressure and volume [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Changes in ECF volume are therefore related to changes in total body sodium content [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* Most electrolytes (small ions) freely pass between plasma and the interstitium, resulting in nearly identical electrolyte composition [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* However, tight intercellular junctions between adjacent endothelial cells impede the passage of plasma proteins to outside the intravascular compartment [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* As a result, plasma proteins (mainly albumin) are the only osmotically active solutes in fluid not normally exchanged between plasma and interstitial fluid [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* **Differences within compartments**:
  * Low protein in ISF, higher protein in plasma and ICF [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Na⁺ and Cl⁻ are largely extracellular [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * K⁺ is primarily intracellular [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
	* Trauma is associated with progressive loss of K⁺ through the kidneys - patients undergoing surgery excrete about 100mEq of K⁺ in the first 48 hours postoperatively, and then 25mEq daily [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* 💡 Plasma K⁺ concentrations are not good indicators of total body K⁺ content because K⁺ is intracellular [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
	* Plasma K⁺ does correlate with hydrogen ion content of plasma - in metabolic acidosis, there is net efflux of K⁺ out of cells to compensate for H⁺ ion influx, thus preserving resting potential; K⁺ will fall when measured in acidotic state [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]

### 1.7.4. BLOOD VOLUME

* Average adult blood volume is 5L [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * 3L plasma, 2L erythrocytes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* Total intravascular volume combines intravascular extracellular fluid (3 liters) and cellular components (2 liters) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Varies with age, weight, and gender [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * In non-obese individuals, averaging 70ml/kg for lean men and women [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * The greater the ratio of fat to body weight, the less blood volume per kg (adipose tissue has decreased vascular supply) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* Hematocrit normally:
  * 45% for men and postmenopausal women [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * ~38% for menstruating women [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Range of approximately +/- 5% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]

### 1.7.5. MEASUREMENT OF BODY FLUID VOLUMES

* **Dilution concept**: Leading principle for measuring body fluid volumes `[H:88]`
  * Substance that distributes in one body fluid space only is injected and allowed to equilibrate `[H:88]`
  * Blood sample taken; volume calculated as dose divided by plasma concentration `[H:88]`
  * Most attractive if turnover of injected substance is slow `[H:88]`
  * If turnover rapid, several samples must account for substance elimination `[H:88]`
* **Historical importance**: These techniques were critically important in 1950s-1960s but now considered too cumbersome for routine clinical use `[H:87]`
  * Still used in research; contributed much knowledge about macroscopic fluid physiology `[H:87]`

#### 1.7.5.1. EXTRACELLULAR FLUID MEASUREMENT

* **Tracers** `[H:88]`:
  * **Bromide**: Has slow turnover `[H:88]`
  * **Iohexol**: Also yields glomerular filtration rate; several samples needed to account for urinary excretion `[H:88]`

#### 1.7.5.2. TOTAL BODY WATER MEASUREMENT

* **Tracers** `[H:88]`:
  * **Tritium**: Radioactive tracer `[H:88]`
  * **Deuterium**: Non-radioactive tracer `[H:88]`
  * **Ethanol**: Proposed as solvent that distributes evenly in water alone `[H:88]`
  * All require several hours for equilibration `[H:88]`

#### 1.7.5.3. PLASMA VOLUME MEASUREMENT

* **Radioiodinated albumin** `[H:88]`:
  * Radioactive tracer `[H:88]`
  * Several blood samples usually taken `[H:88]`
  * Overestimates plasma volume by almost 10% `[H:88]`
* **Evans blue** `[H:88]`:
  * Dye that colors albumin in plasma `[H:88]`
  * Overestimates plasma volume by almost 10% `[H:88]`
* **Indocyanine green (ICG)** `[H:88]`:
  * Dye that binds to plasma albumin `[H:88]`
  * Half-life only 3 minutes due to rapid hepatic uptake `[H:88]`
  * Transit time from central circulation to liver approximately 1 minute `[H:88]`
  * Unknown whether ICG overestimates plasma volume like other plasma tracers `[H:88]`
* **Note on plasma tracers**: Overestimation occurs because they measure both circulating plasma and non-circulating plasma within the glycocalyx layer `[H:88]`

#### 1.7.5.4. RED CELL MASS MEASUREMENT

* **Labeling techniques** `[H:88]`:
  * Chromium `[H:88]`
  * Technetium `[H:88]`
  * Carbon monoxide `[H:88]`

#### 1.7.5.5. BIOIMPEDANCE ANALYSIS (BIA)

* **Principle**: Water volumes oppose electrical currents differently inside vs. outside cells `[H:88]`
* **Method**: Series of electrical currents run through body (usually arm to leg); impedance pattern evaluated in relation to tracer technique quantification `[H:88]`
* **Characteristics**: Takes ~1 minute, painless, but disturbed by body movements `[H:88]`

#### 1.7.5.6. ANTHROPOMETRIC EQUATIONS

* **Basis**: Created from tracer measurements; correlate body fluid volumes with gender, height, weight `[H:88]`
* **Common assumptions** (% of body weight) `[H:88]`:
  * Blood volume: 7% `[H:88]`
  * Interstitial fluid: 15% `[H:88]`
  * ECF volume: 20% `[H:88]`
  * ICF volume: 35–40% `[H:88]`
  * Total body water: 55–60% `[H:88]`
* 💡 Although crude, these assumptions are quite useful in everyday clinical work `[H:88]`

#### 1.7.5.7. IMPLICATIONS FOR INTRAVASCULAR VOLUME ASSESSMENT

* ⚠️ **Critical consideration**: Studies reporting red cell dilution data that don't account for the glycocalyx intravascular volume should be interpreted with caution `[H:88]` `[W:389]`
* **Glycocalyx volume considerations** `[W:389]`:
  * Non-circulating EGL volume is substantial (700–1700 mL) `[W:389]`
  * Red cells are excluded from glycocalyx `[W:389]`
  * Compaction of EGL (by inflammation or hyperosmotic colloid) can significantly affect balance of total intravascular fluid and red cell dilution volumes `[W:389]`
* **Interpretation of hyperoncotic colloid studies** `[W:389]`:
  * Acute increase in circulating plasma COP draws water from non-circulating intravascular volume within EGL `[W:389]`
  * Red cell dilution studies may incorrectly suggest "osmotic absorption" from extravascular to intravascular compartment `[W:389]`
  * Without indicator of whole intravascular volume (e.g., Dextran 40), such conclusions not justified `[W:389]`

**Bottom Line:** Multiple methods exist for measuring body fluid volumes, each with specific applications and limitations. While tracer techniques remain the gold standard for research, they're impractical for routine clinical use. Understanding these measurement principles is important for interpreting research data and appreciating the limitations of various fluid assessment methods. The discovery of the glycocalyx as a non-circulating intravascular compartment requires re-interpretation of many older dilution studies.

**Bottom Line:** Body water is distributed between intracellular (2/3) and extracellular (1/3) compartments, with the extracellular fluid further divided into interstitial fluid and plasma. Sodium and potassium concentrations are the primary determinants of extracellular and intracellular osmotic pressures, respectively. Total body water varies with age, sex, and body composition, with obesity associated with lower water content per kilogram.

## 1.8. FLUID EXCHANGE BETWEEN COMPARTMENTS

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|Part A: Physiology]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|Part B: Perioperative Medicine]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#2. Medical Knowledge (MK)|Part C: Medical Knowledge]]
### 1.8.1. DIFFUSION MECHANISMS

* Rate of diffusion of a substance is governed by:
  * The permeability of that substance through the membrane [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * The concentration difference for that substance between the two sides [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * The pressure difference between either side, because pressure imparts greater kinetic energy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * The electrical potential across the membrane for charged substances [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* **Diffusion through cell membranes** (between ISF and ICF) occurs via:
  * Directly through lipid bilayer of cell membrane - O₂, H₂O, lipid-soluble molecules [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Protein channels - Cations like Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺ [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Reversible binding to carrier protein that can traverse membrane (facilitated diffusion) - Glucose, amino acids [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Fluid exchange is governed by osmotic forces created by differences in nondiffusible solute concentrations [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]

### 1.8.2. CAPILLARY EXCHANGE

* **Diffusion through capillary endothelium** (between intravascular fluid and ISF):
  * Capillary walls are typically 0.5 μm thick, consisting of a single layer of endothelial cells with their basement membrane [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Intercellular clefts, 6 to 7 nm wide, separate each cell from its neighbors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Transport mechanisms include:
	* Direct penetration through lipid cell membrane - O₂, CO₂, H₂O, lipid-soluble substances [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
	* Low molecular weight substances cross intercellular clefts - Na⁺, Cl⁻, K⁺, glucose [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
	* High molecular weight substances penetrate poorly except at liver and lung (where clefts are larger) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Fluid exchange is governed by hydrostatic and osmotic forces - with a tendency for fluid to move out of capillaries at the arterial end and back into capillaries at the venous end [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Normally, all but 10% of the fluid filtered is reabsorbed back into capillaries [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * What is not reabsorbed (about 2 mL/min) enters the interstitial fluid and is then returned by lymphatic flow to the intravascular compartment [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * However, per the revised Starling equation (see section 1.1.4), the traditional view of reabsorption at the venous end is now questioned, with more emphasis on lymphatic return [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 1.8.3. SODIUM AND OSMOLALITY RELATIONSHIP

* Water freely diffuses across cellular membranes between the intracellular space and extracellular space in response to the gradient in effective osmolarity between compartments [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* Large sodium (Na⁺) and potassium (K⁺) gradients are maintained between spaces through Na⁺-K⁺-ATPase pumps [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* This pump creates different major solutes extracellularly and intracellularly:
  * Na⁺ is the primary extracellular solute (ECF [Na⁺] ≈ 140 mEq/L) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * K⁺ is the primary intracellular solute (ICF [K⁺] ≈ 150 mEq/L) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* The result of these gradients is that ECF volume is largely determined by sodium content of the body, which is highly regulated through salt intake and renal excretion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* Water movement between intracellular and intravascular spaces is determined by Starling forces (hydrostatic and osmotic pressure gradients), not effective osmolarity as between ICF and ECF [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* Intracellular and intravascular spaces are separated by capillary membranes which, unlike cellular membranes, are highly permeable to small solutes such as Na⁺ and K⁺ and therefore do not generate any driving force for water movement [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* However, capillary membranes are less permeable to larger molecules, such as albumin, which can exert oncotic pressure (osmotic pressure from colloids) across the barrier [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* Albumin is the most important oncotically active colloid in the ECF [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]

### 1.8.4. VOLUME KINETICS

* **Two-compartment model** can be applied to intravenous fluids with two functional (rather than anatomical) compartments [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
  * V1 corresponds to plasma volume [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] `[H:89]`
  * V2 represents the interstitial fluid volume that can be expanded by fluid moving out of the plasma after intravenous infusion [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] `[H:89]`
  * V2 is smaller than the total interstitial volume because not all interstitial fluid volume can be expanded (e.g., that contained inside the skull or long bones) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] `[H:89]`

#### 1.8.4.1. BASIC CONCEPTS OF VOLUME KINETICS

* **Volume kinetics**: Adaptation of pharmacokinetic theory that makes it possible to analyze and simulate distribution and elimination following infusion of IV fluids `[H:76]`
  * Based on serial analysis of blood Hb concentration and urine volume during/after controlled fluid infusion `[H:90]`
  * Allows determination of infusion rate required to reach predetermined plasma volume expansion by simulation `[H:76]`
* **Key finding**: Interstitial fluid space after expansion by crystalloid is only twice as large as plasma volume (less than commonly assumed) `[H:90]`
* **Distribution kinetics** `[H:90]`:
  * Crystalloid fluid distributes with half-life of 8 minutes under normal conditions `[H:90]`
  * Exception: Distribution temporarily arrested during abrupt drop in arterial pressure `[H:90]`
  * 💡 When blood pressure drops, doesn't matter if crystalloid or colloid infused - both behave similarly `[H:90]`
* **Elimination characteristics** `[H:90]`:
  * Fluid distributed to interstitium governs rate at which infused fluid eliminates from body `[H:90]`
  * Return by lymphatics is slow compared to distribution `[H:90]`
  * Most filtered fluid returns as lymph `[H:90]`
* **Hemodilution concept for volume kinetics** `[H:89]`:
  * Uses hemodilution to capture distribution of infused water volume `[H:89]`
  * Blood contains almost exclusively Hb and water `[H:90]`
  * Decrease in Hb indicates increase in blood water component `[H:90]`
  * However, rise in blood water concentration represents dilution in much larger water volume - adds calculation requirements `[H:90]`

#### 1.8.4.2. CONTEXT SENSITIVITY OF FLUID DISTRIBUTION

* **Concept**: Distribution and elimination of crystalloid fluid greatly depends on clinical context - "context sensitivity" `[H:101]` `[W:389]`
* **In conscious volunteers** (normal capillary pressure) `[H:101]`:
  * Rapid crystalloid infusion cleared at rates >100 mL/min `[H:101]`
  * Indicates increased Jv from supranormal Pc and transendothelial pressure difference `[H:101]`
  * Crystalloids distribute across ECF as expected `[W:389]`
* **During general or spinal anesthesia** (reduced arterial pressure) `[H:101]`:
  * Elimination of crystalloid greatly retarded `[H:101]`
  * Mean arterial pressure is important determinant of crystalloid distribution from intravascular space `[H:101]`
  * Lower pressure → slower clearance from circulation `[H:101]`
  * At onset of anesthesia, fluid movement from V1 to V2 falls dramatically `[W:389]`
  * Fluid transfer out of vascular space might stop completely depending on MAP fall `[W:389]`
  * 💡 Explains why modest crystalloid volumes can cause significant hemodilution during anesthesia `[W:389]`
  * Crystalloid distribution temporarily arrests, behaving similarly to colloids `[W:389]`
* **Colloid fluid distribution** `[H:92]`:
  * Intravascular persistence NOT affected by arterial pressure `[H:92]`
  * Half-life independent of hemodynamic state `[W:390]`
  * In euvolemic haemodilution volunteers: elimination rate from central to peripheral compartments INCREASED during anesthesia (opposite to crystalloid) `[W:390]`
* **"J point" concept** (see section 1.1.4.6) `[W:389]`:
  * Pressure at which Jv approaches zero depends on capillary porosity `[W:389]`
  * J-shaped curve describes Jv and Pc relationship `[W:389]`
  * Below J point: Any transfused fluid (colloid or crystalloid) retained intravascularly until transendothelial pressure difference reaches level where filtration recommences `[W:390]`
  * Above J point: Oncotic pressure difference opposing filtration is maximal; Jv proportional to Pc `[W:390]`

#### 1.8.4.3. CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS OF VOLUME KINETICS

* **Ratio of colloid to crystalloid effectiveness** varies with clinical context `[H:92]`:
  * During infusion: Colloid 2× as effective as crystalloid `[H:92]`
  * During crystalloid distribution phase: Colloid 3× more effective `[H:92]`
  * At 12 hours post-infusion: Difference disappears (ratio = 1.0) in conscious volunteers `[H:92]`
  * Under general anesthesia: Difference disappears by 5–6 hours after 60-min infusion or 10 hours during continuous infusion `[H:92]`
* **Hemorrhage management considerations** `[H:92]`:
  * Finding of transient 50% plasma volume expansion of crystalloid is worrying if hemorrhage not surgically stopped `[H:92]`
  * Traditional recommendation of 3× bled volume leads to hypervolemia `[H:92]`
  * Risk of rebleeding if major vein injured `[H:92]`
  * Urinary excretion remains nearly normal despite hypovolemia (as long as arterial pressure unchanged) → later rebound hypovolemia `[H:93]`
  * **Optimal handling** `[H:93]`:
	* Infuse 1.5× bled volume over 30 min `[H:93]`
	* Don't stop infusion; gradually reduce rate by 50% every 30 min `[H:93]`
	* Restores blood volume while avoiding hyper- and hypovolemia `[H:93]`
* 💡 **Revised paradigm from volume kinetics** `[W:390]`:
  * At low capillary pressures (hypotension, hypovolemia): Crystalloid resuscitation is rational - behaves similarly to colloids `[W:390]`
  * Potential advantage of colloids over crystalloids only manifests at higher capillary pressures `[W:390]`
  * "No absorption rule" means colloids cannot "pull fluid back" from tissues to treat edema `[W:388]`

**Bottom Line:** Volume kinetics demonstrates that fluid distribution is highly context-dependent. Under anesthesia or in hypotensive states, crystalloids behave more like colloids due to arrested distribution. The traditional view that colloids are always superior for plasma volume expansion is overly simplistic - the clinical context (particularly capillary pressure) determines relative effectiveness. Understanding these kinetics explains why crystalloid-colloid differences disappear after 6-12 hours and informs optimal fluid management strategies in different clinical scenarios.

### 1.8.5. FLUID EFFICIENCY

* **Definition**: Intravascular volume expansion resulting from infusing a fluid, related to amount infused `[H:88]`
* **Two characteristics** `[H:88]`:
  * Degree of volume expansion relative to infused volume `[H:88]`
  * Duration of volume expansion `[H:88]`
* **Assessment methods** `[H:88]`:
  * Tracer methods `[H:88]`
  * Physiological endpoints `[H:88]`

#### 1.8.5.1. HEMODILUTION METHOD

* **Principle**: Hemodilution tells over how large a space the infused fluid has distributed `[H:89]`
* **Volume estimation**: Can estimate volume of distribution for fluid spreading across total body water using dilution concept, though hemodilution will be much smaller `[H:89]`
* **Blood volume estimation requirements**: Must assume baseline blood volume, which may or may not be correct `[H:89]`
* **Standard equations** (assuming no bleeding) `[H:89]`:
  * ΔBV(t) = BV(Hb/Hb(t)) − BV
  * Fluid efficiency = ΔBV / infused volume
  * Where Hb = hemoglobin before infusion, Hb(t) = hemoglobin after infusion, BV = blood volume at baseline
* **Clinical utility**: If bleeding occurs, can estimate total hemoglobin mass (BV × Hb), subtract Hb losses, then divide by Hb(t) to yield new BV `[H:89]` `[W:389]`
* **Key insight**: Hemodilution should ideally parallel relationship between bleeding and BV `[H:89]`:
  * **Hypervolemic**: Hemodilution > (blood loss / BV) `[H:89]`
  * **Hypovolemic**: Hemodilution < (blood loss / BV) `[H:89]`

#### 1.8.5.2. TYPICAL FLUID EFFICIENCY VALUES

* **Crystalloid (Ringer's solution)** `[H:88]` `[W:390]`:
  * Infused over 30 min: Plasma volume expansion ≈ 50% of infused volume `[H:85]`
  * After infusion stops: Rapidly falls to 20% within 30 min as distribution completes `[H:85]`
  * Volume of distribution for bolus: 6–8 L (expandable ECF) `[H:90]`
  * Very small amounts (5 mL/kg): Undergo barely any distribution, almost exclusively fill plasma volume `[H:85]`
  * Large-scale infusions (75–100 mL/kg over 30 min): Change integrity of interstitial meshwork, promote edema `[H:85]`
  * ⚠️ Infusing 25 mL/kg appears innocuous regarding glycocalyx degradation `[H:85]`
* **5% Albumin** `[H:87]` `[W:390]`:
  * Expands plasma volume by approximately same amount as infused volume `[H:87]`
  * Half-life of plasma volume expansion: several hours (related to intravascular persistence of albumin) `[H:87]`
  * Intravascular persistence probably shorter in septic patients due to increased capillary leakage `[H:87]`
* **20% Albumin (hyperoncotic)** `[H:87]`:
  * Increases circulating plasma volume by twice the infused volume `[H:87]`
* **Hydroxyethyl starch (e.g., Voluven 6%)** `[H:87]`:
  * Expands plasma volume by as much as infused amount `[H:87]`
  * Elimination complex: mixture of urinary excretion, molecular cleavage, phagocytosis `[H:87]`
* **Gelatin** `[H:87]`:
  * Volume expansion claimed to be short-lived (2 hours) `[H:87]`
  * Elimination by renal excretion `[H:87]`
* **Plasma** `[H:87]`:
  * Expands plasma volume similar to 5% albumin `[H:87]`
  * Should NOT be used for volume expansion (contains coagulation proteins, greater allergic potential than albumin) `[H:87]`

**Bottom Line:** Fluid efficiency quantifies how effectively different fluids expand plasma volume. Crystalloids provide approximately 50% plasma expansion during infusion (falling to 20% after distribution), while colloids typically provide 1:1 expansion (2:1 for hyperoncotic solutions). Understanding these efficiency values is crucial for calculating appropriate fluid volumes for resuscitation and avoiding both inadequate resuscitation and fluid overload.

* **Fluid movement under anesthesia**:
  * Crystalloids are reported to expand plasma volume by only 20% in awake patients, but this is an underestimation in patients under general anesthesia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Under general anesthesia, patients are vasodilated and plasma volume expanding effects are greater and longer-lasting [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * At the onset of general or spinal anesthesia, fluid movement from V1 to V2 falls dramatically (temporarily) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Fluid transfer out of the vascular space might stop completely depending on the fall in mean arterial pressure [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * These changes explain why seemingly modest volumes of crystalloid can sometimes cause significant hemodilution during anesthesia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Fluid exchange between compartments follows fundamental principles of diffusion governed by concentration gradients, membrane permeability, and pressure differentials. Capillary exchange balances hydrostatic and oncotic pressures, with lymphatic return of unabsorbed fluid. The Na⁺-K⁺-ATPase pump maintains critical ion gradients across cell membranes, with sodium concentration being the primary determinant of extracellular fluid volume. Under anesthesia, fluid distribution changes significantly, with reduced movement out of the vascular space resulting in greater plasma volume expansion than expected.

### 2.1.1. PHYSIOLOGIC CONTROL MECHANISMS
#### 2.1.1.1. RENAL REGULATION

* **Glomerular filtration** `[H:82]`:
  * Approximately 20% of renal plasma flow filtered in glomeruli to create primary urine `[H:82]`
  * Renal blood flow and GFR affected by arterial pressure but autoregulated between 80–160 mmHg `[H:82]`
  * Primary urine refined within kidneys regarding volume and composition of small molecules `[H:82]`
  * Kidneys have remarkable capacity to match water intake variability with urinary excretion `[H:82]`
  * Proteins not excreted in healthy humans `[H:82]`
* **Normal urinary excretion**: 1.0–1.5 L per 24 hours in adults `[H:82]`
* **Oliguria**: <400 mL/24 hours `[H:82]`
* **Anuria**: <100 mL/24 hours `[H:82]`
* **Causes of anuria** `[H:82]`:
  * Pre-renal: low arterial pressure `[H:82]`
  * Intra-renal: kidney injury `[H:82]`
  * Post-renal: renal stones, outflow obstruction `[H:82]`
* **Treatment approaches for poor urinary excretion** `[H:82]`:
  * Crystalloid fluid bolus (500 mL) if hypovolemic `[H:82]`
  * Loop diuretics to increase sodium excretion and urine volume `[H:82]`
  * Hyperosmotic mannitol (100–200 mL of 10–20%) for osmotic diuresis - not metabolized and eliminated only by kidneys `[H:82]`

#### 2.1.1.2. NERVOUS SYSTEM CONTROL

* **Autonomic nervous system** maintains balance between parasympathetic and sympathetic impulses `[H:82]`
* **Sympathetic effects on fluid balance** `[H:82]`:
  * Constricts arterioles → raises peripheral resistance `[H:82]`
  * Constricts large veins → increases cardiac output `[H:82]`
  * Stimulates release of noradrenaline and adrenaline from adrenal medulla `[H:82]`
	* Short-acting hormones causing vasoconstriction `[H:82]`
	* Adrenaline causes vasodilation in muscle tissue `[H:82]`
* **Effects on urinary excretion** `[H:82]`:
  * Beta-1 receptor stimulation (e.g., isoprenaline) → reduced urinary excretion `[H:82]`
  * Alpha-1 stimulation (e.g., phenylephrine) → increased diuresis `[H:82]`

#### 2.1.1.3. HORMONAL REGULATION

* **Cortisol** (adrenal cortex) `[H:83]`:
  * Stress hormone with profound metabolic effects `[H:83]`
  * Promotes fluid retention by increasing sodium reabsorption in kidneys `[H:83]`
  * Excretion elevated by surgical stress `[H:83]`
* **Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System** `[H:83]`:
  * **Renin**: Excreted by kidneys in response to low arterial pressure `[H:83]`
  * **Angiotensin**: Activated by renin; acts as vasoconstrictor `[H:83]`
  * **Aldosterone**: Stimulated by angiotensin; reduces sodium excretion and promotes water retention `[H:83]`
* **Vasopressin (ADH)** `[H:83]`:
  * Excreted from brain in response to high serum osmolality `[H:83]`
  * Acts on kidneys to increase water reabsorption `[H:83]`
  * **Physiological concentrations (1–6 pg/mL)**: Important for long-term fluid balance correction `[H:83]`
  * **Very high concentrations (up to several hundred pg/mL)**: Occur during hemorrhagic hypotension `[H:83]`
	* At these levels, also has vasoconstrictive effect `[H:83]`
	* Despite short half-life, elevation sufficient to cause renal fluid retention lasting several hours `[H:83]`
* **Natriuretic Peptides** `[H:83]`:
  * **ANP (Atrial Natriuretic Peptide)**: Excreted in response to atrial muscle cell distention `[H:83]`
  * **BNP (Brain Natriuretic Peptide)**: Released from cardiac ventricles in response to distention `[H:83]`
  * **Key effects** `[H:83]`:
	* Decrease blood volume by increasing sodium excretion `[H:83]`
	* Increase capillary leakage of proteins `[H:83]`
	* ANP and BNP act on same receptors, but ANP exerts stronger effect `[H:83]`
* **Temporal characteristics of hormonal effects** `[H:83]`:
  * **Immediate effects with short half-lives**: Vasopressin, ANP, noradrenaline, adrenaline `[H:83]`
  * **Slower-acting**: Steroid hormones (cortisol, aldosterone) `[H:83]`

#### 2.1.1.4. CARDIAC RESPONSE TO FLUID LOADING

* **Typical hemodynamic response** to volume loading `[H:83]`:
  * Increase in cardiac output `[H:83]`
  * No change in arterial pressure `[H:83]`
  * Decrease in peripheral resistance `[H:83]`
* **Requirements for increased cardiac output** `[H:83]`:
  * Sufficient venous return `[H:83]`
  * Ability of heart to pump more fluid `[H:83]`
* **Fluid responsiveness**: Ability of heart to pump more volume in response to fluid loading `[H:83]`
  * Cardiac output does not increase if vascular system already adequately filled `[H:83]`
  * Can be tested by `[H:83]`:
	* Infusing fluid bolus (during general anesthesia) `[H:83]`
	* Passive leg raising test (in conscious patient) `[H:83]`
  * 💡 Patient can be made more fluid-responsive by administration of adrenergic drugs `[H:83]`
* **Consequences of fluid loading in non-fluid-responsive patient** `[H:83]`:
  * Impairs oxygen delivery `[H:83]`
  * Raises central venous pressure `[H:83]`
  * Hardly meaningful clinically `[H:83]`
* 💡 Monitoring central hemodynamic response to volume loading has key role in guiding fluid therapy `[H:83]`

#### 2.1.1.5. THIRST MECHANISM

* Average adult fluid balance:
  * **Intake**: Ingested liquid + 750ml of water (from solid food) + 350ml (generated metabolically) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * **Output**: Urine output + GI tract losses (100-150ml/day) + insensible losses (1L/day) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* Daily fluid and mineral requirements:
  * 25-30 ml/kg/day water [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * 1 mmol/kg/day each of sodium, potassium, and chloride [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * 50-100 g/day glucose [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Intake of water is regulated through thirst [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* Thirst is caused by:
  * Increased plasma tonicity (as little as 2%) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Decreased effective circulating volume (ECV) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* Mechanisms:
  * Increased plasma osmolality → water drawn from cells into blood by osmosis → detected by hypothalamic osmoreceptors → stimulation of drinking [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Decreased ECV stimulates vascular receptors → brain centers initiate thirst [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Baroreceptors in aortic arch and carotid body and stretch receptors in great veins, pulmonary vasculature, and atria → increased sympathetic tone → juxtaglomerular apparatus (JGA) in kidney releases renin → angiotensin II initiates thirst [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]

#### 2.1.1.6. RENIN-ANGIOTENSIN-ALDOSTERONE SYSTEM

* **Angiotensin II** has multiple effects:
  * Potent vasoconstrictor [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Stimulates sodium reabsorption through multiple mechanisms:
	* Increases renal efferent arteriolar tone → preserves glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and increases filtration fraction in glomerulus → enhanced proximal sodium and water retention (also contributed by sympathetic nervous system indirectly) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
	* Activates apical membrane Na⁺-H⁺ exchangers in proximal tubule [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
	* Stimulates aldosterone release → increased Na⁺ reabsorption in exchange for K⁺ at distal tubules [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]

#### 2.1.1.7. ANTIDIURETIC HORMONE

* **ADH** (vasopressin) increases water reabsorption in collecting ducts via V₂ receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* Synthesized in hypothalamus, released from posterior pituitary gland in response to:
  * Hypertonicity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Severely decreased effective circulating volume [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* Mechanisms:
  * ADH + V₂ receptors → increased synthesis and insertion of aquaporin-2 water channels → increased water reabsorption [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Has synergistic effect with aldosterone on Na⁺ transport in cortical collecting duct (CCD) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Also contributes to systemic vasoconstriction through V₁ receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]

#### 2.1.1.8. ATRIAL NATRIURETIC PEPTIDE

* **Atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP)**, **brain natriuretic peptide (BNP)**, and **C-type natriuretic peptide** stimulate:
  * Natriuresis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Diuresis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Vasodilation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* Released in response to:
  * Atrial distension [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Sodium load [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Increased ventricular diastolic pressure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]

![Regulation of ECF volume](FLUIDS_FLUIDS_image_1.png_3bb28a.png_fd3fb5.png)

![Regulation of plasma osmolality](FLUIDS_FLUIDS_image_2.png_331cca.png_42e679.png)

**Bottom Line:** Multiple integrated hormonal systems regulate fluid balance, with thirst, antidiuretic hormone, the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, and natriuretic peptides working together to maintain appropriate fluid volume and osmolality. These systems respond to changes in plasma osmolality, effective circulating volume, and atrial/ventricular stretch to adjust water intake, renal handling of sodium and water, and vascular tone.

## 2.2. DIFFUSION OF INFUSED FLUIDS

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|Part A: Physiology]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|Part B: Perioperative Medicine]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#2. Medical Knowledge (MK)|Part C: Medical Knowledge]]
### 2.2.1. STATIC VS KINETIC MODELS

* Classical predictions have assumed a static model, as opposed to a kinetic model where body fluid spaces are dynamic [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* The static model leads to oversimplistic predictions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Example of static model for an 80kg male with 2L of acute blood loss (40% of 5L blood volume):
  * Expected plasma volume (PV) increment = volume infused × normal PV/distribution volume [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Rearranging: Volume infused = expected PV increment × distribution volume/normal PV [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * For D5W (assuming distribution volume for sodium-free water is TBW):
	* Required volume = 2L × 48L/3L = 32L [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
	* Where 2L is desired PV increment, 48L is TBW in 80kg person, 3L is normal estimated PV [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * For Ringer's lactate (distributed in ECV):
	* Required volume = 2L × 18L/3L = 12L [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
	* Where 18L is ECV in 80kg person [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]

### 2.2.2. VOLUME DISTRIBUTION PREDICTION

* Svensén and Hahn proposed a more comprehensive kinetic prediction model allowing more accurate estimation of the time course of volume changes produced by infusion of a fluid of particular composition [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* This model permits estimation of:
  * Peak volume expansion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Clearance of infused fluid [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Analysis of effects such as changes in cardiac filling pressure or cardiac output [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Traditional static models of fluid distribution oversimplify the complex dynamics of administered fluids. More sophisticated kinetic models better predict the time course of volume expansion and fluid clearance, which is essential for precise perioperative fluid management.

#### 3.1.1.2. LABORATORY ASSESSMENT

* **Laboratory surrogates** for volume status:
  * Serial hematocrits [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Arterial pH [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Urinary specific gravity or osmolality [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Urinary sodium or chloride concentration [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Serum sodium [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) to creatinine ratio [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Laboratory findings in dehydration**:
  * Rising hematocrit and hemoglobin [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Progressive metabolic acidosis (including lactic acidosis when severe) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Metabolic alkalosis (when mild hypovolemia) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Urinary specific gravity greater than 1.010 [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Urinary sodium less than 10 mEq/L [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Urinary osmolality greater than 450 mOsm/L [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Hypernatremia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * BUN-to-creatinine ratio greater than 10:1 [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Limitations of laboratory tests**:
  * BUN can increase with high protein intake, gastrointestinal bleeding, or accelerated catabolism; can decrease with severe liver dysfunction [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Creatinine has delays and is an insensitive indicator of chronic kidney disease (CKD), as 40-50% of nephrons must become dysfunctional before normal range is exceeded [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

#### 3.1.1.3. NONINVASIVE MONITORING TECHNIQUES

* **Ultrasonography**:
  * Nearly collapsed vena cava or incompletely filled cardiac chambers may indicate hypovolemia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Central venous pressure (CVP)**:
  * Static CVP readings do not provide accurate or reliable indication of volume status [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#3. Patient Care (PC)|ⓘ]]
* **Pulmonary artery occlusion pressure (PAOP)**:
  * Readings less than 8 mm Hg may indicate hypovolemia in patients with normal left ventricular compliance [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Values less than 15 mmHg may be associated with relative hypovolemia in patients with poor ventricular compliance [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * PAOP measurements greater than 18 mm Hg are elevated and may imply left ventricular volume overload [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Advanced monitoring options**:
  * Noninvasive fluid therapy guidance using arterial pulse contour analysis and estimation of stroke volume variation (e.g., LIDCOunity, Vigileo FloTrak) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#3. Patient Care (PC)|ⓘ]]
  * Esophageal Doppler [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#3. Patient Care (PC)|ⓘ]]
  * Transesophageal echocardiography [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#3. Patient Care (PC)|ⓘ]]
  * Transthoracic echocardiography [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#3. Patient Care (PC)|ⓘ]]
  * Should be considered when accurate determination of hemodynamic and fluid status is important [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#3. Patient Care (PC)|ⓘ]]
  * 💡 A 2016 Cochrane review failed to demonstrate any difference in outcomes with advanced hemodynamic monitoring versus usual care in patients undergoing proximal femur surgery [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#3. Patient Care (PC)|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Assessment of fluid status requires integration of history, physical examination, laboratory values, and monitoring techniques. While individual findings may lack sensitivity or specificity, the collective data can guide clinical decision-making. Advanced monitoring techniques offer additional information but haven't consistently shown outcome benefits compared to careful clinical assessment.

## 3.2. INTRAOPERATIVE ASSESSMENT

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|Part A: Physiology]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|Part B: Perioperative Medicine]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#3. Patient Care (PC)|Part C: Patient Care]]
### 3.2.1. STATIC PARAMETERS

* **Blood pressure, heart rate, urine output, CVP, laboratory values** - in general, poor perfusion can go unrecognized with these measures alone [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Blood pressure (BP)**:
  * Ability to maintain normal BP in the setting of potent vasodilators (i.e., volatile inhalational anesthetics) suggests adequate intravascular volume [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Heart rate**:
  * Tachycardia lacks sensitivity and specificity as an indicator of hypovolemia under general anesthesia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Urine output (UOP)**:
  * In the setting of normal renal function and absence of diuretics, UOP usually declines precipitously during moderate-to-severe hypovolemia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Appropriate UOP (0.5 to 1.0 mL/kg/hr) suggests adequate renal perfusion [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Inhalational anesthetics and surgical stress can cause oliguria in the setting of euvolemia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Central venous pressure (CVP)**:
  * CVP of 6 to 12 mmHg was previously thought to suggest adequate intravascular volume [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Now considered to be a poor surrogate of cardiac preload, fluid responsiveness, or impending pulmonary edema [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#3. Patient Care (PC)|ⓘ]]
* **Cardiac output (CO)**:
  * Can be normal despite severely reduced regional blood flow [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Perioperative oxygen consumption is extremely variable [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * These issues limit the usefulness of mixed venous oxygen saturation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 3.2.2. DYNAMIC PARAMETERS

* **Hemodynamic indices** provide a more sensitive and specific assessment of fluid status than static measurements [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#3. Patient Care (PC)|ⓘ]]
* **Intra-arterial pressure waveform analysis**:
  * Pulse pressure variation (PPVar) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Stroke volume variation (SVV) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Systolic blood pressure variation (SPV) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * During inspiration with mechanical ventilation, the intrathoracic pressure increases, reducing venous return and subsequently right ventricle filling and stroke volume [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * This change in stroke volume causes systemic BP variation with the respiratory cycle [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Variations in these parameters are normally less than 10%, but can be greater with hypovolemia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * 💡 Requirements for accurate measurement:
	* Stable vasomotor and cardiac function [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Mechanically ventilated patient without dyssynchrony [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Tidal volume of 8 cc/kg or more [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* PEEP <15 cmH₂O (including auto-PEEP) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Intact thoracic cavity [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* No evidence of atrial fibrillation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* No venous obstruction (e.g., cardiac tamponade, abdominal compartment syndrome) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Fluid challenge techniques**:
	* Passive leg raise or 250ml bolus challenge [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* If a PPVar of over 13% decreases below that threshold (or CO increases over 15%), an increase in cardiac preload will be associated with an increase in CO [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* ⚠️ Contraindicated in those with abdominal hypertension and traumatic brain injury [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 3.2.3. HEMODYNAMIC MONITORING TECHNIQUES

* **Esophageal Doppler**:
  * Measures flow velocity in descending aorta and allows assessment of corrected flow time (FTc) and descending aortic stroke volume [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#3. Patient Care (PC)|ⓘ]]
  * FTc <0.35s: volume expansion should improve CO [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * FTc >0.4s: volume expansion will be ineffective [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Use should be considered if intra-arterial waveform analysis cannot be accomplished [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Echocardiography**: TTE, TEE, POCUS
  * Findings of hypovolemia include:
	* Decreased LV cavity size at end-diastole [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#3. Patient Care (PC)|ⓘ]]
	* Hyperdynamic systolic function [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#3. Patient Care (PC)|ⓘ]]
	* Small collapsible IVC - used in spontaneously breathing patients (limited diagnostic value under mechanical ventilation) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#3. Patient Care (PC)|ⓘ]]
* **Noninvasive monitors** (under research):
  * Pleth variability index [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Pulse wave transit time or contour analysis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Carbon dioxide rebreathing [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Thoracic electrical bioimpedance or bioreactance devices [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

![Evaluation of intravascular volume using bedside ultrasound](FLUIDS_FLUIDS_Untitled.png_2e57b1.png_9bd32f.png)

**Bottom Line:** Intraoperative assessment of fluid status has evolved from reliance on static parameters (BP, HR, UOP) to dynamic indices that evaluate fluid responsiveness. Techniques like pulse pressure variation, esophageal Doppler, and echocardiography provide more accurate assessment but require specific conditions for valid interpretation. Integration of multiple monitoring modalities, coupled with clinical context, offers the most reliable approach to guide fluid therapy.

# 4. INTRAVENOUS FLUID THERAPY

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|Part A: Pharmacology]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|Part B: Perioperative Medicine]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#2. Medical Knowledge (MK)|Part C: Medical Knowledge]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#3. Patient Care (PC)|Part C: Patient Care]]
### 4.1.1. CLASSIFICATION OF IV FLUIDS
#### 4.1.1.1. CRYSTALLOIDS VS COLLOIDS

* **Controversy** remains regarding the use of colloid versus crystalloid fluids for surgical patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **General principles**:
  * Crystalloids, when given in sufficient amounts, are just as effective as colloids in restoring intravascular volume [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Replacing an intravascular volume deficit with crystalloids generally requires three to four times the volume needed when using colloids [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Surgical patients may have an extracellular fluid deficit that exceeds the intravascular deficit [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Severe intravascular fluid deficits can be more rapidly corrected using colloid solutions [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ The rapid administration of large amounts of crystalloids (>4–5 L) is often associated with tissue edema [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Can impair oxygen transport, tissue healing, and return of bowel function following surgery [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* May increase the risk of surgical site infection [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Basic classification**:
  * Intravenous fluids may broadly be classified into crystalloid and colloid solutions [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Crystalloids**: Solutions of inorganic ions and small organic molecules dissolved in water [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Main solute is either glucose or sodium chloride (or both) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Solutions may be isotonic, hypotonic, or hypertonic with respect to plasma [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Colloids**: A homogeneous substance consisting of large molecules or microscopic particles dispersed through a second substance [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

#### 4.1.1.2. EVIDENCE AND CONTROVERSIES

* If capillary membrane permeability is intact, the volume of distribution (Vd) of colloid is limited to plasma volume (PV) → colloids preferentially expand PV rather than interstitial fluid volume (IFV) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Colloid fluids are suspended in a carrier solution that has crystalloid solutes of varying tonicity [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Can be isotonic or hypertonic - exerting sufficient oncotic pressure to completely stay intravascular or translocate substantial volumes of IFV into PV → expansion of PV beyond infused volumes [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Despite theoretical advantage, exhaustive research has failed to demonstrate superiority of either colloid or crystalloid [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Cirrhotic patients may represent a specific subset in whom albumin infusion could be beneficial [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Albumin may prevent hemodynamic changes and worsening renal function after large volume paracentesis (>5 L) in cirrhotic patients with diuretic-resistant ascites, although findings are still controversial [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

![Comparison of fluid types](FLUIDS_FLUIDS_image_3.png_19a327.png_fce22f.png)

**Bottom Line:** The crystalloid vs. colloid debate continues without clear evidence of superiority of either type for most patients. Crystalloids require larger volumes for equivalent intravascular expansion but may better address total extracellular deficits. Colloids expand plasma volume more efficiently but haven't demonstrated consistent outcome advantages to justify their higher cost and potential adverse effects.

## 4.2. CRYSTALLOID SOLUTIONS



### 4.2.1. NORMAL SALINE

* **0.9% NaCl (Normal Saline)**:
  * Contains 154 mmol/L sodium and 154 mmol/L chloride [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Osmolarity 308 mOsm/L [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * pH 4.5-7.0 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Effective osmolality in vivo is 286 mOsm/kg(H2O), making it isotonic despite being described as "hypertonic" in some literature [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * First used in the late 1800s [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ When given in large volumes, produces hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis because of its high chloride content and lack of bicarbonate [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Chloride-rich crystalloids such as normal saline may contribute to perioperative acute kidney injury [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Infusion of 0.9% saline in healthy volunteers has been shown to cause adverse physiological effects [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Metabolic acidosis (hyperchloremic)
	* Renal vasoconstriction
	* Reduced renal blood flow
	* Reduced glomerular filtration rate
	* Abdominal pain
	* Tiredness and difficulty thinking
* **Volume kinetics of isotonic saline** `[H:86]`:
  * Half-life in volunteers approximately twice as long as Ringer's solutions (~90 min) `[H:86]`
  * Indication restricted to hyponatremia and volume replacement after vomiting `[H:86]`
* **Hypertonic saline (3% or 7.5%)** `[H:86]`:
  * Used to combat brain edema or severe hyponatremia `[H:86]`
  * Used for fluid resuscitation in acute trauma care `[H:86]`
  * ⚠️ Should not be infused together with erythrocytes `[H:86]`

### 4.2.2. BALANCED CRYSTALLOIDS

* **Development rationale**: To address the problems with 0.9% sodium chloride, balanced solutions were developed to provide an ion content similar to that of plasma by avoiding excess chloride [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Bicarbonate substitution**: Bicarbonate would be an ideal substituent but difficulties with storage and poor solubility of bicarbonate salts led to the use of bicarbonate precursors [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Common bicarbonate precursors**: Organic anions such as lactate, acetate, or gluconate [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Lactated Ringer's solution**:
  * Contains 130 mmol/L sodium, 109 mmol/L chloride, 28 mmol/L lactate, 4 mmol/L potassium, 3 mmol/L calcium [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Lactate is metabolized to bicarbonate via gluconeogenesis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Nearly isotonic (slightly hypotonic) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * pH approximately 6.5 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Historical development: Ringer's solution appeared in the 1800s as the first balanced crystalloid containing sodium chloride, potassium chloride, and calcium chloride; sodium lactate was later added by Alexis Hartmann in the 1930s [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Commercial variants include Hartmann's solution and compound sodium lactate [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Advantages of lactate**:
	* Can be metabolized at high rates (up to 100 mmol/hr^-1) in patients with normal liver function [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Up to 4 liters can be given quickly to a healthy 70 kg adult without lactate accumulation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Metabolism is to carbon dioxide or via gluconeogenesis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Disadvantages**:
	* Requires functioning liver for metabolism [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Increased oxygen consumption due to metabolism [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* May confound lactate as a marker of tissue hypoperfusion, although without associated acidemia (contains lactate, not lactic acid) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **PlasmaLyte**:
  * Contains 140 mmol/L sodium, 98 mmol/L chloride, 27 mmol/L acetate, 23 mmol/L gluconate, 5 mmol/L potassium, 1.5 mmol/L magnesium [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * pH 7.4 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Osmolarity 308 mOsm/L [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Other similar solutions include Normosol and Ringer's acetate [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Advantages of acetate**:
	* Better solubility of its salts [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Metabolism within and outside the liver at rates up to 300 mmol/hr^-1 (three times faster than L-lactate) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Remains effectively metabolized in experimental shock states [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Rapidly converted to bicarbonate, producing an alkalizing effect [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Disadvantages of acetate**:
	* Has been associated with hemodynamic instability through pro-inflammatory and cardiotoxic effects in hemodialysis solutions [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Removed from hemodialysis fluids in Australia, though evidence for perioperative or ICU concerns is limited [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Gluconate metabolism**: Less understood; appears most is excreted unchanged in urine and may act as an osmotic diuretic, though clinical implications are unclear [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Balanced salt solutions (low-[Cl⁻] crystalloids)** have preserved ionic "balance" by replacing Cl⁻ with lactate, gluconate, or acetate [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Preferred for most intraoperative uses [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Evidence for buffered vs. nonbuffered crystalloids is still controversial [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* ⚠️ Balanced solutions containing calcium (like Hartmann's or Lactated Ringer's) should not be used in the same giving set or line as blood components preserved in citrate-based solutions, as this can result in coagulation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* No proven outcome benefit comparing balanced solutions to 0.9% sodium chloride or comparing different balanced solutions to each other in perioperative or ICU practice (excluding neurosurgery and neurological injury) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 4.2.3. HYPERTONIC SOLUTIONS

* An ideal alternative to conventional crystalloid and colloid fluids would be inexpensive, produce minimal peripheral or pulmonary edema, generate sustained hemodynamic effects, and be effective even if administered in small volumes [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Hypertonic, hypernatremic solutions, with or without added colloid, appear to fulfill some of these criteria [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Hypertonic saline (HS)** solutions:
  * 7.5% NaCl/6% dextran-70 and 7.5% NaCl/6% hetastarch are examples [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Increase serum sodium and osmolality [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Draw water from intracellular to extracellular space → expansion of plasma volume beyond the infused volume [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Favorable effects on cerebral hemodynamics (potential use in decreased intracranial compliance) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Resuscitation from hemorrhagic shock with lactated Ringer's increases ICP, but resuscitation with 7.5% NaCl does not [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Acute increases in serum Na to 155-160 mEq/L produce no apparent harm in hypovolemic trauma patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* HS is preferred to mannitol by some neurosurgeons [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* HS and mannitol of similar osmolarity have similar effects on brain water, intracranial volume, and ICP [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* The most salient difference: infusion of HS increases intravascular volume, while diuresis secondary to mannitol decreases intravascular volume [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 4.2.4. GLUCOSE-CONTAINING SOLUTIONS

* A wide variety of solutions containing glucose with or without sodium chloride are available [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Purpose**: Traditionally used to provide free water to treat dehydration since glucose is rapidly metabolized, leaving water to diffuse throughout the total body water [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Function**: Glucose is added to prevent lysis of cells on infusion into the bloodstream [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Concentrations available**: 5%, 10%, 20%, and 50% [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ 20% and 50% solutions are extremely irritant to veins and should only be given centrally [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Combination solutions**:
  * Numerous solutions available with glucose and various electrolytes [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * 0.18% sodium chloride with 4% glucose has been associated with severe hyponatremia in infants and children and is not recommended for this population [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * 5% glucose is usually combined with sodium chloride 0.45% or 0.9% in pediatric practice [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Glucose is also available combined with Lactated Ringer's and Hartmann's solution, though not widely available [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 4.2.5. OSMOLALITY AND TONICITY CONSIDERATIONS

* **Definitions**:
  * **Osmolarity**: Number of osmoles per liter of solution [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Osmolality**: Number of osmoles per kilogram of solvent [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Tonicity**: The effective osmolality - sum of concentrations of solutes with capacity to exert osmotic force across a membrane [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  
* **Clinical relevance**:
  * The calculated osmolarity of intravenous solutions as determined in the laboratory from the sum of osmotically active particles differs from the effective "in vivo" value once infused [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * This difference is due to rapid glucose metabolism (if present) and incomplete dissociation of ions in solution [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Example: The calculated osmolarity of 0.9% sodium chloride is 308 mOsm/L whereas the effective osmolality is 286 mOsm/kg(H2O) (plasma 275–295 mOsm/kg(H2O)) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * The traditional description of 0.9% sodium chloride as "hypertonic" is incorrect - it is isotonic in vivo [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Although the calculated osmolarity of balanced solutions is within the normal range for plasma, their effective osmolality in vivo is lower, making them hypotonic in vivo [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ This has implications for patients with brain injury or undergoing neurosurgery, where infusion of hypotonic solutions can result in cerebral edema [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Crystalloid selection should consider the specific clinical context. While balanced solutions (Ringer's lactate, PlasmaLyte) are generally preferred due to their more physiologic electrolyte composition and reduced risk of hyperchloremic acidosis, normal saline remains appropriate for specific indications. Hypertonic solutions offer unique advantages in certain clinical scenarios, particularly in neurosurgical and trauma patients. All crystalloids vary in their effective in vivo tonicity, with balanced solutions typically being slightly hypotonic, which may have implications in specific clinical settings such as neurological injury.

* **Comparison with plasma**: The electrolyte composition of IV fluids varies significantly from plasma and from each other, affecting their clinical applications and potential complications [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

* **Key observations from the table**:
  * **0.9% NaCl**: Significantly higher sodium (154 mmol/L) and chloride (154 mmol/L) compared to plasma, with no other electrolytes or buffers [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* This supraphysiologic chloride concentration contributes to hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis with large-volume administration [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Hartmann's solution**: Most similar sodium concentration to plasma among balanced solutions (131 mmol/L), contains lactate as buffer (29 mmol/L) and includes calcium [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* ⚠️ Should not be used in same giving set as blood due to calcium content [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Plasma-Lyte 148®**: Sodium concentration matches plasma (140 mmol/L), uses multiple buffers (acetate 27 mmol/L, gluconate 23 mmol/L), no calcium [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Can be used concurrently with blood products [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Sterofundin®**: Similar sodium to plasma (140 mmol/L), uses acetate (24 mmol/L) and maleate (5 mmol/L) as buffers, contains calcium (2.5 mmol/L) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Clinical implications**:
  * Solutions containing calcium (Hartmann's, Sterofundin®) should not be administered through the same line as citrated blood products to avoid coagulation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Balanced solutions generally have lower chloride content than 0.9% NaCl, reducing risk of hyperchloremic acidosis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * The choice between different balanced solutions may depend on availability, cost, and specific clinical considerations (e.g., need for concurrent blood product administration) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* 💡 **Buffer systems**: Different balanced solutions use different organic anions (lactate, acetate, gluconate, maleate) as bicarbonate precursors, all of which are metabolized to bicarbonate with slightly different kinetics [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** The electrolyte composition of IV fluids varies considerably, with important clinical implications. Balanced crystalloid solutions more closely approximate plasma composition and contain buffer systems (lactate, acetate, gluconate, or maleate) that are metabolized to bicarbonate. Normal saline's high chloride content makes it unsuitable for large-volume resuscitation due to risk of hyperchloremic acidosis. When selecting between balanced solutions, consider factors such as need for concurrent blood administration (avoid calcium-containing solutions), buffer type, and specific electrolyte requirements based on patient pathophysiology.

## 4.3. COLLOID SOLUTIONS



### 4.3.1. ALBUMIN

* **Blood-derived colloids**:
  * Available as 5% and 25% solutions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Derived from pooled donated blood after cold ethanol extraction and ultra-filtration; heat-treated (60°C for 10 hours) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Use 5% for hypovolemia; 25% for hypovolemia in patients with restricted fluid and Na⁺ intake [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Minimal risk for viral infection (hepatitis or HIV); theoretical risk of prion transmission [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Expensive, occasional shortages [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Physiological role**:
	* Albumin accounts for approximately 50% of the five available plasma proteins (the other 50% comprising α1-, α2-, β-globulin and γ-globulin) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Made up of 585 amino acids, has a molecular weight of 69,000 Da and is folded into a series of α helices forming three domains [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Exerts greater colloid osmotic effect than its molecular weight alone due to high negative charge [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Major contributor to colloid oncotic pressure [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Binds and transports drugs and endogenous molecules in plasma [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Acidic drugs (warfarin, aspirin, furosemide, amiodarone) tend to bind to albumin while basic drugs bind to α1-acid glycoprotein [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Reductions in albumin concentration can increase effects of drugs like warfarin by increasing the free fraction [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Carries bilirubin, fatty acids, calcium, and magnesium [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Despite low binding affinity, carries a significant amount of steroid due to its abundance [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Production and metabolism**:
	* Synthesized in the liver (10–15 g/day) in response to changes in plasma oncotic pressure [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Half-life of 20 days [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Just under 50% is intravascular; remainder in skin and muscle with lesser amounts in gut, liver, and subcutaneous tissue [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* In health, synthesis is balanced with metabolism [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Hypoalbuminemia may occur when metabolism or loss (renal or gastrointestinal) exceeds synthetic capacity [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Specific indications**:
	* Treatment of pancreatitis, burns, and as part of plasma exchange [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Established role in patients with ascites who require large volume paracentesis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Hepatorenal syndrome or spontaneous bacterial peritonitis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Some pediatric patients with nephrotic syndrome [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* ⚠️ Associated with worse outcomes when used in head injuries [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Preparation and manufacturing**:
	* Uses plasma from many donors (up to thousands) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Multiple purification steps including cold ethanol fractionation or chromatographic processes [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Pasteurization to eradicate transmissible viruses and other pathogens [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Resultant mixture typically >99% albumin [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* In the UK, plasma derivatives are sourced from imported plasma to reduce variant CJD transmission risk [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Plasma protein fraction (5%)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 4.3.2. SYNTHETIC COLLOIDS

* **Intravascular half-life** of crystalloid solutions is 20-30 minutes, while most colloid solutions have intravascular half-lives between 3 and 6 hours [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Generally accepted indications for colloids**:
  * Fluid resuscitation in patients with severe intravascular fluid deficits (e.g., hemorrhagic shock) prior to the arrival of blood for transfusion [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Fluid resuscitation in the presence of severe hypoalbuminemia or conditions associated with large protein losses such as burns [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Variation in practice**:
  * Some clinicians start colloids once fluid replacement exceeds 3-4L prior to transfusion [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Some suggest that during anesthesia, maintenance (and other) fluid requirements be provided with crystalloid solutions and blood loss be replaced on a milliliter-per-milliliter basis with colloid solutions (including blood products) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Gelatins (e.g., Gelofusine)**:
  * Large molecular weight proteins manufactured from bovine gelatin [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Heated to denature the protein, then cooled to allow new inter-chain bonds to form [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Bonds are either succinylated (Gelofusine) or urea cross-linked (Haemaccel) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Range of molecular weights exists, quoted as weight average or number average (latter better reflects mean osmotically active particle weight) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * > 95% of infused gelatin is excreted unchanged in the urine [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Unlike hydroxyethyl starches, not stored in the reticulo-endothelial system [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Due to the "no absorption rule" and complex Starling forces, plasma volume expansion effect is often similar to crystalloids [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Associated with histamine-mediated allergic reactions (approximately 6.2 per 100,000 administrations, similar to rocuronium) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Relatively short half-life compared to other synthetic colloids [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Evidence on coagulation disturbances is inconclusive [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Haemaccel contains significant calcium; blood should not be infused through a set previously used for this product [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * European guidelines recommend avoiding gelatins in patients with severe sepsis, acute kidney injury risk, head injury, or potential organ donors [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Dextrans**:
  * Complex polysaccharide available as dextran 70 (Macrodex) and dextran 40 (Rheomacrodex), which have average molecular weights of 70,000 and 40,000, respectively [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Volume expander that also reduces blood viscosity, von Willebrand factor antigen, platelet adhesion, and red blood cell aggregation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Used by microsurgeons to improve microcirculatory flow and decrease risk of microthrombus formation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Infusions exceeding 20 mL/kg per day can interfere with blood typing, may prolong bleeding time, and have been associated with bleeding complications [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Associated with acute kidney injury and failure; should not be administered to patients with history of kidney disease or to those at risk for acute kidney injury (e.g., elderly or critically ill) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Dextran 1 (Promit) may be administered prior to dextran 40 or dextran 70 to prevent severe anaphylactic reactions; acts as a hapten and binds any circulating dextran antibodies [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Hydroxyethyl starch (HES)**:
  * Derivative of amylopectin, a highly branched starch compound [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * When anhydroglucose residues are substituted with hydroxyethyl groups, results in increased solubility and reduced metabolism, extending half-life [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Many preparations based on concentration, molecular weights, degree of substitution, and C2:C6 ratio (reflecting position of substitution) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Kinetics**:
	* High molecular weight HES solutions provide longest plasma-volume-expanding effect but more side effects [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Low molecular weight HES solutions have limited duration of action [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Medium molecular weight HES solutions provide best balance [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Available in multiple formulations designated by concentration, molecular weight, degree of starch substitution (on a molar basis), and ratio of hydroxylation between the C2 and C6 positions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Highly effective as a plasma expander and less expensive than albumin [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ **Side effects**:
	* Renal – increased acute kidney injury in all patient groups [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Coagulation – effects vary depending on molecular weight and degree of substitution [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Accumulation – taken up and stored by reticuloendothelial system, causing pruritus [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Anaphylaxis – incidence approximately 1 in 20,000 [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Decreases von Willebrand factor antigen levels, may prolong prothrombin time, and has been associated with hemorrhagic complications [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Fell out of favor due to association with increased mortality and morbidity (coagulopathy, pruritus, nephrotoxicity, acute renal failure) - "6S Trial" [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Removed from Surviving Sepsis Campaign recommendations [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Maximum dose for 6% HES: 15-20 ml/kg/day [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Newer starch formulations called tetrastarches are less likely to cause coagulopathy and anaphylaxis and can be given in larger doses (maximum dose: 50ml/kg/day) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * In 2018, marketing authorizations for HES were suspended across the European Union due to risk of death and acute kidney injury in certain patient populations including critically ill patients and those with sepsis, renal failure, or undergoing cardiac surgery [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Colloid solutions provide more sustained intravascular volume expansion than crystalloids but carry specific risks and higher costs. While albumin remains the safest colloid option, its expense limits routine use. Synthetic colloids (starches, gelatins, dextrans) have fallen out of favor due to evidence of harm, particularly regarding coagulopathy and kidney injury in critically ill patients. Their use should be restricted to specific indications where benefits clearly outweigh risks.

## 4.4. BICARBONATE SOLUTIONS



### 4.4.1. COMPOSITIONS AND INDICATIONS

* **Physiological role**: Bicarbonate forms part of the bicarbonate-carbonic acid buffering system, the major extracellular buffering system [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * In equilibrium with carbonic acid, which rapidly dissociates to dissolved CO₂ and water [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * The ratio of dissolved CO₂ to bicarbonate is 20:1 [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * The bicarbonate-carbonic acid buffering system becomes more effective as pH falls below normal [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Available formulations**:
  * **Isotonic/Mildly hypotonic solutions**: 1.26% and 1.4% solutions, which can be used for maintenance fluids (attractive due to lack of chloride) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* 1.26% contains 150 mmol/L Na⁺ and 150 mmol/L HCO₃⁻ [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Hypertonic solutions**: 4.2% and 8.4% solutions are also available [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* 8.4% preparation contains 1000 mmol/L Na⁺ and 1000 mmol/L HCO₃⁻ [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* ⚠️ These solutions are highly irritant and should only be infused into a central vein [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* ⚠️ They also have a significant sodium load [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Clinical indications**:
  * Severe inorganic metabolic acidosis (pH < 7.1) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Forced alkaline diuresis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Management of hyperkalaemia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Dysrhythmias or QT prolongation in tricyclic antidepressant overdose [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Restoration of plasma [H⁺] towards concentrations at which enzyme activity is usually most efficient [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * To restore cardiovascular stability in severe metabolic acidosis pending resolution of the underlying cause (when given slowly) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * As a buffer in dialysate fluids [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * 💡 Restoration of pH to above 7.1 is thought to reverse myocardial depression and poor catecholamine sensitivity that occurs at more severe acidemia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

### 4.4.2. SIDE EFFECTS AND CONSIDERATIONS

* **Adverse effects**:
  * Hypokalaemia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Hypernatraemia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Decreased ionized calcium with excessive doses, causing impaired myocardial activity and dysrhythmias [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Impaired oxygen release from hemoglobin (left shift of the oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Hypercarbia in ventilated patients if ventilation is not temporarily increased during administration [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Intracellular acidosis can worsen as dissolved CO₂ moves readily across the cell membrane while bicarbonate moves much less so, potentially impairing myocardial contractility [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Administration considerations**:
  * The effect of bicarbonate on cardiovascular performance is complex, depending on the predominant effect (restoration of pH and subsequent optimization of enzyme kinetics, intracellular acidosis, or hypocalcemia) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Administration should be slow to maximize beneficial effects while minimizing negative effects on ionized calcium [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Sodium bicarbonate solutions are available in various concentrations and have specific indications for treating severe metabolic acidosis, hyperkalaemia, and certain toxicological emergencies. While they can be effective in restoring plasma pH and improving cardiovascular stability in severe acidosis, they must be administered with caution due to potential adverse effects including hypokalaemia, hypernatraemia, decreased ionized calcium, and paradoxical intracellular acidosis. Slow administration is recommended to balance therapeutic benefits against potential complications.

### 5.1.1. PRINCIPLES OF IV FLUID PRESCRIPTION


#### 5.1.1.1. MAINTENANCE REQUIREMENTS

* IV fluid therapy is potentially harmful, as harmful as any drug [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Only indicated when:
  * No oral intake allowed [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Oral intake not tolerated [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Requires correction of deficits or ongoing losses [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Maintenance**: covers anticipated losses of water and electrolytes over the upcoming 24hr in a euvolemic patient [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Recommended method**: Holiday-Segar method for 24h estimation; 4-2-1 rule for hourly estimation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Generally, 1 ml of water is required for each calorie consumed in a child with average kidney function [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

#### 5.1.1.2. DAILY FLUID REQUIREMENTS AND TURNOVER

* **Basic fluid need**: 1.0 mL/kg/h in adults `[H:77]`
  * 1.2 L/day in 50 kg patient `[H:78]`
  * 2.4 L/day in 100 kg patient `[H:78]`
  * Usually set 25% higher to obtain reasonable margin `[H:78]`
* **Daily electrolyte requirements** (per 80 kg adult) `[H:76]`:
  * Sodium: 80–120 mmol/day (1–1.5 mmol/kg/day) `[H:76]`
  * Potassium: 60–100 mmol/day (0.75–1.25 mmol/kg/day) `[H:76]`
  * Chloride: 60–100 mmol/day (0.75–1.25 mmol/kg/day) `[H:76]`
  * Phosphate: 20–45 mmol/day (0.2–0.5 mmol/kg/day) `[H:76]`
  * Magnesium: 5–10 mmol/day (0.1–0.2 mmol/kg/day) `[H:76]`
  * Calcium: 5–10 mmol/day (0.1–0.2 mmol/kg/day) `[H:76]`
* **Fluid losses** consist primarily of `[H:78]`:
  * Insensible water losses (evaporation, mostly from airways) `[H:78]`
  * Baseline diuresis `[H:78]`
  * Each accounts for approximately half of water loss in fasting individuals `[H:78]`
  * Small amount (~300 mL/day) created metabolically from glucose oxidation `[H:78]`
  * Sweating normally minimal but increases with fever and exercise `[H:78]`
* **Limited tolerance for fluid losses** `[H:78]`:
  * Blood volume particularly sensitive `[H:78]`
  * Loss of 20% blood volume (~1 L in adult): arterial pressure maintained by catecholamine release, though cardiac output falls `[H:79]`
  * Beyond 20% loss: body switches to vasodilation strategy with abrupt pressure drop `[H:79]`
  * 💡 Low flow and low pressure favor blood clot formation - beneficial in uncontrolled hemorrhage `[H:79]`

#### 5.1.1.3. TERMINOLOGY OF FLUID DEFICITS

* **Hypovolemia**: Loss of blood volume below normal `[H:79]`
  * Arterial pressure maintained by catecholamine release during losses up to 20% `[H:79]`
  * Cardiac output falls despite maintained pressure `[H:79]`
* **Volume depletion**: Loss of extracellular fluid `[H:79]`
  * Occurs with diarrhea and vomiting `[H:79]`
  * Different from hypovolemia (which specifically refers to blood volume) `[H:79]`
* **Dehydration** (hyperosmotic dehydration/intracellular volume depletion) `[H:79]`:
  * Due to insufficient water intake `[H:79]`
  * Common in elderly `[H:79]`
  * Hallmark: rise in serum osmolality to ≥300 mosmol/kg `[H:79]`
  * Quickly leads to poor mental and physical performance in conscious humans `[H:79]`
* **Effect on fluid compartments** (see Table 3.2 from Hahn):
  * **Isotonic fluid loss**: ↓ interstitial volume, ↓ plasma volume (hypovolemia), normal intracellular volume, normal plasma tonicity `[H:79]`
  * **Hypotonic fluid loss**: ↓ interstitial volume, normal/↓ plasma volume, ↓ intracellular volume, ↑ plasma tonicity (hypertonicity) `[H:79]`
  * **Isotonic fluid gain**: ↑ interstitial volume, ↑ plasma volume (hypervolemia), normal intracellular volume, normal plasma tonicity `[H:79]`
  * **Hypotonic fluid gain**: ↑ interstitial volume, normal/↑ plasma volume, ↑ intracellular volume, ↓ plasma tonicity (hypotonicity) `[H:79]`
  * **Hypertonic fluid gain**: ↑ interstitial volume, ↑ plasma volume, ↓ intracellular volume, ↑ plasma tonicity `[H:79]`

      * Diabetes insipidus [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
      * Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
    * Conditions requiring fluid restriction:
      * Congestive heart failure [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

#### 5.1.1.4. DEFICIT CALCULATION

* **Deficit (ml)** calculation:
  * % dehydration = ((First BW - old BW)/old BW) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Deficit = % dehydration/100 × BW (kg) × 1000 [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * In fasted patients - deficit can be estimated by multiplying the normal maintenance rate by the length of the fast [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* But the real deficit is less as a result of renal conservation—after all, how many of us would feel the need to consume nearly 1 L of fluid upon awakening after 8 h of sleep? [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* For children in hypovolemic shock - **10-20 ml/kg crystalloid** (0.9% saline or Ringer's lactate) or colloid over 30-60 minutes [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#3. Patient Care (PC)|ⓘ]]

#### 5.1.1.5. ONGOING LOSSES

* Indicated when excessive fluid losses continue despite initial fluid replacement attempts [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Determined by measuring losses and replacing milliliter for milliliter [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Estimation of blood loss**:
  * Most accurate if sponges and pads are weighed [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * A fully soaked "4 × 4" is generally considered to hold 10 mL of blood, whereas a soaked "lap" may hold 100 to 150 mL [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Obligatory losses**: evaporation, internal redistribution (third spacing) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Replacing redistributive and evaporative losses**:
  * Values provided are only guidelines, and actual needs vary considerably from patient to patient [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Fluid replacement can be guided by goal-directed fluid therapy (GDFT) regimen [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

![Traditional vs. current thinking on third space losses](FLUIDS_FLUIDS_Untitled_20.png_d16842.png_f151e3.png)

* 💡 Traditional intraoperative fluid management included replacement of fluid thought to accumulate extravascularly in surgically manipulated tissue (the "third space") in addition to blood loss and other sensible and insensible losses. However, recent evidence has demonstrated no benefit and perhaps even morbidity with replacing "third space" losses [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#3. Patient Care (PC)|ⓘ]]

#### 5.1.1.6. ELECTROLYTE CONSIDERATIONS

* **Fluid choice considerations**:
  * There is no single "right" fluid; therapy is generally safe if:
	* Fluid therapy is individualized [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Prescribed in appropriate amounts (not excessive) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Monitoring of serum electrolytes if IV fluid is administered for >24h [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Dextrose considerations**:
	* Glucose-containing IVFs are reserved for patients at risk for hypoglycemia (i.e., infants or patients receiving insulin) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* ⚠️ Risk for hyperglycemia from surgical stress can lead to iatrogenic hyperglycemia, which can induce osmotic diuresis, impair resuscitation, and aggravate ischemic neurologic injury [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Composition of fluid losses**:
	* Ideally, deficits should be replaced preoperatively, and fluid choice should be similar in composition to fluids lost [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

![Composition of body fluid losses](FLUIDS_FLUIDS_Untitled_21.png_3d59ed.png_c89ca2.png)

    * Wound, burns, and ascitic fluid - protein-rich with electrolyte concentrations similar to plasma [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
    * GI tract secretions vary greatly in composition [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
      * Gastric fluid is acidic and chloride-rich → hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis → corrected with 0.9% NaCl [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
      * Diarrhea involves bicarbonate loss → hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis → IVF containing bicarbonate or bicarbonate equivalent (e.g., Ringer's lactate) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Pediatric recommendations**:
	* 5% D/S (A - newer evidence shows this doesn't cause hypernatremia, compared to half-strength solutions which increase risk of hyponatremia in children >3 months old) or PlasmaLyte [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

#### 5.1.1.7. MONITORING AND ADJUSTMENT

* **Baseline and repeat measurements** of electrolytes: Na, K, Cl, HCO₃, acid-base status [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * If gastroenteritis develops with hyponatremia while on 5% D/S (R) → change to 5% D/S (A) due to hyponatremia approach → need less free water [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Nursing monitoring**: vital signs (BP, HR, RR), urine output, weight [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Alternative approach**: Goal-directed fluid therapy
  * Current GDFT concepts assume no need for maintenance fluid administration, instead using hemodynamic variables such as stroke volume, cardiac output, cardiac index, and mean arterial blood pressure to determine volume responsiveness and guide fluid administration by bolus [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Appropriate fluid prescription requires considering maintenance needs, addressing deficits, replacing ongoing losses, and selecting appropriate electrolyte composition based on specific patient factors. The Holiday-Segar (4-2-1) method provides a useful starting point for maintenance, but adjustments are necessary based on patient characteristics, clinical context, and monitoring parameters. Modern approaches increasingly emphasize individualized therapy and goal-directed strategies.

### 5.1.2. PERIOPERATIVE FLUID STRATEGIES

> **Syllabus**: [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|Part B: Perioperative Medicine]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#3. Patient Care (PC)|Part C: Patient Care]]
#### 5.1.2.1. LIBERAL VS RESTRICTIVE APPROACHES

* **Blood loss management**:
  * Ideally, blood loss should be replaced with sufficient crystalloid/colloid solutions to maintain normovolemia until the danger of anemia outweighs risk of transfusion [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * In settings other than massive trauma, most clinicians administer lactated Ringer's solution or PlasmaLyte in approximately three to four times the volume of the blood lost, or colloid in a 1:1 ratio, until the transfusion trigger point is reached [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * At that time, blood is replaced unit-for-unit as it is lost, with reconstituted packed red blood cells [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Transfusion threshold determination**:
  * In patients with normal hematocrit, transfusion generally begins when blood loss is 10-20% of blood volume [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Estimation process:
	* First estimate blood volume [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Estimate the RBC volume (RBCV) at preoperative hematocrit (RBCV preop) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Estimate RBCV at a hematocrit of 30% assuming normal blood volume is obtained [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Calculate the RBCV lost [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Allowable blood loss = RBCV lost × 3 [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

![Blood loss calculation](FLUIDS_FLUIDS_Untitled_22.png_8dcd31.png_107aa0.png)

![Blood loss table](FLUIDS_FLUIDS_Untitled_23.png_c22114.png_5b7638.png)

* **Clinical guidelines for transfusion**:
  * Transfusing 1 unit of red blood cells will increase hemoglobin 1 g/dL and the hematocrit 2% to 3% in adults [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * A 10-mL/kg transfusion of red blood cells will increase hemoglobin concentration by 3 g/dL and the hematocrit by 10% [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Consequences of volume overload**:
  * Increased mortality and length of ICU/hospital stay [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Increased myocardial morbidity [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Increased pulmonary, periorbital, and gut edema [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Decreased hematocrit and albumin [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Worsened wound healing/increased anastomosis dehiscence due to edema [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

#### 5.1.2.2. GOAL-DIRECTED THERAPY

* Clinical studies have shown that goal-directed hemodynamic therapy during high-risk surgery (cardiac, hip, major bowel) is associated with improved postoperative outcomes (recommended by Enhanced Recovery After Surgery [ERAS] guidelines) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#3. Patient Care (PC)|ⓘ]]
* In patients undergoing surgery of limited scope, fluid restriction appears to be less well tolerated than more liberal fluid therapy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* In patients undergoing major invasive surgery, data are mixed regarding benefits of different fluid strategies:
  * Brandstrup (Elective Colorectal Surgery) - Cardiopulmonary complications, tissue-healing complications, and total postoperative complications were significantly fewer in the fluid-restricted group [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#3. Patient Care (PC)|ⓘ]]
  * Nisanevich (Intra-abdominal Surgery) - Less prompt return of gastrointestinal function and longer hospital stays in patients receiving conventional fluid therapy (10 mL/kg/hr of LR) than in patients receiving restricted fluid therapy (4 mL/kg/hr) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#3. Patient Care (PC)|ⓘ]]
  * Corcoran (Major Surgery) - Patients in the liberal groups had a higher risk of pneumonia (RR 2.2), pulmonary edema (RR 3.8), and longer hospital stay (mean difference 2 days) than their comparative groups; could not establish whether GDT is superior to non-GDT restrictive fluid therapy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#3. Patient Care (PC)|ⓘ]]
  * Cochrane Review (Elective Abdominal or Orthopedic) - Intraoperative restrictive versus GDT found no difference in major complications, all-cause mortality, length of stay, or renal failure between the two groups [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#3. Patient Care (PC)|ⓘ]]

#### 5.1.2.3. EVIDENCE-BASED GUIDELINES

* **Rational fluid management suggestions**:
  * Use good clinical judgment [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Tailor management to patient, surgery, and clinical picture [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Use balanced fluid therapy: use crystalloid for maintenance, consider use of colloid as discussed [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Consider conservative replacement of interstitial losses or UOP unless vital signs are unstable or there are other signs of inadequate perfusion [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Specific patient populations**:
  * Critically ill with acute lung injury (ALI) may benefit from judicious fluid administration [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* ARDS Clinical Trials Network - Conservative fluid group had better oxygenation, fewer ventilator and ICU days, and had no greater incidence of acute renal failure [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#3. Patient Care (PC)|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Optimal perioperative fluid management requires balancing adequate resuscitation against the risks of volume overload. While the evidence remains mixed, there's a trend toward more individualized approaches with judicious fluid administration during major surgery. Goal-directed therapy using dynamic parameters to guide fluid administration shows promise in high-risk patients but hasn't consistently demonstrated superiority over thoughtful restrictive strategies. The key is to tailor the approach to the specific patient, procedure, and clinical context.

### 5.1.3. SPECIAL CLINICAL SCENARIOS


#### 5.1.3.1. BURNS MANAGEMENT

* **Parkland Formula** for volume calculation:
  * Volume = %BSA × 4 ml/kg × kg [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Give 1/2 over the 1st 8 hours [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Give 1/2 over the next 16 hours [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Replace with Lactated Ringers [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * %BSA is determined by the "Rule of Nines" [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Rule of Nines**:
  * Head and neck = 9% [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Each upper extremity = 9% [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Each lower extremity = 18% [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Anterior trunk = 18% [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Posterior trunk = 18% [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Perineum = 1% [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

![Rule of Nines](FLUIDS_FLUIDS_Untitled_7.png_d1855b.png_802243.png)

* First 8 hours: 2cc/kg × (weight) × (%BSA) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Next 16 hrs: 2cc/kg × (weight) × (%BSA) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

#### 5.1.3.2. INTRAOPERATIVE OLIGURIA

* **Pre-renal causes** (decreased renal perfusion):
  * Hypovolemia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Decreased cardiac output (LV dysfunction, valvular disease) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Decreased mean arterial pressure [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Compromised perfusion due to increased intra-abdominal pressure (e.g., laparoscopy & pneumoperitoneum) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Post-renal causes** (post-renal obstruction):
  * Foley catheter kinked, clogged, displaced, or disconnected [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Surgical manipulation of kidneys, ureters, bladder, or urethra [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Renal causes**:
  * Neuroendocrine response to surgery (activation of renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system with increased ADH), which is age-dependent [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Baroreceptor response to positive pressure ventilation also activates neuroendocrine response [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Treatments**:
  * Relieve obstruction: check Foley catheter; consider IV dyes (e.g., indigo carmine, methylene blue) to check for patency of ureters (i.e., urology cases) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Increase renal perfusion: fluids (bolus vs. increased maintenance rate), vasopressors/inotropes, or furosemide [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* 💡 In general, urine output is a poor marker of renal/organ perfusion [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#3. Patient Care (PC)|ⓘ]]

#### 5.1.3.3. PEDIATRIC CONSIDERATIONS

* **Choosing between 5% dextrose with half-strength or 0.9% saline** for a 5kg child requiring 500ml/day maintenance fluid when nil by mouth:
  * 5% Dextrose with 0.9% normal saline:
	* Slightly hypertonic [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Risk of hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Sodium load = 77 mmol ÷ 5 kg = 15.4 mmol/kg/day (high; normal requirement is 2-4 mmol/kg/day) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* ⚠️ Avoid in patients with renal, cardiac, and liver disease where excessive sodium load may be harmful [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * 5% Dextrose with 0.45% saline - 5% D/S (R):
	* Sodium load = 7.7 mmol/kg/day (higher but acceptable compared to daily requirement of 2-4 mmol/kg/day) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Lower tonicity; small risk of hyponatremia but can be avoided by not ordering excessive fluid [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* ⚠️ Avoid if:
	  * Hyponatremia is present (serum sodium <130 mmol/L) → use 5% D/S (A) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	  * Patient has acute neurological conditions (e.g., meningitis, head injury) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	  * Patient is at risk of SIADH (e.g., pneumonia) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Recommendations for maintenance IV fluid in children**:
  * Well child with normal hydration:
	* 0.45% saline with 5% dextrose (5% DSR) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Add IV 7.45% KCl 1-2 mmol/kg/day if required [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* ⚠️ Be aware of risks of hypotonic solution - do not use if:
	  * Serum Na is low [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	  * For volume resuscitation (use normal saline) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	  * For initial treatment of children with acute neurological conditions (e.g., meningitis) - risk of cerebral edema [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Child with gastroenteritis and dehydration:
	* For shock: Fluid resuscitation with 10-20 ml/kg crystalloid/colloid over 30-60 min [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Calculate fluid deficit if child is dehydrated [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Total volume replacement over 24hr = deficit + maintenance [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	  * Give 1/2 total volume over first 8hrs, remainder over next 16hr using DSR [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	  * Exception: For diabetic ketoacidosis, meningitis, hypernatremic dehydration - slower rehydration (risk of raised ICP) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Replace ongoing excessive losses separately [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

## 6.1. SPECIALTY BODY FLUIDS - OVERVIEW

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|Part A: Pharmacology]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|Part B: Perioperative Medicine]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#2. Medical Knowledge (MK)|Part C: Medical Knowledge]]

### 6.1.1. LYMPHATIC SYSTEM


#### 6.1.1.1. LYMPH FORMATION AND FUNCTION

* **Lymph** is an extracellular fluid that drains via the lymphatic system [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Functions**:
  * Return of fluid and small amount of protein to the circulation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Maintains oncotic gradient between intravascular space and extracellular fluid [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Fat digestion and transport in the GI tract (lymph forms the basis of chyle) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Lymphocytes and antibodies play role in immunity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

#### 6.1.1.2. LYMPHATIC CIRCULATION

* Present throughout the body except bone, spinal cord, and brain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Consists of rudimentary capillaries with no basement membrane and valves [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Maintains approximately 1 mmHg pressure in the system [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* One-way flow is enhanced by external compression and arterial pulsation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Drains via thoracic duct on the left and lymphatic duct on the right into the subclavian veins [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** The lymphatic system plays a crucial role in maintaining fluid balance by returning interstitial fluid and proteins to the circulation, while also supporting immune function and fat transport from the GI tract. Its one-way flow system, though low-pressure, effectively moves fluid through a valved network to ultimately return to the venous circulation.

### 6.1.2. SPECIAL FLUIDS


#### 6.1.2.1. PLEURAL FLUID

* **Production**: Generated by pleural membrane covering the parietal and visceral pleurae [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Characteristics**: Serous fluid with pH around 7.6 and low protein content [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Function**: Serves as a lubricant, allowing the two pleural layers to slide past each other; uses surface tension to maintain contact between pleural layers and facilitate negative pressure ventilation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Drainage**: Mostly via bronchial venous circulation, but approximately 10% drains via lymphatic system [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Pathological accumulation (pleural effusion)** occurs with:
  * Increased capillary permeability [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Increased hydrostatic pressure in the plasma [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Decreased oncotic pressure in the plasma [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Greater negative pressure in the pleural space [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Types of pleural effusions**:
  * **Transudate**: Heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Exudate**: Infection, cancer [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

#### 6.1.2.2. PERICARDIAL FLUID

* **Normal volume**: 20-50ml of pericardial fluid [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Function**: Acts as a lubricant; allows parietal and visceral pericardial layers to slide over each other; provides protection against blunt trauma [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Cardiac tamponade**: Results when volume of pericardial fluid increases rapidly [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * The stiff pericardial sac causes compression of the heart, preventing diastolic filling and reducing preload [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Causes**:
	* **Acute**: Trauma, infection, dissection, post-operative bleeding [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
	* **Chronic**: Pericarditis, cancer [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Beck's triad** (classic signs):
	* Muffled heart sounds [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
	* Increased jugular venous distension [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
	* Hypotension [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Treatment**: Drainage of excess fluid [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

#### 6.1.2.3. PERITONEAL FLUID

* **Production**: Generated by peritoneal cells [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* **Function**: Lubricates intestines to facilitate peristalsis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* **Ascites**: Pathological accumulation of peritoneal fluid, associated with:
  * Heart failure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Malignancy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
  * Cirrhosis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]

#### 6.1.2.4. INTRAOCULAR FLUID

* **Components**: Aqueous and vitreous humor [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* **Production**: Plasma dialysate secreted by choroidal plexus at 0.08ml/hr [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* **Circulation**: Passes through pupil into anterior chamber; drains via canal of Schlemm into venous system [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* **Function**: Provides nutrition and oxygen for anterior chamber [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]
* **Vitreous humor** contains phagocytes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Specialized body fluids serve critical functions in their respective compartments, primarily providing lubrication, maintaining appropriate spatial relationships between tissues, and facilitating physiological functions. Pathological accumulations of these fluids (effusions, tamponade, ascites) can significantly impair organ function and require specific diagnostic and therapeutic approaches.

# 7. MINERALS AND ELECTROLYTES



## 7.1. MINERALS AND ELECTROLYTES - OVERVIEW

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|Part A: Physiology]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|Part B: Perioperative Medicine]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#2. Medical Knowledge (MK)|Part C: Medical Knowledge]]

### 7.1.1. MAGNESIUM



#### 7.1.1.1. PHYSIOLOGICAL ROLE

* **Distribution**: Magnesium is the second most abundant intracellular cation after potassium; 99% is intracellular [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Functions**:
  * Essential cofactor in multiple enzymatic reactions [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Required for ATP function [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Plays a key role in neurotransmission and neuromuscular excitability [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Normal serum range**: 0.7-1.0 mmol/L [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Formulation**: Magnesium sulfate (MgSO₄) is presented as either a 20% or 50% solution [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * A 10 ml vial of 50% contains 5 g or 20 mmol [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

#### 7.1.1.2. CLINICAL USES

* **Asthma**: Intravenous magnesium sulfate 2 g over 20 minutes is used as a bronchodilator in acute severe asthma [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Mechanism of action is thought to be smooth muscle relaxation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Limited evidence of benefit [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Pre-eclampsia and eclampsia**:
  * Indicated to treat eclamptic seizures and prevent seizures in severe pre-eclampsia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Dosing protocol:
	* Loading dose: 4 g intravenously over 5-15 minutes [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Maintenance: Infusion of 1 g per hour maintained for 24 hours [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* If the woman has had an eclamptic fit, continue infusion for 24 hours after the last fit [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Recurrent fits: Treat with further dose of 2-4 g given intravenously over 5-15 minutes [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Arrhythmias**: Specifically indicated for:
  * Torsades de points [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Other ventricular and supraventricular tachycardias [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Analgesia**: Used as an adjunct; may reduce opiate requirements [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

#### 7.1.1.3. ADVERSE EFFECTS

* **Hypermagnesemia**: Defined as serum concentration >1.05 mmol/L [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Usually due to excess parenteral administration [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Rapid or excessive intravenous dosing can cause flushing, hypotension, weakness, bradycardia, and ultimately severe cardiovascular suppression [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Concentration-related effects**:
  * > 4 mmol/L: Nausea, hyporeflexia, slurred speech [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * > 6 mmol/L: Muscle weakness, respiratory depression, and bradycardia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * > 10 mmol/L: Cardiac arrest [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Management**: Use intravenous calcium as physiological antagonist [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Magnesium is a crucial electrolyte with diverse physiological roles in enzymatic reactions, neuromuscular function, and cardiovascular stability. Therapeutically, it's valuable for managing pre-eclampsia/eclampsia, certain arrhythmias, and as an adjunct for bronchodilation in severe asthma. Administration requires careful monitoring as hypermagnesemia can cause progressive neuromuscular and cardiovascular depression, with life-threatening effects at high concentrations.

### 7.1.2. CALCIUM



#### 7.1.2.1. PHYSIOLOGICAL ROLE

* **Function**: Calcium is a widespread cation with central roles in:
  * Muscle contraction [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Excitable tissue function [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Cellular messenger systems [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

#### 7.1.2.2. CLINICAL USES

* **Available preparations**:
  * Calcium gluconate and calcium chloride are the key intravenous preparations [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Calcium chloride contains three times the amount of Ca²⁺ per gram compared to calcium gluconate due to the additional mass associated with gluconate [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Indications**:
  * Hypocalcemic tetany [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Acute hyperkalaemia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * 10 ml calcium chloride 10% (centrally) or 30 ml 10% calcium gluconate are indicated to stabilize the myocardium in hyperkalaemia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Calcium is essential for neuromuscular function, cardiac contractility, and numerous cellular processes. Intravenous calcium preparations are primarily used to treat hypocalcemic tetany and stabilize the myocardium in acute hyperkalemia. Calcium chloride provides three times more elemental calcium than calcium gluconate, making it more potent but requiring central venous administration due to its irritant properties.

### 7.1.3. PHOSPHORUS



#### 7.1.3.1. PHYSIOLOGICAL ROLE

* **Distribution**: Most phosphorus in the body is found in bone, with a small component in tissues and extracellular fluid [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Intracellular function**: Forms vital parts of various intracellular messenger molecules (ATP, cAMP, 2,3-DPG) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Regulation**: Serum phosphate levels regulated by:
  * Dietary intake [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Renal reabsorption controlled by parathyroid hormone [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Normal serum range**: 0.8-1.4 mmol/L [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

#### 7.1.3.2. CLINICAL USES

* **Hypophosphatemia**:
  * **Common causes**:
	* Redistribution into cells (refeeding syndrome, severe respiratory alkalosis, sepsis) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Treatment effects (insulin, adrenaline, glucose administration) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Common in diabetic hyperglycemic disorders during treatment [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Increased urinary excretion and decreased intestinal absorption [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Clinical features**:
	* Arrhythmias [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Respiratory failure due to muscle weakness (particularly diaphragm) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Seizures, confusion, and paresthesia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Treatment**:
	* Intravenous phosphate indicated for severe or symptomatic disease [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* ⚠️ May cause thrombophlebitis or hypocalcemia with rapid administration [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Should be given as a prolonged infusion [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Phosphorus plays a crucial role in energy metabolism, intracellular signaling, and oxygen delivery via 2,3-DPG. Hypophosphatemia commonly occurs during refeeding, treatment of diabetic emergencies, and in critical illness due to intracellular redistribution. Severe deficiency can cause neuromuscular dysfunction including respiratory failure, cardiac arrhythmias, and neurological symptoms. Intravenous replacement must be administered cautiously as a prolonged infusion to avoid thrombophlebitis and hypocalcemia.

# 8. NUTRITION AND REFEEDING


## 8.1. NUTRITION AND REFEEDING - OVERVIEW

> **Syllabus**: [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|Part B: Perioperative Medicine]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#3. Patient Care (PC)|Part C: Patient Care]]

### 8.1.1. PARENTERAL NUTRITION


#### 8.1.1.1. COMPONENTS AND ADMINISTRATION

* **Definition**: Parenteral nutrition (PN) refers to the provision of calories (lipid and carbohydrate), nitrogen (amino acids), electrolytes, vitamins, minerals, trace elements, and water via a parenteral route [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Formulation**:
  * Macronutrients are in elemental forms and do not require digestion as they bypass the gastrointestinal tract [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Generally contained within an "all-in-one" bag [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Most solutions have high osmolality and are irritant to veins [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Standard bags often contain no vitamins or trace elements, which need to be added in a sterile pharmacy preparation area [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Administration route**:
  * Typically requires central venous access due to high osmolality [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Peripheral vein administration possible with low osmolarity solutions [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Nutritional components**:
  * **Energy**:
	* Requirements based on body weight [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Acute phase of critical illness: 20-25 kcal/kg/day [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Recovery phase: 25-30 kcal/kg/day [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* ⚠️ For refeeding risk: Reduced to 10 kcal/kg/day or even 5 kcal/kg/day [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  
  * **Carbohydrate**:
	* Main calorie source, providing up to 60% of non-protein calories [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Administered as glucose [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  
  * **Lipid**:
	* Provides up to 40% of non-protein calories [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Available as various intravenous lipid emulsions [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	  * Based on soybean oil (most common), fish oil, olive oil [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	  * Contain mostly long-chain triglycerides plus phospholipids and essential fatty acids [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	  * Advanced mixtures include medium-chain triglycerides [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* **Advantages of medium-chain triglycerides**:
	  * Hydrolyzed faster by lipases [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	  * Not incorporated into chylomicrons [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	  * Go directly to liver (avoiding lymphatics) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	  * Passively cross mitochondrial membrane [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  
  * **Nitrogen**:
	* Provided as mixtures of essential and non-essential amino acids [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Recommended dose: 0.8-1.5 g protein (0.13-0.24 g nitrogen)/kg/day [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* ⚠️ Should be reduced to 50% in patients at risk of refeeding syndrome [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

#### 8.1.1.2. INDICATIONS AND COMPLICATIONS

* **Monitoring requirements**:
  * Regular measurement of trace elements [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Recommended to measure zinc, selenium, manganese, ferritin, folate, and vitamins B12 and D:
	* On starting parenteral nutrition [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* After six weeks of use [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Complications**:
  * **Related to vascular access device**: Infection, thrombosis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Metabolic complications**:
	* Refeeding syndrome [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Fatty liver and abnormal LFTs [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Hyperglycemia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Micronutrient deficiencies [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Overfeeding has negative consequences in terms of metabolic dysfunction even in those without refeeding syndrome [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Parenteral nutrition provides complete nutritional support when enteral feeding is impossible or inadequate. It requires careful formulation and administration, typically via central venous access. Proper dosing of energy, protein, and micronutrients must be individualized, with particular caution in patients at risk for refeeding syndrome. Regular monitoring of metabolic parameters and trace elements is essential to prevent complications and optimize nutritional status.

### 8.1.2. ENTERAL NUTRITION


#### 8.1.2.1. TYPES OF ENTERAL FEEDS

* **Definition**: Enteral nutrition refers to the provision of calories, protein, electrolytes, vitamins, minerals, trace elements, and fluids via an intestinal route, usually through a gastric or jejunal tube [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Categories of enteral feeds**:
  1. **Polymeric (whole protein)**:
	 * Contain protein in the form normally found in diet (whole protein) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	 * Standard formula for most patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  
  2. **Pre-digested (peptide/semi-elemental/elemental)**:
	 * Contain protein as smaller molecules (short peptides or free amino acids) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	 * Pre-digestion aims to improve nutrient absorption in malabsorption [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	 * May be useful in patients with:
	   * Digestive defects (malabsorptive syndromes unresponsive to pancreatic enzyme supplementation) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	   * Failure to tolerate standard enteral nutrition (persistent diarrhea) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  
  3. **Disease-specific or immune-enhancing feeds**:
	 * Include omega-3, arginine, and antioxidants [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	 * Evidence of benefit is lacking [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Standard enteral feed characteristics**:
  * Calorie density of 1 kcal/ml [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Lactose-free [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Contains simple and complex carbohydrates and fatty acids [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * For volume-restricted patients, concentrated feeds available (up to 2 kcal/ml) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* ⚠️ These are hyperosmolar and may contribute to diarrhea [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Specialized formulations available (low sodium, low potassium, added fiber) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

#### 8.1.2.2. INDICATIONS AND ADMINISTRATION

* **Macronutrient composition**:
  * Typically 50% of calories from carbohydrate [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * 30% from lipid [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Remainder from protein [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Various high/low macronutrient combinations available for specific needs [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Administration considerations**:
  * Preferred over parenteral nutrition when the gastrointestinal tract is functional [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Lower risk of infectious complications compared to parenteral nutrition [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * More physiologic and often better tolerated [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Can be delivered via nasogastric, nasoduodenal, nasojejunal tubes, or via percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy/jejunostomy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Enteral nutrition is the preferred method of nutritional support when the gastrointestinal tract is functional. It comes in various formulations ranging from standard polymeric feeds to specialized pre-digested or disease-specific formulas. The choice of formula and administration route should be tailored to the patient's specific needs, digestive capabilities, and clinical condition. While standard formulations (1 kcal/ml) are suitable for most patients, concentrated or specialized formulations may be needed in specific circumstances.

### 8.1.3. REFEEDING SYNDROME


#### 8.1.3.1. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

* **Definition**: Refeeding syndrome refers to the clinical complications that can occur as a result of fluid and electrolyte shifts during aggressive nutritional rehabilitation of malnourished patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Mechanism**:
  * During starvation, the body depletes intracellular minerals and phosphate [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * When nutrition is reintroduced, increased insulin stimulates cellular uptake of phosphate, potassium, and magnesium [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Rapid refeeding drives glucose and amino acids into cells, requiring phosphate for phosphorylation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * This creates a sudden increased demand for phosphate, which can lead to severe hypophosphatemia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

#### 8.1.3.2. CLINICAL MANIFESTATIONS

* **Common manifestations**:
  * Hypophosphatemia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Hypokalemia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Congestive heart failure [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Peripheral edema [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Rhabdomyolysis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Seizures [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Respiratory failure [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Cardiac arrhythmias [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Neurological complications [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **At-risk patients**:
  * Chronic malnutrition (anorexia nervosa, chronic alcoholism) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Prolonged fasting or low energy intake [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Significant weight loss (>10% in 2-3 months) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Little or no nutritional intake for >5 days [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * History of alcohol abuse, chemotherapy, or certain medications [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

#### 8.1.3.3. PREVENTION AND MANAGEMENT

* **Prevention strategies**:
  * Identify at-risk patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Start nutrition at 5-10 kcal/kg/day in high-risk patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Reduce protein intake to 50% of normal recommendations [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Slowly increase feeding over 3-5 days if no complications occur [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Pre-emptively supplement with vitamins and minerals, especially thiamine [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Management**:
  * Early and frequent measurement of electrolytes [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Aggressive replacement of deficiencies:
	* Phosphate [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Potassium [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Magnesium [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * High doses of B vitamins, especially thiamine [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Careful fluid management to avoid fluid overload [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Consider temporary reduction or cessation of feeds if severe manifestations occur [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Refeeding syndrome is a potentially fatal complication of reinitiating nutrition in malnourished patients, characterized by severe electrolyte disturbances and their sequelae. Prevention focuses on identifying at-risk patients, starting nutrition cautiously, and prophylactic supplementation. Management requires vigilant monitoring of electrolytes with aggressive replacement of deficiencies, vitamin supplementation, and careful titration of nutritional support.

### 8.1.4. VITAMIN SUPPLEMENTATION


#### 8.1.4.1. THIAMINE AND B VITAMINS

* **Thiamine (Vitamin B1) deficiency**:
  * Common in alcohol abuse or malnourished patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Can lead to:
	* Peripheral neuropathy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Cardiomyopathy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome (which can progress to Korsakoff's psychosis) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Prevention**:
  * Recommended that those who drink alcohol regularly take 100 mg/day of thiamine [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * When starting any form of caloric input or nutrition in a B-vitamin-depleted patient, thiamine supplementation should be given first to prevent precipitation of Wernicke's syndrome [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Recommended to give intravenous B vitamins for 3 days when starting parenteral nutrition [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Pabrinex**:
  * Mixture of vitamin B substances and ascorbic acid (vitamin C) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Presented as a pair of ampules:
	* Ampule 1: contains thiamine, riboflavin, and pyridoxine [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Ampule 2: contains ascorbic acid, nicotinamide, and glucose [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Dosing expressed as the number of pairs of ampules [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * For suspected or established Wernicke's encephalopathy: 2-3 pairs, three times daily [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Can be given intravenously or intramuscularly [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Also used to treat general vitamin B and C deficiency [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5: Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Vitamin supplementation, particularly thiamine and other B vitamins, is critical in malnourished patients, especially those with alcohol dependence. Thiamine deficiency can lead to serious neurological and cardiac complications. Prophylactic administration of thiamine before initiating nutritional support in at-risk patients is essential to prevent precipitation of Wernicke's encephalopathy. Pabrinex is commonly used for this purpose, providing a combination of B vitamins and vitamin C.
