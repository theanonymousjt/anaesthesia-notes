# 1. MECHANISM OF ACTION AND NERVE PHYSIOLOGY

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|Part A: Pharmacology]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|Part B: Sedation & General Anaesthesia]]

## 1.2. VOLTAGE-GATED SODIUM CHANNEL BLOCKADE

**Local anesthetics block voltage-gated sodium channels, preventing action potential generation and conduction in neuronal tissue.**

* LAs reversibly bind to the intracellular alpha subunit of voltage-gated sodium channels [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Binding site is located within the inner pore of the channel [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Hydrophobic aromatic residues (phenylalanine at position 1,764 and tyrosine at position 1,771) are essential for drug binding [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * These residues line an inner cavity approximately 11 Å apart, roughly the size of a local anesthetic molecule [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* LA binding prevents the influx of sodium ions, thus preventing an action potential from being reached [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Resting membrane potential and threshold potentials are not affected [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * With increasing LA concentration, more sodium channels bind LA molecules and cannot conduct sodium ions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* LA has greater affinity for sodium channels in the open or inactivated state than in resting state [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Depolarization (open and inactivated states) favors LA binding [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * LA binding is greater when nerve fibers are firing and depolarizing frequently than with infrequent depolarizations (use-dependent block) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Effects of local anesthetic block on nerve conduction:
  * Threshold for excitation and impulse conduction increases [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Rate of rise and magnitude of the action potential decreases [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Impulse conduction velocity slows [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * At sufficient LA concentrations, action potentials can no longer be generated and impulse propagation is abolished [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Alternative mechanism: 'membrane expansion' may also play a role [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Unionized drug dissolves into the phospholipid membrane
  * May cause swelling of the Na+ channel/lipoprotein matrix
  * Results in channel inactivation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Use-dependent block explains why active, rapidly firing pain neurons are often blocked before resting neurons, providing analgesia before complete anesthesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Local anesthetics block voltage-gated sodium channels by binding to a specific site within the inner pore, preventing sodium influx and action potential generation. This blockade is enhanced during high-frequency nerve firing (use-dependent block). Alternative mechanisms like membrane expansion may also contribute to the blocking effect.

## 1.3. NERVE ANATOMY AND PHYSIOLOGY



**Nerve anatomy and physiology determine local anesthetic spread, uptake, and effect.**

### 1.3.1. NERVE STRUCTURE

* Peripheral nerves contain both afferent and efferent fibers, bundled into fascicles within three tissue layers [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * **Endoneurium**: Loose connective tissue surrounding individual nerve fibers [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Perineurium**: Dense layer of collagenous connective tissue surrounding each fascicle [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * **Epineurium**: Final layer of dense connective tissue encasing groups of fascicles [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* These tissue layers protect nerve fibers and act as barriers to passive diffusion of local anesthetics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 1.3.2. MYELINATION

* Myelinated nerves are surrounded by:
  * Schwann cells in the PNS [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Oligodendrocytes in the CNS [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Myelin forms a concentrically wrapped lipid bilayer sheath interrupted at nodes of Ranvier [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Nodes of Ranvier contain densely clustered protein elements essential for transmission of neuronal signals [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Electrical signals are renewed at each node (saltatory conduction) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Unmyelinated nerves lack nodes of Ranvier [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Several nerve fibers may be simultaneously embedded within a single Schwann cell [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Conduction along unmyelinated fibers is relatively slow compared to myelinated fibers [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Maximum conduction velocity for myelinated fibers reaches 120 m/sec [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 1.3.3. ACTION POTENTIAL GENERATION

* Neurons maintain a resting membrane potential of −60 to −70 mV [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Maintained by Na⁺-K⁺-ATPase (electrogenic sodium-potassium pump) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Cell membrane is more permeable to K⁺ than Na⁺, resulting in negative intracellular potential [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Ratio of intracellular to extracellular K+ affects the resting membrane potential [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Hypokalaemia increases (makes more negative) the resting membrane potential [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Na+ concentration has little effect when in the resting state due to impermeability [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Action potentials involve:
  * Initial electrical or chemical triggers cause slow rise in membrane potential [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * When threshold potential (about -50mV) is reached, voltage-sensitive Na+ channels open [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Rapid membrane depolarization from sodium influx [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Na+ permeability increases dramatically and membrane potential reaches +30 mV [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Na+ channels then close and increased K+ efflux returns membrane potential to resting value [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Action potentials propagate as a wave along the nerve [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * A refractory period prevents retrograde spread [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Na+/K+ ATPase restores concentration gradients, though total ions moved is small [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

![Changes in Na+ and K+ conductance during the action potential](https://747b-138-75-252-16.ngrok-free.app/_served_images/LA/LA_LA_Untitled_9.png_7802d0.png_bf1acf.png)

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Local anesthetics only penetrate about 1-2% of the injected dose into nerves due to the perineural barrier, explaining why much higher concentrations are needed clinically than in desheathed nerve experiments [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Understanding nerve anatomy is crucial for effective local anesthetic practice. Perineural barriers limit drug uptake while myelination and nerve size affect sensitivity to blockade. Action potentials result from precisely regulated ion fluxes across neuron membranes, and local anesthetics prevent action potential generation by blocking voltage-gated sodium channels.

## 1.4. NERVE FIBER CLASSIFICATION AND DIFFERENTIAL BLOCKADE



**Nerve fibers of different types and sizes have varying sensitivity to local anesthetics.**

### 1.4.1. NERVE FIBER CLASSIFICATION

* Nerve fibers are classified by size, conduction velocity, and function [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:

| Type | Diameter | Myelination | Conduction Velocity | Function |
|------|----------|-------------|---------------------|----------|
| A-alpha | 12-20 μm | Yes | 70-120 m/s | Motor, proprioception |
| A-beta | 5-12 μm | Yes | 30-70 m/s | Touch, pressure |
| A-gamma | 3-6 μm | Yes | 15-30 m/s | Muscle spindle |
| A-delta | 2-5 μm | Yes | 12-30 m/s | Pain, temperature, touch |
| B | <3 μm | Yes | 3-15 m/s | Pre-ganglionic autonomic |
| C | 0.4-1.2 μm | No | 0.5-2.3 m/s | Pain, temperature, post-ganglionic autonomic |

* General principles [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Fibers with diameter >1 mm are typically myelinated
  * Larger diameter and presence of myelin = faster conduction velocity
  * Large myelinated A fibers are typically involved in motor and sensory functions requiring speed
  * Small unmyelinated C fibers have slower conduction velocity and relay pain, temperature, and autonomic functions

### 1.4.2. DIFFERENTIAL BLOCK

* Sensitivity of nerve fibers to LA inhibition is influenced by:
  * Axonal diameter: Smaller fibers are more sensitive than larger fibers of the same type [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Myelination: Myelinated fibers are more sensitive than unmyelinated fibers [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Order of sensitivity to local anesthetics (most to least): B fibers > A fibers > C fibers [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Small myelinated fibers (B) are easiest to block with least surface area
  * Unmyelinated nerves (C) are most resistant because surface area of available channels to block is largest
* Clinical progression of block in human peripheral nerves: autonomic → sensory → motor [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* "Differential blockade" explains the ordered progression of sensory and motor deficits [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Temperature sensation first (smallest fibers)
  * Followed by proprioception
  * Then motor function
  * Sharp pain
  * Light touch last (largest fibers)

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Understanding differential block helps assess block adequacy - loss of cold sensation typically precedes surgical anesthesia, while preserved motor function doesn't necessarily indicate block failure [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

⚠️ **Safety Note:** The classic theory that smaller fibers are blocked first is not universally true - larger myelinated Aδ fibers (sharp pain) are preferentially blocked over small unmyelinated C fibers (dull pain), which has important implications for assessing block adequacy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Different nerve fibers have varying sensitivity to local anesthetics, generally following the pattern: small myelinated (B) > large myelinated (A) > small unmyelinated (C) fibers. This explains the clinical progression of autonomic block before sensory block before motor block.

## 1.5. MOLECULAR MECHANISMS OF LOCAL ANESTHETICS



**Local anesthetics block transmission of nerve impulses through direct interaction with voltage-gated sodium channels.**

### 1.5.1. VOLTAGE-GATED SODIUM CHANNELS

* Voltage-gated sodium channels are essential for action potential generation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Structure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Principal α-subunit: Single-polypeptide transmembrane protein
  * Contains four homologous α-helical domains (D1 to D4) forming the channel pore
  * Controls ion selectivity, voltage-sensing, and inactivation
  * Contains phosphorylation sites for modulation by protein kinases
  * Auxiliary β-subunits: Short polypeptide proteins with a single transmembrane domain
  * Modulate channel expression, localization, and function
* Channel states [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Resting/closed state: Predominant in absence of stimulus
  * Open state: Conformational change upon membrane depolarization allows sodium influx
  * Inactivated state: Channel becomes refractory to activation
	* Fast inactivation: Completes within milliseconds; sensitive to local anesthetics
	* Slow inactivation: Lasts seconds to minutes; resistant to local anesthetics
* Sodium channel isoforms [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Nine isoforms (NaV 1.1 to NaV 1.9) have been identified
  * Tissue-specific expression (e.g., NaV 1.2 in CNS, NaV 1.6 at nodes of Ranvier)
  * NaV 1.7, 1.8, and 1.9 are found in nociceptors
  * NaV 1.5 is the primary cardiac sodium channel

### 1.5.2. MECHANISM OF LA-CHANNEL INTERACTION

* LAs reversibly bind the intracellular portion of voltage-gated sodium channels [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * QX-314 studies showed that permanently charged derivatives only block when applied intracellularly [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Hydrophobic interactions are primary binding forces [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Unionized lipid-soluble drug passes through phospholipid membrane [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * In axoplasm, it is protonated and in this ionized form binds to Na+ channel [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Prevents channel from leaving inactive state [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* LA binding effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Tonic blockade: Concentration-dependent decrease in peak sodium current
  * Use-dependent blockade: Repetitive stimulation leads to greater block at same concentration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Degree of blockade in vitro is proportional to rate of stimulation due to attraction of local anesthetic to 'open' Na+ channels [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Competing theories for use-dependent block:
	* Modulated-receptor theory: LAs bind more avidly to open/inactivated channels
	* Guarded-receptor theory: Access to binding site increases with channel gating

![Mechanism of action of local anesthetics](https://747b-138-75-252-16.ngrok-free.app/_served_images/LA/LA_LA_Untitled_2.png_7156a3.png_1cee9a.png)

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Variability in sodium channel isoforms explains why some patients experience different sensitivities to local anesthetics and why certain pain conditions (like erythromelalgia) involve sodium channel mutations [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Local anesthetics block nerve impulses by binding to the intracellular portion of voltage-gated sodium channels through hydrophobic interactions. This binding is enhanced by frequent channel activation (use-dependent block), making active pain fibers particularly susceptible. Membrane expansion theory may provide an additional mechanism of action.

# 2. CHEMICAL PROPERTIES AND CLASSIFICATION












## 2.2. CHEMICAL STRUCTURE

**Local anesthetics have a characteristic structure consisting of three main components.**

* Local anesthetics consist of three major chemical moieties [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Lipophilic aromatic benzene ring (hydrophobic portion)
  * Intermediate chain with ester **OR** amide linkage
  * Hydrophilic tertiary amine (hydrophilic portion)

![LA Structure showing benzene ring, linkage, and amine group](LA_LA_Untitled_2.png_7156a3.png_1cee9a.png)

* The nature of the intermediate chain is the basis for classification as either esters or amides [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Substitutions in these groups determine the physicochemical properties of local anesthetics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Individual structures confer different physicochemical and clinical characteristics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

![Structure of some local anesthetics](https://747b-138-75-252-16.ngrok-free.app/_served_images/LA/LA_LA_Untitled_9.png_7802d0.png_bf1acf.png)

**Bottom Line:** Local anesthetics have a characteristic three-part structure: lipophilic aromatic ring, intermediate chain (ester or amide), and hydrophilic tertiary amine. This structure allows them to interact with both membrane lipids and channel proteins, and the specific chemical features determine their unique properties.

## 2.3. PHYSICOCHEMICAL PROPERTIES



**Key physicochemical properties determine local anesthetic behavior and efficacy.**

### 2.3.1. ACID-BASE PROPERTIES

* Local anesthetics are weak bases with pKa values ranging from 7.6-8.9 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* In aqueous solution, local anesthetics exist in equilibrium between:
  * Protonated cationic form (BH⁺) - water-soluble, active form that binds to receptor
  * Uncharged base form (B) - lipid-soluble form that crosses membranes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* The ratio between these forms depends on:
  * The pKa of the local anesthetic
  * The pH of the surrounding tissue

![Ionization equilibrium of local anesthetics](LA_LA_Untitled_5.png_650f0e.png_59c39a.png)

* The pKa is the pH at which equal fractions of ionized and non-ionized forms exist [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Having a pKa closer to physiologic pH means a greater fraction of non-ionized form for faster onset [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* In infected (acidic) environments, the pKa will be further from the environmental pH, resulting in slower onset [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* For lidocaine (pKa = 7.9) at pH 7.4, approximately 75% is ionized and 25% unionized [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* At pH of 7.1 (infected tissue), approximately 86% is ionized and only 14% unionized, resulting in less drug available to penetrate nerves [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 2.3.2. LIPID SOLUBILITY

* Determined by:
  * Alkyl substitutions on the aromatic ring
  * Length of alkyl chain between the aromatic portion and the amine
  * Substitutions on the amine portion
* Measured as octanol:buffer partition coefficient [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Higher lipid solubility correlates with:
  * Greater potency [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Longer duration of action [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Generally slower onset (less water solubility) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* In vitro potency closely correlates with lipid solubility, but relationship is less definite in vivo [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Other factors including vasodilator properties and tissue distribution affect in vivo potency [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 2.3.3. PROTEIN BINDING

* Correlates with lipid solubility [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* In blood, local anesthetics bind primarily to:
  * α1-acid glycoprotein [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * To a lesser extent, albumin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Higher protein binding generally associated with:
  * Longer duration of action [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Reduced risk of systemic toxicity
* Duration of action is closely associated with extent of protein binding [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* LAs with limited protein binding have shorter duration of action [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* When protein binding is increased (pregnancy, myocardial infarction, renal failure, post-operatively and in infancy), the free fraction of drug is reduced [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 2.3.4. STEREOISOMERISM

* Mepivacaine, ropivacaine, and bupivacaine have a chiral carbon atom and can exist as optical isomers (enantiomers) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* The R(+) isomer of bupivacaine:
  * Blocks cardiac Na channels more avidly
  * Dissociates more slowly than the S(-) isomer (levobupivacaine) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Ropivacaine (S-enantiomer):
  * Similar onset time and duration to bupivacaine
  * Produces less motor block at equivalent volumes/concentrations [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * May have greater therapeutic index than racemic bupivacaine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Differs from bupivacaine in substitution of propyl for butyl group and single S-enantiomer [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Dissociates more rapidly from cardiac Na+ channels and produces less myocardial depression [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Slightly shorter duration and less potent than bupivacaine, requiring slightly larger doses [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The relationship between pKa and pH explains why local anesthetics often fail in infected (acidic) tissues - more drug exists in the charged form that cannot cross membranes to reach its site of action [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Key physicochemical properties of local anesthetics include pKa (affecting ionization and onset), lipid solubility (affecting potency and duration), and protein binding (affecting duration and systemic effects). These properties guide clinical selection for specific applications and help explain variable efficacy in different physiological states.

## 2.4. CLASSIFICATION (AMIDES VS. ESTERS) 



**Local anesthetics are classified into two main groups based on their chemical linkage: aminoesters and aminoamides.**

### 2.4.1. AMINOESTERS

* Contain an ester linkage (-CO.O-) between the aromatic portion and intermediate chain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Examples: procaine, chloroprocaine, tetracaine, benzocaine, cocaine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Characteristics:
  * Generally shorter duration of action [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Metabolized by plasma cholinesterases [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Metabolites include para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Higher incidence of allergic reactions (due to PABA) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * More rapid metabolism and elimination [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Less stable in solution (susceptible to hydrolysis) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Exception: Cocaine is primarily metabolized by hepatic ester hydrolysis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 2.4.2. AMINOAMIDES

* Contain an amide linkage (-NH.CO-) between the aromatic portion and intermediate chain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Examples: lidocaine, mepivacaine, prilocaine, bupivacaine, ropivacaine, levobupivacaine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Characteristics:
  * Generally longer duration of action [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Metabolized by hepatic microsomal P-450 enzymes (N-dealkylation and hydroxylation) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Lower incidence of allergic reactions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * More stable in solution [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Rate of metabolism depends on specific agent:
	* Prilocaine > lidocaine > mepivacaine > ropivacaine > bupivacaine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Prilocaine is the only local anesthetic metabolized to o-toluidine, which produces methemoglobinemia in a dose-dependent fashion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Metabolism is much slower than plasma hydrolysis of esters, making amides more prone to accumulation when administered by continuous infusion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Reduced hepatic blood flow or hepatic dysfunction can decrease amide metabolism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

## 2.5. STRUCTURE-ACTIVITY RELATIONSHIPS



**Specific structural features of local anesthetics determine their pharmacological properties.**

### 2.5.1. AROMATIC RING

* The lipophilic aromatic portion:
  * Contributes to lipid solubility and membrane penetration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Substitutions on the ring can alter lipid solubility and potency [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 2.5.2. INTERMEDIATE CHAIN

* Ester or amide linkage determines:
  * Stability in solution
  * Metabolic pathway
  * Allergenic potential
* Length of the intermediate chain affects potency and duration of action [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 2.5.3. AMINE GROUP

* Tertiary amine structure:
  * Provides water solubility in protonated form [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Determines acid-base properties
  * Alkyl substitutions influence potency and duration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Types of amine groups:
  * Primary amines: Low activity and irritant
  * Secondary amines: Good activity but irritant
  * Tertiary amines: Good activity and stability (most common)
  * Quaternary amines: Good activity but very poor tissue penetration

### 2.5.4. STRUCTURAL MODIFICATIONS AND THEIR EFFECTS

* Adding large alkyl groups to a parent molecule increases potency [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Example: Tetracaine (more potent) vs. procaine
  * Example: Bupivacaine (more potent) vs. mepivacaine
* Increased alkyl chain length generally:
  * Increases lipid solubility
  * Increases potency
  * Increases protein binding
  * Increases duration of action
  * May slow onset (due to decreased water solubility)

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Understanding structure-activity relationships explains why bupivacaine (with a butyl group) is more potent but also more cardiotoxic than lidocaine (with an ethyl group) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Structure-activity relationships in local anesthetics are predictable: increased lipophilicity (through alkyl substitutions) increases potency, protein binding, and duration of action. The type of chemical linkage (ester vs. amide) determines metabolic pathway and allergenic potential. Understanding these relationships helps predict clinical properties of different agents.

## 2.6. STEREOISOMERISM AND OPTICAL ACTIVITY 



**Some local anesthetics exist as optical isomers with different pharmacological properties.**

### 2.6.1. OPTICAL ISOMERS IN LOCAL ANESTHETICS

* Mepivacaine, ropivacaine, and bupivacaine have a chiral carbon atom and exist as optical isomers (enantiomers) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Older preparations existed as racemic mixtures (equal proportions of enantiomeric stereoisomers) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Newer agents (ropivacaine and levobupivacaine) are available as specific enantiomers [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Chiral center is marked with an asterisk in the structural diagram [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 2.6.2. DIFFERENCES BETWEEN ENANTIOMERS

* R(+) optical isomer of bupivacaine vs. S(-) isomer (levobupivacaine) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * R(+) blocks cardiac sodium channels more avidly
  * R(+) dissociates more slowly from cardiac sodium channels
  * S(-) has reduced cardiotoxicity (30-40% less toxic than racemic mixture)
  * S(-) may have slightly lower potency and shorter duration
  * Combined with limited human volunteer work, levobupivacaine appears to have less myocardial depression and higher dose threshold for excitatory CNS effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Ropivacaine (S-enantiomer) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Similar physicochemical properties to bupivacaine
  * Similar onset time and duration of action
  * Produces less motor block at equivalent volumes/concentrations
  * Has a greater therapeutic index than racemic bupivacaine

### 2.6.3. MECHANISM OF STEREOSELECTIVITY

* Topographic features at the channel-binding site likely play a key role in stereoselectivity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* The propyl side chain of ropivacaine renders it less cardiodepressive than the larger butyl side chain of bupivacaine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* The R-enantiomer of bupivacaine is less potent and more toxic than the S-enantiomer [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The development of single-enantiomer formulations (levobupivacaine, ropivacaine) provides safer alternatives to racemic bupivacaine, especially for high-risk patients or large-volume blocks [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Some local anesthetics exist as optical isomers with different pharmacological properties. S-enantiomers (levobupivacaine, ropivacaine) generally show reduced cardiotoxicity compared to racemic mixtures or R-enantiomers, albeit with slightly lower potency. This stereospecificity has important clinical implications for safety, particularly in high-dose or high-risk applications.

### 3.2.2. TISSUE VASCULARITY

* Blood flow, determined by tissue vascularity, is a key determinant of absorption rate [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Higher tissue vascularity increases systemic absorption, leading to:
  * Higher peak plasma levels
  * More rapid onset of systemic effects
  * Shorter duration of local effects
* Inadvertent injection into vein or artery results in very high systemic levels [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Less than 10 mg lidocaine inadvertently injected into carotid or vertebral artery can cause coma and possibly cardiac arrest [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 3.2.3. DOSE AND CONCENTRATION

* For a given site, absorption rate and peak plasma level are directly proportional to the dose [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* This relationship is nearly linear and independent of:
  * Drug concentration
  * Speed of injection

### 3.2.4. DRUG PROPERTIES

* More lipid-soluble (potent) agents are generally absorbed more slowly than less lipid-soluble compounds [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* This may be due to:
  * Sequestration into lipid-rich compartments
  * Differential effects on vascular smooth muscle
	* At low concentrations, more potent agents cause more vasoconstriction
	* At high concentrations, vasodilatory effects predominate for most local anesthetics
* Intrinsic vasodilator activity varies between drugs and influences potency and duration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* In general, local anesthetics cause vasodilatation in low concentrations (prilocaine > lidocaine > bupivacaine > ropivacaine) and vasoconstriction at higher concentrations [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Cocaine has solely vasoconstrictor actions by inhibiting neuronal uptake of catecholamines (uptake 1) and inhibiting monoamine oxidase [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 3.2.5. VASOACTIVE ADDITIVES

* Epinephrine decreases absorption by causing vasoconstriction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Reduces peak LA concentration in blood [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Facilitates neuronal uptake [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Enhances quality of analgesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Prolongs duration of analgesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Limits toxic side effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Effects more pronounced with shorter-acting than longer-acting agents [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Addition of epinephrine to lidocaine usually extends duration by at least 50% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Epinephrine has limited effect on bupivacaine peripheral nerve blocks [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Felypressin (synthetic derivative of vasopressin with no antidiuretic effect) is an alternative vasoconstrictor [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 3.2.6. TOPICAL ABSORPTION

* Mucous membranes - minimal barrier = rapid onset of action [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Intact skin - requires high concentration of lipid-soluble LA base [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * EMLA cream contains mixture of lidocaine and prilocaine in emulsion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Typically 1-2g applied per 10cm² area of skin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Dermal analgesia sufficient for IV catheter insertion requires about 1 hour under occlusive dressing [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * When two compounds are mixed to produce a substance with a single set of physical characteristics, it is said to be eutectic [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * EMLA (5%) contains mixture of crystalline bases of 2.5% lidocaine and 2.5% prilocaine in oil:water emulsion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * The mixture has lower melting point, being an oil at room temperature, while individual components would be crystalline solids [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The addition of epinephrine (1:200,000) can reduce systemic absorption of lidocaine by 20-30%, allowing for higher total doses, but has minimal effect on highly protein-bound agents like bupivacaine or ropivacaine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

⚠️ **Safety Note:** Highly vascular injection sites like intercostal spaces can lead to rapid systemic absorption and potential toxicity even at standard doses - consider reducing total dose by 25-30% for these sites [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Absorption of local anesthetics depends primarily on the vascularity of the injection site, dose administered, inherent drug properties, and presence of vasoconstrictors. Understanding these factors is crucial for predicting systemic effects and preventing toxicity. Inadvertent vascular injection represents the highest risk for rapid systemic toxicity.

## 3.3. DISTRIBUTION



**Distribution of local anesthetics throughout the body depends on multiple factors.**

### 3.3.1. DETERMINANTS OF DISTRIBUTION

* Organ perfusion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Highly perfused organs (brain, lung, liver, kidney, heart) are responsible for initial rapid removal of local anesthetics from blood [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Slower redistribution to wider range of tissues follows [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * The lung extracts significant amounts of local anesthetic during "first pass" [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* ⚠️ Patients with right-to-left cardiac shunts are more susceptible to toxic effects of lidocaine injected as an antiarrhythmic agent [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Tissue/blood partition coefficient [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Increasing lipid solubility is associated with:
	* Greater plasma protein binding
	* Greater tissue uptake from aqueous compartment
  * This affects both the volume of distribution and elimination half-life
* Tissue mass [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Muscle provides the greatest reservoir for distribution
  * This is due to its large mass, despite moderate perfusion

### 3.3.2. PROTEIN BINDING

* In blood, local anesthetics bind primarily to:
  * α1-acid glycoprotein (high affinity) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Albumin (lower affinity but higher capacity) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Extent of binding varies by agent:
  * Bupivacaine: >95% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Levobupivacaine: >95% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Ropivacaine: 94% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Lidocaine: 70% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Prilocaine: 50-55% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Esters: generally less protein-bound than amides [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Protein binding has important implications:
  * Only the unbound (free) fraction is:
	* Pharmacologically active
	* Available for metabolism
	* Able to cause toxicity
  * Higher protein binding generally correlates with:
	* Longer duration of action
	* Lower risk of systemic toxicity
	* Greater potency
  * When protein binding is increased (pregnancy, myocardial infarction, renal failure, post-operatively and in infancy), the free fraction of drug is reduced [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Degree of placental transfer affected by protein binding - bupivacaine is more highly bound than lidocaine so less crosses placenta [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * If fetus becomes acidotic, ion trapping may cause local anesthetic accumulation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Ester local anesthetics do not cross placenta in significant amounts due to rapid metabolism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 3.3.3. VOLUME OF DISTRIBUTION

* Volume of distribution at steady state (VDss) varies by agent [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Prilocaine: 2.73 L/kg
  * Etidocaine: 1.9 L/kg
  * Lidocaine: 1.3 L/kg
  * Mepivacaine: 1.2 L/kg
  * Bupivacaine: 1.02 L/kg
  * Procaine: 0.93 L/kg
  * Ropivacaine: 0.84 L/kg
  * Levobupivacaine: 0.78 L/kg
  * Chloroprocaine: 0.50 L/kg

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** α1-acid glycoprotein is an acute phase reactant that increases during stress, inflammation, and postoperative states. This can increase protein binding of local anesthetics and reduce free fraction, potentially altering drug effect and toxicity risk [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Distribution of local anesthetics is determined by organ perfusion, tissue/blood partition coefficient, tissue mass, and protein binding. Highly perfused organs receive drug first, while muscle serves as the largest reservoir. Protein binding significantly affects duration of action and toxicity potential, with highly bound agents having less free drug available for activity and toxicity.

## 3.4. METABOLISM AND EXCRETION



**Metabolism of local anesthetics follows distinct pathways based on their chemical classification.**

### 3.4.1. METABOLISM OF ESTERS

* Predominantly metabolized by pseudocholinesterase (also termed butyrylcholinesterase) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Clinical evidence for increased risk of toxicity in patients with pseudocholinesterase deficiency is lacking [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Most likely because alternative metabolic pathways are available in the liver [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Water-soluble metabolites are excreted in the urine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Esters are hydrolyzed rapidly by plasma cholinesterases and other esterases to inactive compounds [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Procaine and benzocaine are metabolized to p-aminobenzoic acid (PABA) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * PABA has been associated with rare anaphylactic reactions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Especially in atopic patients [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Rapid hydrolysis results in short elimination half-life [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Exception: Cocaine is primarily metabolized by ester hydrolysis in the liver (not plasma) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 3.4.2. METABOLISM OF AMIDES

* Metabolized by microsomal P-450 enzymes in the liver (N-dealkylation and hydroxylation) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Rate of amide metabolism depends on the specific agent (from fastest to slowest) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Prilocaine > lidocaine > mepivacaine > ropivacaine > bupivacaine
* Overall, amide metabolism is consistently slower than ester hydrolysis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Prilocaine is the only local anesthetic metabolized to o-toluidine, which produces methemoglobinemia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Classically occurs at doses of ~10mg/kg [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Recent studies show younger, healthier patients may develop medically important methemoglobinemia after lower doses [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * May require treatment with ascorbic acid or methylene blue as reducing agents [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Neonate at special risk as red blood cells are deficient in methemoglobin reductase [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * EMLA cream may precipitate the same reaction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Specific metabolic pathways:
  * Lidocaine: Dealkylated in liver to monoethylglycine-xylidide and acetaldehyde; further metabolized to 4-hydroxy-2,6-xylidine, excreted in urine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Bupivacaine: Dealkylated in liver to pipecolic acid and pipecolylxylidine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Ropivacaine: Metabolized in liver by aromatic hydroxylation, mainly to 3-hydroxy-ropivacaine, but also to 4-hydroxy-ropivacaine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 3.4.3. CLEARANCE AND ELIMINATION HALF-LIFE

* Total body clearance varies widely among agents [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Procaine: 5.62 L/kg/hr
  * Chloroprocaine: 2.96 L/kg/hr
  * Prilocaine: 2.03 L/kg/hr
  * Etidocaine: 1.05 L/kg/hr
  * Lidocaine: 0.85 L/kg/hr
  * Mepivacaine: 0.67 L/kg/hr
  * Ropivacaine: 0.63 L/kg/hr
  * Bupivacaine: 0.41 L/kg/hr
  * Levobupivacaine: 0.32 L/kg/hr
* Elimination half-life (T1/2) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Very short for esters: Chloroprocaine (0.11 hr), Procaine (0.14 hr)
  * Intermediate for most amides: Lidocaine (1.6 hr), Prilocaine (1.6 hr), Mepivacaine (1.9 hr), Ropivacaine (1.9 hr)
  * Longer for long-acting amides: Levobupivacaine (2.6 hr), Etidocaine (2.6 hr), Bupivacaine (3.5 hr)
* Some metabolic products of lidocaine have anti-arrhythmic properties while others may potentiate lidocaine-induced seizures [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 3.4.4. EFFECT OF ORGAN DYSFUNCTION

* Severe liver disease slows clearance of amide local anesthetics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * May lead to drug accumulation and increased toxicity risk
  * Clearance of lidocaine is reduced in the presence of hepatic or cardiac failure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Renal disease has minimal effect on pharmacokinetic parameters of local anesthetics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Ester local anesthetics (like chloroprocaine) are ideal for patients with severe hepatic dysfunction due to their plasma hydrolysis and very short half-life [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

⚠️ **Safety Note:** Prilocaine doses >600mg (>8mg/kg in 70kg adult) may produce clinically significant methemoglobinemia, particularly in patients with congenital methemoglobin reductase deficiency or hemoglobinopathies [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Ester local anesthetics are rapidly hydrolyzed by plasma pseudocholinesterase, while amides undergo slower hepatic metabolism. This difference explains the generally shorter duration of esters compared to amides and has important implications for patients with organ dysfunction. Prilocaine uniquely produces methemoglobinemia via its metabolite o-toluidine, especially at higher doses.

## 3.5. CLINICAL PHARMACOKINETICS 



**Understanding clinical pharmacokinetics helps predict toxic plasma levels and adjust dosing for special patient populations.**

### 3.5.1. PREDICTING PEAK PLASMA LEVELS

* Peak plasma level (Cmax) is determined by [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Site of injection
  * Dose administered
  * Drug's intrinsic pharmacokinetic properties
  * Addition of vasoconstrictors
* Typical Cmax varies by technique and agent [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Awareness of typical values helps predict toxicity risk

### 3.5.2. SPECIAL POPULATIONS

* Pediatric patients [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Decreased clearance in very young
  * Increased absorption
  * Infants <6 months at sixfold greater risk for LAST compared to older children [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Elderly patients [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Decreased clearance
  * May have increased sensitivity
  * Generally require dose reduction of 20-30%
* Gender differences [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Not well defined for most local anesthetics
  * Some evidence suggests slightly different volumes of distribution and clearance
* Pregnancy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * May decrease clearance
  * Decreased plasma protein binding
  * Increased cardiac output may accelerate absorption
  * Increased sensitivity due to progesterone effects

### 3.5.3. DISEASE STATES AFFECTING PHARMACOKINETICS

* Cardiac disease [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Decreased volume of distribution (0.88 L/kg vs. normal 1.32 L/kg)
  * Decreased clearance (6.3 mL/kg/min vs. normal 10.0 mL/kg/min)
  * Half-life similar to normal (1.9 hr vs. 1.8 hr)
* Hepatic disease [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Increased volume of distribution (2.31 L/kg)
  * Decreased clearance (6.0 mL/kg/min)
  * Prolonged half-life (4.9 hr)
  * ⚠️ Significant impact on amide metabolism
* Renal disease [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Minimal effect on pharmacokinetics
  * VDss: 1.2 L/kg; CL: 13.7 mL/kg/min; T1/2: 1.3 hr

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The correlation between dose and weight is often inconsistent in clinical practice, making rigid mg/kg dosing guidelines sometimes misleading. Consider patient-specific factors (age, comorbidities, tissue vascularity) when determining maximum doses [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

⚠️ **Safety Note:** Maximum "safe" doses (e.g., bupivacaine 2mg/kg) are approximations and not absolute thresholds. The actual safe dose depends on the patient, specific nerve block, rate of injection, and other factors. Tables of purported maximal safe doses should be interpreted cautiously [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Clinical pharmacokinetics helps predict peak plasma levels and toxicity risk. Special populations (pediatric, elderly, pregnant) and disease states (cardiac, hepatic) require dose adjustments. Careful consideration of patient factors rather than rigid adherence to maximum dose tables provides safer practice.

# 4. PHARMACODYNAMICS 












## 4.2. FACTORS AFFECTING ONSET OF ACTION 



**Multiple factors influence the speed of onset of local anesthetic block.**

### 4.2.1. PKA AND TISSUE PH

* pKa is the pH at which equal fractions of ionized and non-ionized forms exist [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Onset of action is faster when more drug exists in the non-ionized form [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Only the non-ionized form can cross lipid membranes to reach the site of action
  * The ionized form is responsible for channel blockade once inside the axon
* Agents with pKa closer to tissue pH (7.4) have more drug in non-ionized form and faster onset [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Lidocaine (pKa 7.9): ~24% non-ionized at pH 7.4, relatively fast onset [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Bupivacaine (pKa 8.1): ~17% non-ionized at pH 7.4, relatively slower onset [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Tissue pH affects onset:
  * Normal tissues: pH ~7.4
  * Infected/inflamed tissues: pH <7.0
  * In acidic environments (infection), onset is delayed because less drug exists in the non-ionized form [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Local anesthetics are generally ineffective in infected tissue due to acidic environment further reducing the unionised fraction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 4.2.2. LIPID SOLUBILITY

* Generally, less lipid-soluble agents have faster onset than more lipid-soluble agents [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Less potent agents (lidocaine, mepivacaine) have faster onset than more potent agents (bupivacaine, ropivacaine) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * This seems counterintuitive but may relate to:
	* Lower lipid solubility allowing more free drug to reach the aqueous channels
	* Less sequestration into surrounding lipid tissues

### 4.2.3. CONCENTRATION AND DOSE

* Higher concentrations generally produce faster onset due to:
  * Steeper concentration gradient for diffusion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Greater driving force for passive diffusion
* Less potent agents typically used at higher concentrations, which partly explains their faster onset

### 4.2.4. DISTANCE FROM NERVE

* Proximity to nerve is critical for onset speed [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Closer proximity = faster onset
  * Perineural sheath acts as barrier to diffusion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Only about 1-2% of injected local anesthetics ultimately penetrate into the nerve [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 4.2.5. OTHER FACTORS

* Alkalinization of local anesthetic solutions:
  * Adding bicarbonate raises pH, increasing non-ionized fraction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * May hasten onset by about 3-5 minutes compared to commercial preparations [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Commercial preparations with epinephrine are more acidic (pH 4-5), which can delay onset [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Warming the solution:
  * Modest effect on increasing onset speed
  * May enhance patient comfort during injection

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** While alkalinization of local anesthetics theoretically speeds onset, the clinical improvement is typically less than 5 minutes, and the preparation must be used within 24 hours due to precipitation concerns [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Onset of local anesthetic action is primarily influenced by: pKa (relative to tissue pH), lipid solubility, concentration, distance from nerve, and solution pH. Understanding these factors helps optimize block performance in clinical practice. Infected tissues are generally resistant to local anesthetics due to acidic environment reducing the available unionized drug.

## 4.3. FACTORS AFFECTING POTENCY



**Local anesthetic potency is primarily determined by lipid solubility and several other factors.**

### 4.3.1. LIPID SOLUBILITY

* The most important determinant of potency [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Measured by octanol:buffer partition coefficient [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Higher lipid solubility correlates with greater potency [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Potency order (highest to lowest) generally follows lipid solubility:
  * Bupivacaine/levobupivacaine > ropivacaine > lidocaine > mepivacaine > prilocaine > procaine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* In vitro potency closely correlates with lipid solubility, but relationship is less definite in vivo [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Other factors such as vasodilator properties and tissue distribution affect in vivo potency [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 4.3.2. CHEMICAL STRUCTURE

* Alkyl substitutions on the aromatic or amine portions increase lipid solubility and potency [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Adding large alkyl groups to a parent molecule increases potency [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Example: Tetracaine (more potent) vs. procaine
  * Example: Bupivacaine (more potent) vs. mepivacaine

### 4.3.3. STEREOISOMERISM

* Some potency differences exist between stereoisomers [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Levobupivacaine (S-enantiomer) is slightly less potent than racemic bupivacaine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Ropivacaine (S-enantiomer) is less potent than bupivacaine when compared at equal concentrations [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 4.3.4. MINIMUM BLOCKING CONCENTRATION

* The minimum concentration required to effect complete nerve blockade [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Reflects the intrinsic potency of the agent and the conduction properties of nerve fibers [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Depends on:
  * Drug's binding affinity to ion channels
  * Degree of drug saturation necessary to halt action potential transmission
  * Individual nerve fiber types differ in their minimum blocking concentration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 4.3.5. OTHER FACTORS

* Tissue and nerve type:
  * Different nerves require different concentrations of the same agent for blockade
  * Order of susceptibility: small myelinated > large myelinated > unmyelinated
* pH and ionization:
  * Acidosis reduces potency by increasing ionized fraction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Electrolyte concentrations:
  * Hypokalemia and hypercalcemia antagonize blockade [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Frequency of nerve stimulation:
  * Higher frequency increases apparent potency due to use-dependent block [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Total dose and concentration affect clinical potency [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The in vivo potency of local anesthetics doesn't always match laboratory predictions based solely on lipid solubility. Factors like tissue binding, vascularity, and pH can significantly alter clinical potency [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Local anesthetic potency is primarily determined by lipid solubility, with more lipid-soluble agents being more potent. Chemical structure, especially alkyl substitutions, directly influences lipid solubility and thus potency. Other factors including stereoisomerism, tissue pH, and nerve type also contribute to effective potency in clinical settings. In vitro correlations may not always predict in vivo potency due to additional factors like vasodilator properties.

## 4.4. FACTORS AFFECTING DURATION OF ACTION 



**Duration of local anesthetic action is influenced by multiple factors, most importantly protein binding and lipid solubility.**

### 4.4.1. PROTEIN BINDING

* Higher protein binding correlates with longer duration of action [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Protein binding acts as a reservoir, slowly releasing active drug
* Agents in order of protein binding (and typically duration):
  * Bupivacaine/levobupivacaine (>95%) > ropivacaine (94%) > lidocaine (70%) > prilocaine (50-55%) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Duration of action is closely associated with extent of protein binding [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* LAs with limited protein binding have shorter duration of action [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 4.4.2. LIPID SOLUBILITY

* Highly lipid-soluble agents have longer duration of action [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Presumed mechanism: slower diffusion from lipid-rich environment to aqueous bloodstream [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Also correlates with greater tissue binding and slower release

### 4.4.3. VASOACTIVITY

* Intrinsic vasodilatory properties of local anesthetics enhance systemic absorption and shorten duration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* At low concentrations, more potent agents may cause vasoconstriction, prolonging duration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Addition of vasoconstrictors (epinephrine) prolongs duration by:
  * Reducing local blood flow
  * Decreasing systemic absorption
  * Keeping more drug at the site of action
* Effects of vasoconstrictors:
  * Epinephrine is added to slow absorption from site of injection and prolong duration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * More pronounced with shorter-acting agents [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Addition of epinephrine to lidocaine extends duration by at least 50% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Limited effect on bupivacaine peripheral nerve blocks [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Felypressin is a synthetic vasopressin derivative with no antidiuretic effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 4.4.4. DOSE AND CONCENTRATION

* Higher doses generally produce longer duration due to:
  * Greater total amount of drug available
  * Longer time required for absorption and metabolism
* Effect of concentration is variable and depends on the specific block

### 4.4.5. SITE OF ADMINISTRATION

* Sites with lower vascularity allow longer duration
* Duration is typically longer in areas with:
  * Lower tissue perfusion
  * Smaller surface area for absorption
  * More connective tissue barriers

### 4.4.6. CLINICAL DURATION BY AGENT

* Approximate duration of peripheral nerve blocks:
  * Short-acting (30-90 minutes): Procaine, chloroprocaine
  * Intermediate-acting (90-180 minutes): Lidocaine, mepivacaine, prilocaine
  * Long-acting (4-24 hours): Bupivacaine, levobupivacaine, ropivacaine, tetracaine

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** For procedures requiring prolonged analgesia but minimal motor block (e.g., joint replacement), consider lower concentrations of long-acting agents (0.0625-0.125% bupivacaine or ropivacaine) delivered via continuous infusion rather than single higher-concentration injections [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Duration of local anesthetic action is primarily determined by protein binding, lipid solubility, and vasoactivity. Higher protein binding and lipid solubility generally correlate with longer duration. Vasoconstrictors significantly prolong duration, especially for intermediate-acting agents. The effect of vasoconstrictors is more pronounced with shorter-acting agents, having limited effect on long-acting, highly protein-bound agents like bupivacaine.

## 4.5. ADDITIVES TO ENHANCE LOCAL ANESTHETIC ACTIVITY 



**Various additives can enhance local anesthetic effectiveness through different mechanisms.**

### 4.5.1. EPINEPHRINE

* Mechanism: Vasoconstriction antagonizes vasodilating effects of local anesthetics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Benefits [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Prolongs local anesthetic block
  * Increases intensity of block
  * Decreases systemic absorption of local anesthetic
  * Reduces bleeding in surgical field
  * Serves as an intravascular marker (tachycardia if inadvertent injection)
* Optimal concentration: 1:200,000 to 1:400,000 (5-2.5 mcg/mL) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Effects vary by agent and site [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Lidocaine: Duration increase 20-30% for nerve blocks and epidurals
  * Mepivacaine: Duration increase 20-30%
  * Bupivacaine: Minimal effect on duration (10-20%)
  * Ropivacaine: No significant effect (has intrinsic vasoconstrictor activity)
* ⚠️ Smallest effective dose should be used due to potential tissue toxicity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 4.5.2. SODIUM BICARBONATE (ALKALINIZATION)

* Mechanism: Increases pH of solution, increasing non-ionized fraction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Hasten onset of neural block
  * Clinical studies show improvement of less than 5 minutes compared to commercial preparations
  * May decrease duration for plain solutions
* Limitations [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Local anesthetics cannot be alkalinized beyond pH 6.05-8 before precipitation occurs
  * Even at maximum alkalinization, only about 10% of drug exists in neutral form
  * Effect is modest in clinical practice

### 4.5.3. OPIOIDS

* Mechanism: Multiple central and peripheral mechanisms of analgesic action [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Central neuraxial coadministration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Results in synergistic analgesia
  * Attenuates C-fiber nociception
  * Independent of supraspinal mechanisms
  * Exception: Chloroprocaine decreases effectiveness of epidurally coadministered opioids
* Peripheral nerve blocks [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Evidence is mixed
  * Some studies show favorable outcomes
  * Others show no increased efficacy
  * Buprenorphine may enhance and prolong postoperative analgesia
* Intra-articular use not supported by evidence [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 4.5.4. Α2-ADRENERGIC AGONISTS (CLONIDINE)

* Mechanism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Analgesic effects via supraspinal and spinal adrenergic receptors
  * Direct inhibitory effects on peripheral nerve conduction (A and C fibers)
  * Analgesic synergy with local anesthetics
* Clinical effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Improves duration of analgesia by about 2 hours
  * Works with both intermediate and long-acting local anesthetics
  * Enhances effects for intrathecal, epidural, and peripheral nerve blocks

### 4.5.5. STEROIDS (DEXAMETHASONE)

* Mechanism: Possibly steroid receptor-dependent and locally mediated [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Prolongs conduction block
  * Extends duration of analgesia by approximately 50-100%
  * Duration effect correlates with potency of glucocorticoid activity
* Dosing [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Dose-response relationship between 1-4 mg
  * Possible ceiling effect above 4 mg
* Debate exists over whether effects are perineurally or systemically mediated [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 4.5.6. LIPOSOMAL FORMULATIONS

* Mechanism: Encapsulated LAs released gradually, ensuring continuous bioavailability [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Liposomal bupivacaine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Approved for wound infiltration, TAP blocks, and interscalene blocks
  * In head-to-head trials against bupivacaine with dexamethasone:
	* Similar analgesic duration (approximately 26-27 hours)
	* Comparable analgesic efficacy and opioid consumption
* Limitations [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Clinical evidence is mixed
  * Unclear superiority over traditional additives
  * Associated with tissue inflammation in animal models

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Dexamethasone (4-8mg) may be given either perineurally or intravenously to extend block duration, though perineural administration may provide slightly greater prolongation effect (~25% longer than systemic administration) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

⚠️ **Safety Note:** The vasoconstrictive effect of epinephrine should be used cautiously in end-arterial territories (digits, penis, ears) and in patients with significant cardiovascular disease, hyperthyroidism, or concurrent MAO inhibitor therapy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Multiple additives can enhance local anesthetic effectiveness. Epinephrine prolongs duration through vasoconstriction, especially for shorter-acting agents. Dexamethasone significantly extends duration for peripheral blocks. Clonidine provides modest prolongation through direct and synergistic effects. The benefits of alkalinization and liposomal formulations appear more limited in clinical practice.

# 5. CLINICAL USE OF LOCAL ANESTHETICS












## 5.2. ROUTES OF ADMINISTRATION



**Local anesthetics can be administered through multiple routes to achieve desired effects.**

### 5.2.1. TOPICAL

* Used for anesthesia of mucous membranes and skin [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Formulations:
  * Sprays (lidocaine, benzocaine) for mucosal surfaces
  * Creams and ointments for skin (EMLA, lidocaine, tetracaine)
  * Patches for localized neuropathic pain (lidocaine 5%)
* Characteristics:
  * Generally requires higher concentrations than injectable forms
  * Mucous membranes allow much faster absorption than intact skin
  * For intact skin, lipid-soluble formulations and/or occlusive dressings enhance penetration
* Examples:
  * EMLA (Eutectic Mixture of Local Anesthetics) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Contains lidocaine 2.5% and prilocaine 2.5%
	* Typically 1-2g applied per 10cm² area of skin
	* Requires 1 hour under occlusive dressing for IV catheter insertion
  * Lidocaine 4-10% solutions/sprays for airway topicalization
  * Cocaine 4% solution for nasal procedures
  * Amethocaine (tetracaine):
	* 0.5% and 1% drops for topical use before local anesthetic block or for lens surgery [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* 4% cream for topical anaesthesia to the skin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Faster onset than EMLA (30 minutes), effects last 4-6 hours [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Produces local vasodilation and erythema which may assist venous cannulation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 5.2.2. INFILTRATION

* Direct injection into tissues to block small nerve endings [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Techniques:
  * Field block: Injection around periphery of surgical site
  * Local infiltration: Multiple small injections throughout the surgical area
  * Wound infiltration: Direct application to surgical wound edges
* Characteristics:
  * Rapid onset (typically 2-5 minutes)
  * Duration varies by agent and additives
  * Commonly used for minor procedures and as adjuncts to other anesthetic techniques

### 5.2.3. PERIPHERAL NERVE BLOCK

* Injection of local anesthetic around specific peripheral nerves or plexuses [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Common techniques:
  * Upper extremity: Interscalene, supraclavicular, infraclavicular, axillary
  * Lower extremity: Femoral, sciatic, popliteal, ankle
  * Head and neck: Cervical plexus, maxillary/mandibular
  * Trunk: Transversus abdominis plane (TAP), ilioinguinal, intercostal
* Characteristics:
  * Can be performed as single injection or with continuous catheter
  * Often guided by ultrasound or nerve stimulation
  * Onset and duration vary by agent, volume, and technique

### 5.2.4. NEURAXIAL

* Injection into or around the spinal cord [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Techniques:
  * Spinal (intrathecal): Injection into CSF in subarachnoid space
  * Epidural: Injection into epidural space
  * Combined spinal-epidural: Both techniques together
  * Caudal: Epidural injection via sacral hiatus
* Characteristics:
  * Spinal: Rapid onset, limited duration without catheter, small doses required
  * Epidural: Slower onset, ability for continuous infusion, larger doses required
  * Wide range of blockade possible (sensory, motor, sympathetic)

### 5.2.5. INTRAVENOUS

* Direct administration into venous circulation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* Applications:
  * Systemic analgesia (lidocaine infusions)
  * Antiarrhythmic therapy (lidocaine)
  * Intravenous regional anesthesia (Bier block)
* Characteristics:
  * Systemic effects onset within 1-2 minutes
  * Careful dose titration required to avoid toxicity
  * Lidocaine IV infusion (1-2 mg/kg/h) used for postoperative pain
  * Bier block: Exsanguination of limb, tourniquet inflation, injection of LA
  * Prilocaine (0.5%) commonly used for Bier blocks although lidocaine may also be used [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Bupivacaine has been used for intravenous regional techniques but following deaths attributed to cardiac toxicity, it is no longer used this way [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** For airway topicalization, "spray-as-you-go" technique with 4% lidocaine (max 4-5mg/kg) provides excellent conditions for awake fiberoptic intubation while minimizing risk of toxicity from excessive dosing [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

⚠️ **Safety Note:** When administering local anesthetics via multiple routes simultaneously (e.g., topical + infiltration + nerve block), calculate the total cumulative dose to avoid exceeding maximum safe limits [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Local anesthetics can be administered via topical application, tissue infiltration, peripheral nerve blockade, neuraxial techniques, or intravenous routes. Each route has specific characteristics regarding onset, duration, and safety considerations that must be considered when selecting the appropriate technique. EMLA and amethocaine provide effective topical anesthesia, while prilocaine is preferred for intravenous regional anesthesia due to safety concerns with bupivacaine.

## 5.3. DOSING GUIDELINES



**Safe and effective dosing of local anesthetics requires consideration of multiple factors.**

### 5.3.1. FACTORS AFFECTING DOSING DECISIONS

* Patient characteristics:
  * Age (reduced dosing for elderly and very young)
  * Weight (traditional mg/kg guidelines)
  * Comorbidities (hepatic, cardiac, neurological disease)
  * Pregnancy status
* Procedural factors:
  * Site of administration (vascularity)
  * Type and duration of procedure
  * Desired effect (anesthesia vs. analgesia)
  * Single injection vs. continuous technique

### 5.3.2. GENERAL DOSING PRINCIPLES

* Calculate maximum allowable dose based on patient weight and agent [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Consider reducing maximum doses by 25-30% for:
  * Elderly patients
  * Patients with significant cardiac or hepatic dysfunction
  * Highly vascular injection sites
* Use the smallest effective dose to achieve desired effect
* For continuous infusions, consider both bolus and hourly infusion in total calculations

### 5.3.3. STANDARD MAXIMUM RECOMMENDED DOSES

| Agent | Without Epinephrine (mg/kg) | With Epinephrine (mg/kg) | Toxic Plasma Level |
|-------|----------------------------|-------------------------|-------------------|
| Lidocaine | 3-5 | 7 | 5 μg/mL |
| Bupivacaine | 2 | 2.5 | 4 μg/mL |
| Levobupivacaine | 2.5 | 3 | - |
| Ropivacaine | 3-3.5 | 3.5 | - |
| Prilocaine | 6 | 8-9 | - |
| Cocaine | 1.5 | - | - |
| Tetracaine | 1.5 | 2.5 | - |
| Chloroprocaine | 10-12 | 14-16 | - |

* Levobupivacaine maximum doses: single dose of 150 mg, maximum dose over 24 hours of 400 mg [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Prilocaine maximum doses: 6 mg/kg or 8 mg/kg when administered with felypressin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Cocaine maximum doses: 1.5 mg/kg or 100mg total dose [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 5.3.4. CONCENTRATION SELECTION

* Higher concentrations:
  * Faster onset
  * More intense block
  * Greater motor block
  * Shorter duration of action per unit dose
* Lower concentrations:
  * Preferential sensory block (relative sparing of motor function)
  * Longer duration per unit dose
  * Useful for continuous infusions

### 5.3.5. COMMON CLINICAL CONCENTRATIONS

* Infiltration: 0.25-1% lidocaine, 0.125-0.25% bupivacaine
* Peripheral nerve blocks:
  * Lidocaine: 1-2%
  * Mepivacaine: 1-1.5%
  * Bupivacaine/levobupivacaine: 0.25-0.5%
  * Ropivacaine: 0.2-0.75%
* Spinal:
  * Lidocaine: 2-5%
  * Bupivacaine: 0.5-0.75%
  * Ropivacaine: 0.5-0.75%
* Epidural:
  * Lidocaine: 1-2%
  * Bupivacaine/levobupivacaine: 0.125-0.5%
  * Ropivacaine: 0.2-0.75%
* Topical:
  * Lidocaine: 2-10% (spray, gel, ointment) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * With epinephrine: 1:100,000 - 1:400,000 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Cocaine: 4-10% solution for ENT procedures [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 5.3.6. VOLUME CONSIDERATIONS

* Sufficient volume is needed to suppress the regeneration of nerve impulse over a critical length [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Larger volumes may allow for effective block even with lower concentrations through the principle of decremental conduction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Typical volumes:
  * Infiltration: Variable based on area
  * Major peripheral nerve blocks: 20-40 mL
  * Brachial plexus: 20-40 mL
  * Epidural: 15-20 mL
  * Spinal: 2-4 mL

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** When performing ultrasound-guided nerve blocks, agent concentration may be more important than volume. Lower volumes (as little as 5-10 mL) of appropriately concentrated agent can provide excellent blocks while reducing total dose and toxicity risk [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

⚠️ **Safety Note:** "Maximum safe dose" tables should be considered guidelines rather than absolute thresholds. The actual safe dose depends on the patient, specific block, rate of injection, and many other factors. Careful technique is more important than rigid adherence to mg/kg guidelines [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Safe and effective local anesthetic dosing requires consideration of patient factors, procedural requirements, and agent-specific properties. While maximum dose guidelines provide a reference, they should be adjusted based on clinical context, with emphasis on using the smallest effective dose with appropriate concentration and volume. The concept of a toxic dose without regard to the site of administration is meaningless since absorption varies widely depending on injection site and vasoconstrictors.

## 5.4. MAXIMUM SAFE DOSES



**Understanding maximum safe doses and their limitations is crucial for preventing local anesthetic toxicity.**

### 5.4.1. STANDARD MAXIMUM DOSES

* Commonly cited maximum safe doses:
  * Lidocaine: 3-5 mg/kg plain; 7 mg/kg with epinephrine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Bupivacaine: 2 mg/kg plain; 2.5 mg/kg with epinephrine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Ropivacaine: 3-3.5 mg/kg [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Levobupivacaine: 2.5 mg/kg plain; 3 mg/kg with epinephrine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Prilocaine: 6 mg/kg plain; 8-9 mg/kg with epinephrine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Cocaine: 1.5 mg/kg (or 100mg total dose) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 5.4.2. LIMITATIONS OF MAXIMUM DOSE GUIDELINES

* Maximum "safe" dose depends on [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * The specific patient
  * The nerve block being performed
  * Rate of injection
  * Many other factors
* Tables of purported maximal safe doses are approximate guidelines, not absolute thresholds [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Toxicity can occur at doses below "maximum" in some patients
* Mixtures of local anesthetics should be considered to have additive toxic effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* The concept of a toxic dose without regard to the site of administration is meaningless [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Absorption of local anesthetic varies widely depending on the site of administration and presence of vasoconstrictors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 5.4.3. FACTORS REQUIRING DOSE REDUCTION

* Patient-specific factors:
  * Advanced age (elderly) - reduce by 20-30%
  * Very young (neonates/infants) - reduce by 30-50%
  * Significant cardiac disease - reduce by 20-30%
  * Hepatic dysfunction - reduce by 30-50% for amides
  * Pregnancy - reduce by 20-30%
  * Low plasma protein levels
  * Acidosis
* Technique-related factors:
  * Highly vascular injection sites (intercostal, head/neck)
  * Multiple injection sites (cumulative dose)
  * Rapid injection rate
  * Continuous infusion techniques (consider 24-hour cumulative dose)

### 5.4.4. ADJUSTING FOR COMBINED TECHNIQUES

* When using multiple local anesthetic techniques simultaneously:
  * Calculate cumulative dose from all sources
  * Consider using different agents with different toxicity profiles
  * Establish clear communication between team members about doses administered
  * Implement standardized documentation of cumulative dose

### 5.4.5. CONTINUOUS INFUSION GUIDELINES

* Typical safe infusion rates:
  * Lidocaine: 1-2 mg/kg/hr
  * Bupivacaine: 0.1-0.2 mg/kg/hr
  * Ropivacaine: 0.1-0.25 mg/kg/hr
* Consider both initial bolus and subsequent infusion in total calculations
* Implement systems to track cumulative dose over time

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** For patients requiring multiple regional techniques (e.g., bilateral blocks or combined plexus/neuraxial anesthesia), consider using a combination of different agents (e.g., ropivacaine for one block, bupivacaine for another) to minimize the risk of reaching toxic levels of any single agent [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

⚠️ **Safety Note:** The "maximum safe dose" is a misleading concept as it implies a clear threshold below which toxicity won't occur. In reality, toxicity risk exists on a continuum and depends on multiple factors beyond simple mg/kg calculations [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** While maximum dose guidelines provide important reference points, they should be adjusted based on patient factors, injection site, and clinical context. The safest approach is to use the minimum effective dose, implement safety systems for dose tracking, and maintain vigilance for early signs of toxicity regardless of the administered dose. The absorption of local anesthetic varies significantly by site and patient factors, making rigid adherence to maximum dose tables potentially misleading.

## 5.5. INDIVIDUAL AGENTS AND CLINICAL APPLICATIONS 



**Different local anesthetic agents have specific properties making them suitable for particular clinical applications.**

### 5.5.1. AMIDE LOCAL ANESTHETICS

#### 5.5.1.1. LIDOCAINE

* Characteristics:
  * Intermediate duration
  * Rapid onset
  * Moderate potency
  * Moderate protein binding (70%) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Moderate vasodilatory properties
* Concentrations: 0.5-5% (topical), 1-2% (injectable) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Clinical applications:
  * Infiltration anesthesia
  * Peripheral nerve blocks
  * Epidural anesthesia
  * Intravenous regional anesthesia (Bier block)
  * Topical anesthesia (mucous membranes)
  * Spinal anesthesia (though association with transient neurological symptoms) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Systemic analgesia (IV infusion)
  * Antiarrhythmic (Class IB) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Specific considerations:
  * Addition of epinephrine extends duration by 50-100% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * IV administration: 1.5 mg/kg bolus can decrease cerebral blood flow and attenuate rise in intracranial pressure during intubation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * IV infusions can reduce MAC of volatile anesthetics by up to 40% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Formulated as hydrochloride and presented as colorless solution (0.5-2%) with or without adrenaline (1 in 80-200,000); 2% gel; 5% ointment; spray delivering 10mg per dose; 4% solution for topical use on mucous membranes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 5.5.1.2. BUPIVACAINE

* Characteristics:
  * Long duration
  * Slower onset than lidocaine
  * High potency
  * High protein binding (>95%) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Significant cardiac toxicity profile
* Concentrations: 0.125-0.75% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Clinical applications:
  * Peripheral nerve blocks
  * Epidural anesthesia/analgesia
  * Spinal anesthesia (0.5% with 80 mg/ml glucose - specific gravity 1.026) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Infiltration (lower concentrations)
  * Labor analgesia
* Specific considerations:
  * Significant cardiotoxicity with high binding to resting/inactivated Na+ channels and slower dissociation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Racemic mixture (Rand S-enantiomers) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Addition of epinephrine has minimal effect on duration due to high protein binding [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Higher risk of toxicity with unintentional intravascular injection
  * Maximum dose 2 mg/kg [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Remains the mainstay of epidural infusions in labor and postoperatively despite concerns about cardiac toxicity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 5.5.1.3. ROPIVACAINE

* Characteristics:
  * Long duration (slightly shorter than bupivacaine)
  * Moderate onset
  * Slightly lower potency than bupivacaine
  * High protein binding (94%) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Reduced cardiotoxicity compared to bupivacaine
* Concentrations: 0.2-1% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Clinical applications:
  * Peripheral nerve blocks
  * Epidural anesthesia/analgesia
  * Spinal anesthesia
  * Labor analgesia
* Specific considerations:
  * Pure S-enantiomer [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Intrinsic vasoconstrictor activity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Addition of epinephrine has minimal effect on duration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Produces less motor block than bupivacaine at equivalent concentrations [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Prepared in three concentrations (2, 7.5 and 10 mg/ml), in two volumes (10 and 100 ml) and as the pure S-enantiomer [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Not prepared with vasoconstrictor as this doesn't alter duration or tissue uptake [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Has propyl group on piperidine nitrogen vs. butyl in bupivacaine and methyl in mepivacaine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 5.5.1.4. LEVOBUPIVACAINE

* Characteristics:
  * Long duration (similar to bupivacaine)
  * Moderate onset
  * Slightly lower potency than racemic bupivacaine
  * High protein binding (>95%) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Reduced cardiotoxicity compared to racemic bupivacaine
* Concentrations: 0.125-0.75% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Clinical applications:
  * Similar to bupivacaine
  * Preferred in situations where cardiotoxicity is of particular concern
* Specific considerations:
  * Pure S-enantiomer of bupivacaine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * 30-40% less cardiotoxic than racemic bupivacaine in animal studies [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Prepared as 2.5, 5 and 7.5 mg/ml solution [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Maximum single dose of 150 mg and maximum dose over 24 hours of 400 mg [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 5.5.1.5. PRILOCAINE

* Characteristics:
  * Intermediate duration
  * Rapid onset
  * Lower potency than lidocaine
  * Moderate protein binding (50-55%) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Less vasodilating than lidocaine
* Concentrations: 0.5-4% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Clinical applications:
  * Infiltration anesthesia
  * Peripheral nerve blocks
  * Intravenous regional anesthesia (Bier block) - often drug of choice [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * EMLA cream (with lidocaine)
  * Spinal anesthesia
  * Dental procedures (3% with felypressin) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Specific considerations:
  * Metabolized to o-toluidine, which can cause methemoglobinemia in doses >600mg [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Faster metabolism than other amides [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Useful for day-case surgery due to rapid recovery [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Maximum dose 6 mg/kg or 8 mg/kg with felypressin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * 2% hyperbaric spinal preparation used at 40-60 mg (2-3 ml) where motor function returns faster than with bupivacaine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 5.5.2. ESTER LOCAL ANESTHETICS

#### 5.5.2.1. CHLOROPROCAINE

* Characteristics:
  * Very short duration
  * Extremely rapid onset
  * Low potency
  * Very rapid metabolism by plasma esterases
* Concentrations: 1-3% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Clinical applications:
  * Short outpatient procedures
  * Infiltration anesthesia
  * Epidural anesthesia for short procedures
* Specific considerations:
  * Ultra-short duration due to rapid hydrolysis
  * Historical association with neurological deficits after inadvertent intrathecal injection (likely due to previous preservatives) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * May decrease effectiveness of subsequently administered epidural opioids [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * First introduced 1952, abandoned after addition of sodium bisulphite preservatives led to neurological injuries in women receiving large epidural doses [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Current preparations preservative-free [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * 30-60 mg of 1% or 2% solutions useful for procedures under 60 minutes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Recovery times shorter than bupivacaine, useful in ambulatory setting [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 5.5.2.2. COCAINE

* Characteristics:
  * Intermediate duration
  * Moderate onset
  * Natural origin
  * Unique vasoconstricting properties
  * CNS stimulant
* Concentrations: 4-10% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Clinical applications:
  * Topical anesthesia for nasal and laryngeal procedures
  * ENT surgery
* Specific considerations:
  * Inhibits the reuptake of norepinephrine, potentiating sympathetic effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Can cause hypertension and ventricular ectopy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Initial treatment of toxicity should include benzodiazepines [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Cocaine-induced arrhythmias respond to α-adrenergic antagonists and amiodarone [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Derived from Erythroxylon coca leaves indigenous to Bolivia and Peru [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Previously used in Moffatt's solution (2 ml 8% cocaine, 2 ml 1% sodium bicarbonate, 1 ml 1:1,000 adrenaline) for nasal cavities [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Also formulated as 1-4% paste [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Maximum dose 1.5 mg/kg or 100 mg [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Highly protein-bound (about 95%) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Undergoes significant hepatic hydrolysis to inactive products, unlike other esters [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

#### 5.5.2.3. TETRACAINE

* Characteristics:
  * Long duration
  * Slow onset
  * High potency
  * High lipid solubility
* Concentrations: 0.2-1% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Clinical applications:
  * Spinal anesthesia
  * Topical ophthalmic anesthesia
  * Topical airway anesthesia
* Specific considerations:
  * Highly potent agent (10x more potent than procaine) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Lower incidence of transient neurological symptoms compared to lidocaine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Common in ophthalmology as topical anesthetic
  * Also known as amethocaine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Available as 0.5% and 1% drops for topical use before local anesthetic block or lens surgery [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * May produce burning sensation on initial instillation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * 4% cream for topical anesthesia has faster onset than EMLA (30 minutes) with effects lasting 4-6 hours [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** For day surgery requiring short-duration blocks with rapid recovery, consider chloroprocaine for neuraxial anesthesia or prilocaine for peripheral nerve blocks, as both provide good surgical conditions with faster resolution than longer-acting agents [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

⚠️ **Safety Note:** The rate of systemic absorption affects the risk of toxicity. Even when using doses below the "maximum safe" limits, consider the site of injection: intercostal > caudal > epidural > brachial plexus > sciatic/femoral > spinal > subcutaneous (highest to lowest risk) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Selection of local anesthetic agents should be based on the specific clinical scenario, considering onset time, duration of action, and toxicity profile. Lidocaine provides rapid onset and intermediate duration suitable for many procedures; bupivacaine and its S-enantiomer formulations offer prolonged analgesia; chloroprocaine and prilocaine excel in rapid-recovery settings; and cocaine's unique vasoconstricting properties make it valuable for specific ENT applications.

# 6. SYSTEMIC EFFECTS ON ORGAN SYSTEMS 












## 6.2. EFFECTS ON CENTRAL NERVOUS SYSTEM 



**Local anesthetics have significant effects on the central nervous system beyond their local actions.**

### 6.2.1. DIRECT CNS EFFECTS

* Local anesthetics readily cross the blood-brain barrier [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Effects follow a biphasic pattern based on increasing plasma concentration:
  * Initial excitatory phase (inhibition of inhibitory pathways):
	* Circumoral and tongue numbness, metallic taste
	* Visual and auditory disturbances (tinnitus)
	* Lightheadedness, dizziness
	* Agitation, confusion
	* Muscle twitching
	* Seizures [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Later depressant phase (global inhibition):
	* CNS depression
	* Unconsciousness
	* Respiratory arrest
	* Cardiovascular collapse [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Mechanism of biphasic response:
  * Initially, inhibitory interneurones are blocked, resulting in excitatory phenomena [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * At higher concentrations, all central neurones are depressed leading to coma and apnoea [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 6.2.2. THERAPEUTIC APPLICATIONS

* IV lidocaine:
  * Bolus 1.5mg/kg can decrease cerebral blood flow and attenuate the rise in intracranial pressure that may accompany intubation in patients with decreased intracranial compliance [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * IV infusions (1-2 mg/kg/hr) can:
	* Reduce MAC of volatile anesthetics by up to 40% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Inhibit inflammation
	* Reduce postoperative pain
	* Reduce postoperative opioid requirements and potentially reduce length of stay [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 6.2.3. NEUROLOGICAL COMPLICATIONS

* Spinal lidocaine:
  * Linked to cauda equina syndrome (urinary and fecal incontinence, gait disturbances) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Association documented with high concentration (5%) intrathecal lidocaine administered via narrow lumen catheters [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Slow administration through these catheters allows for pooling in the region of cauda equina [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Neurotoxicity may be due to demyelination [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Transient neurological symptoms:
  * Reported most commonly after lidocaine 5% spinal anesthesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Characterized by pain or sensory abnormalities in lower back, buttocks, or legs
  * Incidence higher with:
	* Lidocaine (compared to other agents)
	* Lithotomy position
	* Ambulatory anesthesia
  * Many anesthesiologists have abandoned lidocaine for spinal anesthesia due to this risk [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Other neurological concerns:
  * Chloroprocaine: Historical reports of prolonged neurological deficits after unintentional subarachnoid injection of large volumes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * High spinals: Can cause apnea primarily due to hypotension and brain ischemia, rather than direct phrenic blockade [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 6.2.4. MODIFYING FACTORS

* Benzodiazepines, propofol, and hyperventilation raise the threshold for local anesthetic-induced seizures [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Both respiratory and metabolic acidosis reduce the seizure threshold [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** IV lidocaine infusion (1.5 mg/kg bolus followed by 1-2 mg/kg/hr) is increasingly used in multimodal analgesia protocols for major abdominal surgery, as it appears to reduce pain scores, opioid consumption, and possibly ileus while modulating inflammatory responses [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

⚠️ **Safety Note:** The CNS is generally more sensitive to local anesthetic toxicity than the cardiovascular system. Seizures typically occur at plasma concentrations that are 1/3 to 1/2 of those causing cardiovascular collapse, providing an early warning sign for impending severe toxicity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Local anesthetics have biphasic effects on the CNS, with initial excitation followed by depression at higher concentrations. While these agents can be harnessed therapeutically (e.g., IV lidocaine for pain management), they may also cause neurological complications ranging from transient symptoms to severe toxicity. Factors affecting the CNS threshold include acid-base status, concomitant medications, and specific agent properties.

## 6.3. EFFECTS ON CARDIOVASCULAR SYSTEM 



**Local anesthetics have multiple direct and indirect effects on the cardiovascular system.**

### 6.3.1. DIRECT CARDIAC EFFECTS

* All local anesthetics depress myocardial automaticity (spontaneous phase 4 depolarization) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Effects on cardiac function are dose-dependent:
  * Lower concentrations: Minimal effects
  * Higher concentrations: Depression of contractility and conduction velocity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Major cardiovascular toxicity typically requires approximately three times the local anesthetic concentration as that required to produce seizures [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Cardiac sodium channel effects:
  * Block cardiac sodium channels (antiarrhythmic effect) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Decrease rate of depolarization (phase 0 of action potential) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Slow conduction velocity
  * Increase PR interval and QRS duration
  * May prolong effective refractory period
* Differential cardiotoxicity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Bupivacaine > ropivacaine > lidocaine
  * Bupivacaine is more cardiotoxic due to:
	* High binding to resting or inactivated Na+ channels
	* Slower dissociation from channels during diastole
  * Bupivacaine is 10 times slower at dissociating from Na+ channels, resulting in persistent depression [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * May lead to re-entrant arrhythmias and ventricular fibrillation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Tachycardia may enhance frequency-dependent blockade, adding to toxicity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Life-threatening arrhythmias may reflect disruption of Ca2+ and K+ channels [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 6.3.2. VASCULAR EFFECTS

* At low concentrations, inhibit nitric oxide - causing vasoconstriction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* At higher concentrations, most LAs cause vasodilation
* Differential effects based on agent:
  * Cocaine: Unique vasoconstricting properties
  * Ropivacaine: Mild vasoconstricting properties
  * Lidocaine, mepivacaine, bupivacaine: Primarily vasodilating
* In general, local anesthetics cause vasodilatation in low concentrations (prilocaine > lidocaine > bupivacaine > ropivacaine) and vasoconstriction at higher concentrations [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 6.3.3. HEMODYNAMIC EFFECTS

* Direct effects:
  * Decreased cardiac output (negative inotropic effect)
  * Decreased peripheral vascular resistance (vasodilation)
  * Decreased arterial blood pressure
  * Potential bradycardia (SA and AV node depression)
* Indirect effects (from neural blockade):
  * Sympathetic blockade can cause:
	* Vasodilation
	* Reduced preload
	* Hypotension (especially with neuraxial techniques)
  * Blockade of cardioaccelerator fibers (T1-T4) can cause bradycardia

### 6.3.4. UNIQUE CHARACTERISTICS OF SPECIFIC AGENTS

* Bupivacaine:
  * Binds cardiac sodium channels with high affinity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Slow dissociation during diastole prevents complete recovery during cardiac cycle [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * More likely to cause refractory arrhythmias and cardiovascular collapse [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * R(+) isomer more cardiotoxic than S(-) isomer (levobupivacaine) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Ropivacaine and levobupivacaine:
  * S-enantiomers with reduced cardiotoxicity
  * 30-40% less cardiovascular toxicity than bupivacaine in animal models [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Cardiotoxicity difference less striking in human studies [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Ropivacaine dissociates more rapidly from cardiac Na+ channels and produces less myocardial depression than bupivacaine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Cocaine:
  * Cardiovascular reactions unlike other local anesthetics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Inhibits normal reuptake of norepinephrine by adrenergic nerve terminals
  * Potentiates effects of adrenergic stimulation leading to hypertension and ventricular ectopy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Treatment includes benzodiazepines, α-adrenergic antagonists, and amiodarone [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 6.3.5. BENEFICIAL CARDIOVASCULAR EFFECTS

* Antiarrhythmic properties (Class Ib for lidocaine) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* May depress bronchial smooth muscle and block reflex bronchoconstriction associated with intubation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Local anesthetics mildly depress normal blood coagulation and enhance fibrinolysis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * May contribute to lower incidence of thromboembolic events in patients receiving epidural anesthetics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Levobupivacaine and ropivacaine offer a significant safety advantage over racemic bupivacaine for high-dose applications (e.g., intercostal blocks, high-volume fascial plane blocks) due to their reduced cardiotoxicity profile [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

⚠️ **Safety Note:** Cardiac arrest from bupivacaine toxicity is particularly difficult to treat due to its persistent binding to cardiac sodium channels. Successful resuscitation often requires prolonged CPR, high-dose epinephrine, and lipid emulsion therapy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Local anesthetics affect the cardiovascular system through direct myocardial depression, conduction slowing, and vasodilation. Bupivacaine shows greater cardiotoxicity than other agents due to its strong binding and slow dissociation from cardiac sodium channels. S-enantiomer formulations (levobupivacaine, ropivacaine) offer improved cardiac safety profiles while maintaining clinical efficacy. Cocaine has unique cardiovascular effects due to its inhibition of catecholamine reuptake.

## 6.4. EFFECTS ON RESPIRATORY SYSTEM



**Local anesthetics have several important effects on the respiratory system.**

### 6.4.1. DIRECT EFFECTS ON RESPIRATORY FUNCTION

* Lidocaine depresses hypoxic drive [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* May cause hypoventilation through:
  * Phrenic and intercostal nerve paralysis (during high spinal anesthesia) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Depression of medullary respiratory center following direct exposure (e.g., after retrobulbar blocks) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Effects on bronchial smooth muscle:
  * Relax bronchial smooth muscle
  * May block reflex bronchoconstriction associated with intubation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Potential benefit in reactive airway disease
* Topical airway applications:
  * Suppress protective airway reflexes
  * Risk of aspiration due to diminished laryngeal sensation
  * Potential for post-procedural airway edema after repeated application

### 6.4.2. IMPORTANT DISTINCTIONS

* Apnea after high spinal or epidural anesthesia is nearly always the result of hypotension and brain ischemia, rather than direct phrenic block [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* IV lidocaine (1-1.5 mg/kg) can suppress airway reflexes during intubation without significant respiratory depression

### 6.4.3. CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS

* When using topical anesthesia for airway procedures:
  * Maintain NPO status to reduce aspiration risk
  * Be prepared for post-procedure monitoring of airway patency
  * Consider oxygen supplementation
  * Be aware of the potential for delayed respiratory complications
* With neuraxial anesthesia:
  * High blocks (T1-T4) may affect intercostal muscles
  * Cervical blocks may affect the phrenic nerve (C3-C5)
  * Address hypotension promptly to prevent medullary hypoperfusion and respiratory depression

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Nebulized lidocaine (4-5 mL of 2% solution) can provide effective airway anesthesia for awake intubation while causing less suppression of protective reflexes than direct spray application because of more gradual absorption and lower peak tissue concentrations.

**Bottom Line:** Local anesthetics affect the respiratory system by depressing hypoxic drive, potentially paralyzing respiratory muscles (via neural blockade), relaxing bronchial smooth muscle, and suppressing protective airway reflexes. Most respiratory depression associated with neuraxial anesthesia is due to hypotension-induced brain ischemia rather than direct phrenic nerve blockade.

## 6.5. EFFECTS ON OTHER SYSTEMS



**Local anesthetics affect multiple body systems beyond the CNS, CVS, and respiratory system.**

### 6.5.1. MUSCULOSKELETAL SYSTEM

* Mildly myotoxic [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Direct toxic effects on skeletal muscle cells
  * Hypercontracted myofibrils, followed by lytic degeneration
  * Regeneration typically occurs within 4 weeks after injection [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Compounding with steroid or epinephrine worsens myonecrosis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Joint effects:
  * When infused into joints for prolonged periods, local anesthetics can produce severe chondromalacia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * May affect cartilage metabolism

### 6.5.2. HEMATOLOGICAL SYSTEM

* Mildly depresses normal blood coagulation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Reduced thrombosis
  * Decreased platelet aggregation
  * Enhanced fibrinolysis of whole blood (measurable by thromboelastography) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* May contribute to lower incidence of thromboembolic events in patients receiving epidural anesthetics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 6.5.3. IMMUNOLOGICAL SYSTEM

* Allergic reactions:
  * Esters more likely to induce allergic reaction, especially derivatives of PABA (procaine, benzocaine) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Commercial multidose preparations of amides often contain methylparaben preservatives, which may cause allergic reactions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Multi-dose bottles contain 1 mg/ml of preservative methyl parahydroxybenzoate [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Immune modulation:
  * May have anti-inflammatory properties
  * Can inhibit granulocyte priming
  * May reduce release of inflammatory mediators

### 6.5.4. NEUROLOGICAL SYSTEM

* Local neurotoxicity:
  * Direct neural tissue toxicity possible with all agents
  * Concentration-dependent effect
  * Risk factors include:
	* High concentration
	* Prolonged exposure
	* Intrafascicular injection
	* Additives
  * Transient neurological symptoms:
	* Most common after lidocaine spinal anesthesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Characterized by pain or dysesthesia in buttocks, thighs or lower limbs
	* Self-limiting in most cases
  * Neurotoxicity correlates with higher drug concentration and intrafascicular placement [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 6.5.5. HEPATIC SYSTEM

* Potential for hepatotoxicity is rare
* More relevant is the effect of hepatic disease on amide local anesthetic metabolism
* Reduction in clearance can lead to drug accumulation with repeated doses

### 6.5.6. REPRODUCTIVE SYSTEM

* Cross placenta via passive diffusion
* Fetal/maternal ratio varies by agent:
  * Highly protein-bound agents (bupivacaine) have lower F/M ratios
  * Less protein-bound agents (lidocaine) have higher F/M ratios
* No evidence of teratogenicity at clinical doses
* Degree of placental transfer affected by protein binding - bupivacaine is more highly bound than lidocaine so less crosses placenta [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* If fetus becomes acidotic, ion trapping may cause local anesthetic accumulation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Ester local anesthetics do not cross placenta in significant amounts due to rapid metabolism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Local anesthetics' effects on coagulation may be beneficial in the perioperative period. Epidural anesthesia/analgesia appears to reduce thromboembolic events beyond what can be explained by anticoagulant prophylaxis alone, possibly due to these mild anticoagulant properties [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

⚠️ **Safety Note:** Avoid prolonged continuous intra-articular infusion of local anesthetics, particularly in weight-bearing joints, due to the risk of chondrolysis. This complication has been reported particularly after shoulder arthroscopy with post-operative pain pumps [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Local anesthetics have widespread effects beyond their intended neural targets. They can cause mild myotoxicity, affect coagulation, modulate immune function, and potentially damage cartilage with prolonged exposure. Understanding these effects is important for comprehensive patient care and preventing unintended consequences. Esters are more likely to induce allergic reactions due to their PABA metabolites.

# 7. LOCAL ANESTHETIC SYSTEMIC TOXICITY (LAST) [PART A/B/C]












## 7.2. RISK FACTORS AND PREVENTION [PART A/B/C]



**Preventing local anesthetic systemic toxicity requires understanding risk factors and implementing preventive strategies.**

### 7.2.1. PATIENT RISK FACTORS

* Age extremes:
  * Neonates/infants: Decreased protein binding, immature metabolism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Elderly: Decreased clearance, increased sensitivity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Low muscle mass: Smaller volume of distribution [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Female gender: Possible increased sensitivity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Pre-existing conditions:
  * Cardiac arrhythmias or heart failure: Decreased clearance, increased sensitivity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Liver dysfunction: Reduced metabolism of amides [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Metabolic diseases (especially acidosis): Lower threshold for toxicity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * CNS disease: Potentially lower seizure threshold [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Low plasma protein states (pregnancy, malnutrition, liver disease): Increased free fraction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 7.2.2. AGENT-RELATED RISK FACTORS

* Potency:
  * More potent agents (bupivacaine, ropivacaine) have narrower therapeutic margins [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * CNS:CVS toxicity ratio is lower for bupivacaine (2.0) vs. lidocaine (7.1) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Dose and concentration:
  * Higher doses increase risk proportionally [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Exceeded maximum recommended doses
* Vascularity of injection site:
  * Higher risk: Intercostal > caudal > epidural > brachial plexus > sciatic/femoral > subcutaneous [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Duration of exposure:
  * Continuous infusions may lead to accumulation

### 7.2.3. PREVENTIVE STRATEGIES

* Dose considerations:
  * Use minimum effective dose
  * Calculate maximum dose based on lean body weight
  * Reduce doses for high-risk patients and vascular areas
  * Consider cumulative dosing with multiple blocks
* Technical precautions:
  * Careful aspiration before injection [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Incremental injection (3-5 mL with continuous monitoring) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Use of test doses with epinephrine (15 μg epinephrine in adults) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Ultrasound guidance to visualize needle placement and LA spread
  * Use of vasoconstrictor additives when appropriate [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Monitoring:
  * Continuous verbal contact during initial injection
  * Standard monitoring (ECG, BP, pulse oximetry)
  * Vigilance for 30 minutes after injection (covers most immediate LAST)
  * Extended monitoring for high-risk blocks/patients
* Institutional measures:
  * Standardized dosing protocols
  * Availability of lipid emulsion in all locations where LAs are used
  * Regular simulation training for recognition and management
  * Checklist implementation
  * Limiting concentrations of more toxic agents (e.g., bupivacaine)

### 7.2.4. INCIDENCE AND EPIDEMIOLOGY

* Overall incidence of LAST: 0.04-0.18% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Varies by block type and technique:
  * Upper extremity blocks have higher incidence than lower extremity blocks [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Penile blocks and local tissue infiltrations account for many LAST events [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Trend of gradual decrease in yearly reported incidence of LAST [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* All ages affected, but extremes of age at higher risk:
  * Infants <6 months at sixfold greater risk compared to older children [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The "4-2-1" rule helps remember the relative risk of LAST based on the ratio of CNS:CVS toxicity thresholds: Chloroprocaine/Procaine 4:1, Prilocaine 3:1, Lidocaine/Mepivacaine 2:1, Bupivacaine/Ropivacaine/Levobupivacaine 1:1. Agents with ratios closer to 1:1 have less safety margin between CNS symptoms and cardiovascular collapse [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

⚠️ **Safety Note:** Epinephrine as a marker of intravascular injection is not 100% reliable. The hemodynamic response may be absent in patients taking beta-blockers, and false positives can occur due to pain, anxiety, or adrenal release. Use multiple safety strategies rather than relying on a single approach [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** LAST prevention requires understanding patient risk factors, agent properties, and implementing systematic safety measures including appropriate dose calculation, technical precautions, continuous monitoring, and institutional protocols. Ultrasound guidance, test dosing, incremental injection, and the use of less cardiotoxic agents in high-risk situations are key prevention strategies.

## 7.3. CLINICAL MANIFESTATIONS 



**LAST presents with a spectrum of CNS and cardiovascular manifestations that typically follow a progressive pattern.**

### 7.3.1. CNS MANIFESTATIONS

* Local anesthetics readily cross the blood-brain barrier [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Progression of CNS symptoms (in order of usual presentation) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  1. Prodromal symptoms:
	 * Circumoral and tongue numbness, metallic taste
	 * Visual disturbances (blurred vision, difficulty focusing)
	 * Auditory disturbances (tinnitus)
	 * Lightheadedness, dizziness
  2. Excitatory phase (inhibition of inhibitory pathways):
	 * Agitation, confusion, nervousness
	 * Slurred speech
	 * Muscle twitching, tremors
	 * Seizures
  3. Depressive phase:
	 * CNS depression
	 * Unconsciousness
	 * Coma
	 * Respiratory arrest
* CNS toxicity is typically seen at lower concentrations than cardiovascular toxicity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Lidocaine plasma concentrations associated with CNS effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
	* Analgesia: 1-5 μg/mL
	* Lightheadedness, tinnitus, tongue numbness: 5-10 μg/mL
	* Seizures, unconsciousness: 10-15 μg/mL
	* Coma, respiratory arrest: 15-25 μg/mL
	* Cardiovascular depression: >25 μg/mL

### 7.3.2. CARDIOVASCULAR MANIFESTATIONS

* Cardiovascular toxicity typically occurs at plasma concentrations 3x higher than those causing CNS toxicity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Progression of cardiovascular symptoms:
  1. Initial phase:
	 * Hypertension (initially)
	 * Tachycardia
  2. Intermediate phase:
	 * Myocardial depression
	 * Peripheral vasodilation
	 * Hypotension
	 * Conduction abnormalities (PR prolongation, QRS widening)
  3. Late phase:
	 * Severe hypotension
	 * Dysrhythmias (various forms):
	   * Bradycardia
	   * Ventricular arrhythmias
	   * Wide complex tachycardia
	   * Ventricular fibrillation
	 * Conduction blocks
	 * Asystole
	 * Cardiovascular collapse
* Bupivacaine more likely to cause malignant arrhythmias due to:
  * Stronger binding to cardiac sodium channels [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Slower dissociation during diastole [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * More difficult to resuscitate than lidocaine toxicity
  * Blocks cardiac Na+ channels and decreases maximum rate of increase of phase 0 of cardiac action potential [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Direct myocardial depressant properties (bupivacaine > lidocaine) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Increases PR and QRS intervals and prolongs refractory period [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * 10 times slower at dissociating from Na+ channels, resulting in persistent depression [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 7.3.3. PATTERN OF PRESENTATION

* Timing of onset:
  * Majority of cases occur within minutes of injection [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * 75% of cases noted within 5 minutes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Delayed presentations (hours later) usually associated with continuous infusion catheters [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Frequency of specific symptoms (from published case reports) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Most common CNS symptoms:
	* Seizures (68-77%)
	* Agitation/confusion (50-67%)
	* Unconsciousness (30-43%)
  * Most common cardiovascular symptoms:
	* Hypotension (51%)
	* Arrhythmias (48%)
	* Bradycardia (28%)
	* Asystole (12%)
* Isolated presentations:
  * Classic progression: CNS symptoms followed by cardiovascular symptoms
  * However, 10-20% of cases present with isolated cardiovascular manifestations without preceding CNS symptoms [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Any unexplained cardiovascular instability during LA administration should raise suspicion for LAST

### 7.3.4. ATYPICAL PRESENTATIONS

* Delayed onset: May occur hours after block placement or during continuous infusion
* Prolonged duration: Symptoms may recur or persist despite initial treatment
* Rebound toxicity: Recurrence after initial improvement
* Predominant cardiovascular manifestations without CNS symptoms
* Atypical presentations more common with bupivacaine and ropivacaine

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** While the classic teaching is that CNS symptoms precede cardiovascular collapse, up to 20% of LAST cases present with cardiovascular symptoms first or exclusively. Always maintain vigilance for cardiovascular signs even in the absence of CNS manifestations [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

⚠️ **Safety Note:** The onset of tinnitus, perioral numbness, metallic taste, agitation, or confusion during or shortly after local anesthetic administration should prompt immediate cessation of injection and implementation of monitoring and supportive measures. These early warning signs should never be ignored [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** LAST typically presents with progressive CNS symptoms (perioral numbness, tinnitus, agitation, seizures, unconsciousness) followed by cardiovascular manifestations (dysrhythmias, conduction defects, hypotension, cardiac arrest). However, presentations vary widely, and some patients present with isolated cardiovascular symptoms. The onset is usually rapid (within minutes) but can be delayed, especially with continuous infusions.

## 7.4. MANAGEMENT AND TREATMENT [PART A/B/C]



**Management of LAST requires prompt recognition, cessation of local anesthetic, and systematic supportive and specific therapies.**

### 7.4.1. IMMEDIATE MANAGEMENT

* Recognition and alert:
  * Recognize symptoms of LAST [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Call for help and LAST kit (including lipid emulsion) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Assign team roles
  * Consider LAST in any patient with altered mental status or cardiovascular instability after LA administration
* Initial actions:
  * Stop local anesthetic administration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Maintain airway and ensure adequate ventilation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Administer 100% oxygen [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Establish IV access if not already present
  * Continuous monitoring (ECG, BP, SpO2)

### 7.4.2. SEIZURE MANAGEMENT

* First-line:
  * Benzodiazepines (midazolam or diazepam) are preferred agents [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Midazolam 2-5 mg IV
	* Diazepam 5-10 mg IV
  * Advantages: Raise seizure threshold, minimal cardiac depression [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Alternative agents (use with caution):
  * Small doses of propofol (if benzodiazepines unavailable) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Be aware propofol can worsen hypotension and cardiac depression
* For prolonged/refractory seizures:
  * Consider neuromuscular blockade (succinylcholine) with airway control [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Note: This stops muscular activity but not cerebral seizure activity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 7.4.3. CARDIOVASCULAR MANAGEMENT

* Basic principles:
  * Follow ACLS protocols with specific modifications for LAST [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Goal is to maintain coronary and cerebral perfusion
* For hypotension:
  * IV fluid bolus
  * Small doses of vasopressors (ephedrine, phenylephrine)
  * ⚠️ Epinephrine doses should be reduced (initial bolus <1 μg/kg) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* For arrhythmias:
  * Amiodarone is preferred for ventricular arrhythmias [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ AVOID: vasopressin, calcium channel blockers, beta blockers, and local anesthetics (Class I antiarrhythmics like lidocaine) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* For cardiac arrest:
  * Begin high-quality CPR immediately
  * Consider early mechanical CPR device for prolonged resuscitation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Administer lipid emulsion (see below)
  * Be prepared for prolonged resuscitation efforts
  * Consider ECMO/cardiopulmonary bypass for refractory cases [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 7.4.4. LIPID EMULSION THERAPY

* Indications:
  * Any significant cardiovascular symptoms attributed to LAST [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Should be given at the first signs of serious LAST
  * Don't delay administration while pursuing other treatments
* Dosing for 20% lipid emulsion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  1. Initial bolus: 1.5 mL/kg (100 mL for 70 kg adult) over 2-3 minutes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  2. Start infusion: 0.25 mL/kg/min (approximately 18 mL/min for 70 kg adult; 1000ml/hr) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  3. Continue for at least 10 minutes after hemodynamic stability achieved
  4. If instability persists:
	 * Repeat bolus up to twice (maximum three boluses total) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	 * Increase infusion to 0.5 mL/kg/min (30ml/kg/h) if needed [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	 * Maximum cumulative dose: approximately 12 mL/kg [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Mechanism of action:
  * "Lipid sink" theory: Lipid particles sequester lipophilic local anesthetics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Redistribution from target tissues to lipid phase [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Direct cardiotonic effects via alternative energy substrate [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Modification of intracellular signaling downstream of sodium channel blockade [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * May prevent Na+ channel inhibition [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Functions as a "lipid sink" binding the local anesthetic and effectively removing it from its target organ [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Provides alternative energy substrate, offsetting LA inhibition of fatty acid metabolism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 7.4.5. POST-RESUSCITATION CARE

* Admission for observation:
  * ICU setting recommended
  * Monitoring for at least 2-6 hours after the event (longer for severe cases)
  * Watch for recurrence of symptoms (especially with highly lipid-soluble agents)
  * Monitor for complications of treatment (including pancreatitis from lipid emulsion)
* Documentation and reporting:
  * Document the event thoroughly
  * Report to the appropriate safety system (institutional and national)
  * Consider reporting to a LAST registry

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Consider creating a cognitive aid or checklist for LAST management in all locations where local anesthetics are administered. This should include the symptoms of LAST, immediate management steps, and the lipid emulsion dosing protocol [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].

⚠️ **Safety Note:** LAST from bupivacaine, ropivacaine, and levobupivacaine may be especially difficult to resuscitate. Resuscitation efforts should be continued for an extended period (30-45 minutes minimum), as successful resuscitation after prolonged CPR has been reported. Consider early activation of ECMO if available [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** LAST management requires prompt recognition, cessation of local anesthetic, airway support, seizure control with benzodiazepines, cardiovascular support following modified ACLS protocols, and lipid emulsion therapy. Early lipid emulsion administration is critical for significant cardiovascular toxicity. Be prepared for potentially prolonged resuscitation efforts, especially with bupivacaine toxicity. Intralipid has transformed the management of severe LAST through multiple mechanisms including lipid sink effect and cardiotonic actions.

## 7.5. LIPID EMULSION THERAPY [PART A/B/C]



**Lipid emulsion therapy has revolutionized the management of severe LAST, particularly cardiovascular toxicity.**

### 7.5.1. HISTORICAL DEVELOPMENT

* Introduced in early 2000s based on laboratory evidence [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Successive clinical reports of dramatic rescues after LA-induced cardiotoxicity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Now incorporated into all major LAST treatment guidelines
* Expanded application to other lipid-soluble drug toxicities

### 7.5.2. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* Multiple proposed mechanisms:
  1. "Lipid sink" effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
	 * Lipid particles act as scavengers to bind circulating LA
	 * Based on drug partitioning principles
	 * Removes LA from tissue binding sites
	 * Redistributes LA away from target organs (heart, brain)
  
  2. Cardiotonic effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
	 * Provides alternative energy substrate to cardiac myocytes
	 * Overcomes LA-induced inhibition of fatty acid transport
	 * Directly enhances cardiac contractility
	 * Restores vascular tone
  
  3. Cellular effects:
	 * Modifies intracellular signaling cascades
	 * May alter ion channel function
	 * Counteracts inhibition downstream of sodium channel blockade
	 * May prevent Na+ channel inhibition [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Likely involves multiple complementary mechanisms

### 7.5.3. INDICATIONS AND TIMING

* Clear indications:
  * Cardiovascular collapse or serious arrhythmias from LAST
  * Persistent seizures unresponsive to anticonvulsants
  * Progressive signs of LA toxicity
* Timing:
  * Emphasis on early administration at first sign of severe LAST [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Do not delay administration for other treatments
  * Society guidelines recommend initiation at first sign of dysrhythmia from suspected LAST [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 7.5.4. ADMINISTRATION PROTOCOL

* Lipid emulsion preparation:
  * 20% lipid emulsion (Intralipid®, ClinOleic®, or equivalent) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Available in 100, 250, and 500 mL bottles
  * Stored at room temperature
  * Part of dedicated "LAST kit"
* ASRA dosing protocol [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  1. Bolus: 1.5 mL/kg IV over 2-3 min (approximately 100 mL for 70 kg adult)
  2. Continuous infusion: 0.25 mL/kg/min (approximately 18 mL/min for 70 kg adult)
  3. Continue for minimum of 10 minutes after circulation restored
  4. If circulatory stability not achieved:
	 * Repeat bolus up to twice (maximum 3 boluses)
	 * Double infusion rate to 0.5 mL/kg/min if needed
	 * Maximum recommended dose: 12 mL/kg

### 7.5.5. PRACTICAL CONSIDERATIONS

* Implementation requirements:
  * Immediately available wherever LAs are used in significant quantities
  * Part of dedicated LAST kit with treatment algorithm
  * Regular staff education and simulation training
  * Clear labeling and storage protocols
* Potential adverse effects:
  * Hypertriglyceridemia (usually transient)
  * Pancreatitis (rare)
  * Interference with laboratory tests
  * Allergic reactions (rare)
  * ARDS (case reports, causality uncertain)
  * Acute kidney injury, cardiac arrest, ventilation perfusion mismatch, acute lung injury, venous thromboembolism, hypersensitivity, fat embolism, fat overload syndrome, pancreatitis, allergic reaction, and increased susceptibility to infection [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * These appear related to both the rate of infusion and total dose administered [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Post-administration monitoring:
  * Continued hemodynamic monitoring
  * Check serum triglyceride levels
  * Monitor for signs of pancreatitis
  * Consider removal via plasmapheresis for extreme hypertriglyceridemia

### 7.5.6. EFFECTIVENESS AND LIMITATIONS

* Most effective for:
  * Bupivacaine and other highly lipid-soluble LA toxicity
  * Cardiovascular toxicity more than CNS effects
  * Earlier administration generally yields better results
* Limitations:
  * Less effective for water-soluble medications
  * Not a substitute for standard ACLS measures
  * Optimal dosing regimen still being refined
  * May interfere with efficacy of some resuscitation medications

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** When preparing for a regional anesthetic procedure, mentally calculate the maximum dose of local anesthetic for that specific patient and have lipid emulsion immediately available. In the event of suspected LAST, having these calculations already done saves critical time [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].

⚠️ **Safety Note:** After administering lipid emulsion, blood samples may be lipaemic and interfere with laboratory testing. Inform the laboratory that lipid emulsion has been administered, and if possible, obtain baseline samples before administration [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Lipid emulsion therapy is a critical component of LAST management, particularly for cardiovascular toxicity. It should be administered early when serious LAST is suspected, following the bolus-infusion protocol, and continued until hemodynamic stability is achieved. Multiple mechanisms likely contribute to its effectiveness, including the "lipid sink" effect and direct cardiotonic actions. Despite potential adverse effects, its benefits in severe LAST far outweigh the risks.

# 8. ADVERSE EFFECTS AND COMPLICATIONS 












## 8.2. NEUROTOXICITY 



**Local anesthetics can cause direct neurotoxic effects ranging from transient symptoms to permanent injury.**

### 8.2.1. DIRECT NEURAL TOXICITY

* Histopathologic changes consistent with neuronal injury [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Demyelination
  * Wallerian degeneration
  * Disruption of axonal transport
  * Disruption of blood-nerve barrier
  * Decreased blood flow to the vasa nervorum
  * Loss of cell membrane integrity
* Causative mechanisms (speculative) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Direct toxicity to neural membrane
  * Effects on intracellular organelles
  * Alteration in axonal transport
  * Ischemic injury from vasoconstriction
  * Activation of apoptotic pathways
* Risk factors for neural toxicity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Intraneural injection (especially intrafascicular)
  * High concentration of local anesthetic
  * Prolonged exposure
  * Addition of additives (especially vasoconstrictors)
  * Pre-existing neural compromise
* Correlation with nerve location [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Intrafascicular injections cause more histologic changes
  * Extrafascicular and extraneural placements associated with milder damage
  * Direct needle trauma may compound chemical neurotoxicity

### 8.2.2. CLINICAL MANIFESTATIONS OF NEUROTOXICITY

* Incidence of serious complications is low:
  * After approximately 2.7 million central neuraxial blocks [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
	* Radiculopathy: approximately 0.03%
	* Paraplegia: approximately 0.0008%
  * Intraneural injections do not invariably lead to detectable neurologic symptoms [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Specific clinical scenarios with increased neurotoxicity risk:
  1. Microcatheters with high-concentration lidocaine for continuous spinal anesthesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
	 * Associated with increased incidence of radiculopathy and cauda equina syndrome
	 * High local concentrations in limited CSF space may be responsible

  2. Chloroprocaine with preservatives [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
	 * Previous formulations containing sodium bisulfite preservative
	 * Linked to prolonged sensory and motor deficits
	 * Modern preservative-free formulations appear safer

### 8.2.3. TRANSIENT NEUROLOGIC SYMPTOMS

* Characteristics:
  * Pain or sensory abnormalities in lower back, buttocks, or lower extremities [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Typically begins within 24 hours of spinal resolution
  * Usually resolves within 1 week
  * No permanent neurologic injury reported [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Incidence varies by agent [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Lidocaine: 4-36% (highest incidence)
  * Mepivacaine: 23%
  * Procaine: 6%
  * Bupivacaine, levobupivacaine, prilocaine, ropivacaine: approximately 1% (lowest)
* Risk factors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Use of lidocaine (vs. other agents)
  * Lithotomy position
  * Ambulatory anesthesia
  * Not related to baricity of solution or dose of local anesthetic
* Debated etiology:
  * Not clearly neurotoxic (no dose relationship)
  * No abnormalities on electrophysiologic testing
  * May involve patient positioning, sciatic nerve stretch, muscle spasm, or myofascial strain rather than neural toxicity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Responds to NSAIDs and trigger point injections (suggesting myofascial origin)

### 8.2.4. PREVENTION AND MANAGEMENT

* Prevention:
  * Avoid intraneural injection using appropriate technique
  * Use ultrasound guidance when possible
  * Use minimum effective concentration and volume
  * Consider alternative agents to lidocaine for spinal anesthesia if TNS is a concern
  * Maintain meticulous technique for neuraxial procedures
* Management of TNS:
  * Reassurance about benign, self-limiting nature
  * NSAIDs for symptom management
  * Muscle relaxants if indicated
  * Physical therapy for persistent symptoms
  * Documentation and follow-up
* Management of serious neurotoxicity:
  * Immediate neurological consultation
  * Consider MRI or other imaging
  * Electrophysiologic studies
  * Steroids (controversial benefit)
  * Supportive care and rehabilitation

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** To reduce the risk of TNS after spinal anesthesia, consider using bupivacaine, levobupivacaine, or ropivacaine instead of lidocaine, particularly for patients in the lithotomy position or undergoing ambulatory procedures [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

⚠️ **Safety Note:** Any persistent neurological deficit after regional anesthesia warrants prompt evaluation. Early imaging may be needed to rule out compressive etiologies (hematoma, abscess) requiring urgent intervention [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Local anesthetics can cause direct neurotoxicity ranging from transient symptoms to permanent injury, though serious complications are rare. Transient neurologic symptoms (TNS) occur in up to 36% of patients after lidocaine spinal anesthesia but are self-limiting. Risk factors for neurotoxicity include intraneural injection, high concentrations, and prolonged exposure. Prevention focuses on proper technique, appropriate agent selection, and using minimum effective doses.

## 8.3. MYOTOXICITY



**Local anesthetics can cause dose-dependent myotoxicity, which is usually subclinical and reversible.**

### 8.3.1. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

* All local anesthetics can cause histopathologic changes in skeletal muscle [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Manifestations include:
  * Hypercontracted myofibrils
  * Lytic degeneration of striated muscle sarcoplasmic reticulum
  * Diffuse myonecrosis
  * Edema and cellular infiltration

![Skeletal muscle changes after bupivacaine exposure](LA_Untitled_6.png_9b7294.png)

* Mechanisms:
  * Disruption of mitochondrial oxidative function [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Dysregulation of intracellular calcium homeostasis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Activation of apoptosis pathways
  * Direct effects on membrane integrity
* Characteristics of myotoxicity:
  * Drug-specific severity: bupivacaine (most) > ropivacaine > lidocaine > procaine/tetracaine (least) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Dose and duration dependent
  * Affects young more than old patients [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Compounding with steroid or epinephrine worsens myonecrosis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Usually subclinical and reversible
  * Regeneration occurs within 4 weeks after injection [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 8.3.2. CLINICAL PRESENTATIONS

* Muscle pain post-injection (may be difficult to distinguish from surgical pain)
* Muscle weakness in the affected area
* Rarely, focal muscle atrophy
* Most cases are subclinical with minimal functional consequences
* When infused into joints for prolonged periods, local anesthetics can produce severe chondromalacia (cartilage damage) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 8.3.3. RISK FACTORS

* High local concentrations
* Repeated injections into the same site
* Prolonged continuous infusions
* Addition of adjuvants (especially epinephrine)
* Young age
* Direct intramuscular injection

### 8.3.4. PREVENTION AND MANAGEMENT

* Preventive strategies:
  * Avoid intramuscular injections when possible
  * Use minimum effective concentration and volume
  * Limit duration of continuous local anesthetic infusions
  * Caution with intra-articular infusions (risk of chondrolysis)
  * Consider alternative techniques for prolonged analgesia
* Protective agents (experimental):
  * Erythropoietin may diminish myotoxic effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * N-acetylcysteine shows protective effects in laboratory studies [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Management of myotoxicity:
  * Usually self-limiting and requires no specific treatment
  * Symptomatic care (analgesics, anti-inflammatory drugs)
  * Physical therapy for significant weakness
  * Regular follow-up until complete resolution

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** When planning repeated blocks in the same anatomical location, consider alternating approaches or injection sites to minimize repeated exposure of the same muscle groups to high concentrations of local anesthetics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

⚠️ **Safety Note:** Avoid continuous intra-articular infusion of local anesthetics, particularly in weight-bearing joints, due to the risk of chondrolysis. This complication has been particularly associated with glenohumeral joints after arthroscopic procedures [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Local anesthetics can cause dose-dependent myotoxicity, manifesting as muscle damage with hypercontracted myofibrils and myonecrosis. This toxicity is usually subclinical and reversible within 4 weeks. Risk factors include high concentrations, prolonged exposure, young age, and additives like epinephrine. While usually self-limiting, prevention should focus on minimizing unnecessary muscle exposure to high local anesthetic concentrations.

## 8.4. ALLERGIC REACTIONS 



**True allergic reactions to local anesthetics are rare, though often suspected clinically.**

### 8.4.1. TYPES OF ALLERGIC REACTIONS

* Type I (immediate hypersensitivity):
  * IgE-mediated
  * Manifestations: urticaria, angioedema, bronchospasm, anaphylaxis
  * Rapid onset (minutes)
  * Potentially life-threatening
  * Estimated incidence <1% of all reported adverse reactions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Type IV (delayed hypersensitivity):
  * T-cell mediated
  * Manifestations: contact dermatitis (dermal erythema, pruritus, papules, vesicles)
  * Delayed onset (12-48 hours)
  * Usually self-limiting
  * More common than Type I reactions

### 8.4.2. RISK FACTORS AND ALLERGENIC COMPOUNDS

* Ester local anesthetics:
  * Higher allergenic potential than amides [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Often due to metabolism to para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA), a known allergen [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Examples: procaine, benzocaine, tetracaine, cocaine
  * Has been associated with hypersensitivity reactions especially in atopic patients [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Preservatives and additives:
  * Methylparaben: Chemical structure similar to PABA, often in multidose vials [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Metabisulphite: Used as antioxidant, especially in epinephrine-containing solutions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Latex: From injection port stoppers or procedural equipment
  * Contrast agents: Used in some image-guided procedures
* Amide local anesthetics:
  * True allergies extremely rare
  * Cross-reactivity between amides is possible but uncommon
  * Most reported "allergies" represent:
	* Vasovagal reactions
	* Anxiety-related symptoms
	* Reactions to preservatives
	* Inadvertent intravascular injection
	* Toxic reactions misinterpreted as allergic

### 8.4.3. DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS

* Conditions commonly misdiagnosed as LA allergy:
  * Vasovagal syncope (most common)
  * Anxiety reaction
  * Local anesthetic toxicity
  * Reactions to epinephrine
  * Contrast media reaction
  * Latex allergy
  * Underlying medical conditions (e.g., carcinoid)

### 8.4.4. EVALUATION AND TESTING

* History and physical:
  * Detailed description of previous reaction
  * Timing relative to injection
  * Specific symptoms
  * Agents used (including preservatives)
  * Treatment required
  * Past reactions to other medications
* Allergy testing for suspected reactions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Skin prick tests
  * Intradermal testing
  * Subcutaneous provocative dose challenges
  * Graded challenge testing
  * Referral to allergist/immunologist for formal evaluation

### 8.4.5. MANAGEMENT

* Acute reaction management:
  * Standard anaphylaxis protocol (ABCs, epinephrine, fluids, antihistamines, steroids)
  * Identify and remove offending agent
  * Document details for future reference
* For patients with documented or suspected allergy:
  * Change local anesthetic class (ester to amide or vice versa)
  * Use preservative-free formulations
  * Consider alternative anesthetic techniques
  * Premedication with antihistamines/steroids (limited evidence)
  * Graded challenge in controlled setting if appropriate

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** For patients with reported "allergy" to amide local anesthetics, preservative-free single-dose formulations can often be used safely. The majority of reactions attributed to amides are actually due to preservatives, vasovagal reactions, or inadvertent intravascular injection [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

⚠️ **Safety Note:** Maintain a high index of suspicion for LAST in cases of "allergic reaction." Many reported allergies are actually manifestations of local anesthetic toxicity. Always assess for progressive neurological and cardiovascular symptoms consistent with LAST [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** True allergic reactions to local anesthetics are rare, with esters more commonly implicated than amides due to their metabolism to PABA. Many reported "allergies" represent vasovagal reactions, toxic reactions, or responses to preservatives like methylparaben. Evaluation with skin testing and graded challenges is recommended for suspected cases, and alternative agents or preservative-free formulations can be used for patients with confirmed allergies.

## 8.5. TRANSIENT NEUROLOGIC SYMPTOMS 



**Transient neurologic symptoms (TNS) represent a specific complication primarily associated with spinal anesthesia, especially using lidocaine.**

### 8.5.1. CLINICAL CHARACTERISTICS

* Definition: Pain or sensory abnormalities in the lower back, buttocks, or lower extremities following spinal anesthesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Timing:
  * Typically begins within 24 hours after resolution of spinal anesthesia
  * Usually resolves within 7 days
* Symptoms:
  * Bilateral or unilateral pain (often radiating to buttocks and posterior thighs)
  * Dysesthesia or paresthesia
  * No motor weakness or sphincter dysfunction
  * No permanent neurologic sequelae reported [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Severity ranges from mild discomfort to severe pain requiring analgesics

### 8.5.2. EPIDEMIOLOGY AND RISK FACTORS

* Incidence varies by agent [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Lidocaine: 4-36% (highest incidence)
  * Mepivacaine: 23%
  * Procaine: 6%
  * Bupivacaine, levobupivacaine, prilocaine, ropivacaine: approximately 1% (lowest)
* Risk factors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Agent-related:
	* Use of lidocaine (strongest risk factor)
	* Mepivacaine (intermediate risk)
  * Position-related:
	* Lithotomy position (30-36% incidence) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Knee arthroscopy (18-22%)
	* Supine position (4-8%)
  * Other factors:
	* Ambulatory surgery
	* Early mobilization
	* History of back pain
	* Needle type/size (controversial)
* Not associated with:
  * Baricity of solution
  * Dose of local anesthetic [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Concentration of lidocaine (0.5% vs. 5%) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Addition of epinephrine

### 8.5.3. ETIOLOGY

* Exact mechanism remains unclear [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Proposed mechanisms:
  * Neural toxicity (controversial - lack of dose-concentration relationship argues against)
  * Muscle spasm
  * Stretching of musculoligamentous structures
  * Sciatic nerve irritation (especially in lithotomy position)
  * Myofascial pain syndrome
* Evidence against pure neurotoxic etiology:
  * Normal electrophysiologic testing in patients with TNS
  * Effective treatment with NSAIDs and trigger point injections
  * No correlation with concentration or dose of lidocaine
  * No permanent neurologic sequelae

### 8.5.4. PREVENTION

* Agent selection:
  * Consider alternatives to lidocaine for spinal anesthesia, especially for high-risk cases [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Bupivacaine, ropivacaine, and levobupivacaine associated with lowest incidence
* Patient positioning:
  * When possible, avoid lithotomy position with lidocaine spinal
  * Careful positioning and padding
* Patient selection:
  * Inform patients with previous TNS about recurrence risk
  * Consider regional techniques other than spinal for patients with history of TNS

### 8.5.5. MANAGEMENT

* Reassurance about benign, self-limiting nature
* Symptomatic treatment:
  * Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (first-line)
  * Muscle relaxants if indicated
  * Trigger point injections for persistent pain
  * Physical therapy modalities
* Documentation and follow-up
* Consider alternative anesthetic techniques for future procedures

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** When spinal anesthesia is required for procedures in the lithotomy position, bupivacaine is a better choice than lidocaine due to the significantly lower risk of TNS (1% vs. 30-36%) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Transient neurologic symptoms are a common complication after spinal anesthesia, particularly with lidocaine (4-36% incidence) and especially in the lithotomy position. While the exact etiology remains debated, TNS appears to be a benign, self-limiting condition without permanent sequelae. Prevention focuses on agent selection (alternatives to lidocaine when appropriate) and careful positioning, while management is primarily symptomatic with NSAIDs and reassurance.

# 9. SPECIFIC LOCAL ANESTHETIC AGENTS 












## 9.2. LIDOCAINE 



**Lidocaine is a versatile amide local anesthetic with intermediate duration of action and multiple clinical applications.**

### 9.2.1. BASIC PHARMACOLOGY

* Chemical class: Aminoamide local anesthetic
* Structure: Contains an aromatic ring connected to a tertiary amine via an amide linkage [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Physicochemical properties:
  * pKa: 7.9 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Protein binding: 70% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Lipid solubility: Moderate (150 relative to prilocaine) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Onset: Rapid due to favorable pKa
  * Duration: Intermediate

### 9.2.2. PHARMACOKINETICS

* Metabolism: Hepatic via CYP3A4 and CYP1A2 (90%) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Active metabolite: MEGX (monoethylglycinexylide)
  * Further metabolized to 4-hydroxy-2,6-xylidine (main metabolite) excreted in urine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Some metabolites have anti-arrhythmic properties while others may potentiate seizures [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Volume of distribution: 1.3 L/kg [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Clearance: 0.85 L/kg/hr [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Elimination half-life: 1.6 hours [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Toxic plasma concentration: 5 μg/mL [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Clearance reduced in presence of hepatic or cardiac failure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 9.2.3. CLINICAL USE

* Formulations:
  * Solution for injection: 0.5%, 1%, 2% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * With epinephrine: 1:100,000 - 1:400,000 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Topical: 2-10% (spray, gel, ointment) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * IV solution: 1%, 2%
  * EMLA cream: 2.5% (with prilocaine 2.5%) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Maximum safe doses:
  * Without epinephrine: 3-5 mg/kg [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * With epinephrine: 7 mg/kg [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Clinical applications:
  * Infiltration anesthesia
  * Peripheral nerve blocks
  * IV regional anesthesia (Bier block)
  * Epidural anesthesia
  * Spinal anesthesia (less common due to TNS risk) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Topical anesthesia of mucous membranes
  * IV administration:
	* Antiarrhythmic (Class IB) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Reduction of MAC
	* Perioperative analgesia
	* ICP control during intubation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 9.2.4. SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS

* Addition of epinephrine:
  * Extends duration by 50-100% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Decreases systemic absorption by 20-30% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Allows higher maximum dose
* CNS effects:
  * Readily crosses blood-brain barrier
  * Seizure threshold: 10-15 μg/mL plasma concentration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * IV bolus 1.5 mg/kg can decrease cerebral blood flow and attenuate ICP rise during intubation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * IV infusions can reduce MAC of volatile anesthetics by up to 40% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Neurological considerations:
  * Associated with transient neurological symptoms after spinal anesthesia (4-36% incidence) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Linked to cauda equina syndrome with 5% concentration via narrow-lumen catheters [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Cardiovascular effects:
  * Class IB antiarrhythmic properties [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * CNS:CVS toxicity ratio is 7.1 (relatively cardio-stable) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Cardiovascular collapse typically requires plasma concentrations >25 μg/mL [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** For procedures requiring both rapid onset and intermediate duration, lidocaine with epinephrine (1:200,000) provides an excellent profile. The onset remains rapid while duration extends to 120-180 minutes for most peripheral nerve blocks [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

⚠️ **Safety Note:** Lidocaine with epinephrine should not be used for blocks in end-arterial territories (digits, penis, nose, ears) due to risk of ischemia from vasoconstriction [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Lidocaine is a versatile amide local anesthetic with rapid onset and intermediate duration. Its moderate protein binding (70%) and favorable pKa (7.9) contribute to its clinical utility. While widely used for various regional techniques and topical applications, it has largely been abandoned for spinal anesthesia due to transient neurological symptoms. Addition of epinephrine significantly extends its duration and reduces systemic absorption.

## 9.3. BUPIVACAINE AND LEVOBUPIVACAINE 



**Bupivacaine and its S-enantiomer levobupivacaine are potent, long-acting amide local anesthetics with important differences in their safety profiles.**

### 9.3.1. BASIC PHARMACOLOGY

* Chemical class: Aminoamide local anesthetics
* Structure: Contains a butyl group on the piperidine nitrogen, increasing potency and duration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Physicochemical properties:
  * pKa: 8.1 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Protein binding: >95% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Lipid solubility: Very high (1000 relative to prilocaine) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Onset: Moderate (slower than lidocaine due to higher pKa)
  * Duration: Long (6-12 hours peripheral, 2-4 hours spinal)
* Stereoisomerism:
  * Bupivacaine: Racemic mixture of R(+) and S(-) enantiomers [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Levobupivacaine: Pure S(-) enantiomer [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * R(+) enantiomer has greater affinity for cardiac sodium channels and dissociates more slowly [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * S(-) enantiomer has reduced cardiotoxicity but slightly lower potency [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 9.3.2. PHARMACOKINETICS

* Bupivacaine:
  * Metabolism: Hepatic (slowest among amides) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Metabolized by dealkylation to pipecolic acid and pipecolylxylidine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Volume of distribution: 1.02 L/kg [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Clearance: 0.41 L/kg/hr [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Elimination half-life: 3.5 hours [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Toxic plasma concentration: 4 μg/mL [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Levobupivacaine:
  * Volume of distribution: 0.78 L/kg [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Clearance: 0.32 L/kg/hr [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Elimination half-life: 2.6 hours [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 9.3.3. CLINICAL USE

* Formulations:
  * Bupivacaine: 0.125%, 0.25%, 0.5%, 0.75% solutions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Levobupivacaine: 0.125%, 0.25%, 0.5%, 0.75% solutions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Bupivacaine with epinephrine: 1:200,000 - 1:400,000 (minimal effect on duration) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Bupivacaine 0.5% with 80 mg/ml glucose (specific gravity 1.026) for spinal anesthesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Maximum safe doses:
  * Bupivacaine: 2 mg/kg plain, 2.5 mg/kg with epinephrine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Levobupivacaine: 2.5 mg/kg plain, 3 mg/kg with epinephrine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Levobupivacaine: maximum single dose of 150 mg, maximum 24-hour dose of 400 mg [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Clinical applications:
  * Peripheral nerve blocks
  * Epidural anesthesia/analgesia
  * Spinal anesthesia
  * Labor analgesia
  * Infiltration (lower concentrations)
  * Continuous catheter techniques
  * Not suitable for Bier block (toxicity risk) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Bupivacaine remains the mainstay of epidural infusions in labor and post-operatively [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 9.3.4. SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS

* Cardiotoxicity:
  * Bupivacaine has highest cardiotoxicity among LAs [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Binds avidly to cardiac sodium channels with slow dissociation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * CNS:CVS toxicity ratio is 2.0 for bupivacaine (narrow safety margin) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Cardiac arrest from bupivacaine may be extremely resistant to resuscitation
  * Levobupivacaine is approximately 30-40% less cardiotoxic in animal studies [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Combined with limited human volunteer work, levobupivacaine appears to have less myocardial depression and higher dose threshold for excitatory CNS effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Clinical differences between bupivacaine and levobupivacaine:
  * Levobupivacaine has slightly lower potency (requires ~5-10% higher doses)
  * Levobupivacaine produces less motor block at equivalent sensory blockade
  * Duration of action is comparable
  * Both have minimal vasodilatory effects at clinical concentrations
* Addition of epinephrine:
  * Has minimal effect on duration due to high protein binding [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Only modest effect on maximum safe dose
  * May enhance onset slightly
* Obstetric use:
  * Low fetal/maternal ratio due to high protein binding [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Levobupivacaine may offer safety advantages
  * 0.125-0.25% concentrations common for labor epidural analgesia

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** For prolonged postoperative analgesia with minimal motor block, 0.1-0.125% bupivacaine or levobupivacaine via continuous catheter provides excellent sensory blockade while preserving motor function, facilitating early mobilization [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

⚠️ **Safety Note:** The cardiotoxicity of bupivacaine is significantly greater than lidocaine, with resuscitation often difficult. For high-volume blocks or blocks in highly vascular areas, consider using levobupivacaine or ropivacaine for improved safety margin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Bupivacaine and levobupivacaine are highly potent, long-acting amide local anesthetics valuable for procedures requiring prolonged analgesia. Their high protein binding (>95%) contributes to extended duration but limits the value of adding vasoconstrictors. Levobupivacaine (S-enantiomer) offers improved cardiac safety compared to racemic bupivacaine, albeit with slightly lower potency, making it preferable for high-dose applications or patients with cardiac risk factors.

## 9.4. ROPIVACAINE 



**Ropivacaine is a long-acting amide local anesthetic with reduced cardiotoxicity and differential sensory-motor blocking properties.**

### 9.4.1. BASIC PHARMACOLOGY

* Chemical class: Aminoamide local anesthetic
* Structure:
  * Propyl group (instead of butyl in bupivacaine) on the piperidine nitrogen [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Pure S-enantiomer formulation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Physicochemical properties:
  * pKa: 8.1 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Protein binding: 94% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Lipid solubility: Moderate (300 relative to prilocaine, less than bupivacaine) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Onset: Moderate (similar to bupivacaine)
  * Duration: Long (slightly shorter than bupivacaine)

### 9.4.2. PHARMACOKINETICS

* Metabolism: Hepatic (primarily aromatic hydroxylation and N-dealkylation) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Metabolized to 3-hydroxy-ropivacaine, but also to 4-hydroxy-ropivacaine, both with some local anesthetic activity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Volume of distribution: 0.84 L/kg [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Clearance: 0.63 L/kg/hr [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Elimination half-life: 1.9 hours [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Intrinsic vasoconstrictor activity (unlike most other LAs) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 9.4.3. CLINICAL USE

* Formulations: 0.2%, 0.5%, 0.75%, 1.0% solutions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Prepared in three concentrations (2, 7.5 and 10 mg/ml), in two volumes (10 and 100 ml) and as the pure S-enantiomer [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Not prepared in combination with a vasoconstrictor as this does not alter its duration of action or uptake from tissues [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Maximum safe dose: 3-3.5 mg/kg [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Addition of epinephrine has minimal effect on maximum dose due to intrinsic vasoconstrictor activity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Clinical applications:
  * Peripheral nerve blocks
  * Epidural anesthesia/analgesia
  * Spinal anesthesia
  * Labor analgesia (widely used)
  * Continuous catheter techniques
  * Infiltration

### 9.4.4. SPECIAL CHARACTERISTICS

* Cardiotoxicity:
  * Approximately 30-40% less cardiotoxic than bupivacaine in animal models [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * CNS:CVS toxicity ratio is 2.0 (similar to other long-acting agents) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Propyl side chain renders it less cardiodepressive than bupivacaine's butyl side chain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Dissociates more rapidly from cardiac Na+ channels and produces less myocardial depression [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Differential blockade:
  * Produces less motor block than bupivacaine at equivalent volumes/concentrations [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Greater separation between sensory and motor block, especially at lower concentrations
  * Valuable for postoperative analgesia where motor function preservation is desired
  * Lower lipid solubility may result in reduced penetration of large myelinated Aβ motor fibers [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * During continuous infusion, motor fibers will eventually become blocked resulting in similar degrees of block between Aβ and C fibers [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Vasoactive properties:
  * Intrinsic vasoconstrictor activity at clinical concentrations [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Addition of epinephrine has minimal effect on duration or systemic absorption [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Comparison to bupivacaine:
  * Similar onset time and duration of action [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Less potent (approximately 60-70% of bupivacaine's potency) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Less motor block at equipotent doses
  * Greater therapeutic index than racemic bupivacaine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Slightly shorter duration and requires slightly larger doses [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 9.4.5. SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS

* Obstetric use:
  * Widely used for labor epidural analgesia
  * Lower concentrations (0.1-0.2%) provide effective analgesia with minimal motor block
  * Good safety profile for mother and fetus
* Concentration-dependent effects:
  * 0.2%: Primarily sensory block, minimal motor effect
  * 0.5%: Moderate sensory block with partial motor block
  * 0.75%: Dense sensory and motor block for surgical anesthesia
* Increasing clinical role:
  * Often preferred for continuous peripheral nerve blocks
  * Commonly used for ambulatory procedures where early mobilization is important
  * Lower concentrations (0.2%) ideal for postoperative analgesia
  * Theoretically more appropriate than bupivacaine for epidural infusion due to sensory/motor discrimination and greater clearance [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** When transitioning from a surgical block to postoperative analgesia via catheter, consider using 0.5% ropivacaine for initial surgical anesthesia followed by 0.2% for maintenance infusion. This provides adequate surgical conditions followed by effective analgesia with minimal motor block during recovery [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

⚠️ **Safety Note:** While ropivacaine has improved cardiac safety compared to bupivacaine, it is not free from cardiotoxic potential. Cardiotoxicity can still occur with systemic absorption, though resuscitation may be more successful than with bupivacaine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Ropivacaine is a long-acting amide local anesthetic with reduced cardiotoxicity compared to bupivacaine. Its unique properties include intrinsic vasoconstrictor activity and differential sensory-motor blockade, making it particularly valuable for labor analgesia, continuous peripheral nerve blocks, and situations where preservation of motor function is desirable. Though slightly less potent than bupivacaine, its improved safety profile has led to widespread clinical adoption.

## 9.5. PRILOCAINE 



**Prilocaine is an intermediate-acting amide local anesthetic with rapid metabolism and unique considerations regarding methemoglobinemia.**

### 9.5.1. BASIC PHARMACOLOGY

* Chemical class: Aminoamide local anesthetic
* Physicochemical properties:
  * pKa: 7.9 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Protein binding: 50-55% (lowest among amides) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Lipid solubility: Low (50 relative to itself) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Onset: Rapid
  * Duration: Intermediate (shorter than lidocaine) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 9.5.2. PHARMACOKINETICS

* Metabolism:
  * Hepatic and extra-hepatic (lungs, kidneys) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Fastest metabolism among amides: prilocaine > lidocaine > mepivacaine > ropivacaine > bupivacaine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Unique metabolite: o-toluidine, which can produce methemoglobinemia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Volume of distribution: 2.73 L/kg (highest among LAs) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Clearance: 2.03 L/kg/hr (fastest among amides) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Elimination half-life: 1.6 hours [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 9.5.3. CLINICAL USE

* Formulations:
  * Solution for injection: 0.5%, 1%, 2%, 4% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Hyperbaric 2% solution for spinal anesthesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * 3% solution with felypressin (0.03 unit/ml) for dental use [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * EMLA cream: 2.5% (with lidocaine 2.5%) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Maximum safe doses:
  * Without epinephrine: 6 mg/kg [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * With epinephrine or felypressin: 8-9 mg/kg [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Clinical applications:
  * Infiltration anesthesia
  * Peripheral nerve blocks
  * IV regional anesthesia (Bier block) - often drug of choice [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Spinal anesthesia (low incidence of TNS compared to lidocaine) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Topical anesthesia (EMLA)
  * Dental procedures

### 9.5.4. SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS

* Methemoglobinemia:
  * Only local anesthetic metabolized to o-toluidine, which oxidizes hemoglobin to methemoglobin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Classically occurs at doses ~10 mg/kg [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Recent studies show younger, healthier patients may develop medically important methemoglobinemia after lower doses [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Risk factors: large doses, infants, G6PD deficiency, congenital methemoglobin reductase deficiency [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Neonate at special risk as red blood cells are deficient in methemoglobin reductase [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * EMLA cream may precipitate the same reaction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Advantages:
  * Rapid metabolism makes it suitable for day-case surgery [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Lower systemic toxicity risk than many other agents
  * Low incidence of transient neurologic symptoms after spinal anesthesia (1% vs. 4-36% with lidocaine) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Ideal for Bier blocks due to rapid metabolism and low toxicity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Low vasodilatory effect compared to lidocaine
* Less intense motor block than equivalent doses of lidocaine
* 2% hyperbaric spinal preparation used at 40-60 mg (2-3 ml) where motor function returns faster than with bupivacaine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 9.5.5. MANAGEMENT OF METHEMOGLOBINEMIA

* Prevention:
  * Avoid doses >600 mg in adults
  * Use with caution in at-risk populations
  * Monitor for clinical signs (cyanosis unresponsive to oxygen, chocolate-brown blood)
* Treatment:
  * Mild cases (<20% methemoglobin): Observation only
  * Symptomatic cases: Methylene blue 1-2 mg/kg IV over 5 minutes
  * Severe cases: Hyperbaric oxygen therapy, exchange transfusion
  * Avoid methylene blue in G6PD deficiency (may cause hemolysis)
  * May require treatment with ascorbic acid or methylene blue as reducing agents [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** For Bier blocks (IVRA), prilocaine is often the agent of choice due to its rapid metabolism, lower cardiovascular toxicity, and shorter half-life. This allows for safer tourniquet release after shorter inflation times compared to other agents [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

⚠️ **Safety Note:** When using prilocaine, especially at doses >400 mg, monitor for signs of methemoglobinemia: cyanosis unresponsive to oxygen supplementation, chocolate-brown blood, and pulse oximetry reading around 85% that doesn't improve with oxygen. Have methylene blue available for severe cases [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Prilocaine is an intermediate-acting amide local anesthetic with rapid metabolism and lower protein binding than other amides. Its unique metabolism to o-toluidine can cause methemoglobinemia at higher doses (>600 mg). Its favorable safety profile makes it ideal for IV regional anesthesia (Bier blocks) and day-case procedures, while its low incidence of TNS makes it a good alternative to lidocaine for spinal anesthesia.

## 9.6. COCAINE 



**Cocaine is a naturally occurring ester local anesthetic with unique sympathomimetic properties and specific ENT applications.**

### 9.6.1. BASIC PHARMACOLOGY

* Drug interactions:

  * Risk of malignant hyperthermia with volatile anesthetics
* Legal/regulatory status:

  * Requires special storage and documentation
  * Limited access and specific protocols for use

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** For nasal procedures requiring topical anesthesia with vasoconstriction, cocaine is uniquely effective. ⚠️ **Safety Note:** Never administer beta-blockers to treat cocaine-induced tachyarrhythmias or hypertension. Blocking beta receptors leaves alpha-adrenergic stimulation unopposed, potentially worsening hypertension and coronary vasoconstriction. Use benzodiazepines for initial management and consider alpha-blockers if needed [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Cocaine is a unique ester local anesthetic with powerful sympathomimetic properties due to inhibition of norepinephrine reuptake. Its primary clinical use is topical anesthesia for ENT procedures, where its combination of anesthetic and vasoconstrictor properties is advantageous. Its significant cardiovascular effects, potential for abuse, and regulatory restrictions limit its broader application. Toxicity management differs from other local anesthetics, with benzodiazepines as first-line treatment and avoidance of beta-blockers.

### 9.7.4. ARTICAINE

* Chemical class: Unique (amide linkage but contains a thiophene ring instead of benzene ring)

* Clinical use:

* Special considerations:

### 9.7.5. BENZOCAINE

* Chemical class: Aminoester
* Properties:
  * Very low water solubility
  * Limited tissue penetration

* Clinical use:

### 9.7.6. PROCAINE

* Chemical class: Aminoester
* Properties:
  * Very short duration
  * Slow onset
  * Low potency
* Pharmacokinetics:

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** For ophthalmologic procedures, tetracaine 0.5% eye drops provide excellent topical anesthesia with duration of approximately 15-20 minutes. For prolonged procedures, reapplication may be necessary, but excessive use should be avoided as it can cause corneal epithelial damage.

⚠️ **Safety Note:** Benzocaine sprays used for airway topicalization carry a significant risk of methemoglobinemia, especially with repeated application. Consider alternative agents like lidocaine for airway anesthesia, particularly in pediatric and elderly patients.

**Bottom Line:** Beyond the commonly used local anesthetics, several specialized agents have important clinical roles. Chloroprocaine provides ultra-short duration, making it ideal for brief outpatient procedures. Tetracaine offers excellent topical anesthesia for ophthalmic applications. Articaine's bone penetration makes it popular in dentistry. Mepivacaine provides an intermediate option between lidocaine and bupivacaine. Understanding the properties of these less commonly discussed agents expands the anesthesiologist's armamentarium for specific clinical situations.

# 10. FUTURE DEVELOPMENTS 












## 10.2. NOVEL LOCAL ANESTHETICS `[EMERGING]`



**New local anesthetic molecules aim to improve selectivity, duration, and safety profiles.**

### 10.2.1. SITE 1 SODIUM CHANNEL BLOCKERS

* Neosaxitoxin and related compounds [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Origins: Group of potent paralytic neurotoxins
  * Mechanism: Reversibly antagonize voltage-gated sodium channels by binding to the channel α-subunit extracellularly at site 1 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Key differences from traditional LAs:
	* Bind to different site on sodium channels
	* Exhibit greater selectivity for specific channel isoforms [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
	* Act on channel from extracellular side
	* Different safety profile with less cardiovascular toxicity
* Clinical investigations:
  * Subcutaneous neosaxitoxin produces modest duration hypoesthesia when used alone [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * In combination with bupivacaine and epinephrine, extended hypoesthesia almost fivefold compared to bupivacaine alone (median duration 50 vs. 10 hours) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Respiratory effects: Dose-dependent decrease in respiratory and skeletal muscle strength with systemic absorption [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Cardiovascular safety: Better cardiac sodium channel selectivity, with maintained cardiac output and fewer arrhythmias compared to traditional LAs [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Toxicity: Limited evidence of myotoxicity or neurotoxicity with local injections [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 10.2.2. QUATERNARY LIDOCAINE DERIVATIVES

* QX-314 and related compounds:
  * Structure: Permanently charged lidocaine derivatives
  * Traditional limitations: Cannot cross membranes to reach intracellular binding site
  * Novel approach: Pairing with TRPV1/TRPA1 activators to gain cell entry [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 10.2.3. METABOLICALLY ENGINEERED LOCAL ANESTHETICS

* Concept: Chemical modifications to existing LAs to optimize:
  * Metabolism rate
  * Channel selectivity
  * Tissue distribution
  * Reduced systemic effects
* Approaches:
  * Soft drugs (rapidly metabolized once they leave the site of action)
  * Prodrugs (activated at target site)
  * Ultraselective sodium channel isoform blockers

### 10.2.4. POTENTIAL ADVANTAGES OF NOVEL AGENTS

* Greater selectivity for sensory versus motor fibers
* Reduced cardiac toxicity
* Extended duration without need for catheters or delivery systems
* Reduced allergenic potential
* Capability for reversal or antagonism

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The development of sodium channel subtype-selective agents targeting Nav 1.7, 1.8, and 1.9 (predominantly found in pain neurons) could revolutionize pain management by providing analgesia without effects on motor, proprioceptive, or autonomic function [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Novel local anesthetics under development include site 1 sodium channel blockers like neosaxitoxin, which bind to different sites on the channel and show greater isoform selectivity. These agents, particularly when combined with traditional local anesthetics, may provide significantly extended duration of action with improved cardiovascular safety profiles. While promising, these compounds remain investigational and require further research before clinical implementation.

## 10.3. TARGETED DELIVERY SYSTEMS  `[EMERGING]`



**Advanced delivery systems aim to extend local anesthetic duration, improve targeting, and reduce systemic absorption.**

### 10.3.1. LIPOSOMAL FORMULATIONS

* Commercially available preparations of liposomal bupivacaine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Concept: Encapsulation of bupivacaine in multivesicular liposomes
  * Mechanism: Slow, sustained drug release from liposomal carriers
  * Current approvals: Local infiltration (bunionectomy, hemorrhoidectomy), TAP blocks, interscalene brachial plexus blocks [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  
* Clinical evidence:
  * Mixed results in clinical trials [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Many early trials against placebo controls failed to show statistical significance [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Head-to-head trials against bupivacaine with dexamethasone show similar analgesic duration (approximately 26-27 hours) and efficacy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Limited effect on cumulative pain scores and opioid consumption in many studies [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Safety considerations:
  * Animal studies show tissue inflammation and myotoxicity comparable to 0.5% bupivacaine, though inflammation persisted longer [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * No evidence of neurotoxicity or cardiac toxicity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

### 10.3.2. MICROSPHERE AND NANOPARTICLE TECHNOLOGIES

* Polymer-based microspheres:

* Nanoparticle formulations:

### 10.3.3. NOVEL PHYSICAL DELIVERY METHODS

* Iontophoresis:
  * Uses electrical current to drive ionized LA molecules through tissue
  * Enhanced penetration of intact skin and mucous membranes
  * Controlled delivery rate based on current parameters
  * Commercial systems for transdermal lidocaine delivery
* Sonophoresis:
  * Utilizes ultrasound to increase tissue permeability
  * Can enhance topical LA delivery
  * Potential for targeted delivery to deeper structures
* Magnetofection:
  * Magnetic nanoparticles carrying LA
  * External magnetic fields direct delivery to target tissues
  * Experimental stage with proof-of-concept studies

### 10.3.4. TARGETED RELEASE SYSTEMS

* Trigger-based release:
  * pH-sensitive liposomes that release contents in acidic environments (e.g., inflammation)
  * Thermosensitive systems that release LA at specific temperatures
  * Enzyme-triggered release mechanisms
* Site-specific targeting:
  * Receptor-mediated targeting using ligands
  * Antibody-conjugated delivery systems
  * Molecules designed to preferentially accumulate in neural tissue

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** While liposomal bupivacaine has been marketed as a breakthrough for extended analgesia, the current evidence suggests similar efficacy to much less expensive alternatives like bupivacaine with dexamethasone. Cost-benefit analysis should guide clinical decision-making until more compelling evidence emerges [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

⚠️ **Safety Note:** Novel delivery systems may have unexpected tissue effects or toxicity profiles different from the free drug. Careful monitoring and long-term follow-up are essential as these technologies enter clinical practice [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Advanced local anesthetic delivery systems aim to extend duration, improve targeting, and reduce systemic toxicity. Currently available liposomal bupivacaine provides extended release but shows similar clinical efficacy to traditional formulations with adjuvants like dexamethasone. Future developments include polymer microspheres, nanoparticles, and trigger-based release systems that may offer truly prolonged analgesia while maintaining safety and efficacy.

## 10.4. SELECTIVE SENSORY BLOCKADE `[EMERGING]`



**Novel approaches to achieve selective sensory blockade without affecting motor function represent an important direction in local anesthetic development.**

### 10.4.1. TRPV1/TRPA1-FACILITATED ENTRY SYSTEMS

* Mechanism and components [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * TRPV1 and TRPA1 are large-pore cation channels found primarily on nociceptor neurons [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Response to heat, capsaicin, or noxious stimuli allows passage of large molecules [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * QX-314 (permanently charged lidocaine derivative) cannot normally cross cell membranes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Coadministration strategy:
	* TRPV1/TRPA1 activator (e.g., capsaicin) opens channels
	* QX-314 enters only cells expressing these channels (primarily nociceptors)
	* Results in selective blockade of sensory neurons with minimal motor or autonomic effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Experimental findings:
  * Coadministration of capsaicin and QX-314 on sciatic nerves produced long-lasting sensory block with minimal motor deficit in animal studies [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Addition of lidocaine further prolongs block duration at expense of initial non-selective motor block [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Differential blockade of approximately 16 hours (prolonged sensory block with shorter motor block) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Promising results in animal models, not yet validated in clinical studies

### 10.4.2. ISOFORM-SELECTIVE SODIUM CHANNEL BLOCKERS

* Targeting specific sodium channel subtypes:
  * NaV 1.7, 1.8, and 1.9 found predominantly in nociceptor neurons [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * NaV 1.4 (skeletal muscle) and NaV 1.5 (cardiac) can be avoided
  * Potential for pain relief without motor or cardiac effects
  * Genetic evidence: humans with NaV 1.7 mutations have altered pain perception [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Development approaches:
  * Small molecule inhibitors with isoform selectivity
  * Peptide toxins with natural selectivity for specific channel subtypes
  * Antibody-based therapies targeting specific channel regions
  * Gene therapy to modulate channel expression

### 10.4.3. MOLECULARLY TARGETED SYSTEMS

* Neurotoxin-based delivery:
  * Botulinum toxin conjugated with LA molecules
  * Tetanus toxin fragments for retrograde transport
  * Targets neurons through specific uptake mechanisms
* Receptor-targeted approaches:
  * Conjugation of LAs with ligands for receptors expressed on sensory neurons
  * Opioid receptor-targeted delivery systems
  * NGF-receptor (TrkA) targeting

### 10.4.4. POTENTIAL CLINICAL APPLICATIONS

* Postoperative analgesia without motor block (facilitates mobilization)
* Chronic pain management (preserve motor function)
* Labor analgesia (pain relief without affecting motor function for delivery)
* Procedural analgesia where motor function assessment is needed
* Diagnostic nerve blocks with sensory-motor differentiation

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The holy grail of local anesthetics would be an agent that selectively blocks nociceptive fibers without affecting motor, proprioceptive, or autonomic function - allowing pain relief while preserving normal function. TRPV1-facilitated entry systems represent a promising approach to this goal [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Selective sensory blockade represents an important frontier in local anesthetic development. The TRPV1/TRPA1-facilitated entry system using QX-314 with capsaicin shows promising results in animal studies, producing prolonged sensory blockade with minimal motor effects. Other approaches include development of sodium channel isoform-selective drugs targeting pain-specific subtypes, and targeted delivery systems. While still experimental, these approaches could revolutionize pain management by separating analgesic effects from undesired motor and autonomic effects.
