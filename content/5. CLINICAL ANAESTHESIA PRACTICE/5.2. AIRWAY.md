# 1. THE LARYNX




> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|Part A]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|Part B]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C]]

## 1.1. UPPER AIRWAY STRUCTURES

**The upper airway consists of the pharynx, nose, mouth, larynx, trachea, and mainstem bronchi.**

* The human airway has **two openings**: the nose leading to the nasopharynx and the mouth leading to the oropharynx [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]].
* These two passages are separated by the palate and join posteriorly in the pharynx [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]].
* The nasopharynx and oropharynx are separated by an imaginary plane, while the oropharynx is separated from the laryngopharynx by the epiglottis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]].

### 1.1.1. LARYNGEAL ANATOMY

* The larynx is a **cartilaginous skeleton** held together by ligaments and muscles [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]].
* It is composed of **9 cartilages**: thyroid, cricoid, epiglottic, and in pairs: arytenoid, corniculate, and cuneiform [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]].
* The thyroid cartilage shields the conus elasticus (vocal cords) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]].
* The trachea begins beneath the cricoid cartilage and extends to the carina, where it divides into right and left mainstem bronchi [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]].
* Anteriorly, the trachea consists of cartilaginous rings; posteriorly, it is membranous [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]].

### 1.1.2. CRICOTHYROID MEMBRANE

* The cricothyroid membrane (CTM) extends from the cricoid cartilage to the thyroid cartilage [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]].
* In an adult, the membrane is typically **8-12 mm in width and 10.4-13.7 mm in height** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].
* It is composed of yellow elastic tissue beneath the skin and a fascial layer [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].
* The CTM can be identified 1-1.5 fingerbreadths below the laryngeal prominence (thyroid notch) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].
* The membrane has a central portion known as the conus elasticus and two lateral thinner portions [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].
* Directly beneath the membrane is the laryngeal mucosa [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].

⚠️ **Due to anatomic variability in the course of veins and arteries and the membrane's proximity to the vocal cords, any incisions or needle punctures to the CTM should be made in its inferior third and directed posteriorly** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].

## 1.2. AIRWAY INNERVATION

* **Mucous membrane of nose**:
  * CNV1 (ophthalmic division of trigeminal nerve - anterior ethmoidal nerve) anteriorly [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
  * CNV2 (maxillary division - sphenopalatine nerve) posteriorly [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
  * CN1 (olfactory nerve) innervates the nasal mucosa for smell [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
* **Tongue innervation**:
  * The lingual nerve (branch of CNV3) provides general sensation to the anterior 2/3 of tongue [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
  * CN9 (glossopharyngeal) provides general sensation to the posterior 1/3 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
  * CN7 (facial) and CN9 provide taste to the anterior 2/3 and posterior 1/3 respectively [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
* **Pharynx and Larynx**:
  * The glossopharyngeal nerve (CN9) innervates the roof of the pharynx, tonsils, and undersurface of the soft palate [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
  * CN10 (vagus) provides sensation to the airway below the epiglottis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

### 1.2.1. SUPERIOR LARYNGEAL NERVE

* The superior laryngeal branch of the vagus divides into:
  * An **external (motor) nerve** - innervates the cricothyroid muscle [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
	* Unilateral denervation causes minimal effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
	* Bilateral palsy may result in hoarseness or easy tiring of the voice, but airway control is not jeopardized [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
  * An **internal (sensory) laryngeal nerve** - provides sensory supply to the larynx between the epiglottis and the vocal cords [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

### 1.2.2. RECURRENT LARYNGEAL NERVE

* Branch from CN10 that innervates the larynx below the vocal cords and trachea [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
* Innervates all muscles of the larynx **except the cricothyroid** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
* The posterior cricoarytenoid muscles abduct the vocal cords, while the lateral cricoarytenoid muscles are the principal adductors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
* Unilateral denervation of RLN leads to hoarseness [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
* Bilateral denervation:
  * If acute - causes stridor and respiratory distress [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
  * If chronic - aphonia (the intact superior laryngeal nerve causes remaining unopposed tension of the cricothyroid muscles) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
  * Airway problems are less frequent in chronic bilateral RLN loss due to compensatory mechanisms (e.g., atrophy of laryngeal musculature) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
* **Complete vagus nerve denervation effects**:
  * Unilateral - hoarseness [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
  * Bilateral - aphonia; produces flaccid, midpositioned vocal cords similar to those after succinylcholine administration. Although phonation is severely impaired, airway control is rarely a problem [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

## 1.3. VASCULAR SUPPLY

* The larynx is supplied by branches of the thyroid artery [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
* The cricothyroid artery arises from the superior thyroid artery (first branch of the external carotid artery) and crosses the upper cricothyroid membrane [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
* The superior thyroid artery is found along the lateral edge of the CTM [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Understanding airway anatomy is fundamental for successful airway management. The complex relationships between cartilages, nerves, and vasculature must be appreciated to safely perform techniques from basic mask ventilation to advanced cricothyroidotomy.

# 2. THE LARYNX





> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|Part A]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|Part B]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C]]

## 2.1. LARYNGEAL STRUCTURE

* The larynx functions primarily as a valve to protect the lower respiratory tract from food and liquids [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
* The larynx is located at the level of C3-C6 vertebrae [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
* Components at specific vertebral levels:
  * Hyoid bone - C3 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
  * Thyroid cartilage - C4 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
  * Cricoid cartilage - C6 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

### 2.1.1. CARTILAGES

* **Thyroid**:
  * The largest of the cartilages, with two large shield-shaped **lamina** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
  * The thyroid notch is used as a landmark for identifying the cricothyroid membrane during emergency front of neck access [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
* **Cricoid**:
  * 'Signet' ring shape at the level of C6 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
  * Only complete ring of cartilage in the airway [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
* **Arytenoid**:
  * Triangular pyramidal cartilages that sit on the superolateral aspect of the lamina of the cricoid [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
  * Has a lateral muscular process, forming insertions for posterior and lateral **cricoarytenoid muscles** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
  * Has an anterior **vocal** process for the attachment of the vocal ligament [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
* **Epiglottis**:
  * Leaf-shaped cartilage attached to the posterior aspect of the thyroid cartilage via the **thyroepiglottic ligament** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
  * Functions to cover the laryngeal inlet during swallowing [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
  * It has a central **glossoepiglottic** fold, and lateral **valleculae**, small pouches where foreign bodies may become impacted [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
  * The anterior aspect of the epiglottis joins the back of the hyoid bone via the **hyoepiglottic ligament** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
* **Corniculate**:
  * Small nodule lying at the tip of the arytenoid cartilage [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
* **Cuneiform**:
  * A small segment of cartilage found within the aryepiglottic fold [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

### 2.1.2. LIGAMENTS AND MEMBRANES

* **Extrinsic**:
  * Thyrohyoid membrane and ligament
	* Pierced by superior laryngeal nerve and blood vessels [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
  * Cricotracheal ligament [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
  * Cricothyroid ligament [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
  * Hyoepiglottic ligament [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
* **Intrinsic**:
  * Capsules of joints between arytenoid and cricoid cartilages [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
* **Cords**:
  * Upper vestibular fold (false cords) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
  * Lower vocal fold (true cords) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

## 2.2. MUSCLES OF THE LARYNX

### 2.2.1. INTRINSIC MUSCLES

* **Cricothyroid**:
  * Tenses vocal cords, providing changes in pitch [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
  * Only intrinsic muscle innervated by **superior** laryngeal nerve [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
* **Vocalis**:
  * Relaxes vocal cords, lowering pitch [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
* **Thyroarytenoid**:
  * Relaxes vocal cords [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
* **Thyroepiglottis**:
  * Opens laryngeal inlet [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
* **Aryepiglottis**:
  * Closes laryngeal inlet [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
* **Lateral and transverse cricoarytenoid**:
  * **Adducts** vocal cords [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
* **Posterior cricoarytenoid**:
  * **Abducts** vocal cords [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
  * At risk of paralysis in **partial bilateral** recurrent laryngeal nerve damage [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

### 2.2.2. FUNCTIONAL SUMMARY

| Function | Muscle |
| --- | --- |
| Cord adductor | Lateral and transverse cricoarytenoids |
| Cord abductor | Posterior cricoarytenoids |
| Cord tension adjuster | Cricothyroid, Thyroarytenoid, Vocalis |
| Inlet opening/closure | Aryepiglottis, Thyroepiglottis |

### 2.2.3. EXTRINSIC MUSCLES

* **Inferior constrictor**:
  * Constricts glottic opening [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
* **Thyrohyoid**:
  * Elevates larynx [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]
* **Sternothyroid**:
  * Depresses larynx [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

## 2.3. DEVELOPMENTAL CONSIDERATIONS

* The infant/pediatric larynx differs from the adult:
  * Larynx positioned more cephalad (C3-C5 versus C4-C6 in adult) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * More horizontal, funnel-shaped [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Narrowest at the cricoid ring (rather than vocal cords) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Epiglottis is longer, floppier, and more deeply 'V' shaped to facilitate nasal breathing while feeding [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Infant vocal cords slant in a more anterior/caudal direction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** The larynx is a complex structure with critical functions in airway protection, phonation, and respiration. Understanding its detailed anatomy is essential for effective airway management, particularly in emergency situations requiring surgical airway access.

### 3.1.1. EVIDENCE-BASED AIRWAY EVALUATION [ASA 2022]

According to the 2022 ASA Practice Guidelines for Management of the Difficult Airway, comprehensive evaluation should include:

**Airway Risk Assessment:**
* **Patient demographic and personal characteristics**: age, sex, body mass index, weight, and height
* **Clinical characteristics**: history of difficult intubation, distorted airway anatomy, snoring, obstructive sleep apnea, diabetes mellitus
* **Diagnostic test findings**: radiography, computed tomography, patient interviews, and questionnaires
* **Multiple demographic and clinical characteristics** should be assessed to determine potential for difficult airway or aspiration

**Physical Examination Components:**
* **Facial and jaw features**: mouth opening, ability to prognath, head and neck mobility, prominent upper incisors, presence of beard, upper lip bite test
* **Anatomical measurements**: Mallampati and modified Mallampati scores, thyromental distance, sternomental distance, interincisor distance, neck circumference, hyomental distance
* **Advanced evaluation tools**: bedside endoscopy, virtual laryngoscopy/bronchoscopy, three-dimensional printing when indicated

⚠️ **Key Finding**: No single characteristic was consistently more predictive than another across studies. Multivariate measures showed too much diversity among studies to determine common predictors.

**ASA 2022 Definitions of Difficult Airway:**

**Difficult Facemask Ventilation**: Not possible to provide adequate ventilation (confirmed by end-tidal carbon dioxide detection) because of inadequate mask seal, excessive gas leak, or excessive resistance to gas ingress or egress.

**Difficult Laryngoscopy**: Not possible to visualize any portion of the vocal cords after multiple attempts at laryngoscopy.

**Difficult Supraglottic Airway Ventilation**: Not possible to provide adequate ventilation because of difficult supraglottic airway placement, requiring multiple attempts, inadequate seal, excessive gas leak, or excessive resistance to gas flow.

**Difficult or Failed Tracheal Intubation**: Tracheal intubation requires multiple attempts or fails after multiple attempts.

**Difficult or Failed Tracheal Extubation**: Loss of airway patency and adequate ventilation after removal of tracheal tube or supraglottic airway from a patient with known or suspected difficult airway.

**Difficult or Failed Invasive Airway**: Anatomic features or abnormalities reducing or preventing successful placement of an airway into the trachea through the front of the neck.

**Inadequate Ventilation Indicators**: absent or inadequate exhaled carbon dioxide, absent or inadequate chest movement, absent or inadequate breath sounds, auscultatory signs of severe obstruction, cyanosis, gastric air entry or dilatation, decreasing or inadequate oxygen saturation, absent or inadequate exhaled gas flow, anatomic lung abnormalities on lung ultrasound, and hemodynamic changes associated with hypoxemia or hypercarbia.

* Relative alignment of the oral and pharyngeal axes is achieved by having the patient in the "sniffing" position [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

* Patients with morbid obesity should be positioned on a 30° upward ramp, as the functional residual capacity (FRC) of obese patients deteriorates in the supine position, leading to more rapid deoxygenation should ventilation be impaired [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

## 3.4. PREOXYGENATION

* When possible, preoxygenate all patients with face mask O₂ [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* This allows the functional residual capacity (patient's O₂ reserve) to be purged of nitrogen [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Up to 90% of the normal FRC of 2 L following preoxygenation is filled with oxygen. Considering the normal oxygen demand of 200 to 250 mL/min, the preoxygenated patient may have a 5 to 8 min oxygen reserve [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Increasing duration of apnea without desaturation improves safety if ventilation following anesthetic induction is delayed [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Conditions that increase O₂ demand (sepsis, pregnancy) and decrease FRC (morbid obesity, pregnancy, ascites) reduce the apneic period [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

### 3.4.1. ASA 2022 SUPPLEMENTAL OXYGEN RECOMMENDATIONS

**Continuous Oxygen Administration:**
* **Administer supplemental oxygen before initiating management of the difficult airway** and continue throughout the process
* **Deliver supplemental oxygen whenever feasible throughout difficult airway management**, including extubation
* Use **low- or high-flow nasal cannula** during attempts
* Maintain **head elevated position** throughout procedure when possible
* Consider **noninvasive ventilation during preoxygenation** for high-risk patients

**Apneic Oxygenation Techniques:**
* **High-flow nasal cannula oxygen** (e.g., Optiflow, "THRIVE") for continuous oxygen delivery
* **Nasal oxygen during all airway management efforts**
* **Transnasal humidified rapid insufflation ventilatory exchange** when available

💡 Assuming a patent air passage is present, oxygen insufflated into the pharynx may increase the duration of apnea tolerated by the patient [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].

* Because oxygen enters the blood from the FRC at a rate faster than CO₂ leaves the blood, a negative pressure is generated in the alveolus, drawing oxygen into the lung (apneic oxygenation) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* With flow of 100% oxygen and a patent airway, arterial saturation can be maintained for a longer period despite no ventilation, permitting multiple airway interventions should a difficult airway be encountered [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

**Enhanced Preoxygenation Methods:**
* **Standard**: 3-5 minutes of tidal volume breathing with FiO₂ = 1.0
* **Rapid**: 4-12 deep breaths at forced vital capacity within 1 minute
* **Target**: End-tidal oxygen concentration ≥ 0.90
* **Special populations**: Noninvasive ventilation with pressure support and PEEP for obese or critically ill patients

## 3.5. BAG AND MASK VENTILATION

* Bag and mask ventilation (BMV) is the first step in airway management in most situations, with the exception of patients undergoing rapid sequence intubation or elective awake intubation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Rapid sequence inductions avoid BMV to minimize stomach inflation and reduce the potential for aspiration of gastric contents in nonfasted patients and those with delayed gastric emptying [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* In emergency situations, BMV precedes attempts at intubation to oxygenate the patient, with the understanding that there is an implicit risk of aspiration [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* If ventilation is ineffective (no sign of chest rising, no end-tidal CO₂ detected, no condensation in the clear mask), oral or nasal airways can be placed to relieve airway obstruction secondary to lax upper airway muscle tone or redundant pharyngeal tissues [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

### 3.5.1. RISK FACTORS FOR DIFFICULT MASK VENTILATION

* Morbid obesity [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Beards [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Edentulous patients (cannot form an adequate mask fit) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Older age (>55y) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* History of snoring [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

💡 Having just two of the above five features indicates a high likelihood of difficult mask ventilation with a sensitivity and specificity of 70-75% [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].

* Additional important predictors include:
  * Mallampati classification III or IV [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Severely limited mandibular protrusion [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Mouth opening (inter-incisor distance) < 3 cm [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Thyromental distance of < 6 cm [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Craniofacial deformities [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Thorough airway assessment, proper positioning, and effective preoxygenation form the foundation of safe airway management. Understanding risk factors for difficult mask ventilation allows for better preparation and potentially lifesaving anticipatory planning.

# 4. ASA 2022 DIFFICULT AIRWAY MANAGEMENT ALGORITHMS




> **Syllabus**: [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|Part B]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C]]

## 4.1. ANTICIPATED DIFFICULT AIRWAY MANAGEMENT [ASA 2022]

### 4.1.1. STRATEGIC PLANNING APPROACH

**Have a preformulated strategy for management of the anticipated difficult airway:**
* Strategy depends on anticipated surgery, patient condition, patient cooperation/consent, age, and clinician skills/preferences
* Identify strategies for:
  1. **Awake intubation**
  2. **Patient who can be adequately ventilated but is difficult to intubate**
  3. **Patient who cannot be ventilated or intubated**
  4. **Difficulty with emergency invasive airway rescue**

### 4.1.2. AWAKE INTUBATION INDICATIONS [ASA 2022]

**Perform awake intubation when appropriate if the patient is suspected to be a difficult intubation AND one or more of the following apply:**
1. **Difficult ventilation** (face mask/supraglottic airway anticipated)
2. **Increased risk of aspiration**
3. **Patient likely incapable of tolerating a brief apneic episode**
4. **Expected difficulty with emergency invasive airway rescue**

**Awake Intubation Techniques Include:**
* Flexible bronchoscope/videoscope
* Videolaryngoscopy
* Direct laryngoscopy
* Combined techniques
* Retrograde wire-aided intubation

### 4.1.3. ANESTHETIZED INTUBATION CONSIDERATIONS

**Proceed with airway management after induction when benefits outweigh risks:**
* **Uncooperative or pediatric patients** may restrict awake intubation options
* **Airway maneuvers** may be attempted to facilitate intubation
* **Determine benefit of noninvasive vs. invasive approach**

## 4.2. UNANTICIPATED DIFFICULT AIRWAY MANAGEMENT [ASA 2022]

### 4.2.1. IMMEDIATE RESPONSE PROTOCOL

**Essential Actions:**
1. **Call for help**
2. **Optimize oxygenation**
3. **Refer to algorithm/cognitive aid when appropriate**

### 4.2.2. DECISION-MAKING FRAMEWORK

**Upon encountering unanticipated difficult airway:**
* **Determine benefit of waking/restoring spontaneous breathing**
* **Determine benefit of noninvasive vs. invasive approach**
* **Identify preferred sequence of noninvasive devices** if noninvasive approach selected

### 4.2.3. ATTEMPT LIMITATION PRINCIPLES [ASA 2022]

**Critical Safety Measures:**
* **Be aware of passage of time, number of attempts, and oxygen saturation**
* **Provide and test mask ventilation after each attempt when feasible**
* **Limit number of attempts** at tracheal intubation or supraglottic airway placement to avoid injury and complications
* **Use combination techniques** if difficulty encountered with individual techniques

💡 **ASA Recommendation**: The intent of limiting attempts is to reduce risk of bleeding, edema, and trauma that may increase difficulty of mask ventilation and/or subsequent attempts to secure definitive airway.

## 4.3. INVASIVE AIRWAY MANAGEMENT [ASA 2022]

### 4.3.1. EMERGENCY INVASIVE AIRWAY INDICATIONS

**"Cannot Intubate, Cannot Ventilate" Situation:**
* **Identify preferred intervention** for invasive approach
* **Ensure invasive airway performed by trained individual** whenever possible
* **Ensure invasive airway performed as rapidly as possible**
* **Identify alternative invasive intervention** if selected approach fails

### 4.3.2. INVASIVE TECHNIQUES AVAILABLE [ASA 2022]

**Emergency Invasive Options:**
* **Surgical cricothyrotomy** (e.g., scalpel-bougie technique)
* **Needle cricothyrotomy** with pressure-regulated ventilation
* **Large-bore cannula cricothyrotomy** (including Seldinger techniques)
* **Surgical tracheostomy**
* **Percutaneous tracheostomy**

**Additional Advanced Options:**
* **Retrograde wire-guided intubation**
* **Rigid bronchoscopy** when available
* **ECMO** when appropriate and available

## 4.4. CONFIRMATION AND MONITORING [ASA 2022]

### 4.4.1. INTUBATION CONFIRMATION

**Primary Method:**
* **Confirm tracheal intubation using capnography or end-tidal CO₂ monitoring**

**When Uncertain About Tube Location:**
* **Determine whether to remove tube and attempt ventilation** OR
* **Use additional confirmation techniques** (visualization, flexible bronchoscopy, ultrasonography, radiography)

### 4.4.2. CONTINUOUS MONITORING REQUIREMENTS

**ASA Standards for Basic Anesthesia Monitoring:**
* Apply **immediately before, during, and after airway management**
* **Pulse oximetry** for continuous oxygen saturation monitoring
* **Capnography** for ventilation confirmation
* **Blood pressure and ECG monitoring**

**Bottom Line:** The ASA 2022 Guidelines emphasize systematic preparation, algorithm-based decision making, attempt limitation, and early recognition of the need for invasive airway management to optimize patient safety during difficult airway encounters.

## 5.1. TRACHEAL TUBES

**Tracheal tubes provide a means of securing the patient's airway, allowing spontaneous and controlled ventilation.** These disposable plastic tubes are made of polyvinyl chloride (PVC), which could be clear, ivory or siliconized [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] .

### 5.1.1. FEATURES AND TYPES

Tracheal tubes have several key features:

* Standard 15mm universal connector for breathing circuit [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Transparent PVC Tubing with a radio-opaque line running along its length which enables their position to be determined on chest X-rays [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

* Glottis Line [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Murphy Eye [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Bevelled tip [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

### 5.1.2. SIZE AND LENGTH

* The 'size' of a tracheal tube refers to its internal diameter (ID), which is marked on the outside of the tube in millimetres [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Narrower tubes increase the resistance to gas flow, therefore the largest possible ID should be used [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* This is especially important during spontaneous ventilation in which the patient's own respiratory effort must overcome the tube's resistance [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* A size 4-mm tracheal tube has 16 times more resistance to gas flow than a size 8-mm tube [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Usually, a size 8.5–9-mm ID tube is selected for an average size adult male and a size 7.5–8-mm ID tube for an average size adult female [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Paediatric sizes are determined on the basis of age and weight [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

### 5.1.3. BEVEL AND MURPHY'S EYE

* **The bevel**: Left-facing and oval in most tube designs. A left-facing bevel improves the view of the vocal cords during laryngoscopy and intubation as the tube is inserted from the right-hand side [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

### 5.1.4. CUFF TYPES AND PRESSURE MANAGEMENT

* Tracheal tubes can be either cuffed or uncuffed [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The cuff, when inflated, provides an air-tight seal between the tube and the tracheal wall [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* This air-tight seal protects the patient's airway from aspiration and allows efficient ventilation during intermittent positive pressure ventilation (IPPV) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The cuff is connected to its pilot balloon, which has a self-sealing valve for injecting air [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The pilot balloon also indicates whether the cuff is inflated or not [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* **Adult vs. Pediatric Considerations**:
  * The narrowest point in the adult's airway is the glottis (which is hexagonal). In order to achieve an air-tight seal, cuffed tubes are used in adults [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
  * The narrowest point in a child's airway is the cricoid cartilage. Since this is essentially circular, a correctly sized uncuffed tube will fit well [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
  * Because of the narrow upper airway in children, post-extubation subglottic oedema can be a problem. To minimize the risk, the presence of a small leak around the tube at an airway pressure of 15 cm H₂O is desirable [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

**Cuff types:**

1. **High-pressure/low-volume cuffs**:
   * Can prevent the passing of vomitus, secretions or blood into the lungs [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
   * At the same time, they exert a high pressure on the tracheal wall. If left in position for long periods, they may cause necrosis of the tracheal mucosa [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

   ![Postmortem tracheal specimen showing necrosis from a high-pressure cuffed tube](AIRWAY_pdf_AIRWAY.pdf_p8_img1_cf525b.jpeg)

2. **Low-pressure/high-volume cuffs**:
   * Exert minimal pressure on the tracheal wall as the pressure equilibrates over a wider area [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
   * This allows the cuff to remain inflated for longer periods [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
   * They are less capable of preventing the aspiration of vomitus or secretions due to the possibility of wrinkles forming in the cuff [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

   ![Comparison of low-volume vs. high-volume cuffs](AIRWAY_pdf_AIRWAY.pdf_p11_img1_8ff44a.jpeg)

* The pressure in the cuff should be checked at frequent and regular intervals, maintaining a pressure of 15–20 mmHg (20–30 cm H₂O) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The pressure may increase mainly because of diffusion of nitrous oxide into the cuff. Expansion of the air inside the cuff due to the increase in its temperature from room to body temperature and the diffusion of oxygen from the anaesthetic mixture (about 33%) into the air (21%) in the cuff can also lead to increase in the intracuff pressure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* An increase in pressure of about 10–12 mmHg is expected after 30 minutes of anaesthesia with 66% nitrous oxide [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Some designs use cuff material (Soft Seal, Portex) that allows minimum diffusion of nitrous oxide into the cuff with a pressure increase of 1–2 mmHg only [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The pressure may decrease because of a leak in the cuff or the pilot balloon's valve [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

![Cuff pressure gauge](AIRWAY_pdf_AIRWAY.pdf_p13_img1_cb1aa7.jpeg)

* Recent designs allow suction above the cuff. A designated suction port runs along the wall of the tube to remove secretions above the cuff. This can reduce the incidence of ventilator-associated pneumonia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

![Tracheal tube with suction above the cuff; Portex SACETT](AIRWAY_pdf_AIRWAY.pdf_p14_img1_26f826.jpeg)

### 5.1.5. ROUTE OF INSERTION

* Tubes can be inserted orally or nasally [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The indications for nasal intubation include:
  * Surgery where access via the mouth is necessary, e.g., maxillofacial procedures [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
  * Long-term ventilated patients in intensive care units. Patients tolerate a nasal tube better and cannot bite on the tube. However, long-term nasal intubation may cause sinus infection [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Nasal intubation is usually avoided, if possible, in children up to the age of 8–11 years. Hypertrophy of the adenoids in this age group increases the risk of profuse bleeding if nasal intubation is performed [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Ivory PVC nasotracheal tubes cause less trauma to the nasal mucosa [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

### 5.1.6. CONNECTORS

### 5.1.7. SPECIALIZED TRACHEAL TUBES

💡 A 'Reinforced' or 'Armoured' ETT has a metal wire coil embedded in its wall which maintains the lumen patency even when it is bent. This makes it very useful for certain shared airway cases where the surgeon may need to manipulate the tube to gain access to the surgical site. One thing to watch out for, however, is that if a patient bites down on a reinforced tube, it will deform and remain kinked, potentially obstructing airflow [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].

**Armoured/Reinforced Tracheal Tube**
* Made of plastic or silicone rubber [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The walls contain an embedded spiral of metal wire or tough nylon to prevent kinking and occlusion of the tracheal tube when the head and/or neck is rotated or flexed [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* An introducer stylet is used to aid intubation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Because of the spiral, it is not possible to cut the tube to the desired length. This increases the risk of bronchial intubation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

**Polar and Ring, Adair and Elwyn (RAE) Tracheal Tubes**

The south-facing RAE tube facilitates oral/facial surgery with a south-facing bend in the tube to facilitate surgical access [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]. Depth of tube insertion is largely determined by the pre-formed bend in the tube [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].

* The north-facing polar or RAE tube is a preformed nasal cuffed or uncuffed tracheal tube [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* It is used mainly during anaesthesia for maxillofacial procedures as it does not impede surgical access [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Because of its design and shape, it lies over the nose and the forehead [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* It can be converted to an ordinary nasal tracheal tube by cutting it at the scissors mark just proximal to the pilot tube and reconnecting the 15-mm connector [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The south-facing polar or RAE tube has a preformed shape to fit the mouth without kinking [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The connections to the breathing system are at the level of the chin and not interfering with the surgical access [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Such tubes can be either cuffed or uncuffed [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Because of its preformed shape, there is a higher risk of bronchial intubation than with ordinary tracheal tubes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The cuffed tracheal tube has one Murphy eye, whereas the uncuffed version has two eyes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The tube can be temporarily straightened to insert a suction catheter [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

**Laser-Resistant Tracheal Tubes**
* Used in anaesthesia for laser surgery on the larynx or trachea [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Designed to withstand the effect of carbon dioxide and potassium-titanyl-phosphate (KTP) laser beams, avoiding the risk of fire or damage to the tracheal tube [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* One design has a flexible stainless steel body. Reflected beams from the tube are defocused to reduce the accidental laser strikes to healthy tissues [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Other designs have a laser-resistant metal foil wrapped around the tube for protection [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The cuff is filled with methylene blue–coloured saline. If the laser manages to damage the cuff, the colouring will help identify rupture and the saline will help prevent an airway fire [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Some designs have two cuffs. This ensures a tracheal seal should the upper cuff be damaged by laser [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* An air-filled cuff, hit by the laser beam, may ignite and so it is recommended that the cuffs are filled with saline instead of air [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

**Evoked Potential Tracheal Tubes**
* Used in surgical procedures that have risks of nerve damage, e.g., thyroid surgery [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Bipolar stainless steel contact electrical electrodes are embedded in the tracheal tubes above the cuff where they are in contact with the vocal cords [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* These electrodes are connected to a nerve stimulator. An additional earth electrode is attached to the skin of the patient [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The use of such tubes allows continuous nerve monitoring throughout surgery providing visual and audible warnings [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

**Microlaryngeal Tube**
* Allows better exposure and surgical access to the larynx [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* It has a small diameter (usually a 5-mm ID) with an adult-sized cuff [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Its length is sufficient to allow nasal intubation if required [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The tube is made of ivory PVC to reduce trauma to the nasal mucosa [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

**Oxford Tracheal Tube**
* This anatomically L-shaped tracheal tube is used in anaesthesia for head and neck surgery because it is non-kinking [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The tube can be made of rubber or plastic and can be cuffed or uncuffed [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The bevel is oval and faces posteriorly, and an introducing stylet is supplied to aid the insertion of the tube [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Its thick wall adds to the tube's external diameter, making it wider for a given ID. This is undesirable especially in paediatric anaesthesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The distance from the bevel to the curve of the tube is fixed. If the tube is too long, the problem cannot be corrected by withdrawing the tube and shortening it because this means losing its anatomical fit [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* This tube is not widely used in the current anaesthetic practice [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

**Neural Integrity Monitor (NIM) Electromyogram Tube**
* Used for Head & Neck procedures where the Laryngeal Nerve is at risk of injury [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Color-coded contact band must be positioned between the vocal cords - these detect vocal cord movement if a Laryngeal Nerve is stimulated [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Surgeons use a sterile contact probe (passing 0.5-2.0 mA current) to test anatomical sites in question [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Return electrodes are placed over the patient sternum [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* If the surgeon stimulates a Laryngeal Nerve, the motion of the Vocal Cords is detected by the contact band on the ETT and the electrical signal is detected and displayed on the monitor (with an audible 'click') [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

## 5.2. FACE MASKS AND ANGLE PIECES

* Some designs have clamps for a harness to be attached [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The angle piece has a 90-degree bend with a 22-mm end to fit into a catheter mount or a breathing system [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

### 5.2.2. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* Face masks are made of transparent plastic. Previously, masks made of silicon rubber were used [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The transparent plastic allows the detection of vomitus or secretions. It is also more acceptable to the patient during inhalational induction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Some masks are 'flavoured', e.g., strawberry flavour [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The cuff helps to ensure a snug fit over the face, covering the mouth and nose. It also helps to minimize the mask's pressure on the face [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Cuffs can be either air-filled or made from a soft material [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The design of the interior of the mask determines the size of its contribution to apparatus dead space. The dead space may increase by up to 200 mL in adults [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Paediatric masks are designed to reduce the dead space as much as possible [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

**Problems and safety features:**
* Excessive pressure by the mask may cause injury to the branches of the trigeminal or facial nerves [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Sometimes it is difficult to achieve an air-tight seal over the face. Edentulous patients and those with nasogastric tubes pose particular problems [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Imprecise application of the mask on the face can cause trauma to the eyes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

### 5.2.3. CATHETER MOUNT

**This is the flexible link between the breathing system tubing and the tracheal tube, face mask, supraglottic airway device or tracheostomy tube.** The length of the catheter mount varies from 45 to 170 mm [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] .

**Components:**
* It has a corrugated disposable plastic tubing. Some catheter mounts have a concertina design, allowing their length to be adjusted [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The distal end is connected to either a 15-mm standard tracheal tube connector, usually in the shape of an angle piece, or a 22-mm mask fitting [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The proximal end has a 22-mm connector for attachment to the breathing system [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Some designs have a condenser humidifier built into them [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* A gas sampling port is found in some designs [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

**Mechanism of action:**
* The mount minimizes the transmission of accidental movements of the breathing system to the tracheal tube. Repeated movements of the tracheal tube can cause injury to the tracheal mucosa [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Some designs allow for suction or the introduction of a fibreoptic bronchoscope. This is done via a special port [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

**Problems and safety features:**
* The catheter mount contributes to the apparatus dead space. This is of particular importance in paediatric anaesthesia. The concertina design allows adjustment of the dead space from 25 to 60 mL [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Foreign bodies can lodge inside the catheter mount causing an unnoticed blockage of the breathing system. To minimize this risk, the catheter mount should remain wrapped in its sterile packaging until needed [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

## 5.3. OXYGEN DELIVERY DEVICES

**A variety of delivery devices are used that differ in their ability to deliver a set fractional inspired oxygen concentration (FiO₂).** These devices can be divided into variable and fixed performance devices. The former deliver a fluctuating FiO₂ whereas the latter deliver a more constant and predictable FiO₂ [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] .

| Variable performance devices | Fixed performance devices |
|------------------------------|---------------------------|
| Hudson face masks and partial rebreathing masks | Venturi-operated devices |
| Nasal cannulae (prongs or spectacles) | Anaesthetic breathing systems with a suitable large reservoir |
| Nasal catheters | |

### 5.3.1. VARIABLE PERFORMANCE MASKS

* These masks are used to deliver oxygen-enriched air to the patient [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* They are also called low-flow delivery devices [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* They are widely used in the hospital because of greater patient comfort, low cost, simplicity and the ability to manipulate the FiO₂ without changing the appliance [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Their performance varies between patients and from breath to breath within the same patient [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* These systems have a limited reservoir capacity, so in order to function appropriately, the patient must inhale some ambient air to meet the inspiratory demands [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The FiO₂ is determined by the oxygen flow rate, the size of the oxygen reservoir and the respiratory pattern [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

**Components:**
* The plastic body of the mask has holes on both sides [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* A port is connected to an oxygen supply [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Elastic band(s) fix(es) the mask to the patient's face [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

![Adult and Paediatric variable performance face masks](AIRWAY_pdf_AIRWAY.pdf_p97_img1_eb7e70.jpeg)

**Mechanism of action:**
* Ambient air is entrained through the holes on both sides of the mask. The holes also allow exhaled gases to be vented out [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* During the expiratory pause, the fresh oxygen supplied helps in venting out the exhaled gases through the side holes. The body of the mask (acting as a reservoir) is filled with a fresh oxygen supply and is available for the start of the next inspiration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

**Factors affecting delivered FiO₂:**
* Oxygen supply flow rate [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Pattern of ventilation: If there is a pause between expiration and inspiration, the mask fills with oxygen and a high concentration is available at the start of inspiration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Patient's inspiratory flow rate: During inspiration, oxygen is diluted by the air drawn in through the holes when the inspiratory flow rate exceeds the flow of oxygen supply [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* During normal tidal ventilation, the peak inspiratory flow rate is 20–30 L/min, which is higher than the oxygen supplied to the patient and the oxygen that is contained in the body of the mask, so some ambient air is inhaled to meet the demands, thus diluting the fresh oxygen supply [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The peak inspiratory flow rate increases further during deep inspiration and during hyperventilation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* How tight the mask fits on the face [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

**Additional considerations:**
* If there is no expiratory pause, alveolar gases may be rebreathed from the mask at the start of inspiration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The rebreathing of CO₂ from the body of the mask (apparatus dead space of about 100 mL) is usually of little clinical significance in adults but may be a problem in some patients who are not able to compensate by increasing their alveolar ventilation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* CO₂ elimination can be improved by increasing the fresh oxygen flow and is inversely related to the minute ventilation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The rebreathing is also increased when the mask body is large and when the resistance to flow from the side holes is high (when the mask is a good fit) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The patients may experience a sense of warmth and humidity, indicating significant rebreathing [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* A typical example of 4 L/min of oxygen flow delivers an FiO₂ of about 0.35–0.4, providing there is a normal respiratory pattern [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

**Partial rebreathing masks:**
* Adding a 600–800-mL bag to the mask will act as an extra reservoir. Such masks are known as 'partial rebreathing masks' or 'nonrebreather masks' [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* A one-way valve is fitted between mask and reservoir to prevent rebreathing to ensure a 100% O₂ in the reservoir [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The inspired oxygen is derived from the continuous fresh oxygen supply, oxygen present in the reservoir and the entrained ambient air [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Higher variable FiO₂ (0.6–0.8) can be achieved with such masks [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

![A variable performance mask with a reservoir bag](AIRWAY_pdf_AIRWAY.pdf_p99_img1_222caa.jpeg)

* Some designs have an extra port attached to the body of the mask, allowing it to be connected to a side-stream CO₂ monitor. This allows it to sample the exhaled CO₂ so monitoring the patient's respiration, e.g., during sedation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

![A variable performance mask with an exhaled CO₂ monitoring port](AIRWAY_pdf_AIRWAY.pdf_p101_img1_9c5a64.jpeg)

* Similar masks can be used in patients with tracheostomy. As with the face mask, similar factors will affect its performance. Care must be taken to humidify the inspired dry oxygen as the gases delivered bypass the nose and its humidification [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

![Variable performance tracheostomy mask](AIRWAY_pdf_AIRWAY.pdf_p102_img1_3494b3.jpeg)

⚠️ **These devices are used only when delivering a fixed oxygen concentration is not critical. Patients whose ventilation is dependent on a hypoxic drive must not receive oxygen from a variable performance mask** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] .

### 5.3.2. NASAL CANNULAE AND CATHETERS

**Nasal cannulae** are ideal for patients on long-term oxygen therapy. A flow rate of 2–4 L/min delivers an FiO₂ of 0.28–0.36, respectively. Higher flow rates are uncomfortable. Humidifying oxygen allows higher flow rates to be used. They are available in various sizes suitable for adult and paediatric patients [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] .

**Components:**
* It has two prongs that protrude about 1 cm into the nose. Different design prongs are available (straight, curved and flared) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* These are held in place by an adjustable head strap [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

![Oxygen nasal cannula](AIRWAY_pdf_AIRWAY.pdf_p103_img1_07b116.jpeg)

**Mechanism of action:**
* There is entrainment of ambient air through the nostrils. The nasopharynx acts as a reservoir [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The FiO₂ achieved is proportional to the flow rate of oxygen, the patient's tidal volume, inspiratory flow and respiratory rate, and the volume of the nasopharynx [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Mouth breathing causes inspiratory air flow. This produces a Venturi effect in the posterior pharynx, entraining oxygen from the nose [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* There is increased patient compliance with nasal cannulae compared with facial oxygen masks. The patient is able to speak, eat and drink [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Some designs have the facility to sample exhaled CO₂ in addition to delivering oxygen. One prong is used to deliver oxygen while the other prong samples the exhaled CO₂ [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* When connected to a side-stream CO₂ monitor, it is possible to monitor the patient's respiration, e.g., during sedation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

![Nasal cannula with exhaled CO₂ sampling](AIRWAY_pdf_AIRWAY.pdf_p106_img1_6148a5.jpeg)

**Problems and safety features:**
* The cannulae and the dry gas flow cause trauma and irritation to the nasal mucosa [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* They are not appropriate in patients with blocked nasal passages [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

**Nasal catheters** comprise a single lumen catheter, which is lodged into the anterior naris (nostril) by a foam collar. Oxygen flows of 2–3 L/min can be used. The catheter can be secured to the patient's face by using tape. It can be useful in patients undergoing certain surgical procedures, such as lacrimal surgery under regional anaesthesia. It should not be used when a nasal mucosal tear is suspected because of the risk of surgical emphysema [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] .

![Oxygen nasal catheter](AIRWAY_pdf_AIRWAY.pdf_p107_img1_3cb0e9.jpeg)

**Open oxygen delivery systems** are designed to offer the maximum comfort to patients while delivering variable FiO₂ concentrations. They fit around the patient's head like headphones, so making minimal physical contact. Such systems are suitable for both nasaland mouth-breathing patients. They may be more suitable for patients on long-term oxygen therapy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] .

![Open oxygen supply system](AIRWAY_pdf_AIRWAY.pdf_p107_img2_995d5b.jpeg)

### 5.3.3. FIXED PERFORMANCE DEVICES

**Venturi Mask**
These masks are fixed performance devices (sometimes called high-air-flow oxygen enrichment, or HAFOE) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] .

**Components:**
* The plastic body of the mask has holes on both sides [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The proximal end of the mask consists of a Venturi device [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The Venturi devices are colour-coded and marked with the recommended oxygen flow rate to provide the desired oxygen concentration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

![Colour coding for Venturi devices in the United Kingdom](AIRWAY_pdf_AIRWAY.pdf_p110_img1_262697.jpeg)

**Mechanism of action:**
* The Venturi mask uses the Bernoulli principle, described in 1778 (in order to maintain a constant flow, a fluid must increase its velocity as it flows through a constriction), in delivering a predetermined and fixed concentration of oxygen to the patient [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The size of the constriction determines the final concentration of oxygen for a given gas flow. This is achieved despite the patient's respiratory pattern by providing a higher gas flow than the peak inspiratory flow rate [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* As the flow of oxygen passes through the constriction, a negative pressure is created. This causes the ambient air to be entrained and mixed with the oxygen flow [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The FiO₂ is dependent on the degree of air entrainment. Less entrainment ensures a higher FiO₂ is delivered. This can be achieved by using smaller entrainment apertures or bigger 'windows' to entrain ambient air [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The smaller the orifice is, the greater the negative pressure generated, so the more ambient air entrained, the lower the FiO₂ [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The oxygen concentration can be 0.24, 0.28, 0.3, 0.35, 0.4, 0.5 or 0.6 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

![Mechanism of action of the fixed performance Venturi mask](AIRWAY_pdf_AIRWAY.pdf_p112_img1_e5193d.jpeg)

* Because of the high FGF rate, the exhaled gases are rapidly flushed from the mask via its holes. Therefore there is no rebreathing and no increase in dead space [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* These masks are recommended when a fixed oxygen concentration is desired in patients whose ventilation is dependent on the hypoxic drive [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* For example, a 24% oxygen Venturi mask has an air:oxygen entrainment ratio of 25:1. This means an oxygen flow of 2 L/min delivers a total flow of 50 L/min, well above the peak inspiratory flow rate [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The mask's side holes are used to vent the exhaled gases only (as above) in comparison to the side holes in the variable performance mask in which the side holes are used to entrain inspired air in addition to expel exhaled gases [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The Venturi face masks are designed for both adult and paediatric use [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The Venturi attachments, with a reservoir tubing, can be attached to a tracheal tube or a supraglottic airway device as part of a T-piece breathing system. This arrangement is usually used in postanaesthesia care units to deliver oxygen-enriched air to patients [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

![Adult and paediatric Venturi masks](AIRWAY_pdf_AIRWAY.pdf_p114_img1_a18e86.jpeg)

* A more recent design is the adjustable Venturi device. The FiO₂ can be changed without changing the valve by changing the size of the entrainment window and adjusting the oxygen flow rate [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

![Adjustable Venturi valve](AIRWAY_pdf_AIRWAY.pdf_p116_img1_482616.jpeg)

**Problems and safety features:**
* These masks are recommended when a fixed oxygen concentration is desired in patients whose ventilation is dependent on their hypoxic drive, such as those with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. However, caution should be exercised as it has been shown that the average FiO₂ delivered in such masks is up to 5% above the expected value [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The Venturi mask with its Venturi device and the oxygen delivery tubing is often not well tolerated by patients because it is noisy and bulky [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

**Anaesthetic breathing systems** are other examples of the fixed performance devices. The reservoir bag acts to deliver a FGF that is greater than the patient's peak inspiratory flow rate [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] .

### 5.3.4. HUMIDIFIED HIGH-FLOW NASAL OXYGENATION

This device delivers warm and humidified high inspiratory flow of accurate oxygen concentrations. It acts as a fixed performance device. Its use in intensive care, high dependency units, theatres and A&E has increased during the COVID pandemic in attempts to reduce the incidence of tracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation. It can also be used as part of a ventilation weaning strategy. It is also useful when preoxygenating a patient who may be difficult to intubate [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] .

![Humidified high-flow nasal oxygenation](AIRWAY_pdf_AIRWAY.pdf_p117_img1_a9acd6.jpeg)

**Components:**
* Air/oxygen blender to adjust FiO₂ [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* An active humidifier [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* A single heated circuit of corrugated tubing [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* A nasal cannula [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

**Mechanism of action:**
* Flows of up to 70 L/min of warm (up to 37°C) and humidified gas (with an absolute humidity of 44 mg H₂O/L) of a more reliable FiO₂ are delivered to a patient via a nasal cannula [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* It is thought that it produces a reduction of pharyngeal anatomical dead space with a moderate positive airway pressure (CPAP) effect of about 2.7–7.4 cm H₂O [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The above can lead to reduced work of breathing, improved oxygenation and ventilatory efficiency [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Such systems enhance patient comfort, mucociliary clearance and tolerance compared with other oxygen delivery systems [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

**Problems and safety features:**
* Contraindicated in recent nasal surgery and severe epistaxis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Base of skull fracture and/or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* High flows generate significant noise [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The heated, humidified gases can make the nose feel hot [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

* The blade is fitted to the handle and can be either curved or straight. There is a wide range of designs for both curved and straight blades [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

### 5.4.2. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* Usually the straight blade is used for intubating neonates and infants. The blade is advanced over the posterior border of the relatively large, floppy, V-shaped epiglottis, which is then lifted directly in order to view the larynx [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Larger-size straight blades can be used in adults [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The curved blade (Macintosh blade) is designed to fit into the oral and oropharyngeal cavity. It is inserted through the right angle of the mouth and advanced gradually, pushing the tongue to the left and away from the view until the tip of the blade reaches the vallecula [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The blade has a small bulbous tip to help lift the larynx. The laryngoscope is lifted upwards, elevating the larynx and allowing the vocal cords to be seen [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The Macintosh blade is made in five sizes: neonate (0), infant (1), child (2), adult (3) and large adult (4) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

* In the standard designs (colour-coded black), the light source is a bulb screwed/positioned onto the blade and an electrical connection is made when the blade is opened and ready for use [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* In more recent designs, the bulb is placed in the handle and the light is transmitted to the tip of the blade by means of fibreoptics (colour-coded green) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Opening the blade turns the light on by forcing the bulb down to contact the battery terminal [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Acrylic fibre is used in the disposable blades [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The two systems are not cross-compatible [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

* A left-sided Macintosh blade is available. It is used in patients with right-sided facial deformities, making the use of the right-sided blade difficult [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

### 5.4.3. PROBLEMS AND SAFETY FEATURES

* The risk of trauma and bruising to the different structures (e.g., epiglottis) is higher with the straight blade [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* It is of vital importance to check the function of the laryngoscope before anaesthesia has commenced. Reduction in power or total failure due to the corrosion at the electrical contact point is possible [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

* The polio blade was first designed to intubate patients ventilated in the iron lung during the poliomyelitis epidemic in the 1950s [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* A Macintosh laryngoscope blade attached to a short handle can also be useful in this situation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* To prevent cross-infection among patients, a disposable blade is used [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Laryngoscope handles must be appropriately decontaminated between patients to prevent cross-infection [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

### 5.5.2. RIGID VIDEOLARYNGOSCOPES

### 5.5.3. ASA 2022 META-ANALYSIS: VIDEOLARYNGOSCOPY VS DIRECT LARYNGOSCOPY

**Systematic Review Evidence (Category A1-B):**
Meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials comparing video-assisted laryngoscopy with direct laryngoscopy in patients with predicted difficult airways demonstrated:

**Significant Benefits of Videolaryngoscopy:**
* **Improved laryngeal views** in difficult airway patients
* **Higher frequency of successful intubations** overall
* **Higher frequency of first attempt intubations** 
* **Fewer intubation maneuvers** required for success

**Equivocal Findings:**
* **Time to intubation** showed no significant difference between techniques

**Clinical Implementation:**
* **First-line consideration**: Videolaryngoscopy may be considered as initial approach rather than rescue technique
* **Training requirements**: Learning curve needed before routine use
* **Device familiarity**: Success depends on operator experience with specific device
* **Backup planning**: Direct laryngoscopy skills remain essential

**Device Categories:**
* **Hyperangulated vs. Nonangulated**: Equivocal findings for laryngoscopic view, intubation success, first attempt success, and time to intubation
* **Channel-guided vs. Non-channel-guided**: Equivocal findings across all measured outcomes
* **Standard blade videolaryngoscopes**: May allow both direct and indirect laryngoscopy

### 5.5.4. ASA 2022 META-ANALYSIS: VIDEOLARYNGOSCOPY VS DIRECT LARYNGOSCOPY

**Systematic Review Evidence (Category A1-B):**
Meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials comparing video-assisted laryngoscopy with direct laryngoscopy in patients with predicted difficult airways demonstrated:

**Significant Benefits of Videolaryngoscopy:**
* **Improved laryngeal views** in difficult airway patients
* **Higher frequency of successful intubations** overall
* **Higher frequency of first attempt intubations** 
* **Fewer intubation maneuvers** required for success

**Equivocal Findings:**
* **Time to intubation** showed no significant difference between techniques

**Clinical Implementation:**
* **First-line consideration**: Videolaryngoscopy may be considered as initial approach rather than rescue technique
* **Training requirements**: Learning curve needed before routine use
* **Device familiarity**: Success depends on operator experience with specific device
* **Backup planning**: Direct laryngoscopy skills remain essential

**Device Categories:**
* **Hyperangulated vs. Nonangulated**: Equivocal findings for laryngoscopic view, intubation success, first attempt success, and time to intubation
* **Channel-guided vs. Non-channel-guided**: Equivocal findings across all measured outcomes
* **Standard blade videolaryngoscopes**: May allow both direct and indirect laryngoscopy

Videolaryngoscopes improve the view of the glottis, as the camera eye is only centimetres away from the glottis. Their use requires minimal neck movement and can make laryngoscopy and successful tracheal intubation easier. These devices can be used both in difficult tracheal intubation (e.g., anterior larynx and limited neck extension) and routine tracheal intubation. Following local anaesthesia to the airway, they can also be used in awake intubation. Such devices may well supersede the classic laryngoscopes. However, a learning curve is needed before their routine use [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] .

# 6. EXTUBATION OF THE DIFFICULT AIRWAY [ASA 2022]

## 6.1. EXTUBATION STRATEGY DEVELOPMENT [ASA 2022]

### 6.1.1. PREFORMULATED STRATEGY REQUIREMENTS

**Have a preformulated strategy for extubation and subsequent airway management:**
* Strategy depends on surgery/procedure, perioperative circumstances, patient condition, and clinician skills/preferences
* **Extubation strategy is a logical extension of the intubation strategy**
* Plan should address potential complications and reintubation needs

### 6.1.2. PATIENT READINESS ASSESSMENT

**Assess patient readiness for extubation:**
* **Neuromuscular function recovery**
* **Adequate spontaneous ventilation**
* **Protective airway reflexes**
* **Hemodynamic stability**
* **Absence of residual anesthetic effects**

## 6.2. PREPARATION FOR EXTUBATION [ASA 2022]

### 6.2.1. PERSONNEL AND ENVIRONMENT

**Critical Preparation Elements:**
* **Ensure skilled individual present to assist with extubation** when feasible
* **Select appropriate time and location for extubation** when possible
* **Optimal conditions**: awake patient, adequate lighting, suction available
* **Avoid extubation during shift changes** or when assistance unavailable

### 6.2.2. EQUIPMENT PREPARATION

**Essential Equipment Available:**
* **Bag-mask ventilation capability**
* **Laryngoscopes** (direct and video)
* **Supraglottic airway devices**
* **Reintubation equipment**
* **Emergency airway cart**
* **Suction devices**

## 6.3. EXTUBATION TECHNIQUES [ASA 2022]

### 6.3.1. AWAKE VS. DEEP EXTUBATION

**Evaluate risks and benefits of awake extubation vs. extubation before return to consciousness:**

**Awake Extubation Advantages:**
* **Intact protective reflexes**
* **Ability to maintain airway patency**
* **Patient cooperation** if reintubation needed
* **Preferred for most difficult airways**

**Deep Extubation Considerations:**
* **May be appropriate** in select patients with increased risk of laryngospasm
* **Higher risk** in patients with difficult mask ventilation
* **Requires careful risk-benefit analysis**

### 6.3.2. AIRWAY EXCHANGE CATHETERS [ASA 2022]

**Assess relative clinical merits and feasibility of short-term use of airway exchange catheter:**

**Indications for Airway Exchange Catheter:**
* **History of difficult intubation**
* **Airway edema or trauma**
* **High risk of reintubation failure**
* **Bridge to facilitate expedited reintubation**

**Limitations and Risks:**
* **Minimize use with pediatric patients**
* **Risk of esophageal misplacement**
* **Patient discomfort with coughing/gagging**
* **Potential for wire trauma**

**ASA Evidence:**
* Observational study reported 92% success rate for staged extubation and reintubation with Cook airway exchange catheter
* Case reports noted risks of esophageal misplacement during extubation

### 6.3.3. SUPRAGLOTTIC AIRWAY AS EXTUBATION GUIDE

**Consider supraglottic airway that can serve as guide for expedited reintubation:**
* **Bridge device** between extubation and potential reintubation
* **Allows oxygen delivery** while maintaining airway access
* **Second-generation devices** preferred for ventilation capability
* **Requires training and familiarity** with technique

## 6.4. OXYGEN ADMINISTRATION DURING EXTUBATION [ASA 2022]

### 6.4.1. CONTINUOUS SUPPLEMENTAL OXYGEN

**Use supplemental oxygen throughout extubation process when feasible:**
* **Nasal cannula** (low or high-flow)
* **Face mask** oxygen delivery
* **Noninvasive ventilation** when appropriate
* **Head elevated position** maintained when possible

### 6.4.2. POST-EXTUBATION MONITORING

**Assess clinical factors that may produce adverse impact on ventilation after extubation:**
* **Airway edema** from instrumentation
* **Residual neuromuscular blockade**
* **Respiratory depression** from opioids or residual anesthetics
* **Upper airway obstruction** from soft tissue collapse
* **Laryngospasm** or bronchospasm

## 6.5. ELECTIVE SURGICAL TRACHEOSTOMY [ASA 2022]

### 6.5.1. INDICATIONS FOR ELECTIVE TRACHEOSTOMY

**Before attempting extubation, evaluate risks and benefits of elective surgical tracheostomy:**

**Consider Tracheostomy When:**
* **Multiple previous difficult intubations**
* **Significant airway edema or distortion**
* **Prolonged procedure with airway manipulation**
* **High likelihood of reintubation failure**
* **Patient factors favoring surgical airway**

### 6.5.2. TIMING CONSIDERATIONS

**Optimal Timing:**
* **Before inflammation peaks** (usually within 24-48 hours)
* **While patient stable** under controlled conditions
* **Coordination with surgical team**
* **Discussion with patient/family** when appropriate

## 6.6. COMPLICATIONS AND MANAGEMENT [ASA 2022]

### 6.6.1. IMMEDIATE POST-EXTUBATION COMPLICATIONS

**Common Issues:**
* **Upper airway obstruction**
* **Laryngospasm**
* **Aspiration**
* **Negative pressure pulmonary edema**
* **Cardiovascular instability**

### 6.6.2. PHARMACOLOGICAL INTERVENTIONS

**Use post-extubation steroids and/or racemic epinephrine when appropriate:**
* **Dexamethasone**: 0.1-0.5 mg/kg for airway edema
* **Racemic epinephrine**: 0.25-0.5 ml in 3-5 ml saline via nebulizer
* **Indications**: airway edema, stridor, or inflammation
* **Consider prophylactic use** in high-risk patients

**Bottom Line:** Safe extubation of the difficult airway requires as much planning and preparation as the initial intubation. The ASA 2022 guidelines emphasize systematic preparation, appropriate timing, personnel availability, and consideration of bridge techniques to ensure patient safety during this high-risk period.

# 8. SUPRAGLOTTIC AIRWAY DEVICES




> **Syllabus**: [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|Part B]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C]]

## 8.1. TYPES AND CLASSIFICATION

**The introduction of the LMA heralded an era of hands-free airway maintenance without the need for tracheal intubation.** Many other airway devices (well over 100 designs) that lie outside the trachea and attempt to provide a leak-free seal for spontaneous ventilation, while some provide an adequate seal for positive pressure ventilation under normal conditions, have been used. These devices are collectively known as supraglottic airways devices (SADs) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] .

Worldwide, millions of such devices are used each year. SADs can be divided into:

### 8.1.1. FIRST AND SECOND GENERATION SADS

* **First-generation SADs**
  * These fit the description 'simple airway devices', including the original LMA Classic with only one ventilation channel [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
  * They may or may not protect against aspiration in the event of regurgitation but have no specific design features that lessen this risk [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* **Second-generation SADs**
  * These have design features to reduce the risk of aspiration with a separation of ventilatory and gastric access channels; e.g., LMA ProSeal, LMA Supreme, AuraGain and i-gel [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
  * The LMA Protector Airway has dual gastric channels to aid in channelling gastric contents away from the airway [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
  * Such devices offer an oropharyngeal seal (first seal) and an oesophageal seal (second seal) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
  * Some designs have a 'bite block' to prevent the patient biting the tube and occluding it during emergence from anaesthesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

![The LMA Protector Airway with two gastric channels](AIRWAY_pdf_AIRWAY.pdf_p63_img1_7e4bf7.jpeg)

**SADs, in general, provide the following advantages:**

1. The ability to be placed without direct visualization of the larynx [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
2. Increased speed and ease of placement when compared with tracheal intubation, both by experienced and less experienced operators [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
3. Increased cardiovascular stability on insertion and emergence [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
4. During emergence, improved oxygen saturation and lower frequency of coughing [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
5. Minimal rise in intraocular pressure on insertion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
6. When the device is properly placed, it can act as a conduit for oral tracheal intubation due to the anatomical alignment of its aperture with the glottic opening [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
7. In the 'can't intubate, can't ventilate' scenario, the decision to use such devices should be made early to gain time while attempts are made to secure a definite airway [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
8. First-generation SADs normally provide little or no protection against aspiration of refluxed gastric contents and are therefore contraindicated in patients with full stomachs or prone to reflux. However, second-generation devices offer many improvements such as high cuff seal, second seal, gastric access and drain tube [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
9. SADs would normally elicit airway reflexes such as the gag reflex and therefore require depression of pharyngeal reflexes by general or topical anaesthesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
10. These devices are increasingly used in a variety of settings, including routine anaesthesia, emergency airway management, as an aid to intubation and during oesophago-gastro-duodenscopy (OGD) under general anaesthesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

![LMA Gastro Airway has a special channel allowing the insertion of an oesophago-gastro-duodenscopy scope](AIRWAY_pdf_AIRWAY.pdf_p65_img1_fac107.jpeg)

## 8.2. LARYNGEAL MASK AIRWAY (LMA)

**Insertion and use of LMA is a core airway management skill with specific indications, contraindications, and techniques.**

### 8.2.1. COMPONENTS AND TYPES

* It has a transparent tube of wide ID. The proximal end is a standard 15-mm connection [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* An elliptical cuff is found at the distal end. The cuff resembles a small face mask to form an air-tight seal around the posterior perimeter of the larynx and is inflated via a pilot balloon with a self-sealing valve [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* A nonmetallic self-sealing valve is available for use during magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The original design, LMA Classic, had two slits or bars at the junction between the tube and the cuff to prevent the epiglottis from obstructing the lumen of the laryngeal mask. Other designs, such as Intersurgical Solus, omit the bars with no adverse clinical effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

![Teleflex Classic Laryngeal Mask Airway size 4](AIRWAY_pdf_AIRWAY.pdf_p66_img1_c2f089.jpeg)

### 8.2.2. LMA INSERTION TECHNIQUE

* Choose the appropriate size, check for leaks before insertion [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Leading edge of deflated cuff should be wrinkle-free, facing away from the aperture [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Lubricate only the back side of cuff [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Ensure adequate anesthesia before attempting insertion [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Place patient's head in sniffing position [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Use index finger to guide the cuff along the hard palate and down into the hypopharynx until increased resistance is felt. The longitudinal black line should always be pointing directly cephalad (facing patient's upper lip) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Inflate with correct amount of air [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Ensure adequate anesthetic depth during patient positioning [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

⚠️ **Obstruction after insertion is usually due to downfolded epiglottis or transient laryngospasm** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

⚠️ **Avoid pharyngeal suction, cuff deflation or LMA removal until patient is awake (opening mouth on command)** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

![LMA insertion](AIRWAY_AIRWAY_Untitled_4.png_ce6e6f.png_e4a85b.png)

* An ideally positioned cuff is bordered by the base of the tongue superiorly, the pyriform sinuses laterally, and the upper esophageal sphincter inferiorly [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* If an LMA is not functioning properly after attempts to improve the "fit" have failed, most practitioners will try another LMA one size larger or smaller [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* The LMA partially protects the larynx from pharyngeal secretions (but not gastric regurgitation), and it should remain in place until the patient has regained airway reflexes [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

⚠️ **Relative contraindications to LMA include patients with pharyngeal pathology (e.g., abscess), pharyngeal obstruction, aspiration risk (e.g., pregnancy, hiatal hernia), or low pulmonary compliance (e.g., restrictive airways disease) requiring peak inspiratory pressures greater than 30 cm H₂O** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

![LMA positioning](AIRWAY_AIRWAY_Untitled_5.png_5619f4.png_6bf37f.png)

* A variety of techniques have been described for the insertion of the laryngeal mask. It should provide an adequate seal for spontaneous and mechanical ventilation with a minimal leak at a pressure of 20–25 cm H₂O. A seal pressure of up to 35 cm H₂O can be achieved with the LMA ProSeal [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The cuff is deflated and lubricated before use. It is inserted through the mouth. The cuff lies over the larynx [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Once the cuff is in position, it is inflated according to the recommended sizes and cuff inflation volumes (see table below). It is important to measure and monitor the cuff pressure throughout the procedure as the pressure can increase due to N₂O diffusion and warming of the inserted air. This can cause trauma to the surrounding tissues [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Cuff Pilot Technology allows continuous cuff pressure monitoring [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Partial inflation of the cuff before insertion is used by some anaesthetists [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The laryngeal masks have wide IDs in order to reduce the flow resistance to a minimum (e.g., the IDs of sizes 2, 3, 4 and 5 are 7, 10, 10 and 11.5 mm, respectively). This makes them suitable for long procedures using a spontaneous ventilation technique [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

**The recommended sizes and cuff inflation volumes:**

| Size | Size of patient | Cuff inflation volume |
|------|----------------|---------------------|
| Size 1 | Neonates, infants up to 5 kg | Up to 4 mL |
| Size 1.5 | Infants 5–10 kg | Up to 7 mL |
| Size 2 | Infants/children 10–20 kg | Up to 10 mL |
| Size 2.5 | Children 20–30 kg | Up to 14 mL |
| Size 3 | Paediatric 30–50 kg | Up to 20 mL |
| Size 4 | Adults 50–70 kg | Up to 30 mL |
| Size 5 | Adults 70–100 kg | Up to 40 mL |
| Size 6 | Large adults over 100 kg | Up to 60 mL |

![Teleflex Cuff Pilot Technology used to monitor the cuff pressure](AIRWAY_pdf_AIRWAY.pdf_p72_img1_db8f3d.jpeg)

**The recommended safety checks before the use of laryngeal masks:**
* Inflate the cuff and look for signs of herniation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Check that the lumen of the tube is patent [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The tube can be bent to 180 degrees without kinking or occlusion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Inspect the device for signs of dehiscence of the tube or mask aperture bars and cuff separations. In the reusable devices, look for signs of damage or weakness where the teeth were in contact with the tube [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The device should also be inspected after removal from the patient for signs of bleeding [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

### 8.2.3. ADVANTAGES AND DISADVANTAGES

**Advantages of LMA Spontaneous Ventilation with Sevoflurane Strategy:**
* Decreases risk of barotrauma and disadvantages of positive pressure ventilation such as aspiration [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* If cuff not inflated to full - reduces risk of mucosal pressure injury; avoids risk of tracheal mucosal necrosis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Ease of facilitation as a senior when tasked with multiple OTs - as can easily bring out to PACU without changing settings of vent [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Allows titration of analgesia to RR [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Avoids issue of over or under dosing of GA - when using sevoflurane; as if patient lightens, he breathes faster and breathes more sevoflurane and deepens again [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

**Disadvantages of LMA Spontaneous Ventilation Strategy:**
* Patient must be able to tolerate low TV [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Patient must be able to tolerate elevated PaCO₂ [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

**Problems and safety features:**
* The laryngeal mask does not protect against the aspiration of gastric contents [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Despite the presence of the slits or bars, about 10% of patients develop airway obstruction because of down-folding of the epiglottis. Although clinically often insignificant, a higher proportion of obstructions by the epiglottis can be observed endoscopically. Down-folding of the epiglottis can occur in 20%–56% of patients with some having an airway obstruction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The manufacturers recommend using the reusable laryngeal masks for a maximum of 40 times. The cuff is likely to perish after autoclaving. A record card that accompanies the laryngeal mask registers the number of autoclaving episodes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Unlike the tracheal tube, rotation of the laryngeal mask may result in complete airway obstruction. In order to assess the orientation of the laryngeal mask when inserted, a black line is present on the tube. This should face the upper lip of the patient when the laryngeal mask is in position [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Cricoid pressure may prevent correct placement of the laryngeal mask [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

### 8.3.1. I-GEL AIRWAY

The i-gel airway (Intersurgical, Wokingham, UK) is a single-use extraglottic airway that uses an anatomically designed mask to fit the perilaryngeal and hypopharyngeal structures without the use of an inflatable cuff [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] .

**Components:**
* The large lumen is for ventilation with a proximal 15-mm connector. Distally, it ends in a non-inflatable gel-like cuff with a ridge at the superior anterior edge [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Two separate ventilation and gastric channels or lumens. The distal end of the integrated gastric lumen is positioned in the upper oesophagus [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The body is a wide oval in cross section [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

![The Intersurgical i-gel airway](AIRWAY_pdf_AIRWAY.pdf_p78_img2_f25986.jpeg)

**Mechanism of action:**
* The soft, gel-like plastic from which the i-gel is manufactured is intended to mould into place without the use of an inflatable cuff [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The gastric channel allows direct suctioning or passage of a gastric tube [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The wide oval in cross section body is designed to prevent rotation and to act as an integral bite block [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The epiglottic blocking ridge is intended to reduce the possibility of epiglottic down-folding [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* It is available in adult, paediatric and neonatal sizes 1–5 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* It is intended for use with fasted patients, with both spontaneous and controlled ventilation, and can be used as a conduit for tracheal intubation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

**Problems and safety features:**
Despite its gastric channel, the i-gel does not offer absolute protection from aspiration of gastric contents [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] .

### 8.3.2. AURAGAIN AIRWAY

The AuraGain (Ambu) is an anatomically curved, single-use, second-generation SAD. It incorporates a gastric channel to allow drainage of gastric contents and the insertion of a wide-bore gastric tube, up to 16 FR. * Its wide ventilation channel allows tracheal intubation, usually aided using a flexible fibreoptic scope. The ID of the ventilation channel is between 6.6 mm (size 1) and 12.9 mm (sizes 5 and 6) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

* The proximal end of the ventilation channel has a proximal 15-mm connector [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Using the AuraGain, high seal pressures up to 40 cm H₂O can be successfully achieved [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

## 9.1. CONFIRMATION OF PROPER TUBE PLACEMENT

* The "gold standard" is direct visualization of the ETT situated between the vocal cords [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

### 9.1.1. CONFIRMATION METHODS AFTER INTUBATION

**Primary Confirmation (ASA 2022 Recommendation):**
* **Capnography or end-tidal carbon dioxide monitoring** - This is the ASA recommended method for confirming tracheal intubation
* Studies with observational findings indicate that capnography confirms tracheal intubation in 88.5 to 100% of difficult airway patients

**Secondary Confirmation Methods:**
* Auscultate the chest and epigastrium [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* It cannot, however, detect bronchial intubation - the earliest evidence of which is an increase in peak inspiratory pressure [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Other methods: repeat laryngoscopy, Fibreoptic bronchoscopy through the tube (and visualization of tracheal rings and carina) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

**ASA 2022 Guidance on Uncertain Tube Position:**
When uncertain about the location of the tracheal tube, determine whether to:
* **Remove the tube and attempt ventilation**, OR
* **Use additional techniques to confirm positioning** of the tube

**Additional Confirmation Techniques:**
* **Visualization techniques**: Direct or video laryngoscopy to confirm tube position
* **Flexible bronchoscopy**: Direct visualization of tracheal rings and carina through the tube
* **Ultrasonography**: Assessment of tube position and lung expansion
* **Radiography**: Chest X-ray for tube position (typically for post-procedure confirmation)

![ETCO₂ confirmation](AIRWAY_AIRWAY_Untitled_10.png_38a229.png_2dec15.png)

* Ultrasound examination of the airway has also been suggested to be of assistance in airway assessment and management [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Ultrasound can be used as an adjunct to confirm ETT placement as well as to assist in identification of the cricothyroid membrane during emergent cricothyroidotomy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

### 9.1.2. FAILED INTUBATION MANAGEMENT

* A failed intubation should not be followed by identical repeated attempts [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Changes must be made to increase the likelihood of success, such as:
  * Repositioning the patient [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Decreasing the tube size [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Adding a stylet [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Selecting a different blade [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Using an indirect laryngoscope [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Attempting a nasal route [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Requesting the assistance of another anesthesia provider [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

⚠️ **If the patient is also difficult to ventilate with a mask, alternative forms of airway management (e.g., second-generation supraglottic airway devices, jet ventilation via percutaneous tracheal catheter, cricothyrotomy, tracheostomy) must be immediately pursued** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].

## 9.2. RISK FACTORS FOR DIFFICULT INTUBATION

### 9.2.1. PHYSICAL EXAMINATION FINDINGS PREDICTING DIFFICULT INTUBATION

| **Clinical Test** | **Finding** | **Sensitivity** | **Specificity** | **Positive LR** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Class 3 ULBT** | Lower incisors cannot reach the lower border of the upper lip | 0.60 | 0.96 | 14 |
| **Class 2 or 3 ULBT** | Lower incisors cannot reach the upper lip or the vermilion border of the upper lip | 0.60 | 0.95 | 12 |
| **Wilson score** | ≥ 2 or ≥ 3 | 0.43 | 0.95 | 9.1 |
| **Hyomental distance** | < 3-5.5 cm when measured in neutral position | 0.20 | 0.97 | 6.4 |
| **Retrognathia** | Mandible < 9 cm (angle of the jaw to the tip of the chin) or subjectively short | 0.19 | 0.98 | 6.0 |
| **Mandibular protrusion** | Impaired; cannot move lower teeth past upper teeth | 0.25 | 0.95 | 5.5 |
| **Ratio of height to thyromental distance** | ≥ 17-25 | 0.69 | 0.87 | 5.2 |
| **Neck mobility** | Impaired, < 30-90° of flexion or extension or subjectively limited | 0.28 | 0.93 | 4.2 |
| **Sternomental distance** | < 12-15 cm | 0.41 | 0.90 | 4.1 |
| **Mallampati score** | ≥ 3 | 0.55 | 0.87 | 4.1 |
| **Mouth opening** | Impaired; interincisor gap < 2-5 cm | 0.36 | 0.90 | 3.6 |
| **Thyromental distance** | < 4-7 cm | 0.45 | 0.86 | 3.3 |
| **Palm print/prayer sign** | Based on the scoring system from each study | 0.77 | 0.84 | 3.0 |

💡 **The Upper Lip Bite Test (ULBT) had the highest positive LR for predicting difficult intubation** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]. When a grade 3 ULBT was present on physical examination, the incidence of difficult intubation rose from 10% to > 60% [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].

The ULBT is performed by asking the patient to bite their upper lip by using their lower incisors:

* **Class 1** (most reassuring): The patient can extend their lower incisors beyond the vermilion border of the upper lip [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].
* **Class 2**: The lower incisors can bite the upper lip but cannot extend beyond the vermilion border [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].
* **Class 3** (most concerning): The lower incisors cannot bite the upper lip at all [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].

## 9.3. INDICATIONS FOR INTUBATION

* Intubation is not necessary in all GA cases [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* It is indicated in patients who are at risk of aspiration and in those undergoing surgical procedures involving body cavities, head and neck, and those who will be positioned so that the airway will be less accessible (i.e., those undergoing surgery in the prone position or where the head is rotated away) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Mask ventilation or ventilation with an LMA is usually satisfactory for short minor procedures such as cystoscopy, examination under anesthesia, inguinal hernia repairs, extremity surgery, and so forth [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Intubation is a fundamental airway management skill requiring proper equipment selection, technique, and confirmation of proper placement. Understanding risk factors for difficult intubation allows for better planning and preparation for alternative approaches when needed.

* Call for help after initial unsuccessful intubation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Place LMA immediately after unsuccessful intubation AND mask ventilation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Do not postpone a life-saving surgical airway [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

### 10.1.1. ASA 2022 ANTICIPATED DIFFICULT AIRWAY MANAGEMENT

**Preformulated Strategy Components:**
* Strategy depends on: anticipated surgery, patient condition, patient cooperation/consent, age, and skills/preferences of anesthesiologist
* Identify strategy for:
  1. **Awake intubation**
  2. **Patient who can be adequately ventilated but is difficult to intubate**
  3. **Patient who cannot be ventilated or intubated**
  4. **Difficulty with emergency invasive airway rescue**

**Awake Intubation Indications:**
Perform awake intubation when appropriate if patient is suspected to be difficult intubation AND one or more of the following apply:
* **Difficult ventilation** (face mask/supraglottic airway)
* **Increased risk of aspiration**
* **Patient likely incapable of tolerating brief apneic episode**
* **Expected difficulty with emergency invasive airway rescue**

**Noninvasive vs. Invasive Approach Decision:**
* If **noninvasive approach** selected: identify preferred sequence of devices
* **Combination techniques** may be performed if difficulty encountered with individual techniques
* **Be aware of passage of time, number of attempts, and oxygen saturation**
* **Limit attempts** at tracheal intubation or supraglottic airway placement to avoid injury and complications

**Critical Time and Attempt Awareness:**
* **Provide and test mask ventilation after each attempt** when feasible
* **Monitor oxygen saturation continuously**
* **Count and limit total attempts** with any single technique

### 10.1.2. ASA 2022 UNANTICIPATED/EMERGENCY DIFFICULT AIRWAY MANAGEMENT

**Immediate Actions:**
* **Call for help** immediately
* **Optimize oxygenation** using all available methods
* **Use cognitive aids/algorithms** when appropriate

**Decision Points:**
* **Determine benefit of waking** and/or restoring spontaneous breathing
* **Determine noninvasive vs. invasive approach** to airway management
* **If noninvasive approach**: identify preferred sequence of devices

**Emergency Invasive Airway Criteria:**
If invasive approach necessary (cannot intubate, cannot ventilate):
* **Identify preferred intervention** for emergency invasive airway
* **Ensure invasive airway performed by trained individual** whenever possible
* **Perform invasive airway as rapidly as possible**
* **Have alternative invasive intervention identified** if first approach fails

**ECMO Consideration:**
* **Initiate ECMO when/if appropriate and available** for extreme cases

## 10.3. PREPARATION PRINCIPLES

* Oxygenation is paramount [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Difficult mask ventilation is more dangerous than difficult intubation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * So long as you can mask, you can oxygenate the patient through almost any anesthetic [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Preparation is key! Set yourself up for success [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Do a thorough airway exam [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Ensure the airway equipment you need is readily available and tested [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Take the time to correctly position your patient. Poor positioning can turn a Cormack-Lehane grade 1 view into a grade 4 view [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Proper positioning is worth your effort, even at the start of an emergent case [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

## 10.6. 2022 ASA DIFFICULT AIRWAY ALGORITHMS AND INFOGRAPHICS

### 10.6.1. ASA 2022 ADULT DIFFICULT AIRWAY ALGORITHM

**Key Algorithm Features:**
* **Preformulated strategy development** based on airway assessment
* **Decision points** for awake vs. asleep management
* **Emphasis on oxygenation** throughout all procedures
* **Time and attempt awareness** as core safety principles
* **Clear progression** from noninvasive to invasive techniques

**Algorithm Decision Tree:**
1. **Airway Assessment** → Anticipated vs. Unanticipated Difficulty
2. **Awake Intubation Pathway** for high-risk scenarios
3. **Anesthesia Induction Pathway** with backup plans
4. **Can Ventilate/Cannot Intubate** → Alternative intubation approaches
5. **Cannot Ventilate/Cannot Intubate** → Emergency invasive airway

**Critical Algorithm Elements:**
* **Supplemental oxygen** before, during, and after all airway management
* **Call for help** early in unanticipated difficult scenarios
* **Cognitive aids** and algorithms should be available
* **Limit attempts** to avoid trauma and maintain oxygenation
* **Emergency invasive airway** when other methods fail

### 10.6.2. ASA 2022 PEDIATRIC DIFFICULT AIRWAY ALGORITHM

**Pediatric-Specific Considerations:**
* **Anatomical differences** requiring modified approaches
* **Limited cooperation** restricting awake intubation options
* **Higher oxygen consumption** reducing apneic tolerance
* **Smaller airway** increasing obstruction risk
* **Age-appropriate equipment** selection critical

**Algorithm Modifications for Pediatrics:**
* **Awake techniques limited** due to cooperation requirements
* **Rapid desaturation** necessitating faster decision-making
* **Supraglottic airways** as important rescue devices
* **Emergency invasive airway** requires specialized pediatric techniques
* **Team-based approach** with designated roles essential

## 10.7. STRATEGIES IN DIFFICULT INTUBATION

### 10.7.1. AWAKE INTUBATION

* Key is topicalization [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Non-invasive options:
  * Conventional fiberoptic intubation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Also consider laryngoscopy (direct, video) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Topicalize airway with local anesthetic or perform select nerve blocks [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * A fully-awake patient can tolerate a Glidescope if the airway is properly topicalized! [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Invasive options:
  * Tracheostomy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Cricothyroidotomy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Retrograde intubation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

### 10.7.2. ASLEEP INTUBATION

* Post-Induction Intubation
  * If your initial attempt is unsuccessful…Call for help! [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Attempt to mask ventilate
  * If you can mask, head down the non-emergency pathway [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * If you cannot mask, place an SGA [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * If successful, head down the non-emergency pathway [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* If you cannot mask or place an SGA, head down the Emergency Pathway [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

### 10.7.3. ASLEEP NON-EMERGENT

* Ventilation is adequate, so you may wake the patient up, or try alternatives [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Alternative approaches:
  * SGA (as bridge to ETT or destination throughout case) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Video laryngoscopy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Fiberoptic intubation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Light wand [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Blind nasal intubation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Limit direct laryngoscopy attempts [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
	* Don't repeat same DL attempt – change blade, positioning, provider [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Be very careful to avoid causing airway trauma [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
	* Oropharynx and larynx are delicate [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
	* Bleeding and swelling can turn a maskable airway into a "Cannot Intubate, Cannot Oxygenate" emergency [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

### 10.7.4. ASLEEP EMERGENT

* If at any time, ventilation becomes inadequate, you enter the Emergency Pathway [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * You should already have called for help. If not, do so now [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Perform emergency noninvasive airway ventilation
  * Different SGA (intubating LMA?) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Combitube [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Apneic oxygenation (e.g., Optiflow) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Rigid bronchoscopy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Perform emergency invasive airway access before SpO₂ drops
  * Cricothyrotomy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Surgical tracheostomy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Trans-tracheal jet ventilation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

### 10.7.5. VORTEX APPROACH

* No more than 3 attempts for each technique - at least one by most experienced clinician [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Then proceed to surgical airway [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Do something differently each attempt to optimize - airway, positioning, devices [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* If you are even thinking about a cric kit, call for one early - better to have it and not need it, than need it and not have it [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

## 10.8. SURGICAL AIRWAY

* Even in an emergency, always invest 20 seconds to:
  * Identify someone to assist [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Place a shoulder roll to expose the trachea [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Direct a light source at the neck [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Cannula-based (aka percutaneous) techniques have a far higher failure rate than surgical techniques, which are successful >90% of the time [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Know how and where to get the tools you need [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Cricothyroidotomy can be performed in under 60 seconds:
  * Scalpel, #10 or #11 blade [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * +/- Trousseau dilator or Kelly clamp [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Bougie introducer [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * 6.0 cuffed ETT [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Scalpel-Bougie Surgical technique [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

![Surgical airway technique](AIRWAY_AIRWAY_Untitled_20.png_93ab5a.png_d03fc1.png)

* Identify the cricothyroid membrane [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Make a vertical midline incision [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Palpate the cricothyroid membrane, make a horizontal stab incision and extend to the edge of the cricothyroid membrane [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Turn scalpel 180 degrees and extend incision to the opposite edge [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Use your finger to palpate the incision you just created, and pass the bougie through [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Railroad an endotracheal tube over the bougie, advancing until the balloon is in the trachea but being careful not to advance too deeply [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Confirm placement with end-tidal CO₂ [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Knowledge of difficult airway algorithms, early recognition of difficulty, and preparation for alternative approaches are essential to patient safety. The Vortex approach simplifies decision-making in emergency scenarios, and familiarity with surgical airway techniques can be lifesaving when other methods fail.

## 12.1. EXTUBATION RISK FACTORS

* 12% of the closed claim cases with perioperative difficult airway were from the time of extubation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
* ASA practice for management of difficult airway has not decreased the number of claims arising from injury at extubation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
* Incidence of respiratory complications may be higher with extubation than intubation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]

### 12.1.1. ASA 2022 EXTUBATION STRATEGY REQUIREMENTS

**Preformulated Extubation Strategy:**
* Strategy depends on: surgery/procedure, perioperative circumstances, patient condition, and clinician skills/preferences
* **Assess patient readiness for extubation** systematically
* **Ensure skilled individual present** to assist with extubation when feasible
* **Select appropriate time and location** for extubation when possible

**Assessment Components:**
* **Assess relative clinical merits and feasibility** of short-term airway exchange catheter and/or supraglottic airway use
* **Evaluate risks and benefits of elective surgical tracheostomy** before attempting extubation
* **Evaluate risks and benefits of awake extubation vs. extubation before return to consciousness**
* **Use supplemental oxygen throughout extubation process** when feasible
* **Assess clinical factors** that may adversely impact ventilation after extubation

**Special Considerations:**
* **Minimize airway exchange catheter use with pediatric patients**
* **Consider staged extubation techniques** when indicated
* **Plan for possible reintubation** scenarios

### 12.1.2. AIRWAY EXCHANGE CATHETERS (AEC) [ASA 2022]

**Evidence Base:**
* Observational study reported **92% success rate** with Cook airway exchange catheter for staged extubation and reintubation in known/presumed difficult extubation patients
* **Potential complications**: wire in esophagus, intolerable cough, gagging, salivation
* **Esophageal misplacement risk** documented in case reports

**Clinical Applications:**
* **Short-term use** as guide for expedited reintubation
* **Staged extubation** in high-risk patients
* **Temporary bridge** allowing assessment of spontaneous ventilation

## 12.2. DAS 2012 GUIDELINES

* Low risk - awake vs deep extubation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

  * AEC - hollow catheters similar in shape to bougie [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

## 12.4. DEEP EXTUBATION

* Adequate depth of anesthesia - no response to pharyngeal suctioning or jaw thrust, breath holding etc. [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Compared to awake extubation - no tachycardia, hypertension, coughing, reduced risk of wound dehiscence, bleeding, bronchospasm [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* But patients remain at risk for laryngospasm as they emerge from anesthesia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

## 12.5. ROUTINE EXTUBATION CRITERIA

1. **Vital signs stable**
   * BP/HR stable within acceptable ranges (on minimal pressors) – T > 35.5°C [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
   * Spontaneous RR >6 and <30, SpO₂ > 90% [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
2. **ABG "reasonable" with FiO₂ ≤ 40%**
   * pH ≥7.30, PaO₂ ≥60 mmHg, PaCO₂ ≤50-60, normal electrolytes [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
   * As a surrogate, ETCO₂ can be used and should be <60 [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
3. **Adequate reversal of neuromuscular blockade**
   * TOF ratio >0.9, tetany >5 secs [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
	 * The "direct palpation" method cannot determine if the TOF ratio is > 0.9 [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
   * Sustained head lift or hand grasp >5 secs (sensitive but not specific) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
	 * Not adequate to rule out residual paralysis or incomplete reversal [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
4. **Respiratory mechanics adequate**
   * Spontaneous VT >5 mL/kg, Vital Capacity >15mL/kg [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
5. **Protective reflexes (gag, swallow, cough) returned*** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
6. **Awake, alert, able to follow commands*** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
7. **Optimize the patient**: 100% O₂, consider positioning in slight reverse Trendelenburg, suction oropharynx, consider small dose of lidocaine to reduce coughing [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

*These need not be present in the case of a deep extubation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]].

## 12.6. CAUSES OF FAILED EXTUBATION

![Failed extubation causes](AIRWAY_AIRWAY_Untitled.png_f93542.png_341690.png)

* **Cuff leak test**:
  * While ventilated on VC mode - deflate the ETT cuff [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * In absence of significant airway edema - leak should be present [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Calculate the difference between programmed TV and observed expiratory TV - cuff leak [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Adequate cuff leak is 10-15% of TV [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

## 12.7. STANDARD PREPARATION FOR EXTUBATION

1. Ensure back-up airway / re-intubation equipment available
   * LMA, bougie, Mac/Miller blade nearby on hand [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
2. Pre-oxygenate with 100% O₂; consider recruitment maneuver to reduce atelectasis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
3. Reverse neuromuscular blockade [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
4. Turn off primary anesthetic agent [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
5. Insert a soft bite block (rolled gauze); suction as appropriate [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
6. Position patient and bed appropriately
   * Is the patient still turned 180 degrees? Lithotomy position? [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
   * Consider reverse Trendelenburg positioning to improve ventilation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
7. Minimize touching patient during Stage 2 ("light") anesthesia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
8. Confirm that all "Routine Extubation Criteria" are met [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
9. Extubate:
   * Deflate cuff, remove tube with positive pressure [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
   * Provide 100% O₂, ensure patent airway, adequate breathing [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
   * Use an oral airway or nasal trumpet as needed [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
10. Transport to PACU on continuous oxygen [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Extubation requires careful planning, assessment of risk factors, and preparation for potential complications. The decision between awake and deep extubation should be based on patient factors, surgical considerations, and provider experience.

  * If present, leave NGT to suction [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Apply cricoid pressure until ETT position confirmed [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
	* Although this practice is debated, one could contend it is considered the 'standard of care' [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Minimize bag-mask PPV and/or keep pressure <20 cmH₂O [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Extubate after recovery of protective reflexes [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Remain vigilant: aspiration occurs during emergence and maintenance and not just during induction [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]

### 13.3.2. ASPIRATION PNEUMONITIS

* Sterile, chemical pneumonitis caused by aspiration of acidic and particulate material [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
* Highest risk in patients with gastric volume >25 ml and pH <2.5 [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
* Aspiration does NOT always cause pneumonia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
* **Management**:
  * Place patient in head-down position (allow to drain from lungs) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Immediately suction pharynx and trachea before PPV [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * 100% O₂, intubate (if needed), apply PEEP or CPAP [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Supportive care - monitor for chemical PNA/ARDS [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Possible bronchoscopy for removal of particulate matter, if suspected [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Antibiotics are not necessary unless subsequent infection develops (or, as happens more commonly in pediatrics, fecal matter is aspirated) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Steroids are not indicated [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]

### 13.4.1. STANFORD PROTOCOL FOR DELAYED EMERGENCE

* Confirm that all anesthetic agents (inhalational/IV) are off [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Check for residual NMB paralysis, reverse as appropriate [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Consider opiate reversal (medications delivered, evaluate pupils & respiratory rate)
  * Start with 40 mcg naloxone IV, repeat Q2 mins up to 200 mcg total [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Consider inhalational anesthetic reversal (rare)
  * 1.25 mg of physostigmine IV [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Consider benzodiazepine reversal
  * Start with 0.2 mg flumazenil IV, repeat Q1 min up to 1 mg total [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Check blood glucose level & treat hypo or hyperglycemia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Check ABG and electrolytes
  * Rule out CO₂ narcosis and hypo or hypernatremia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Check patient temperature and actively warm if <34 degrees C [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Perform neuro exam if possible: examine pupils, symmetric motor movements, gag reflex/cough [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* Obtain stat head CT and consult neurology/neurosurgery to rule out possible CVA [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
* If residual sedation/coma persists despite the evaluating all possible causes, ICU admit with neurology follow up, frequent neuro exams, repeat head CT in 6-8 hrs if no improvement [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]

## 13.5. RAPID SEQUENCE INDUCTION

* Although regurgitation and aspiration are potential complications of any anesthetic, there are factors which place some patients at higher risk [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * The obvious risk factor is recent intake of solid food [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * However, even a prolonged period of fasting does not guarantee an "empty stomach" if gastric emptying is delayed [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Examples of conditions which impair gastric emptying include diabetes, trauma, recent opioid administration, and bowel obstruction [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Finally, decreased integrity of the lower esophageal sphincter, as occurs in pregnancy and obesity, increases the risk of regurgitation of stomach contents [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
* In patients deemed to be at increased risk for aspiration, the time between inducing anesthesia and securing the airway with a cuffed endotracheal tube must be minimized [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
* Such a technique is termed a "rapid sequence induction" [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]

### 13.5.1. RSI PROCEDURE STEPS

1. Suction apparatus is checked and kept readily available [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
2. Pre-oxygenation of patient with 100% oxygen for 3-5 minutes [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
3. Application of cricoid pressure (Sellick's maneuver) by assistant [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
4. Induction with pre-calculated dose of induction agent followed immediately by intubating dose of depolarizing muscle relaxant (succinylcholine). A rapidly acting non-depolarizing agent (e.g., rocuronium) is commonly used in a so-called "modified" rapid sequence induction [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
5. Intubation of trachea, cuff inflation, and verification of proper tube position [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]

### 13.5.2. ASA 2022 CONSIDERATIONS FOR RSI

**Cricoid Pressure (Sellick Maneuver):**
* **Traditional teaching**: Provides occlusion of esophagus between cricoid cartilage and cervical vertebrae
* **ASA 2022 Evidence**: Literature is insufficient to evaluate benefit or harm of cricoid pressure
* **Current practice**: Use remains controversial, with some practitioners questioning effectiveness
* **Application technique**: Firm posterior pressure on cricoid cartilage until intubation confirmed

**Neuromuscular Blockade Options:**
* **Rocuronium with Sugammadx**: 
  * **Advantages**: Rapid onset, reliable reversal available
  * **Considerations**: Higher cost, sugammadx availability required
* **Succinylcholine**:
  * **Advantages**: Rapid onset and offset, reliable paralysis
  * **Contraindications**: Hyperkalemia risk, malignant hyperthermia susceptibility
* **ASA 2022**: Insufficient evidence to prefer one approach over another

**Pressure-Limited Mask Ventilation:**
* **Consideration**: Balance between avoiding gastric insufflation and maintaining oxygenation
* **ASA 2022**: Insufficient evidence to guide pressure-limited vs. ablation of spontaneous ventilation
* **Clinical practice**: Gentle mask ventilation often used if oxygen saturation declining

**Preoxygenation Enhancement:**
* **Standard approach**: 3-5 minutes of tidal volume breathing
* **Rapid technique**: 4-12 deep breaths at forced vital capacity
* **High-risk patients**: Consider noninvasive ventilation during preoxygenation
* **Target**: End-tidal oxygen concentration ≥ 0.90

💡 **The purpose of pre-oxygenation is to lessen the risk of hypoxemia occurring during the apneic period after induction** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].

* Although pre-oxygenation does increase the patient's arterial PO₂ prior to induction, its most important effect is the de-nitrogenation of the functional residual capacity of the lungs [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
* Traditional teaching is that the Sellick maneuver provides occlusion of the esophagus between the cricoid cartilage (a complete circumferential cartilage) and the cervical vertebrae thus minimizing the risk of passive regurgitation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]

**ASA 2022 Research Priorities:**
* **Cricoid pressure effectiveness** in preventing aspiration
* **Optimal neuromuscular blocking strategy** for RSI
* **Pressure-limited ventilation** vs. complete apnea during RSI
* **Enhanced preoxygenation techniques** for high-risk patients

**Bottom Line:** Recognizing and managing airway complications is essential for patient safety. Laryngospasm, negative pressure pulmonary edema, and aspiration are significant complications that require prompt recognition and appropriate management. Following proper protocols for extubation and rapid sequence induction can help minimize these risks.

## 14.1. TRACHEOSTOMY

**These are curved plastic tubes usually inserted through the second, third and fourth tracheal cartilage rings** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] .

### 14.1.1. COMPONENTS AND TYPES

* An introducer is used for insertion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Wings are attached to the proximal part of the tube to fix it in place with a ribbon or suture. Some designs have an adjustable flange to fit the variable thickness of the subcutaneous tissues [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* They can be cuffed or uncuffed. The former has a pilot balloon [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The proximal end can have a standard 15-mm connector [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* The tip is usually cut square rather than bevelled. This decreases the risk of obstruction by lying against the tracheal wall [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Some designs have an additional suctioning lumen, which opens just above the cuff. The cuff shape is designed to allow the secretions above it to be suctioned effectively through the suctioning lumen [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* In patients with a difficult anatomy, some designs use a reinforced tube to prevent kinking [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Some tubes have an inner cannula. Secretions can collect and dry out on the inner lumen of the tube, leading to obstruction. The internal cannula can be replaced instead of changing the complete tube in such cases. The cannula leads to a slight reduction of the ID of the tube [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Different sizes of tracheostomy tubes are available to fit neonates to adults [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Older uncuffed metal tracheostomy tubes made of a non-irritant and bactericidal silver are rarely used in current practice. Some designs have a one-way flap valve and a window at the angle of the tube to allow the patient to speak [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

### 14.1.2. INDICATIONS AND BENEFITS

**Indications for tracheostomy:**
* Long-term ventilation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Upper airway obstruction that cannot be bypassed with an oral/nasal tracheal tube [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Maintenance of an airway and to protect the lungs in patients with impaired pharyngeal or laryngeal reflexes and after major head and neck surgery (e.g., laryngectomy) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Long-term control of excessive bronchial secretions especially in patients with a reduced level of consciousness [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* To facilitate weaning from a ventilator. This is due to a reduction in the sedation required, as the patients tolerate tracheostomy tubes better than tracheal tubes. Also there is a reduction in the anatomical dead space [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

**Benefits of tracheostomy:**
* Increased patient comfort [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Less need for sedation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Improved access for oral hygiene [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Possibility of oral nutrition [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Bronchial suctioning aided [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Reduced dead space [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Reduced airway resistance [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Reduced risk of glottic trauma [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

**Problems and safety features:**

Surgical tracheostomy has a mortality rate of <1% but has a total complications rate as high as 40%. The complications rate is higher in the intensive care unit and emergency patients [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] .

The complications can be divided into:

1. **Immediate:**
   * Haemorrhage [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
   * Tube misplacement (e.g., into a main bronchus) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
   * Occlusion of tube by cuff herniation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
   * Occlusion of the tube tip against carina or tracheal wall [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
   * Pneumothorax [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

2. **Delayed:**
   * Blockage of the tube by secretions, which can be sudden or gradual; this is rare with adequate humidification and suction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
   * Infection of the stoma [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
   * Overinflation of the cuff leads to ulceration and distension of the trachea [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
   * Mucosal ulceration because of excessive cuff pressures, asymmetrical inflation of the cuff or tube migration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
   * Surgical emphysema, pneumothorax and pneumomediastinum [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

3. **Late:**
   * Tracheal stenosis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
   * Granulomata of the trachea may cause respiratory difficulty after extubation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
   * Persistent sinus at the tracheostomy site [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
   * Tracheal dilatation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
   * Tracheal stenosis at the cuff site [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
   * Scar formation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

**The Fenestrated Tracheostomy Tube:**
* The fenestration (window) in the greater curvature channels air to the vocal cords, allowing the patient to speak [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* After deflation of the cuff, the patient can breathe around the cuff and through the fenestration as well as through the stoma. This reduces airway resistance and assists in weaning from tracheostomy in spontaneously breathing patients [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
* Some tubes have a fenestrated inner cannula [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

### 14.1.3. PERCUTANEOUS TRACHEOSTOMY

These tubes are inserted between the first and second or second and third tracheal rings, usually at the bedside in the intensive care unit. Percutaneous tracheostomy tubes are becoming more popular and have fewer complications than the surgical technique [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] .

1. If the patient is intubated, the tracheal tube is withdrawn until the tip is just below the vocal cords. Then the cuff is inflated and rested on the vocal cords. A laryngeal mask can be used instead [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
2. Through an introducing needle, a Seldinger guidewire is inserted into the trachea. A fibreoptic bronchoscope should be used throughout the procedure. It helps to ensure the initial puncture of the trachea is in the midline and free of the tracheal tube. It can also ensure that the posterior tracheal wall is not damaged during the procedure. Finally, it can assess the position of the tracheostomy tube relative to the carina [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
3. A series of curved dilators can be used. The diameter of the stoma is serially increased until the desired diameter is achieved. A single curved dilator of a graduated diameter can be used instead. A tracheostomy tube can then be inserted [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
4. A pair of specially designed Griggs forceps are inserted over the guidewire instead of the dilator(s). These forceps are used to dilate the trachea. A tracheostomy tube is threaded over the guidewire and advanced into the trachea. The guidewire is then removed [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
5. An adjustable flange percutaneous tracheostomy is available. The flange can be adjusted to suit the patient's anatomy, e.g., in the obese patient. The flange can be moved away from the stoma site to aid in cleaning around the stoma [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
6. The procedure can be performed in the intensive care unit with a lower incidence of complications than the conventional open surgical method (infection rate, subglottic stenosis and bleeding problems). The operative time is about half that of a formal surgical procedure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
7. Percutaneous tracheostomy can be performed faster using the dilating forceps technique compared to the dilator technique [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
8. There is an increased risk of surgical emphysema due to air leaks from the trachea to the surrounding tissues. Loss of the airway, bleeding and incorrect placement of the needle are potential difficulties during the procedure. The risk of aspiration is increased when the tracheal tube has to be withdrawn at the start of the procedure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
9. Relative contraindications include enlarged thyroid gland, non-palpable cricoid cartilage, paediatric application, previous neck surgery and positive end expiratory pressure (PEEP) of more than 15 cm H₂O. The latter is because of the difficulty in applying a high PEEP during the process of insertion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
10. Reinsertion of a percutaneously fashioned tube can be more difficult than the surgical one as the stoma may close immediately. A track is formed after long-term intubation, and the tracheostomy tube can be removed. In order to protect the patency of the tract, a tracheostomy button is inserted into the stoma. It also acts as a route for tracheal suction. Tracheostomy buttons are made of straight rigid plastic [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

### 14.1.4. MINITRACHEOSTOMY AND CRICOTHYROTOMY

**Minitracheostomy tube:**
This tube is inserted percutaneously into the trachea through the avascular cricothyroid membrane [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] .

**Components:**
1. A siliconized PVC tube 10 cm in length with an ID of 4 mm. Some designs have lengths ranging from 3.5 to 7.5 cm with IDs from 2 to 6 mm [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
2. The proximal end of the tube has a standard 15-mm connector that allows attachment to breathing systems. The proximal end also has wings used to secure the tube with the ribbon supplied [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
3. A 2-cm, 16-G needle is used to puncture the cricothyroid cartilage. A 50-cm guidewire is used to help in the tracheal cannulation. A 10-mL syringe is used to aspirate air to confirm the correct placement of the needle [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
4. A 7-cm curved dilator and a curved introducer are used to facilitate the insertion of the cricothyrotomy tube in some designs [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

**Mechanism of action:**
1. The Seldinger technique is used to insert the tube [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
2. It is an effective method to clear tracheobronchial secretions in patients with an inefficient cough [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
3. In an emergency, it can be used to administer oxygen in patients with upper airway obstruction that cannot be bypassed with a tracheal tube [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

**Problems and safety features:**
* Percutaneous insertion of minitracheostomy has the risk of pneumothorax, perforation of the oesophagus, severe haemorrhage, ossification of the cricothyroid membrane, and incorrect placement [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

**Cricothyrotomy tube:**
This tube is used to maintain the airway in challenging situations such as on the battlefield or in a 'can't intubate, can't oxygenate' (CICO) emergency. It is inserted into the trachea through the cricothyroid cartilage [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] .

**Components:**
1. A scalpel and syringe [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
2. A needle with a Veress design and a dilator. The needle has a 'red flag' indicator. This helps in locating the tissues [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
3. A 6-mm cuffed tube [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

**Mechanism of action:**
1. After a 2-cm horizontal skin incision has been made, the needle is inserted perpendicular to the skin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
2. As the needle enters the trachea, the red indicator disappears. The needle is advanced carefully until the red reappears, indicating contact with the posterior wall of the trachea [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
3. As the cricothyrotomy tube is advanced into the trachea, the needle and the dilator are removed [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

**Problems and safety features:**
* The cricothyrotomy tube has complications similar to the minitracheostomy tube [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

## 14.2. DOUBLE LUMEN ENDOBRONCHIAL TUBES

**During thoracic surgery, one lung needs to be deflated.** This offers the surgeon easier and better access within the designated hemithorax. In order to achieve this, double lumen tubes are used, which allows the anaesthetist to selectively deflate one lung while maintaining standard ventilation of the other [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] .

### 14.2.1. COMPONENTS AND MECHANISM OF ACTION

**Components:**
1. The double lumen tube has two separate colour-coded lumens, each with its own bevel. One lumen ends in the trachea and the other lumen ends in either the left or right main bronchus. Blue colour is usually used for the bronchial lumen [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
2. Each lumen has its own cuff (tracheal and bronchial cuffs) and colour-coded pilot balloons; blue for the bronchial components. Both lumens and pilot balloons are labelled [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
3. There are two curves to the tube: the standard anterior curve to fit into the oropharyngeal laryngeal tracheal airway and the second curve, either to the right or left, to fit into the right or left bronchus, respectively [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
4. The proximal end of these tubes is connected to a Y-shaped catheter mount attached to the breathing system [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
5. More recent designs have a high-definition camera positioned at the tip of the tracheal lumen. This allows visual confirmation of correct positioning during insertion of the tube. Since per-op tube displacement is a problem, the continuous real-time monitoring of the tube position is invaluable. An extra port is used to 'jet clean' the camera lens by flushing saline, removing unwanted secretions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

![Rüsch right-sided double lumen tube](AIRWAY_pdf_AIRWAY.pdf_p47_img1_fcf63c.jpeg)

**Mechanism of action:**
1. Because of the differing anatomy of the main bronchi and their branches, both right and left versions of double lumen tubes must exist [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
2. Once the tubes are correctly positioned, the anaesthetist can selectively ventilate one lung. So for procedures requiring that the right lung is deflated, a left-sided double lumen tube would be used that enabled selective ventilation of the left lung alone and vice versa [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
3. It is desirable, when possible, to insert a left double lumen tube instead of a right one. This reduces the risk of upper lobe bronchus obstruction by the bronchial cuff in the right-sided version [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
4. The right-sided version has an eye in the bronchial cuff to facilitate ventilation of the right upper lobe. The distance between the right upper lobe bronchus and the carina in an adult is only 2.5 cm, so there is a real risk of occluding it with the bronchial cuff. There is no eye in the left-sided version because the distance between the carina and the left upper lobe bronchus is about 5 cm, which is adequate to place the cuff [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
5. The tubes come in different sizes to fit adult patients, but they do not come in paediatric sizes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

### 14.2.2. TUBE POSITIONING

1. The position of the tube should be checked by auscultation immediately after intubation and after positioning the patient for the operation. It is also recommended to use a bronchoscope to confirm correct positioning of the double lumen tube. Designs with a camera can visually confirm the position during insertion and throughout the procedure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
2. The tracheal cuff is inflated first until no leak is heard. At this point, both lungs can be ventilated. Next, the tracheal limb of the Y-catheter mount is clamped and disconnected from the tracheal lumen tube. Then the bronchial cuff is inflated with only a few millilitres of air until no leak is heard from the tracheal tube. At this stage, only the lung ventilated via the bronchial lumen should be ventilated. The ability to selectively ventilate the other lung should also be checked by clamping the bronchial limb of the Y-catheter mount and disconnecting it from the bronchial lumen having already reconnected the tracheal lumen. At this stage, only the lung ventilated via the tracheal lumen should be ventilated [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

**The commonly used double lumen bronchial tubes are:**
1. **Robertshaw (rubber) tubes**
2. **Single-use plastic tubes**. These tubes require an introducer for insertion. Some designs the facility of applying continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) to the deflated lung to improve arterial oxygenation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
3. **Carlens (left-sided version) and White (right-sided version) tubes** that use a carinal hook to aid final positioning of the tube. The hook can cause trauma to the larynx or carina. Because of the relatively small lumens (6 and 8 mm), the Carlens tube causes an increase in airway resistance and difficulty in suctioning thick secretions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]]

## 14.3. ENDOBRONCHIAL BLOCKERS

The endobronchial blocker is an alternative means to the double lumen tube for providing one-lung ventilation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] .

**Components:**
1. The endobronchial blocker catheter. This is a thin catheter that has a unique bifurcated distal end. The pair of colour-coded distal cuffs are inflated via their individual pilot balloon [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 
2. Multiport adaptor. This allows ergonomic alignment of the bronchoscope port and endobronchial blocker port to approach the single lumen tube. The fresh gas supply is also attached at an angle [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] 

**Mechanism of action:**
The patient is intubated with as large a single lumen standard tracheal tube as is possible. A specially designed multiport adapter is connected to the tube's standard 15-mm connector. The blocker is then advanced under direct vision of a narrow bronchoscope into the desired position, straddling the carina. The selected cuff is then inflated to block the corresponding main bronchus while maintaining ventilation to the contralateral side. Ventilation of the contralateral lung is maintained via the tracheal tube [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#4.1 Head and Neck (Including Airway)|ⓘ]] .
