# 1. ACID-BASE PHYSIOLOGY

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|Part A: Acid Base Physiology]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|Part B: C1]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C: Medical Knowledge]]

## 1.2. BASIC CONCEPTS AND DEFINITIONS

**Acid-base homeostasis is the maintenance of a relatively constant pH in body fluids despite constant acid production from metabolism.**

### 1.2.1. ACID-BASE DEFINITIONS

* **Brønsted-Lowry Definition:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Acid:** A proton donor - a substance that donates hydrogen ions (H+) in solution
	* Strong acids (e.g., HCl) completely dissociate in solution
	* Weak acids (e.g., carbonic acid) partially dissociate in solution
  * **Base:** A proton acceptor - a substance that accepts hydrogen ions (H+) in solution
* **pH:** The negative logarithm of hydrogen ion concentration. pH = -log10[H+] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Normal blood pH range: 7.35-7.45 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * For every pH change of 1 unit, there is a 10-fold change in hydrogen ion concentration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
	* pH 7.4 = 40 nmol/L H+
	* pH 7.3 = 50 nmol/L H+
	* pH 7.0 = 100 nmol/L H+
* **pKa:** The negative logarithm of the acid dissociation constant (Ka) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Represents the strength of an acid
  * Equals the pH at which equal concentrations of the acid and its conjugate base are present
  * Low pKa (high Ka) = stronger acid (more dissociation)
  * High pKa (low Ka) = weaker acid (less dissociation)
* **Ka and pKa Relationship:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * For the equilibrium: HA ⇌ H+ + A-
  * Ka = [H+][A-]/[HA]
  * pKa = -log10Ka

### 1.2.2. CLINICAL TERMINOLOGY AND SIGNIFICANCE

* **Acidemia vs. Acidosis:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Acidemia:** State of blood pH being below 7.35 (actual measured condition)
  * **Acidosis:** Process that would cause pH to decrease below 7.35 without compensation
  * Similar distinction applies to alkalemia (pH > 7.45) vs. alkalosis
* **Clinical Significance of pH:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Proteins denature and lose function outside normal pH range
  * Affects enzymatic reactions, ion channel function, and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation
  * Disrupts cellular function and ultimately organ performance
  * Small pH changes represent large changes in H+ concentration
* **Acid-Base Relationship to pH:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * When [HA] = [A-], pH = pKa
  * Henderson-Hasselbalch equation: pH = pKa + log10([A-]/[HA])

**Bottom Line:** pH represents the negative logarithm of H+ concentration, with normal blood values between 7.35-7.45. Small numerical changes in pH represent large changes in H+ concentration, with significant clinical consequences when outside the normal range. The Brønsted-Lowry definition defines acids as proton donors and bases as proton acceptors.

## 1.3. NORMAL ACID PRODUCTION AND ELIMINATION

**The body continuously produces acids that must be buffered and eliminated to maintain pH homeostasis.**

* **Daily Acid Production:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Volatile acids:** 10,000-15,000 mmol/day of CO2 from cellular metabolism
	* Produced primarily through aerobic metabolism and the citric acid cycle
	* Continuously generated during cellular respiration
  * **Nonvolatile (fixed) acids:** 50-100 mEq/day from protein metabolism and other sources
	* Includes sulfuric acid (from sulfur-containing amino acids)
	* Phosphoric acid (from phospholipids)
	* Organic acids (lactic acid, ketoacids)
* **Acid Elimination Pathways:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Respiratory system:** Eliminates volatile acids (CO2) via the lungs
	* 10,000-15,000 mmol/day of CO2 eliminated through alveolar ventilation
	* Rate can be rapidly adjusted based on metabolic demands
  * **Renal system:** Eliminates fixed acids via the kidneys
	* 50-100 mEq/day of fixed acids eliminated
	* Achieved through H+ secretion, ammoniagenesis, and phosphate buffering
  * **Buffering systems:** Temporarily neutralize acids until they can be eliminated
* **Acid Handling Timeline:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Immediate (seconds):** Chemical buffers act
	* Bicarbonate, proteins, phosphates, and hemoglobin provide immediate buffering
  * **Minutes:** Respiratory compensation begins
	* Changes in ventilation can alter CO2 levels rapidly
  * **Hours to days:** Renal compensation develops
	* More complete but slower adaptation
  * **Variable time:** Treatment of underlying cause

**Bottom Line:** The body generates approximately 10,000-15,000 mmol of volatile acid (CO2) and 50-100 mEq of nonvolatile acid daily, which are buffered in cells and extracellular fluid before being eliminated via the lungs and kidneys respectively. This continuous process requires coordinated function of buffer systems, respiratory system, and kidneys to maintain pH homeostasis.

## 1.4. ACID-BASE BUFFER SYSTEMS

**Buffer systems rapidly neutralize acids or bases to minimize pH changes while awaiting physiological compensation.**

* **Buffer Definition:** A substance that resists change in pH by releasing or absorbing hydrogen ions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Typically consists of a weak acid and its conjugate base
  * Most effective at pH values near its pKa (within 1 pH unit)
  * Act immediately to minimize pH changes before respiratory and renal mechanisms respond
* **Buffer Power:** The quantity of acid or base needed to change pH by 1 unit [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Depends on buffer concentration, pKa proximity to physiological pH, and whether it's an open or closed system
  * Higher buffer concentration = greater buffer capacity
 
* **Major Buffer Systems:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Bicarbonate-Carbonic Acid System (Extracellular):** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
	* Main extracellular buffer (pKa = 6.1)
	* Open system allowing regulation via respiratory (CO2) and renal (HCO3-) mechanisms
	* Reaction: CO2 + H2O ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ H+ + HCO3-
	* Can buffer approximately 18 mmol H+
	* Most important buffer due to:
	  * High concentration in plasma
	  * Ability to be regulated by both lungs and kidneys
	
  * **Hemoglobin System:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
	* Primary buffer within red blood cells
	* Multiple mechanisms of buffering:
	  * Histidine residues (38 per hemoglobin molecule, pKa ≈ 6.8)
	  * Reduced hemoglobin is a more effective buffer than oxyhemoglobin (Haldane effect)
	  * Forms carbamino compounds with CO2
	* Can buffer approximately 8 mmol H+
	
  * **Protein Buffer System:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
	* Proteins contain amino groups (pKa ≈ 9) and carboxyl groups (pKa ≈ 2)
	* Important intracellularly where protein concentration is high
	* Plasma proteins can buffer approximately 1.7 mmol H+
	* Both positively and negatively charged groups can participate in buffering
	
  * **Phosphate Buffer System:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
	* pKa = 6.8, making it effective near physiological pH
	* Limited capacity in extracellular fluid due to low concentration
	* More important intracellularly and in renal tubular fluid
	* Can buffer approximately 0.3 mmol H+
	* Reaction: H2PO4- ⇌ H+ + HPO42-
* **Isohydric Principle:** All buffer systems within the same environment are in equilibrium with the same H+ concentration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Changes in one buffer system affect all others
  * Ensures coordinated buffering throughout the body
* **Relative Importance of Buffer Systems:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Extracellular:** Bicarbonate > plasma proteins > phosphate
  * **Intracellular:** Proteins > phosphate > bicarbonate > hemoglobin (in RBCs)
  * **Overall:** Bicarbonate system most important due to regulation by both respiratory and renal systems

**Bottom Line:** Multiple buffer systems work together to immediately neutralize excess acids or bases. The bicarbonate-carbonic acid system is the primary extracellular buffer due to its open nature (regulated by lungs and kidneys), while intracellular buffering relies heavily on proteins, hemoglobin, and phosphates. These systems provide the first line of defense against pH changes, buying time for respiratory and renal compensatory mechanisms.

## 1.5. HENDERSON-HASSELBALCH EQUATION

**The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation describes the relationship between pH, buffer components, and their dissociation constant.**

* **Original Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * pH = pKa + log([Base]/[Acid])
  * pH = pKa + log([HCO3-]/[H2CO3])
  * For bicarbonate system: pH = 6.1 + log([HCO3-]/(0.03 × PCO2))
	* Where 6.1 is the pKa of carbonic acid
	* 0.03 is the solubility coefficient of CO2 at 37°C
	* PCO2 is measured in mmHg

![Original HH equation; 6.1 is the pKa of carbonic acid and 0.3 is the blood solubility coefficient of carbon dioxide at 37°C. Plasma pH is directly determined by concentration of CO2 and HCO3.](ABG_ABG_image.png_58a136.png_561a5b.png)

* **Significance of the Equation:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Demonstrates that pH depends on the ratio of bicarbonate to dissolved CO2
  * Shows how respiratory system (controls PCO2) and kidneys (control HCO3-) regulate pH
  * Explains why the body can maintain normal pH despite large changes in absolute values
* **Simplified Clinical Formula:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * H+ (nmol/L) = 24 × (PCO2/HCO3-)
  * Direct relationship between [H+], PCO2, and HCO3-
  * Linear approximation: [H+] ≈ 80 - (last two digits of pH × 10)
	* At pH 7.4: [H+] ≈ 80 - (40) = 40 nmol/L

![Linear method: 80 - last 2 digits of pH](ABG_ABG_Untitled.png_2b575e.png_db2515.png)

* **Normal Plasma Values:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * pH = 7.4
  * PCO2 = 40 mmHg (5.3 kPa)
  * HCO3- = 24 mmol/L
  * These values provide the baseline for assessing acid-base disorders
* **Clinical Application:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Verifies internal consistency of ABG values
  * Predicts pH changes based on variations in PCO2 or HCO3-
  * Helps distinguish respiratory from metabolic disorders
  * Estimates expected compensatory responses

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation shows that pH depends on the ratio of bicarbonate to carbon dioxide, not their absolute values. This explains why patients with chronic respiratory disorders can have normal pH despite markedly abnormal PCO2 and HCO3values.

**Bottom Line:** The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation mathematically relates pH to the ratio of bicarbonate and dissolved CO2, serving as the foundation for understanding acid-base disorders and verifying ABG result consistency. It explains how the lungs (controlling PCO2) and kidneys (regulating HCO3-) work together to maintain acid-base homeostasis.

## 1.6. REGULATION OF ACID-BASE BALANCE

**Acid-base balance is maintained through coordinated action of chemical buffers, respiratory system, and renal mechanisms.**

* **Integrated Response to Acid-Base Disturbances:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Chemical Buffers:** Immediate response (seconds)
  * **Respiratory System:** Rapid compensation (minutes)
  * **Renal System:** Complete compensation (hours to days)
  * Each system has distinct timeframes, mechanisms, and limitations
* **Respiratory Regulation:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * **Mechanism:** Alters alveolar ventilation to change PCO2
	* ↑ ventilation = ↓ PCO2 = ↑ pH
	* ↓ ventilation = ↑ PCO2 = ↓ pH
  * **Control:** Central (medullary) and peripheral (carotid/aortic) chemoreceptors
	* Central chemoreceptors respond to CSF pH changes (80% of ventilatory drive)
	  * Located in ventrolateral medulla
	  * Detect H+ in CSF, which equilibrates with plasma via blood-brain barrier
	* Peripheral chemoreceptors respond to arterial pH and PO2 changes (20% of ventilatory drive)
	  * Located in carotid bodies and aortic arch
	  * Detect arterial pH directly
  * **Response:**
	* Acidemia → ↑ ventilation → ↓ PCO2 → ↑ pH
	* Alkalemia → ↓ ventilation → ↑ PCO2 → ↓ pH
  * **Limitations:**
	* Cannot completely correct metabolic disorders
	* Constrained by hypoxic drive (PO2 must be maintained)
	* Limited by physical capabilities and other respiratory demands
	* May be impaired by lung disease, neuromuscular disorders, or CNS depression
* **Renal Regulation:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Mechanisms:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
	* Bicarbonate reabsorption and generation
	* Hydrogen ion secretion
	* Ammonia production and secretion
	* Titratable acid excretion (primarily phosphate)
  * **Transport Systems:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
	* Primary active transport: H+ATPase pumps H+ into tubular lumen
	* Secondary active transport: Na+/H+ antiporter exchanges Na+ for H+
	* Bicarbonate handling via carbonic anhydrase
  * **Specific Processes:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
	* **Bicarbonate Reabsorption:**
	  * 80% reabsorbed in proximal tubule
	  * Process involves:
		1. H+ secretion into tubular lumen
		2. H+ combines with HCO3forming H2CO3
		3. Carbonic anhydrase converts H2CO3 to CO2 + H2O
		4. CO2 diffuses into cell where reverse reaction occurs
		5. HCO3moves into blood, H+ is secreted back into lumen

![Renal regulation of acid-base balance](ABG_ABG_nse-8749538897384763986-113154.jpg_761cad.jpg_9f1234.jpg)

    * **Ammoniagenesis:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
      * Glutamine metabolism in proximal tubule cells produces NH4+ and HCO3-
      * NH4+ is excreted in urine, HCO3- returns to blood
      * Process increases in acidosis, producing more HCO3-
      * Key steps:
        1. Glutamine metabolism produces NH4+ and HCO3-
        2. NH4+ secreted into tubular lumen
        3. In medullary interstitium, NH4+ converts to NH3
        4. NH3 diffuses into collecting duct
        5. NH3 combines with H+ to form NH4+ which is excreted

* **Hepatic Contribution:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Metabolism of organic acids
  * Synthesis of plasma proteins (buffers)
  * Regulation of ureagenesis (produces CO2)
	* Reduced during acidosis
	* Increased during alkalosis
  * Glutamine synthesis and metabolism
  * Lactate metabolism
* **Acid-Base Regulation in Different Conditions:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Metabolic Acidosis:**
	* Immediate chemical buffering
	* Respiratory compensation: increased ventilation (Kussmaul breathing)
	* Renal compensation: increased HCO3reabsorption, NH4+ excretion, and H+ secretion
  * **Metabolic Alkalosis:**
	* Chemical buffering
	* Respiratory compensation: decreased ventilation (limited by hypoxic drive)
	* Renal compensation: decreased HCO3reabsorption, increased HCO3excretion
  * **Respiratory Acidosis:**
	* Chemical buffering
	* Renal compensation: increased HCO3reabsorption and generation
  * **Respiratory Alkalosis:**
	* Chemical buffering
	* Renal compensation: decreased HCO3reabsorption, increased HCO3excretion

**Bottom Line:** Acid-base regulation involves multiple systems working in sequence: chemical buffers provide immediate neutralization (seconds), respiratory compensation adjusts CO2 levels (minutes), and renal mechanisms restore bicarbonate balance (hours to days). The liver contributes through metabolic processes and protein synthesis. These systems ensure pH homeostasis despite continuous acid production and various physiological challenges.

## 1.7. TEMPERATURE EFFECTS ON PH

**Blood pH changes with temperature due to altered equilibrium constants and gas solubility.**

* **Basic Principle:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Water dissociation is temperature-dependent: H2O ⇌ H+ + OH-
  * Lower temperatures shift equilibrium left, reducing free H+ concentration
  * Higher temperatures shift equilibrium right, increasing free H+ concentration
* **Quantitative Relationship:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Blood pH increases by approximately 0.015 units for every 1°C decrease in temperature
  * Blood pH decreases by approximately 0.015 units for every 1°C increase in temperature
* **Effects on Blood Gas Measurements:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Blood gas analyzers operate at 37°C
  * For hypothermic patients, measured values differ from actual in vivo values:
	* Measured pH appears lower than actual in vivo pH
	* Measured PCO2 appears higher than actual in vivo PCO2
  * Mathematical correction can be applied to determine actual values at patient's temperature
* **Clinical Relevance - Hypothermic Cardiopulmonary Bypass:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **pH-stat Method:**
	* Adds CO2 to maintain normal pH when corrected to patient's temperature
	* Potential benefits:
	  * Counteracts leftward shift of oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve
	  * May improve oxygen delivery during hypothermia
	* Potential risks:
	  * Causes cerebral vasodilation
	  * Increases cerebral blood flow
	  * May increase embolic load and neurological complications
	  * Abolishes cerebral autoregulation
  * **Alpha-stat Method:**
	* Permits alkaline drift with hypothermia
	* Targets normal pH at 37°C (not corrected for temperature)
	* Accepts that actual in vivo pH will be alkalotic
	* Potential benefits:
	  * Maintains cellular electrochemical neutrality
	  * Preserves enzyme function
	  * Maintains cerebral autoregulation
	* Current practice in most centers
* **Controversy in Optimal Management:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Ongoing debate about pH-stat versus alpha-stat approach
  * Different techniques may be optimal for different procedures:
	* pH-stat may be better for deep hypothermic circulatory arrest
	* Alpha-stat may be better for moderate hypothermia during routine cardiac surgery

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** When interpreting blood gases in hypothermic patients, be aware that uncorrected values may not reflect the actual in vivo situation. The decision to use temperature-corrected values (pH-stat) or uncorrected values (alpha-stat) depends on clinical context and institutional protocol.

**Bottom Line:** Blood pH increases as temperature decreases due to altered dissociation constants. This has significant implications for blood gas interpretation in hypothermic patients and for management during cardiopulmonary bypass. The choice between pH-stat and alpha-stat approaches remains controversial, with each offering potential advantages in different clinical scenarios.

# 2. ABG INTERPRETATION - SYSTEMATIC APPROACH




## 2.2. STEP 1: VERIFY INTERNAL CONSISTENCY
**Ensuring ABG values are internally consistent is the first step in accurate interpretation.**

* **Method:** Use Henderson-Hasselbalch equation to verify consistency between measured pH, PCO2, and HCO3- [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * H+ (nmol/L) = 24 × (PCO2/HCO3-)
  * Alternative linear approximation: [H+] ≈ 80 - (last two digits of pH × 10)
  * Expected relationships:
	* pH ∝ 1/[H+]
	* [H+] ∝ PCO2/HCO3-
	* PCO2 ∝ [H+] and ∝ 1/pH
	* HCO3- ∝ 1/[H+] and ∝ pH
* **Consistency Checking:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Calculate expected [H+] from PCO2 and HCO3values
  * Compare with [H+] derived from measured pH
  * If significant discrepancy exists, consider:
	* Laboratory error
	* Specimen collection issues (air bubbles, delayed analysis)
	* Analyzer calibration problems
	* Transcription errors

![](ABG_ABG_Untitled_1.png_f4dccf.png_ca8d58.png)

* **Example Calculation:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * For ABG with pH 7.35, PCO2 40 mmHg, HCO322 mmol/L:
	* Expected [H+] = 24 × (40/22) = 43.6 nmol/L
	* [H+] from pH = 80 - (35 × 10) = 45 nmol/L
	* Values are consistent (minimal discrepancy)
* **Handling Inconsistencies:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Minor discrepancies (<5%): Likely due to rounding or measurement variability
  * Significant discrepancies (>10%): Consider repeating ABG or questioning validity
  * Critical discrepancies (>20%): Highly suggestive of pre-analytical or analytical error

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Always verify consistency before proceeding with interpretation. Inconsistent values may lead to diagnostic errors and inappropriate treatment. The most common sources of error are air bubbles in the sample, delayed analysis, or analyzer malfunction.

**Bottom Line:** Verifying internal consistency using the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation is essential before proceeding with ABG interpretation. Significant discrepancies suggest potential laboratory or specimen collection errors that require investigation before making clinical decisions based on the results.

## 2.3. STEP 2: IDENTIFY ACIDEMIA OR ALKALEMIA

**Determine the primary acid-base status based on arterial pH.**

* **pH Classification:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * **Acidemia:** pH < 7.35
  * **Normal:** pH 7.35-7.45
  * **Alkalemia:** pH > 7.45
* **Severity Classification:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * **Mild acidemia:** pH 7.30-7.35
  * **Moderate acidemia:** pH 7.20-7.30
  * **Severe acidemia:** pH < 7.20 (clinically significant)
  * **Mild alkalemia:** pH 7.45-7.50
  * **Moderate alkalemia:** pH 7.50-7.60
  * **Severe alkalemia:** pH > 7.60 (clinically significant)

![](ABG_ABG_Untitled_2.png_596c7a.png_fee393.png)

* **Clinical Significance of pH Derangements:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * **Severe Acidemia (pH < 7.20):**
	* Impaired myocardial contractility
	* Arrhythmias
	* Decreased response to catecholamines
	* Systemic vasodilation
	* Mental status changes
  * **Severe Alkalemia (pH > 7.60):**
	* Coronary and cerebral vasoconstriction
	* Increased neuromuscular excitability
	* Seizures, tetany
	* Arrhythmias
	* Decreased oxygen delivery to tissues
* **Normal pH with Abnormal PCO2/HCO3-:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Suggests a mixed disorder with opposing effects on pH
  * Possibilities:
	* Respiratory acidosis with metabolic alkalosis
	* Respiratory alkalosis with metabolic acidosis
  * Requires careful evaluation of additional parameters

![](ABG_ABG_Untitled_3.png_a8b83e.png_4306a1.png)

![Untitled](ABG_ABG_Untitled_4.png_3796ea.png_af5bc7.png)

* **Severe pH Derangements:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * pH < 7.20 or > 7.60 often indicates combined respiratory and metabolic disorders in the same direction
  * Requires urgent intervention
  * May indicate presence of two primary disorders working in same direction
	* Example: Combined respiratory and metabolic acidosis in cardiac arrest

⚠️ **Safety Warning:** Severe acidemia (pH < 7.20) or alkalemia (pH > 7.60) are medical emergencies requiring immediate intervention. These extreme derangements suggest either very severe single disorders or combined disorders acting in the same direction. Patient outcomes worsen significantly as pH moves further from the normal range.

**Bottom Line:** Identifying acidemia (pH < 7.35) or alkalemia (pH > 7.45) is the second step in ABG interpretation. Normal pH with abnormal PCO2 and HCO3suggests mixed acid-base disorders with opposing effects. Severe pH derangements indicate either profound single disorders or multiple disorders acting in the same direction and require urgent assessment and intervention.

## 2.4. STEP 3: DETERMINE PRIMARY DISTURBANCE

**Identify whether the primary disorder is respiratory or metabolic by examining PCO2 and HCO3values.**

* **Basic Principles:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * **CO2 is acidic:** ↑ PCO2 → ↓ pH (respiratory acidosis)
  * **HCO3is alkaline:** ↑ HCO3- → ↑ pH (metabolic alkalosis)
  * Primary disturbance usually shows the most marked abnormality
  * Changes in PCO2 reflect respiratory component
  * Changes in HCO3reflect metabolic component
* **Decision Rules:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * **Respiratory Acidosis:** ↑ PCO2 (>45 mmHg) + ↓ pH
  * **Respiratory Alkalosis:** ↓ PCO2 (<35 mmHg) + ↑ pH
  * **Metabolic Acidosis:** ↓ HCO3- (<22 mEq/L) + ↓ pH
  * **Metabolic Alkalosis:** ↑ HCO3- (>28 mEq/L) + ↑ pH

![](ABG_ABG_Untitled_5.png_7f0702.png_770e29.png)

* **pH and Primary Disturbance:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * Consider which parameter (PCO2 or HCO3-) best explains the observed pH
  * In isolated disorders:
	* If pH is low and PCO2 is high → Respiratory acidosis
	* If pH is high and PCO2 is low → Respiratory alkalosis
	* If pH is low and HCO3is low → Metabolic acidosis
	* If pH is high and HCO3is high → Metabolic alkalosis
* **Mixed Disorders - Pattern Recognition:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * **High PCO2 and High HCO3with acidemia:** Primary respiratory acidosis with secondary metabolic compensation
  * **Low PCO2 with Low HCO3with alkalemia:** Primary respiratory alkalosis with secondary metabolic compensation
  * **PCO2 and HCO3moving in opposite directions:** Suggests mixed primary disorders
	* High PCO2 and Low HCO3with severe acidemia: Combined respiratory and metabolic acidosis
	* Low PCO2 and High HCO3with severe alkalemia: Combined respiratory and metabolic alkalosis
* **Direction of Change Concept:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C1 Airway Oxygenation Ventilation|ⓘ]]
  * In a single disorder with compensation:
	* PCO2 and HCO3move in the same direction
  * In mixed disorders:
	* PCO2 and HCO3often move in opposite directions
  * Exception: Two primary disorders in the same direction (both acidosis or both alkalosis)

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** When PCO2 and HCO3change in the same direction, it usually indicates a primary disorder with compensation. When they change in opposite directions, it suggests mixed primary disorders. Remember: respiratory parameters mainly affect PCO2 while metabolic parameters mainly affect HCO3-.

**Bottom Line:** Determining the primary disturbance involves examining whether abnormalities in PCO2 (respiratory) or HCO3- (metabolic) best explain the pH change. PCO2 and HCO3typically move in the same direction during compensated single disorders and in opposite directions during mixed disorders. This pattern recognition helps identify the primary problem and guides further diagnostic workup.

## 2.5. STEP 4: ASSESS COMPENSATION ADEQUACY

**Evaluate whether the compensatory response is appropriate for the primary disorder.**

* **Expected Compensatory Responses:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Body attempts to normalize pH through secondary mechanisms
  * Compensation is rarely complete (pH doesn't fully normalize)
  * Predictable relationships exist between primary and compensatory changes
  * Time course varies by mechanism (respiratory faster than renal)

![image.png](ABG_ABG_image_1.png_0eb662.png_eb78a0.png)

* **Respiratory Acidosis Compensation:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Acute:** For every PCO2 increase by 10 mmHg, HCO3increases by **1** mEq/L
	* Example: PCO2 = 60 mmHg → Expected HCO3- = 24 + 2 = 26 mEq/L
	* Timeframe: Minutes to hours
	* Mechanism: Chemical buffering
  * **Chronic:** For every PCO2 increase by 10 mmHg, HCO3increases by **4** mEq/L
	* Example: PCO2 = 60 mmHg → Expected HCO3- = 24 + 8 = 32 mEq/L
	* Timeframe: 3-5 days
	* Mechanism: Renal adaptation with increased H+ secretion and HCO3reabsorption
* **Respiratory Alkalosis Compensation:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Acute:** For every PCO2 decrease by 10 mmHg, HCO3decreases by **2** mEq/L
	* Example: PCO2 = 20 mmHg → Expected HCO3- = 24 - 4 = 20 mEq/L
	* Timeframe: Minutes to hours
	* Mechanism: Chemical buffering
  * **Chronic:** For every PCO2 decrease by 10 mmHg, HCO3decreases by **5** mEq/L
	* Example: PCO2 = 20 mmHg → Expected HCO3- = 24 - 10 = 14 mEq/L
	* Timeframe: 3-5 days
	* Mechanism: Renal adaptation with decreased H+ secretion and HCO3reabsorption
* **Metabolic Acidosis Compensation:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Winter's Formula:** Expected PCO2 = 1.5 × HCO3- + 8 (±2)
	* Example: HCO3- = 15 mEq/L → Expected PCO2 = 1.5 × 15 + 8 = 30.5 mmHg
	* Alternative: For every 1 mEq/L decrease in HCO3-, PCO2 decreases by ~1.2 mmHg
  * Reflects respiratory system's attempt to normalize pH by hyperventilation
  * Timeframe: Minutes to hours (begins quickly, maximum effect in 12-24 hours)
  * Mechanism: Hyperventilation stimulated by peripheral chemoreceptors
* **Metabolic Alkalosis Compensation:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * For every HCO3increase by 1 mEq/L, PCO2 increases by 0.75 mmHg
  * Alternative: PCO2 = 0.7 × HCO3- + 21 mmHg (±2)
  * Or: PCO2 = 0.9 × HCO3- + 16 mmHg (±2)
	* Example: HCO3- = 40 mEq/L → Expected PCO2 = 0.7 × 40 + 21 = 49 mmHg
  * Timeframe: Hours (limited by hypoxic drive)
  * Mechanism: Hypoventilation (limited by need to maintain oxygenation)
* **Inappropriate Compensation Suggests Mixed Disorder:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * If PCO2 > predicted in metabolic acidosis: Concomitant respiratory acidosis
  * If PCO2 < predicted in metabolic acidosis: Concomitant respiratory alkalosis
  * If HCO3changes more than expected for respiratory disorder: Additional metabolic component
  * Compensation outside of expected range strongly suggests mixed disorder

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Compensation takes time to develop. Full renal compensation for respiratory disorders requires 3-5 days, while respiratory compensation for metabolic disorders occurs within minutes to hours. An "inappropriate" compensatory response most likely indicates a second primary acid-base disorder.

⚠️ **Safety Warning:** Inappropriate or inadequate compensation for the degree of primary disorder strongly suggests the presence of a mixed acid-base disturbance requiring broader diagnostic consideration. Never assume that normal or near-normal pH indicates absence of acid-base disturbance.

**Bottom Line:** Assessing compensation adequacy involves comparing observed compensatory changes with expected values based on established formulas. Inappropriate compensation indicates mixed acid-base disorders, requiring investigation for multiple underlying pathologies. Understanding the expected degree and timeframe of compensation helps distinguish between single disorders with appropriate compensation versus more complex mixed disorders.

## 2.6. STEP 5: CALCULATE ANION GAP (IN METABOLIC ACIDOSIS)

**In metabolic acidosis, calculate the anion gap to differentiate between high anion gap and normal anion gap types.**

* **Anion Gap Definition:** Difference between measured cations and measured anions [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * AG = [Na+] - ([Cl-] + [HCO3-])
  * Represents unmeasured anions in serum (proteins, phosphates, sulfates, organic acids)
  * Normal range: 8-12 mmol/L (or 3-11 mmol/L depending on laboratory)

![](ABG_ABG_Untitled_6.png_b1b97b.png_9f93e8.png)

* **Anion Gap Interpretation:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **High AG (>12 mmol/L):** Accumulation of unmeasured anions
	* Indicates addition of acid with unmeasured anions
	* Examples: lactic acidosis, ketoacidosis, toxic ingestions
  * **Normal AG (8-12 mmol/L):** Bicarbonate loss or chloride excess
	* Indicates loss of bicarbonate or gain of chloride with measured anions
	* Examples: diarrhea, renal tubular acidosis, normal saline infusion

![](ABG_ABG_Untitled_7.png_870037.png_197309.png)

![](ABG_ABG_Untitled_8.png_d0f2c2.png_92091e.png)

* **High Anion Gap Metabolic Acidosis (HAGMA) Causes:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Mnemonic: "GOLDMARK"**
	* **G:** Glycols (ethylene glycol, propylene glycol)
	* **O:** Oxoproline (pyroglutamic acid - chronic paracetamol use)
	* **L:** L-lactate (shock, hypoperfusion, sepsis)
	* **D:** D-lactate (short bowel syndrome)
	* **M:** Methanol
	* **A:** Aspirin (salicylates)
	* **R:** Renal failure, uremia
	* **K:** Ketoacidosis (diabetic, alcoholic, starvation)
* **Normal Anion Gap Metabolic Acidosis (NAGMA) Causes:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Gastrointestinal bicarbonate loss (diarrhea, small bowel fistula)
  * Renal tubular acidosis
  * Rapid saline administration (dilutional acidosis)
  * Ureterosigmoidostomy
  * Early renal failure (retention of chloride before organic acids accumulate)
* **Albumin Correction:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Albumin is an unmeasured anion - AG falls when albumin falls
  * Expected AG = Normal AG - ((40-Albumin)/4) [Albumin in g/L]
  * For every 10 g/L decrease in albumin, expected AG decreases by 2.5 mmol/L
  * Corrected AG = Measured AG + 2.5 × ((40-measured albumin)/10)

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Always correct the anion gap for albumin, especially in critically ill patients who often have hypoalbuminemia. Failure to correct may cause you to miss a high anion gap acidosis. A seemingly normal anion gap in a hypoalbuminemic patient may actually represent a high anion gap acidosis when properly corrected.

**Bottom Line:** Calculating the anion gap is essential in evaluating metabolic acidosis, differentiating between disorders with accumulated unmeasured anions (HAGMA) and those with bicarbonate loss or chloride excess (NAGMA). Always correct the anion gap for albumin concentration to avoid misclassification, particularly in critically ill patients with low albumin levels.

## 2.7. STEP 6: EVALUATE DELTA GAP (MIXED DISORDERS)

**Evaluate the relationship between anion gap change and bicarbonate change to detect concomitant metabolic disorders.**

* **Delta Gap Calculation:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Delta Gap = Measured AG - Normal AG (typically 12)
  * Delta Ratio = Delta Gap / (24 - Measured HCO3-)
  * Measures relationship between AG increase and HCO3decrease
  * Normally, for each 1 mEq/L increase in anion gap, bicarbonate decreases by 1 mEq/L

![](ABG_ABG_Untitled_9.png_8bfffc.png_cf65ea.png)

* **Delta Ratio Interpretation:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **~1.0:** Pure high anion gap metabolic acidosis (HAGMA)
	* Each 1 mmol/L increase in unmeasured anions decreases HCO3by 1 mmol/L
	* Acceptable range: 0.8-1.2
  * **<0.8:** HAGMA plus normal anion gap metabolic acidosis (NAGMA)
	* HCO3decreased more than AG increased
	* Additional source of bicarbonate loss or chloride gain
  * **>1.2:** HAGMA plus metabolic alkalosis
	* HCO3decreased less than AG increased
	* Concurrent process generating or retaining bicarbonate
  * **Specific Patterns:**
	* <0.4: Predominant hyperchloremic NAGMA
	* 0.4-0.8: Combined HAGMA and NAGMA
	* 1.0-2.0: Pure HAGMA
	  * Lactic acidosis ~1.6
	  * DKA ~1.0 (due to urinary ketone loss)
	* > 2.0: Concurrent metabolic alkalosis or compensated chronic respiratory acidosis
* **Clinical Examples:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Example 1 - Pure HAGMA:**
	* Na+ 140, Cl100, HCO315, AG = 25
	* Delta Gap = 25 - 12 = 13
	* Delta Ratio = 13/(24-15) = 13/9 = 1.4 (within acceptable range for pure HAGMA)
  * **Example 2 - HAGMA + NAGMA:**
	* Na+ 140, Cl110, HCO310, AG = 20
	* Delta Gap = 20 - 12 = 8
	* Delta Ratio = 8/(24-10) = 8/14 = 0.57 (suggests additional NAGMA)
  * **Example 3 - HAGMA + Metabolic Alkalosis:**
	* Na+ 140, Cl95, HCO320, AG = 25
	* Delta Gap = 25 - 12 = 13
	* Delta Ratio = 13/(24-20) = 13/4 = 3.25 (suggests additional metabolic alkalosis)
* **Limitations:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Assumes normal anion gap of 12 mEq/L (laboratory variations exist)
  * Requires accurate electrolyte measurements
  * May be affected by other factors influencing anion gap (albumin, phosphate, calcium)
  * Less reliable in severe renal dysfunction

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** DKA often has a delta ratio closer to 1.0 because ketones are excreted in urine, while lactic acidosis typically has a higher ratio (~1.6) since lactate is not readily excreted. This difference in delta ratio can provide a clue to the underlying etiology of HAGMA.

**Bottom Line:** The delta ratio helps identify mixed metabolic disorders by comparing the degree of anion gap increase with bicarbonate decrease. Values significantly above or below 1.0 suggest the presence of concurrent metabolic alkalosis or normal anion gap metabolic acidosis, respectively. This additional analysis is valuable for detecting complex acid-base disturbances that might be missed by simpler approaches.

# 3. METABOLIC ACIDOSIS




## 3.2. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND CLASSIFICATION
**Metabolic acidosis is characterized by primary reduction in serum bicarbonate resulting in acidemia.**

* **Definition:** Primary decrease in serum bicarbonate (<22 mmol/L) with compensatory decrease in PCO2 and acidemia (pH <7.35) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Defined as an accumulation of acid or loss of alkali (bicarbonate)
  * Results in a fall in the ratio of bicarbonate to carbon dioxide
  * Characterized by decreased pH, low bicarbonate, and compensatory hyperventilation
* **Pathophysiological Mechanisms:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Increased acid production or intake:** Overproduction of endogenous acids or ingestion of acids
	* Endogenous: lactic acid, ketoacids, uremic acids
	* Exogenous: salicylates, methanol, ethylene glycol
  * **Decreased acid excretion:** Impaired renal elimination of daily acid load
	* Acute kidney injury
	* Chronic kidney disease
	* Renal tubular acidosis
  * **Bicarbonate loss:** Excessive loss from gastrointestinal tract or kidneys
	* Diarrhea
	* Renal tubular acidosis
	* Pancreatic or biliary fistulas
  * **Bicarbonate dilution:** Large volume resuscitation with non-bicarbonate containing fluids
	* Rapid saline administration
	* Dilutional acidosis in resuscitation
* **Classification by Anion Gap:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **High Anion Gap Metabolic Acidosis (HAGMA):** Due to accumulation of unmeasured anions
	* Anion gap >12 mmol/L
	* Represents addition of acids with unmeasured anions (e.g., lactate, ketones, toxins)
	* Examples: diabetic ketoacidosis, lactic acidosis, toxic ingestions, renal failure
  * **Normal Anion Gap Metabolic Acidosis (NAGMA):** Due to bicarbonate loss or chloride retention
	* Anion gap 8-12 mmol/L
	* Also called hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis
	* Examples: diarrhea, renal tubular acidosis, ureterosigmoidostomy, rapid saline infusion
* **Bicarbonate Deficit Calculation:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Deficit (mmol) = 0.3 × Weight (kg) × (Desired HCO3- - Current HCO3-)
  * Used to calculate replacement requirements when indicated
  * Assumes bicarbonate distribution throughout extracellular fluid (approximately 30% of body weight)

![image.png](ABG_ABG_image_2.png_9ec6ca.png_8e3b83.png)

* **Compensatory Response:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Respiratory Compensation:**
	* Hyperventilation (Kussmaul breathing) to eliminate CO2
	* Expected PCO2 = 1.5 × HCO3- + 8 (±2) (Winter's formula)
	* Begins within minutes but has physiological limitations
  * **Renal Compensation:**
	* Increased H+ excretion
	* Increased ammoniagenesis
	* Occurs over days
	* Not relevant in acute metabolic acidosis

**Bottom Line:** Metabolic acidosis results from either accumulation of acids (HAGMA) or loss of bicarbonate (NAGMA). Classification by anion gap directs diagnostic work-up and guides management approaches. The body compensates through hyperventilation (respiratory) and increased renal acid excretion (renal), though complete correction requires addressing the underlying cause.

## 3.3. HIGH ANION GAP METABOLIC ACIDOSIS (HAGMA)

**High anion gap metabolic acidosis results from accumulation of unmeasured anions, often due to impaired metabolism or excretion of organic acids.**

* **Mnemonic: "GOLDMARK"** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **G:** Glycols - ethylene glycol, propylene glycol, diethylene glycol
	* Ethylene glycol: antifreeze ingestion; metabolized to glycolic and oxalic acids
	* Propylene glycol: vehicle for IV medications (lorazepam, phenytoin)
  * **O:** Oxoproline (pyroglutamic acid) - chronic paracetamol use in malnourished patients
	* Depletes glutathione, leading to pyroglutamic acidemia
	* Seen in critically ill patients on acetaminophen/paracetamol
  * **L:** L-lactate - from tissue hypoperfusion or impaired metabolism
	* Most common cause of HAGMA in hospitalized patients
	* Produced during anaerobic metabolism
  * **D:** D-lactate - bacterial fermentation in short bowel syndrome (not measurable by standard assays)
	* Produced by intestinal bacteria in patients with short gut syndrome
	* May cause encephalopathy
  * **M:** Methanol
	* Metabolized to formic acid
	* Causes visual disturbances, blindness
  * **A:** Aspirin (salicylates)
	* Uncovers uncoupling of oxidative phosphorylation
	* Stimulates respiratory center (mixed respiratory alkalosis early)
  * **R:** Renal failure, uremia
	* Accumulation of organic acids, phosphates, sulfates
	* Impaired excretion of daily acid load
  * **K:** Ketoacidosis - diabetic, alcoholic, starvation
	* Accumulation of ketoacids (acetoacetic acid, β-hydroxybutyric acid)
	* ↑ anion gap from ketones and organic acids
* **Lactic Acidosis:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Type A:** Tissue hypoperfusion and hypoxia
	* Septic shock, cardiogenic shock, hypovolemic shock
	* Severe hypoxemia
	* Carbon monoxide poisoning
	* ⚠️ **Unexplained lactic acidosis with abdominal pain:** Consider mesenteric ischemia
  * **Type B:** Normal oxygen delivery but impaired utilization
	* **B1 - Associated with underlying diseases:**
	  * Liver disease
	  * Malignancy (leukemia, lymphoma)
	  * Thiamine deficiency
	  * HIV infection
	* **B2 - Associated with drugs and toxins:**
	  * Metformin (especially with renal failure)
	  * Nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors
	  * Propofol infusion syndrome
	  * Cyanide, carbon monoxide
	* **B3 - Associated with inborn errors of metabolism:**
	  * Mitochondrial myopathies
	  * Pyruvate dehydrogenase deficiency
* **Ketoacidosis:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA):**
	* Diagnostic criteria: Glucose >14 mmol/L + pH <7.3 or HCO3- <18 mmol/L + ketosis (urine ketones positive or serum beta-hydroxybutyrate >3 mmol/L)
	* Pathophysiology: Insulin deficiency → increased lipolysis → ketone production
	* Often mixed with hyperosmolar dehydration and electrolyte abnormalities
  * **Alcoholic Ketoacidosis:**
	* Occurs in chronic alcoholics who suddenly reduce intake
	* Combination of starvation, dehydration, and impaired gluconeogenesis
	* Typically lower glucose levels than DKA
  * **Starvation Ketoacidosis:**
	* Less severe than DKA or alcoholic ketoacidosis
	* Develops after prolonged fasting (>72 hours)
	* Particularly in pregnancy (accelerated starvation)
* **Uremic Acidosis:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Diminished excretion of daily acid load in acute kidney injury or severe CKD
  * Initially presents as NAGMA but progresses to HAGMA with accumulation of uremic toxins
  * Combined mechanisms:
	* Impaired ammoniagenesis
	* Decreased tubular H+ secretion
	* Accumulation of sulfates, phosphates, and organic anions
* **Diagnostic Tools for HAGMA:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Plasma Osmolar Gap:**
	* Calculated osmolality: 2[Na+] + [glucose mmol/L] + [urea mmol/L]
	* Osmolar gap = measured - calculated osmolality
	* Normal <10 mOsm/L; elevated in toxic alcohol ingestion
	* Methanol: 3.2 mOsm/L per 10 mg/dL
	* Ethylene glycol: 1.6 mOsm/L per 10 mg/dL
	* Ethanol: 2.2 mOsm/L per 10 mg/dL
  * **Lactate Gap:**
	* Discrepancy between point-of-care and laboratory lactate measurements
	* May indicate ethylene glycol poisoning (glycolate cross-reacts with lactate oxidase)
	* > 5 mmol/L difference suggests toxic alcohol poisoning

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** In suspected toxic alcohol ingestion, the combination of high anion gap metabolic acidosis and elevated osmolar gap (>10 mOsm/L) strongly suggests methanol or ethylene glycol poisoning. Early recognition is critical as treatment with fomepizole or ethanol (to inhibit alcohol dehydrogenase) and hemodialysis can prevent irreversible end-organ damage.

⚠️ **Safety Warning:** Delay in recognizing and treating toxic alcohol ingestions can result in permanent neurological damage or death. Consider early consultation with toxicology and potential dialysis if blood pH is <7.3, anion gap >20, or clinical suspicion is high regardless of laboratory values.

**Bottom Line:** High anion gap metabolic acidosis results from accumulation of unmeasured anions due to various conditions including lactic acidosis, ketoacidosis, renal failure, and toxic ingestions. The mnemonic "GOLDMARK" helps recall common causes. Additional diagnostic tools include osmolar gap and lactate gap measurements, which are particularly valuable in identifying toxic alcohol ingestions.

## 3.4. NORMAL ANION GAP METABOLIC ACIDOSIS (NAGMA)

**Normal anion gap metabolic acidosis occurs due to bicarbonate loss or chloride retention without accumulation of unmeasured anions.**

* **Major Causes of NAGMA:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Gastrointestinal bicarbonate loss:**
	* Diarrhea (most common cause)
	* Ileostomy or colostomy drainage
	* Pancreatic or biliary drainage
	* Small bowel or urinary diversions
	* External GI fistulae
  * **Renal bicarbonate loss:**
	* Renal tubular acidosis (RTA)
	* Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (e.g., acetazolamide)
	* Early kidney injury
	* Hypoaldosteronism
	* Ureterosigmoidostomy
  * **Chloride excess:**
	* Large volume resuscitation with normal saline (dilutional acidosis)
	* Parenteral nutrition with excess chloride
	* Ammonium chloride administration
	* Post-hypocapnia (compensation for chronic respiratory alkalosis)

![](ABG_ABG_Untitled_10.png_eab8bf.png_5c6a69.png)

* **Diagnostic Approach to NAGMA:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Check urine pH and urine anion gap (UAG = [Na+] + [K+] - [Cl-])
  * **Urine pH:**
	* Appropriately acidic (pH <5.5) in normal renal response to acidosis
	* Inappropriately alkaline (pH >5.5) in distal RTA
	* Can be misleading if recent alkali therapy, UTI, or diuretics
  * **Urine Anion Gap:**
	* **Negative UAG:** Non-renal cause (adequate renal ammonium excretion)
	  * Urine Clserves as surrogate for NH4+ excretion
	  * Suggests GI losses or exogenous causes
	* **Positive UAG:** Renal cause (impaired ammonium excretion)
	  * Suggests RTA or other renal acidification defects
	* Invalid if significant ketonuria or other unmeasured anions in urine
* **Renal Tubular Acidosis (RTA):** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Type 1 (Distal) RTA:**
	* Impaired H+ secretion in distal nephron
	* Features: Hypokalemia, inability to acidify urine (pH >5.5)
	* Causes:
	  * Autoimmune diseases (Sjögren's syndrome, SLE)
	  * Drugs (amphotericin B, lithium)
	  * Genetic disorders (mutations in H+-ATPase)
	  * Multiple myeloma
	* Clinical presentation: Nephrolithiasis, nephrocalcinosis, bone disease
  * **Type 2 (Proximal) RTA:**
	* Decreased bicarbonate reabsorption in proximal tubule
	* Features: Hypokalemia, high urine bicarbonate, variable urine pH
	* Associated with Fanconi syndrome (phosphaturia, glycosuria, aminoaciduria)
	* Causes:
	  * Multiple myeloma
	  * Wilson's disease
	  * Drugs (acetazolamide, topiramate)
	  * Cystinosis
	* Clinical presentation: Less severe acidosis, less bone disease, no nephrocalcinosis

![](ABG_ABG_Untitled_11.png_17208a.png_181ce5.png)

  * **Type 4 RTA:**
	* Hypoaldosteronism or aldosterone resistance
	* Features: Hyperkalemia, mild-moderate acidosis, reduced NH4+ excretion
	* Causes:
	  * Diabetic nephropathy
	  * Medications (ACEIs, ARBs, K+-sparing diuretics, NSAIDs)
	  * Adrenal insufficiency
	  * Advanced kidney disease
	  * Obstructive uropathy
	* Clinical presentation: Hyperkalemia often dominates clinical picture
* **Chronic Kidney Disease:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Initial presentation: NAGMA (stage 4 CKD, eGFR 15-29 mL/min)
  * Progression: HAGMA (as uremic toxins accumulate)
  * Mixed pattern: Combined HAGMA and NAGMA in advanced disease
  * Pathophysiology:
	* Decreased ammoniagenesis
	* Reduced H+ secretion
	* Accumulation of organic acids with disease progression

![](ABG_ABG_Untitled_12.png_4d5c08.png_0da80c.png)

![](ABG_ABG_Untitled_13.png_561892.png_f2b122.png)

![](ABG_ABG_Untitled_14.png_d4c2e2.png_7b63c1.png)

![](ABG_ABG_Untitled_15.png_78753f.png_0fcaef.png)

* **Saline-Induced Hyperchloremic Acidosis:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C5 Perioperative Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Mechanism: Large volume infusion of 0.9% saline (154 mmol/L of Cl-)
  * Dilutes plasma bicarbonate concentration
  * Increases chloride concentration (hyperchloremia)
  * Common in perioperative and critical care settings
  * Risk factors: Renal insufficiency, large volume resuscitation

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** In critically ill patients, normal saline resuscitation can cause hyperchloremic NAGMA (dilutional acidosis). Consider balanced crystalloid solutions (e.g., lactated Ringer's, Plasma-Lyte), especially in patients with kidney injury or at risk for AKI. The SMART and SALT-ED trials have demonstrated reduced adverse kidney events with balanced solutions.

**Bottom Line:** Normal anion gap metabolic acidosis results from bicarbonate loss or chloride retention. Diagnostic evaluation includes urine pH and anion gap to differentiate renal from non-renal causes. Renal tubular acidosis subtypes have distinct clinical and laboratory profiles. Saline-induced hyperchloremic acidosis is an important iatrogenic cause in hospital settings, which can be mitigated with balanced crystalloid solutions.

## 3.5. DIAGNOSTIC TOOLS (OSMOLAR GAP, LACTATE GAP, URINE AG)

**Specialized diagnostic tools help differentiate subtypes of metabolic acidosis and identify specific etiologies.**

* **Osmolar Gap:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Calculation:**
	* Calculated Posm (mOsm/L) = 2[Na+] + [glucose mmol/L] + [urea mmol/L]
	* Osmolar gap = Measured osmolality - Calculated osmolality
  * **Interpretation:**
	* Normal: <10 mOsm/L
	* Elevated: >10 mOsm/L suggests presence of unmeasured osmotically active substances
  * **Clinical Applications:**
	* Toxic alcohol ingestion (methanol, ethylene glycol, isopropyl alcohol)
	  * Methanol: 3.2 mOsm/L per 10 mg/dL
	  * Ethylene glycol: 1.6 mOsm/L per 10 mg/dL
	* Ethanol intoxication
	  * Ethanol: 2.2 mOsm/L per 10 mg/dL
	* Mannitol administration
	* Alcoholic ketoacidosis
  * **Limitations:**
	* May be normal early in ingestion (before metabolism of parent alcohols)
	* Laboratory variations in measured osmolality
	* Decreased sensitivity in late presentations
* **Lactate Gap:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Definition:** Discrepancy between point-of-care and laboratory analyzer lactate measurements
  * **Mechanism:** Point-of-care analyzers using lactate oxidase may cross-react with glycolate (metabolite of ethylene glycol)
  * **Calculation:** Lactate gap = Point-of-care lactate - Laboratory lactate
  * **Interpretation:**
	* Normal: <2 mmol/L
	* > 5 mmol/L strongly suggests ethylene glycol poisoning
  * **Clinical Significance:** Unexplained discrepancy suggests ethylene glycol poisoning
  * **Limitations:**
	* Requires both point-of-care and laboratory measurements
	* Not all point-of-care analyzers use lactate oxidase method
* **Urine Anion Gap (UAG):** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Calculation:** [Na+] + [K+] - [Cl-] in urine
  * **Physiological Basis:**
	* In metabolic acidosis, kidneys should excrete NH4Cl
	* NH4+ excretion accompanied by Cl- (not measured in UAG)
	* Higher Clexcretion relative to Na+ + K+ results in negative UAG
  * **Interpretation:**
	* **Negative UAG:** Appropriate renal response to acidosis (high NH4+ excretion)
	  * Suggests non-renal cause (GI bicarbonate loss)
	  * Normal renal ammoniagenesis
	* **Positive UAG:** Impaired renal acidification
	  * Suggests renal tubular acidosis or other renal causes
	  * Inadequate ammonium excretion
  * **Limitations:**
	* Invalid if significant ketonuria or other unmeasured anions in urine
	* Requires normal renal function for interpretation
	* Affected by diuretics and volume status
* **Urine pH:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Interpretation:**
	* In metabolic acidosis, urine pH should be <5.5
	* Urine pH >5.5 in acidemic patient suggests distal RTA
  * **Limitations:**
	* Can be affected by UTI (urease-producing bacteria alkalinize urine)
	* Recent alkali therapy
	* Diuretics can interfere with interpretation
	* Not useful in proximal RTA once serum bicarbonate falls below reabsorption threshold
* **Delta Gap (Delta Ratio):** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Calculation:** (Measured AG - Normal AG) / (Normal HCO3- - Measured HCO3-)
  * **Interpretation:** See Section 2.6 (Step 6: Evaluate Delta Gap)

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** In a patient with high anion gap metabolic acidosis, unexplained by history and common causes, check the osmolar gap. An elevated osmolar gap suggests toxic alcohol ingestion even when history is unreliable or denied. A normal osmolar gap does not exclude toxic alcohol ingestion if presentation is delayed (after metabolism of parent alcohol).

**Bottom Line:** Specialized diagnostic tools including osmolar gap, lactate gap, and urine anion gap provide valuable information for differentiating causes of metabolic acidosis, particularly in complex or unclear clinical scenarios. Each tool has specific applications and limitations that should be considered when interpreting results in the context of the patient's overall clinical picture.

## 3.6. PHYSIOLOGICAL CONSEQUENCES

**Severe metabolic acidosis affects multiple organ systems and physiological processes, increasing morbidity and mortality.**

* **Cardiovascular Effects:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Negative inotropy:** Decreased myocardial contractility
	* Direct depression of myocardial fiber function
	* Reduced response to catecholamines
	* Impaired calcium handling
  * **Vasodilation:** Systemic arterial vasodilation, particularly at pH <7.2
	* Reduced vascular responsiveness to catecholamines
	* Direct effect of H+ on vascular smooth muscle
  * **Vasoconstriction:** Pulmonary vasoconstriction
	* Increases pulmonary vascular resistance
	* May worsen right heart function
  * **Catecholamine resistance:** Reduced responsiveness to endogenous and exogenous catecholamines
	* Downregulation of adrenergic receptors
	* Impaired signal transduction
  * **Arrhythmias:** Increased risk, especially with concomitant electrolyte abnormalities
	* Ventricular ectopy
	* Ventricular tachycardia/fibrillation
	* Conduction disturbances

![image.png](ABG_ABG_image_3.png_f0412b.png_29f15b.png)

* **Respiratory Effects:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Hyperventilation:** Kussmaul breathing (deep, rapid respirations)
	* Compensatory response mediated by peripheral chemoreceptors
	* Increases minute ventilation to eliminate CO2
  * **Respiratory muscle fatigue:** With prolonged hyperventilation
	* Increased work of breathing
	* Potential respiratory failure
  * **Impaired hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction:** Worsens V/Q mismatch
	* May contribute to hypoxemia
  * **Bronchodilation:** Mild direct bronchodilatory effect
* **Neurological Effects:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Altered mental status:** Confusion to coma depending on severity
	* Directly proportional to severity of acidemia
	* May be due to associated conditions (hypoxemia, hypoperfusion)
  * **Increased intracranial pressure:** Due to cerebral vasodilation
	* Acidosis impairs cerebral autoregulation
	* Vasodilation of cerebral vessels
  * **Impaired cellular function:** Enzyme dysfunction affecting neurotransmission
	* Altered neurotransmitter release and reuptake
	* Changed neuronal membrane excitability
* **Metabolic Effects:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Insulin resistance:** Impaired glucose utilization
	* Decreased insulin receptor affinity
	* Post-receptor signaling defects
  * **Hyperkalemia:** Due to transcellular shift (H+ in, K+ out)
	* Approximately 0.6 mmol/L increase in K+ for each 0.1 unit decrease in pH
	* Can cause life-threatening arrhythmias
  * **Protein catabolism:** Increased skeletal muscle breakdown
	* Activation of proteolytic enzymes
	* Negative nitrogen balance
  * **Impaired enzymatic function:** Multiple metabolic pathways affected
	* pH-sensitive enzymes dysfunction
	* Altered metabolic regulation
* **Hematologic Effects:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Impaired oxygen delivery:** Right shift of oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve (Bohr effect)
	* Facilitates oxygen unloading to tissues
	* Potentially beneficial effect in acidosis
  * **Coagulopathy:** Impaired platelet function and coagulation factor activity
	* Decreased platelet aggregation
	* Reduced activity of coagulation factors
	* Increased risk of bleeding
* **Renal Effects:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Increased renal workload:** Enhanced ammoniagenesis and acid excretion
  * **Renal vasoconstriction:** In severe acidosis
  * **Increased risk of acute kidney injury:** Particularly with shock or nephrotoxins

⚠️ **Safety Warning:** pH <7.20 is associated with significantly increased mortality, particularly in critically ill patients with septic or hypovolemic shock. Prompt recognition and management are essential. Mortality increases exponentially as pH decreases below 7.20, with pH <7.0 associated with >50% mortality in many studies.

**Bottom Line:** Severe acidemia (pH <7.2) has profound effects on multiple organ systems, including decreased cardiac contractility, vasodilation, arrhythmias, altered mental status, and metabolic derangements. These physiological consequences contribute to increased morbidity and mortality in critically ill patients. Understanding these effects guides therapeutic interventions and monitoring approaches.

## 3.7. TREATMENT APPROACHES

**Treatment of metabolic acidosis targets the underlying cause while supporting physiological compensation and addressing severe acidemia when indicated.**

* **General Principles:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Treatment of underlying cause is the primary approach
  * Support physiological compensation mechanisms
  * Consider alkali therapy only in severe, refractory cases
  * Individualize approach based on etiology, severity, and clinical context
  * Address complications (e.g., hyperkalemia, hypocalcemia)
* **Treatment of Underlying Cause:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * **HAGMA:**
	* **Lactic acidosis:** Restore tissue perfusion, treat sepsis, discontinue offending drugs
	  * Volume resuscitation
	  * Antimicrobial therapy in sepsis
	  * Discontinue metformin if relevant
	  * Correct hypoxemia
	* **Ketoacidosis:** Insulin, fluids, electrolyte replacement
	  * Insulin infusion (0.1 units/kg/hr initially)
	  * Volume repletion
	  * Potassium, phosphate, magnesium replacement
	* **Toxic ingestions:** Specific antidotes, enhanced elimination (dialysis if indicated)
	  * Methanol/ethylene glycol: fomepizole, hemodialysis
	  * Salicylates: urinary alkalinization, hemodialysis
	* **Renal failure:** Dialysis if indicated
	  * Consider earlier initiation with severe acidemia
  * **NAGMA:**
	* **GI losses:** Fluid and electrolyte replacement
	  * Volume replacement with balanced solutions
	  * Potassium supplementation
	* **RTA:** Bicarbonate supplementation
	  * Type 1 & 2: Oral sodium bicarbonate 1-2 mEq/kg/day
	  * Type 4: Treat hyperkalemia, consider fludrocortisone
	* **Iatrogenic hyperchloremia:** Switch to balanced crystalloids
	  * Lactated Ringer's or Plasma-Lyte
* **Supportive Measures:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * **Respiratory Support:**
	* Maintain adequate ventilation to support respiratory compensation
	* Consider intubation if respiratory muscle fatigue develops
	* In intubated patients, avoid hyperventilation in chronic respiratory acidosis
  * **Hemodynamic Support:**
	* Volume resuscitation with balanced solutions when indicated
	* Vasopressors for hypotension refractory to volume resuscitation
	* Consider higher doses in severe acidemia (catecholamine resistance)
  * **Electrolyte Management:**
	* Aggressive treatment of hyperkalemia
	* Monitor and replace calcium, phosphate, magnesium
	* Frequent monitoring during treatment
* **Bicarbonate Therapy:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * **Indications:**
	* Severe acidemia (pH <7.2) with hemodynamic instability
	* HCO3- <12 mmol/L (local NUH guidelines)
	* Specific indications: RTA, uremic acidosis with contraindications to dialysis
  * **Administration:**
	* Calculate deficit: 0.3 × Weight (kg) × (Desired - Current HCO3-)
	* Administer as IV 8.4% NaHCO3 infusion over 2-4 hours
	* In infants (<1 year), use 4.2% solution to prevent phlebitis
	* Typical dosing: 1-2 mEq/kg initially, followed by continuous infusion if needed
  * **Cautions:**
	* Monitor for hypokalemia and hypocalcemia
	* ⚠️ **Contraindicated in DKA** - risk of cerebral edema
	* Potential adverse effects: volume overload, hypernatremia, paradoxical CSF acidosis
	* Overshoot alkalosis
	* CO2 generation may worsen intracellular acidosis
* **Evidence Base:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * **BICAR-ICU Trial:** Multicenter open-label trial showing no overall mortality benefit of bicarbonate therapy in severe acidemia, but suggested benefit in AKI subgroup with reduced need for renal replacement therapy
	* 389 ICU patients with severe metabolic acidemia (pH ≤7.20)
	* No difference in primary outcome (composite of 28-day mortality and organ failure at day 7)
	* Reduced need for renal replacement therapy in AKI subgroup
  * **Alternative Buffers:**
	* THAM (Trishydroxymethyl aminomethane): Buffers without generating CO2; limited clinical evidence
	  * May have theoretical advantages in hypercapnic states
	  * Excreted renally (contraindicated in renal failure)
	* Carbicarb: 1:1 mixture of sodium bicarbonate and sodium carbonate; experimental
	  * Generates less CO2 than bicarbonate
	  * Limited clinical experience
* **Specific Treatment Approaches by Etiology:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * **DKA:**
	* Fluids, insulin, potassium replacement
	* Avoid bicarbonate unless pH <6.9 or life-threatening hyperkalemia
  * **Lactic Acidosis:**
	* Focus on improving tissue perfusion
	* Avoid sodium bicarbonate except in extreme acidemia
  * **Renal Failure:**
	* Consider earlier dialysis in severe acidemia
	* Sodium bicarbonate if dialysis contraindicated/unavailable
  * **RTA:**
	* Oral sodium bicarbonate (1-2 mEq/kg/day in divided doses)
	* Potassium replacement as needed

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** In patients with lactic acidosis due to shock, focus on improving tissue perfusion rather than normalizing pH with bicarbonate. Volume resuscitation, vasopressors, and treating the underlying cause are more effective than buffer therapy in improving outcomes.

⚠️ **Safety Warning:** Rapid bicarbonate administration can cause paradoxical intracellular acidosis, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypernatremia, and volume overload. When indicated, administer slowly and monitor electrolytes closely. In patients with limited cardiac reserve, use smaller boluses with careful hemodynamic monitoring.

**Bottom Line:** Treatment of metabolic acidosis focuses primarily on addressing the underlying cause, supporting physiological compensation, and considering alkali therapy only in severe cases (pH <7.2 or HCO3- <12) with careful monitoring for adverse effects. The BICAR-ICU trial suggests possible benefit in patients with acute kidney injury, but overall evidence for bicarbonate therapy remains limited outside specific indications.

# 4. METABOLIC ALKALOSIS




## 4.2. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND CLASSIFICATION
**Metabolic alkalosis is characterized by primary elevation in serum bicarbonate resulting in alkalemia.**

* **Definition:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Plasma bicarbonate >28 mmol/L
  * pH >7.40
  * Not a specific disease but typically a response to NaCl and K+ deficit
  * Represents either H+ loss or HCO3gain
* **Pathophysiological Mechanisms:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Generation Phase:** Initial increase in serum bicarbonate
	* Loss of H+ (vomiting, NG suction)
	  * Loss of gastric acid (HCl) leads to retention of HCO3-
	* Gain of bicarbonate (exogenous administration)
	  * IV NaHCO3, citrate in massive transfusion, antacids
	* Contraction alkalosis (loss of Cl and H2O with stable HCO3-)
	  * Diuretics cause disproportionate loss of water and chloride
	  * Dehydration with same HCO3amount in smaller ECF volume
	* Hypokalemia (intracellular shift of H+)
	  * K+ depletion leads to H+ movement into cells to maintain electroneutrality
	  * Enhances renal H+ secretion and HCO3reabsorption
  * **Maintenance Phase:** Factors preventing renal correction
	* Volume depletion (enhances proximal HCO3reabsorption)
	  * Stimulates Na+ reabsorption and secondarily HCO3reabsorption
	* Chloride depletion (impairs HCO3excretion)
	  * Kidneys prioritize Clreabsorption over HCO3excretion
	* Hypokalemia (stimulates H+ secretion)
	  * K+ depletion enhances distal H+ secretion
	* Mineralocorticoid excess (increases distal H+ secretion)
	  * Stimulates distal Na+/K+/H+ exchange
* **Physiological Response:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Respiratory:** Limited compensation (hypoventilation)
	* Constrained by hypoxic drive
	* PCO2 rises by approximately 0.7 mmHg for each 1 mEq/L rise in HCO3-
  * **Renal:** Normally responds to ECF contraction by excreting Na+ and Clpoor urine
	* In chloride depletion, kidneys cannot excrete excess bicarbonate
	* Kidneys preferentially retain chloride over bicarbonate excretion
* **Classification Approaches:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **By Chloride Responsiveness:**
	* Saline-responsive (chloride-responsive)
	  * Volume depletion, chloride depletion
	  * Responds to NaCl administration
	* Saline-resistant (chloride-resistant)
	  * Mineralocorticoid excess, severe hypokalemia
	  * Persists despite NaCl administration
  * **By Volume Status and Blood Pressure:**
	* Hypertensive (typically mineralocorticoid excess)
	* Normotensive with volume depletion (vomiting, diuretics)
	* Normotensive with volume expansion (edematous states)
* **Unique Features:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Self-perpetuating once established (positive feedback loop)
  * Relatively rare as primary disorder compared to metabolic acidosis
  * Often iatrogenic (diuretics, NG suction, exogenous alkali)
  * Bidirectional relationship with hypokalemia

**Bottom Line:** Metabolic alkalosis results from either excessive loss of hydrogen ions or gain of bicarbonate, perpetuated by factors that prevent normal renal correction (volume depletion, chloride deficiency, hypokalemia, or mineralocorticoid excess). Classification by chloride responsiveness guides therapeutic approaches. Once established, metabolic alkalosis can be self-perpetuating due to the interaction between renal adaptations and ongoing stimuli.

## 4.3. SALINE-RESPONSIVE VS. SALINE-RESISTANT

**Classifying metabolic alkalosis by response to saline administration provides a practical approach to diagnosis and treatment.**

* **Saline-Responsive (Chloride-Responsive) Alkalosis:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Pathophysiology:**
	* Volume and chloride depletion maintain alkalosis by enhancing bicarbonate reabsorption
	* Correction of volume and chloride deficits allows kidneys to excrete excess bicarbonate
	* Urinary chloride conservation (contraction of ECF around fixed HCO3content)
  * **Clinical Features:**
	* Signs of volume depletion (tachycardia, orthostasis, poor skin turgor)
	* Urinary chloride typically <20 mmol/L (except during active diuretic use)
	* Often associated with concurrent hypokalemia
	* Responds to NaCl administration with correction of alkalosis
  * **Common Causes:**
	* Vomiting or nasogastric suction
	  * Direct loss of H+ from gastric secretions
	  * Secondary hyperaldosteronism from volume depletion
	* Diuretic use (may have high urine Clduring active effect)
	  * Loop and thiazide diuretics cause chloride depletion
	  * Contraction alkalosis from ECF volume reduction
	* Chloride-losing diarrhea (rare, villous adenoma)
	  * Secretion of chloride without bicarbonate
	* Post-hypercapnic state
	  * Renal compensation for chronic respiratory acidosis
	  * When respiratory acidosis resolves, bicarbonate remains elevated
	* Cystic fibrosis (excessive sweat chloride loss)
	  * Hypochloremic alkalosis in hot weather

![](ABG_ABG_Untitled_16.png_57436e.png_802baa.png)

* **Saline-Resistant (Chloride-Resistant) Alkalosis:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Pathophysiology:**
	* Enhanced renal H+ secretion and bicarbonate reabsorption independent of volume status
	* Usually due to mineralocorticoid excess or severe hypokalemia
	* Increased distal sodium reabsorption with enhanced H+/K+ secretion
  * **Clinical Features:**
	* Hypertension common
	* Urinary chloride typically >20 mmol/L
	* Often associated with hypokalemia
	* Alkalosis persists despite volume repletion with saline
	* May have signs of mineralocorticoid excess (hypertension, edema)
  * **Common Causes:**
	* Primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn's syndrome)
	  * Adrenal adenoma or hyperplasia
	  * Autonomous aldosterone secretion
	* Secondary hyperaldosteronism (renovascular hypertension)
	  * Increased renin production stimulates aldosterone
	* Cushing's syndrome
	  * Cortisol has mineralocorticoid effects
	  * Often milder alkalosis than primary hyperaldosteronism
	* Severe hypokalemia
	  * Intracellular shift of H+ in exchange for K+
	  * Enhanced renal H+ secretion
	* Bartter and Gitelman syndromes
	  * Genetic disorders affecting renal tubular transport
	  * Mimic chronic loop or thiazide diuretic effects
	* Licorice ingestion (apparent mineralocorticoid excess)
	  * Glycyrrhizic acid inhibits 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase
	  * Allows cortisol to activate mineralocorticoid receptors
	* Liddle's syndrome
	  * Constitutive activation of epithelial sodium channels
	  * Increased Na+ reabsorption and K+/H+ secretion

![](ABG_ABG_Untitled_17.png_676f5f.png_de0a51.png)

* **Diagnostic Approach:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **History and Physical Examination:**
	* Vomiting, NG suction, diuretic use (saline-responsive)
	* Hypertension, edema, family history (saline-resistant)
  * **Laboratory Assessment:**
	* Urine chloride: <20 mmol/L (saline-responsive); >20 mmol/L (saline-resistant)
	  * Exception: urine chloride may be high during active diuretic effect
	* Serum potassium: hypokalemia common in both types
	* Plasma renin and aldosterone levels (for suspected mineralocorticoid excess)

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Urine chloride <20 mmol/L in a patient with metabolic alkalosis strongly suggests saline-responsive alkalosis, except during active diuretic therapy when urine chloride may be elevated despite volume depletion. Collecting urine several hours after the last diuretic dose helps distinguish diuretic-induced (saline-responsive) from mineralocorticoid excess (saline-resistant) alkalosis.

**Bottom Line:** The distinction between saline-responsive and saline-resistant metabolic alkalosis is primarily based on urinary chloride concentration (<20 vs. >20 mmol/L) and the presence of hypertension. This classification guides diagnostic evaluation and treatment approach. Saline-responsive alkalosis requires chloride and volume repletion, while saline-resistant alkalosis requires specific treatments targeting the underlying cause of mineralocorticoid excess.

## 4.4. HYPERALDOSTERONIC STATES

**Hyperaldosteronism causes metabolic alkalosis by increasing distal tubular sodium reabsorption and hydrogen ion secretion.**

* **Pathophysiology:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Aldosterone stimulates renal collecting tubules to:
	* Reabsorb sodium with water
	* Excrete potassium and hydrogen ions
  * Results in hypertension, hypernatremia, hypokalemia, and alkalosis
  * Urinary chloride is not low (typically >20 mmol/L)
  * Enhanced H+/K+ exchange in collecting tubule and H+-ATPase activity
* **Primary Hyperaldosteronism (Conn's Syndrome):** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Etiology:**
	* Aldosterone-producing adenoma (60-70%)
	* Bilateral adrenal hyperplasia (30-40%)
	* Rare causes: adrenal carcinoma, familial hyperaldosteronism
  * **Clinical Features:**
	* Hypertension (often resistant to standard therapy)
	* Hypokalemia (spontaneous or diuretic-induced)
	* Metabolic alkalosis
	* Normal or elevated sodium
	* Symptoms of hypokalemia (weakness, cramps, palpitations)
  * **Diagnosis:**
	* Elevated plasma aldosterone-to-renin ratio (high aldosterone, low renin)
	* Confirmatory testing: Salt loading or saline suppression test
	  * Normal subjects suppress aldosterone with salt loading
	  * Primary hyperaldosteronism shows non-suppressible aldosterone
	* Localization: CT adrenal glands, adrenal vein sampling
  * **Treatment:**
	* Adenoma: Surgical adrenalectomy
	* Bilateral hyperplasia: Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (spironolactone, eplerenone)
* **Secondary Hyperaldosteronism:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Renal Artery Stenosis:**
	* Decreased renal perfusion stimulates juxtaglomerular apparatus
	* Activates renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system
	* Clinical features: hypertension, metabolic alkalosis, hypokalemia
	* Diagnosis: Renal artery ultrasound, CT/MR angiography
	* Treatment: Revascularization, ACE inhibitors (with caution)
  * **Other Causes:**
	* Malignant hypertension
	* Renin-secreting tumors
	* Bartter syndrome (mimics loop diuretic effect)
	* Gitelman syndrome (mimics thiazide diuretic effect)
	* Edematous disorders with effective volume depletion (cirrhosis, heart failure)
* **Steroid Excess States:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Cushing's Syndrome:**
	* Excess cortisol has mineralocorticoid activity
	* Causes hypertension, hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis
	* Additional features: central obesity, striae, muscle weakness, glucose intolerance
	* Diagnosis: Dexamethasone suppression test, 24-hour urinary free cortisol
	* Treatment: Depends on cause (surgery, medication, radiation)
  * **Exogenous Steroid Administration:**
	* Synthetic steroids with mineralocorticoid activity
	* Similar biochemical profile to endogenous Cushing's syndrome
	* Treatment: Taper steroids if possible, use agents with less mineralocorticoid activity
* **Pseudohyperaldosteronism:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Liddle's Syndrome:**
	* Genetic disorder causing constitutive activation of epithelial sodium channels
	* Presents with hypertension, hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis
	* Low renin and aldosterone levels
	* Treatment: Amiloride or triamterene (directly blocks epithelial Na+ channels)
  * **Chronic Licorice Ingestion:**
	* Glycyrrhizic acid inhibits 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase
	* Allows cortisol to activate mineralocorticoid receptors
	* Presents similar to hyperaldosteronism but with suppressed aldosterone
	* Treatment: Discontinue licorice, potassium supplementation, spironolactone
  * **Apparent Mineralocorticoid Excess Syndrome:**
	* Genetic deficiency of 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase
	* Rare inherited disorder
	* Clinical features similar to licorice ingestion
	* Treatment: Spironolactone, dexamethasone
* **Laboratory Evaluation:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Serum: Electrolytes, BUN, creatinine, glucose
  * Urine: Electrolytes (Na+, K+, Cl-), calcium
  * Hormonal assays: Plasma renin activity, plasma aldosterone concentration
  * Aldosterone/renin ratio >20-25 (SI units) with aldosterone >550 pmol/L suggests primary hyperaldosteronism

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** In patients with unexplained hypertension, hypokalemia, and metabolic alkalosis, screen for primary hyperaldosteronism with plasma aldosterone-to-renin ratio. This condition is more common than previously recognized (5-15% of hypertensive patients) and represents a potentially curable form of hypertension.

**Bottom Line:** Hyperaldosteronic states cause saline-resistant metabolic alkalosis through enhanced distal tubular sodium reabsorption and hydrogen/potassium excretion. Diagnostic evaluation includes plasma aldosterone-to-renin ratio and imaging studies to identify the underlying cause. Treatment depends on the specific etiology, ranging from surgical intervention for aldosterone-producing adenomas to pharmacologic blockade of mineralocorticoid receptors for bilateral hyperplasia and secondary causes.

## 4.5. OVERT FLUID OVERLOAD

**Edematous states can paradoxically cause metabolic alkalosis despite apparent volume overload due to decreased effective arterial blood volume.**

* **Pathophysiology:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Metabolic alkalosis occurs in conditions with:
	* Apparent fluid overload but decreased effective arterial blood volume
	* Activation of renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system
	* Enhanced proximal tubular sodium and bicarbonate reabsorption
	* Excess fluid accumulation in third spaces
  * Despite total body fluid excess, the effective arterial blood volume is decreased
  * "Underfilling" hypothesis: primary sodium retention leads to edema formation
* **Clinical Presentations:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Congestive Heart Failure:**
	* Decreased cardiac output → decreased renal perfusion
	* Secondary hyperaldosteronism
	* Often exacerbated by diuretic therapy
	* Apparent volume overload (peripheral edema, pulmonary congestion)
	* Classic findings: elevated JVP, peripheral edema, S3 heart sound
  * **Cirrhosis:**
	* Splanchnic vasodilation and ascites formation
	* Decreased effective circulating volume
	* Secondary hyperaldosteronism
	* Low albumin contributes to decreased oncotic pressure
	* Classic findings: ascites, peripheral edema, spider angiomata
  * **Nephrotic Syndrome:**
	* Hypoalbuminemia → decreased oncotic pressure
	* Decreased effective circulating volume
	* Secondary hyperaldosteronism
	* Classic findings: significant proteinuria, hypoalbuminemia, edema
* **Common Features:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Low-normal blood pressure
  * Decreased renal perfusion
  * Elevated aldosterone levels
  * Physical examination showing edema despite effective intravascular volume depletion
  * Avid sodium and water retention
  * Activation of the sympathetic nervous system
  * Paradoxical diuretic resistance
* **Diagnostic Clues:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Poor response to diuretics
  * Worsening alkalosis with aggressive diuresis
  * Laboratory evidence of secondary hyperaldosteronism (low serum sodium, high urine sodium)
  * Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) often elevated disproportionate to creatinine
  * Elevated plasma renin activity
  * Fractional excretion of sodium (FENa) typically <1%
* **Therapeutic Challenges:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Diuretics worsen alkalosis but are often necessary to manage edema
  * Sodium and fluid restriction limited by patient tolerance
  * Hypokalemia complicates management
  * ACE inhibitors may help but can worsen renal function

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** In edematous patients with heart failure or cirrhosis who develop worsening metabolic alkalosis with diuresis, consider reducing diuretic intensity and adding potassium-sparing diuretics to counter secondary hyperaldosteronism. The combination of acetazolamide with loop diuretics can help correct alkalosis while maintaining diuresis.

**Bottom Line:** Edematous disorders (heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome) can cause metabolic alkalosis despite apparent fluid overload due to decreased effective arterial blood volume activating the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system. Treatment focuses on addressing the underlying disorder and judicious use of diuretics, often with addition of potassium-sparing agents or carbonic anhydrase inhibitors to mitigate alkalosis development.

## 4.6. VOLUME AND CHLORIDE DEPLETION

**Volume and chloride depletion are common causes of saline-responsive metabolic alkalosis.**

* **Pathophysiology:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Volume depletion enhances proximal bicarbonate reabsorption
	* Reduced GFR enhances tubular reabsorption of Na+, water, and HCO3-
	* 60-80% of filtered HCO3reabsorbed in proximal tubule
  * Chloride depletion impairs kidneys' ability to excrete excess bicarbonate
	* Kidneys preferentially reabsorb chloride over bicarbonate excretion
	* "Chloride-depletion alkalosis" concept
  * Low urinary chloride (<20 mmol/L) reflects renal conservation of scarce chloride
  * Volume depletion activates renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system
	* Increases distal tubular Na+/K+/H+ exchange
	* Further perpetuates alkalosis
* **Major Causes:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Vomiting or Nasogastric Suction:**
	* Loss of acidic, chloride-rich gastric secretions
	* Gastric fluid contains ~100-150 mmol/L H+ and ~150 mmol/L Cl-
	* Additional mechanisms:
	  * Direct loss of H+
	  * Volume contraction → enhanced bicarbonate reabsorption
	  * Hypokalemia → increased H+ secretion
	  * Secondary hyperaldosteronism due to volume depletion
  
  * **Contraction Alkalosis:**
	* Rapid recovery from fluid overload (typically with diuretics)
	* Chloride loss during diuresis
	* Contraction of extracellular fluid volume with constant bicarbonate content
	* Increased bicarbonate concentration without absolute increase in amount
	* Example: Patient with 15L ECF and 300 mmol HCO3- (20 mmol/L) loses 5L fluid → same HCO3amount distributed in 10L = 30 mmol/L concentration
  
  * **Diuretic Therapy:**
	* Paradoxically elevated urine chloride (>20 mmol/L) during active diuretic effect
	* Loop and thiazide diuretics cause:
	  * Volume contraction → enhanced bicarbonate reabsorption
	  * Chloride depletion → impaired bicarbonate excretion
	  * Hypokalemia → enhanced H+ secretion
	  * Post-operative patients often have hyperaldosteronism (stress-induced RAAS activation)
		* Increased Na+ delivery to distal tubule with loop diuretics plus hyperaldosteronism leads to increased H+ secretion
* **Genetic Disorders:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Bartter Syndrome:**
	* Defect in Na-K-2Cl cotransporter in thick ascending limb
	* Mimics chronic loop diuretic effect
	* Presents with hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis, normal blood pressure
	* Symptoms often present in childhood
	* Associated with hypercalciuria and nephrocalcinosis
  
  * **Gitelman Syndrome:**
	* Defect in Na-Cl cotransporter in distal tubule
	* Mimics chronic thiazide diuretic effect
	* Presents with hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis, hypomagnesemia
	* Typically milder than Bartter syndrome
	* Usually diagnosed in adolescence or adulthood
* **Clinical Presentation:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Signs of volume depletion: orthostatic hypotension, tachycardia, reduced skin turgor
  * Symptoms of hypokalemia: weakness, muscle cramps, palpitations
  * Symptoms of alkalosis: confusion, seizures (rare)
  * Reduced urine output
  * Laboratory findings:
	* Elevated bicarbonate
	* Hypokalemia
	* Hypochloremia
	* Urinary chloride <20 mmol/L (except during active diuretic effect)
	* Elevated BUN/creatinine ratio
* **Diagnostic Approach:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * History for causes of GI losses or diuretic use
  * Physical examination for volume status
  * Laboratory evaluation:
	* Serum electrolytes, BUN, creatinine
	* Urine electrolytes (especially chloride)
	* If urinary chloride >20 mmol/L during diuretic abstinence, consider other diagnoses

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** In patients with unexplained metabolic alkalosis, check urine chloride. If <20 mmol/L, volume depletion is likely the cause, while values >20 mmol/L suggest either active diuretic use or a mineralocorticoid excess state. For suspected diuretic-induced alkalosis, collect urine several hours after the last diuretic dose to avoid false interpretation.

**Bottom Line:** Volume and chloride depletion from vomiting, nasogastric suction, or diuretic use are common causes of saline-responsive metabolic alkalosis. Diagnosis typically shows urine chloride <20 mmol/L (except during active diuretic effect), and treatment involves volume and chloride repletion. Understanding the bidirectional relationship with hypokalemia is essential for effective management.

## 4.7. PHYSIOLOGICAL CONSEQUENCES

**Severe metabolic alkalosis has significant physiological consequences affecting multiple organ systems.**

* **Cardiovascular Effects:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Decreased coronary blood flow
	* Coronary artery vasoconstriction
	* Potential myocardial ischemia
  * Predisposition to arrhythmias (especially with hypokalemia)
	* Ventricular ectopy
	* Supraventricular tachycardias
	* Torsades de points
  * Decreased cardiac output in severe cases
	* May contribute to shock or hypotension
  * Enhanced sensitivity to digitalis toxicity
	* Particularly with concomitant hypokalemia

![image.png](ABG_ABG_image_4.png_841645.png_c358f2.png)

* **Respiratory Effects:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Hypoventilation (compensatory)
	* Reduced respiratory drive
	* PCO2 increases ~0.7 mmHg for each 1 mEq/L rise in HCO3-
  * Potential hypoxemia due to decreased respiratory drive
	* ⚠️ Limited compensation to prevent severe hypoxemia
	* Compensation constrained by hypoxic drive
  * Bronchoconstriction (especially in asthmatics)
	* May worsen reactive airway disease
  * Respiratory muscle weakness (if severe hypokalemia)
	* May contribute to respiratory failure
* **Neurological Effects:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Cerebral vasoconstriction
	* Decreased cerebral blood flow
	* Potential cerebral hypoxia
  * Decreased seizure threshold
	* Seizures more common in severe alkalosis
  * Symptoms ranging from confusion and irritability to seizures and tetany
	* Related to decreased ionized calcium
	* Neuromuscular hyperexcitability
  * Increased seizure threshold for certain medications
	* Increased susceptibility to benzodiazepines and barbiturates
  * Paresthesias and carpopedal spasm
	* Related to decreased ionized calcium
* **Electrolyte Disturbances:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Hypokalemia:**
	* Common accompaniment due to transcellular shift and renal losses
	* Worsens alkalosis (bidirectional relationship)
	* Increased urinary potassium excretion
	* K+ shifts into cells in exchange for H+
  * **Hypocalcemia:**
	* Decreased ionized calcium with preserved total calcium
	* Increased protein binding of calcium in alkalemic state
	* Can cause tetany, paresthesias, and seizures
	* Chvostek's and Trousseau's signs may be positive
  * **Hypomagnesemia:**
	* Often accompanies chronic alkalosis
	* Contributes to arrhythmia risk and neuromuscular symptoms
	* May impair potassium repletion
* **Metabolic Effects:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Left shift of oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve (decreased oxygen delivery to tissues)
	* Reduced P50
	* Impaired oxygen unloading at tissue level
  * Impaired cellular metabolism
	* Altered enzymatic activity in alkaline environment
  * Increased lactate production in severe cases
	* Tissue hypoxia from decreased cerebral blood flow
	* Altered redox state
* **Compensatory Mechanisms:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Respiratory compensation (hypoventilation)
	* Limited by need to maintain adequate oxygenation
  * Renal excretion of bicarbonate
	* Impaired by volume depletion, chloride depletion, or hypokalemia
  * Increased bone buffering
	* Release of H+ from bone matrix

⚠️ **Safety Warning:** Severe alkalemia (pH >7.60) can cause tetany, seizures, arrhythmias, and decreased tissue oxygen delivery. Respiratory compensation is limited by hypoxic drive, making severe metabolic alkalosis particularly dangerous. The combination of severe alkalosis and hypokalemia substantially increases risk of cardiac arrhythmias and neuromuscular complications.

**Bottom Line:** Severe metabolic alkalosis (pH >7.6) has wide-ranging physiological consequences including cardiac arrhythmias, decreased cerebral blood flow, hypoventilation, hypocalcemia, and impaired tissue oxygen delivery. The combination of alkalosis and hypokalemia is particularly dangerous due to increased risk of cardiac arrhythmias and neuromuscular symptoms, including potentially compromised respiratory function.

## 4.8. TREATMENT APPROACHES

**Treatment of metabolic alkalosis depends on the underlying cause, with different approaches for saline-responsive versus saline-resistant cases.**

* **General Principles:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Address the underlying cause
  * Correct associated electrolyte abnormalities
  * Tailor approach based on etiology, severity, and volume status
  * Consider the maintenance factors perpetuating alkalosis
  * Assess for presence of associated diseases or conditions complicating treatment
* **Treatment Steps:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  1. **Remove Stimulus to Bicarbonate Generation:**
	 * Discontinue diuretics if possible
	 * Address vomiting or NG suction
	 * Treat excess mineralocorticoid state
	 * Discontinue exogenous alkali administration (including citrate in blood products)
  
  2. **Replete Extracellular Fluid Volume and Potassium:**
	 * Critical for enabling renal bicarbonate excretion
	 * Potassium replacement helps correct alkalosis (bidirectional relationship)
	 * Magnesium repletion often necessary to facilitate potassium correction
  
  3. **Specific Approaches by Type:**
	 * **Saline-Responsive Alkalosis:**
	   * NaCl infusion (0.9% saline)
		 * Expands ECF volume, suppresses RAAS
		 * Provides chloride for HCO3excretion
		 * Typical regimen: 1-2 L of 0.9% NaCl over 24 hours
	   * KCl replacement
		 * Corrects hypokalemia
		 * Provides chloride
		 * IV or oral depending on severity
	   * Address underlying cause (e.g., anti-emetics for vomiting)
	 
	 * **Saline-Resistant Alkalosis:**
	   * Treat underlying cause (e.g., primary hyperaldosteronism)
		 * Surgery for aldosterone-producing adenoma
		 * Medical management for bilateral hyperplasia
	   * Potassium-sparing diuretics (spironolactone, amiloride)
		 * Antagonize mineralocorticoid effects
		 * Help correct hypokalemia and alkalosis
		 * Spironolactone: 25-100 mg daily
		 * Amiloride: 5-10 mg daily
	   * ACE inhibitors in appropriate cases
		 * Reduce aldosterone production
		 * Particularly useful in cirrhosis or heart failure
* **Severe Alkalosis Management:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Consider for pH >7.60 or with severe symptoms
  * **Pharmacological Options:**
	* Acetazolamide: Carbonic anhydrase inhibitor promoting bicarbonate excretion
	  * Dose: 250-500 mg IV/PO every 6 hours
	  * ⚠️ Can worsen hypokalemia
	  * Requires adequate renal function
	  * Particularly useful in edematous states where volume expansion is contraindicated
	* Hydrochloric Acid (HCl): For life-threatening alkalosis
	  * Requires central venous access and ICU monitoring
	  * Dosing: 0.1-0.2 mEq/kg/hr
	  * Calculate deficit: 0.5 × weight (kg) × (plasma [HCO3-] - 24)
	  * Administer half the calculated dose over 24 hours
	  * ⚠️ Risk of hemolysis, tissue necrosis with extravasation
	* Ammonium Chloride: Alternative to HCl
	  * Contraindicated in liver disease (risk of hyperammonemia)
	  * Dose: 2-3 mEq/kg/day in divided doses
  
  * **Dialysis:**
	* Consider in severe, refractory cases with kidney failure
	* Use low-bicarbonate dialysate
	* Particularly useful when volume overload limits saline administration
* **Special Considerations:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * **Post-Hypercapnic Alkalosis:**
	* Occurs after rapid correction of chronic respiratory acidosis
	* Allow gradual normalization, avoid overaggressive ventilation
	* Acetazolamide may help increase bicarbonate excretion
  
  * **Diuretic-Induced Alkalosis:**
	* Consider potassium-sparing diuretics
	* Use judicious volume repletion
	* Replace potassium and magnesium
	* Consider switching to different class of diuretic
  
  * **Edematous States:**
	* Challenging due to volume overload but effective volume depletion
	* Potassium-sparing diuretics and ACE inhibitors may help
	* Acetazolamide can be effective
	* Treat underlying condition (heart failure, cirrhosis)
	* Dialysis for severe cases with renal failure
* **Monitoring During Treatment:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Frequent electrolyte checks
  * Arterial or venous blood gases
  * Vital signs and fluid status
  * Neurological status
  * Cardiac monitoring in severe cases

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** In diuretic-induced metabolic alkalosis with heart failure, adding spironolactone or acetazolamide may help correct alkalosis without excessive volume expansion. The DOSE-AHF trial demonstrated acetazolamide's efficacy in this setting, reducing the need for high-dose loop diuretics while improving decongestion and acid-base status.

⚠️ **Safety Warning:** Aggressive correction of chronic metabolic alkalosis can lead to rapid electrolyte shifts and clinical deterioration. Correct gradually with close monitoring of electrolytes, especially potassium and calcium. Too rapid correction can precipitate seizures, arrhythmias, and shock.

**Bottom Line:** Treatment of metabolic alkalosis involves removing the stimulus, repleting volume and electrolytes, and taking specific approaches based on whether the condition is saline-responsive or saline-resistant. Severe alkalosis may require acetazolamide, HCl, or NH4Cl, while addressing the underlying cause remains essential. The approach should be tailored to the individual patient, considering both the etiology and the presence of complicating factors such as heart failure, liver disease, or renal impairment.

# 5. RESPIRATORY ACIDOSIS




## 5.2. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY
**Respiratory acidosis results from impaired elimination of carbon dioxide, leading to hypercapnia and acidemia.**

* **Definition:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Primary increase in PaCO2 (>45 mmHg)
  * Associated acidemia (pH <7.35) without primary metabolic disorder
  * Secondary increase in bicarbonate from renal compensation (in chronic cases)
  * Classified as Type 2 respiratory failure when combined with hypoxemia
* **Pathophysiological Mechanisms:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Alveolar hypoventilation relative to CO2 production
	* Minute ventilation (VE) inadequate for metabolic CO2 production
	* VE = respiratory rate × tidal volume
  * Ventilation-perfusion mismatch with inadequate ventilation
	* Areas of low V/Q contribute to CO2 retention
	* Particularly in obstructive lung diseases
  * Increased CO2 production exceeding ventilatory capacity
	* Hypercatabolic states (fever, sepsis, burns)
	* Excessive carbohydrate administration
  * Increased dead space ventilation
	* Anatomic (e.g., emphysema)
	* Physiologic (e.g., pulmonary embolism)
  * Inadequate respiratory muscle function or neural drive
	* CNS depression
	* Neuromuscular disorders
	* Chest wall restrictions
* **Acid-Base Chemistry:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * CO2 combines with H2O to form carbonic acid (H2CO3)
  * H2CO3 dissociates into H+ and HCO3-
  * Increased CO2 → increased H+ → decreased pH
  * CO2 + H2O ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ H+ + HCO3-
  * Changes in PaCO2 rapidly affect pH due to high CO2 diffusibility
* **Compensatory Mechanisms:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Acute (minutes to hours):**
	* Chemical buffering (primarily hemoglobin and proteins)
	* Limited HCO3increase (1 mEq/L for each 10 mmHg PCO2 increase)
	* Intracellular buffering (proteins, phosphates)
  * **Chronic (3-5 days):**
	* Renal compensation with enhanced H+ excretion and HCO3reabsorption
	* More significant HCO3increase (3-4 mEq/L for each 10 mmHg PCO2 increase)
	* New steady state with normalized pH but elevated PCO2 and HCO3-
	* Mechanism: increased NH4+ production and excretion, increased H+ secretion
* **Beneficial Physiological Effects:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Increased catecholamine release → increased cardiac output and blood pressure
  * Enhanced organ perfusion (brain, heart, kidneys)
  * Right shift of oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve (Bohr effect) → increased oxygen unloading to tissues
  * Respiratory acidosis better tolerated than metabolic acidosis at similar pH
* **Categories of Respiratory Acidosis:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Acute:**
	* Develops over minutes to hours
	* Limited compensation
	* Significant acidemia
  * **Chronic:**
	* Develops over days to weeks
	* Substantial renal compensation
	* Near-normal pH with elevated HCO3-
  * **Acute-on-chronic:**
	* Acute worsening in a patient with chronic hypercapnia
	* Partial compensation present
	* Less severe acidemia than pure acute respiratory acidosis

**Bottom Line:** Respiratory acidosis occurs when CO2 elimination is impaired relative to production, leading to hypercapnia and acidemia. Compensatory mechanisms include immediate chemical buffering and delayed renal bicarbonate retention, with more complete compensation in chronic states. Understanding the pathophysiology guides diagnostic evaluation and treatment approaches, particularly in distinguishing acute from chronic respiratory acidosis.

## 5.3. ACUTE VS. CHRONIC

**Distinguishing between acute and chronic respiratory acidosis is clinically important for management and interpretation of compensation.**

* **Acute Respiratory Acidosis:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Timeframe:** Develops over minutes to hours
  * **Compensation:** Limited
	* HCO3increases by approximately 1 mEq/L for each 10 mmHg rise in PCO2
	* Primarily relies on chemical buffering
	* Example: PCO2 rises from 40 to 60 mmHg → HCO3rises from 24 to 26 mEq/L
  * **Presentation:**
	* More dramatic symptoms due to rapid pH change
	* Significant acidemia despite similar PCO2 to chronic cases
	* Greater hemodynamic effects
	* Prominent neurological symptoms
  * **Clinical Examples:**
	* Acute respiratory failure
	* Airway obstruction
	* Opioid overdose
	* Acute neuromuscular disease
	* Severe pneumonia
	* Status asthmaticus
* **Chronic Respiratory Acidosis:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Timeframe:** Develops over days to weeks
  * **Compensation:** More complete
	* HCO3increases by 3-4 mEq/L for each 10 mmHg rise in PCO2
	* Renal adaptation with enhanced H+ excretion and HCO3reabsorption
	* Example: PCO2 rises from 40 to 60 mmHg → HCO3rises from 24 to 32 mEq/L
  * **Presentation:**
	* Better tolerated despite similar or higher PCO2
	* Near-normal pH due to effective renal compensation
	* Adaptation of central chemoreceptors (decreased sensitivity to CO2)
	* Often more insidious symptoms
  * **Clinical Examples:**
	* COPD
	* Obesity hypoventilation syndrome
	* Severe kyphoscoliosis
	* Chronic neuromuscular disease
	* Central hypoventilation syndromes
	* Severe restrictive lung disease
* **Acute-on-Chronic Respiratory Acidosis:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Definition:** Acute worsening of PCO2 in patient with preexisting chronic hypercapnia
  * **Presentation:**
	* Baseline elevated HCO3from chronic compensation
	* Less severe acidemia than pure acute respiratory acidosis
	* More severe acidemia than stable chronic respiratory acidosis
  * **Clinical Example:**
	* COPD exacerbation
	* Respiratory infection in patient with neuromuscular disease
* **Distinguishing Features on ABG:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Acute:** Marked acidemia with modest HCO3elevation
	* pH <7.30, HCO3mildly elevated
	* PCO2 elevated but HCO3not proportionally increased
  * **Chronic:** Near-normal pH with significantly elevated HCO3-
	* pH 7.35-7.40, HCO3significantly elevated
	* Both PCO2 and HCO3elevated proportionally
  * **Acute-on-Chronic:** Worsening acidemia from baseline with already elevated HCO3-
	* pH <7.35, HCO3elevated but not proportional to PCO2 increase
	* Higher HCO3than would be expected for pure acute respiratory acidosis
* **Clinical Significance:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Affects treatment decisions and ventilation targets
	* Acute: May require more aggressive ventilatory support
	* Chronic: More conservative approach to PCO2 correction
  * Determines safe rate of PCO2 correction
	* Rapid correction of chronic hypercapnia can cause post-hypercapnic alkalosis
  * Influences prognosis and expected symptoms
	* Chronic cases often have fewer symptoms despite severe hypercapnia
  * Guides interpretation of acid-base status
	* Distinguishes compensated chronic respiratory acidosis from mixed disorder

⚠️ **Safety Warning:** Rapid normalization of PaCO2 in chronic respiratory acidosis can lead to severe metabolic alkalosis ("post-hypercapnic alkalosis") and adverse neurological effects. Target gradual PCO2 reduction in chronic cases, aiming for no more than 5-10 mmHg reduction in PCO2 per day.

**Bottom Line:** Acute respiratory acidosis shows limited compensation with significant acidemia, while chronic respiratory acidosis demonstrates substantial renal compensation with near-normal pH despite persistent hypercapnia. This distinction affects management decisions, particularly regarding ventilation strategies and correction targets. Acute-on-chronic respiratory acidosis represents an acute deterioration superimposed on chronic hypercapnia and requires careful assessment and management.

## 5.4. CLINICAL CAUSES

**Respiratory acidosis occurs in various clinical conditions affecting ventilation relative to CO2 production.**

* **Type 2 Respiratory Failure:** Ventilatory failure often with concomitant hypoxemia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]

![Other causes of T2 RF also includes: Spinal cord injury, NMJ: MG, Lambert Eaton, Peripheral neuropathy etc.](ABG_ABG_Untitled_18.png_b1d74b.png_64ddef.png)

* **Central Nervous System Disorders:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Decreased Respiratory Drive:**
	* Drug-induced: Opioids, benzodiazepines, barbiturates, propofol
	  * Common in perioperative setting and chronic pain management
	  * Dose-dependent depression of respiratory center
	* Central sleep apnea
	  * Impaired CO2 sensing during sleep
	* Brain injury: Stroke, trauma, infection affecting brainstem
	  * Damage to respiratory centers in medulla or pons
	* Central hypoventilation syndromes (Ondine's curse)
	  * Congenital or acquired failure of automatic breathing control
  * **CNS Mass Lesions:**
	* Brain tumors
	* Intracranial hemorrhage
	* Posterior fossa lesions particularly important (affecting brainstem)
* **Neuromuscular Disorders:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Spinal Cord:**
	* High cervical spinal cord injury
	  * C3-5 injury affects phrenic nerve function
	* Transverse myelitis
  * **Motor Neuron Disease:**
	* Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (Also)
	* Poliomyelitis
	* Progressive muscular atrophy
  * **Neuromuscular Junction:**
	* Myasthenia gravis
	* Lambert-Eaton syndrome
	* Botulism
	* Neuromuscular blocking agents
	  * Residual effects post-anesthesia
	* Organophosphate poisoning
  * **Muscle:**
	* Muscular dystrophies (Duchenne's, myotonic)
	* Myopathies (metabolic, inflammatory)
	* Critical illness myopathy
	* Electrolyte disorders (hypokalemia, hypophosphatemia)
	* Primary respiratory muscle fatigue
* **Chest Wall Disorders:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Severe kyphoscoliosis
	* Restrictive defect limiting tidal volume
  * Flail chest
	* Paradoxical chest wall movement
  * Thoracoplasty
  * Obesity hypoventilation syndrome
	* Increased work of breathing
	* Decreased chest wall compliance
	* Often associated with sleep apnea
  * Abdominal compartment syndrome
	* Restricts diaphragmatic excursion
  * Massive ascites
  * Tight circumferential chest dressings/casts
* **Pulmonary Disorders:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Airways:**
	* Severe asthma
	  * Bronchospasm, mucus plugging, inflammation
	* COPD exacerbation
	  * Increased airway resistance
	  * Air trapping and dynamic hyperinflation
	* Upper airway obstruction
	  * Foreign body, laryngospasm, angioedema
	* Bronchospasm
  * **Parenchymal:**
	* Severe pneumonia
	* Pulmonary edema
	  * Cardiogenic or non-cardiogenic
	* ARDS
	  * Increased dead space
	  * Decreased compliance
	* Massive atelectasis
  * **Iatrogenic:**
	* Inappropriate mechanical ventilation settings
	  * Inadequate minute ventilation
	  * Excessive dead space
	* Excessive dead space in ventilator circuit
	* Exhausted CO2 absorber in circle system
	* Malposition of endotracheal tube
* **Increased CO2 Production:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Malignant hyperthermia
	* Hypermetabolic crisis with marked increase in CO2 production
  * Thyroid storm
  * Extensive burns
  * Severe sepsis
  * Excessive carbohydrate intake in respiratory compromise
  * Fever
  * Seizures
  * Rigors

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** In mechanically ventilated patients with unexpected respiratory acidosis, consider circuit issues (disconnection, leak), patient-ventilator asynchrony, or increased CO2 production (fever, agitation, overfeeding) before adjusting ventilator settings. The acute development of hypercapnia during anesthesia should prompt immediate consideration of malignant hyperthermia if accompanied by tachycardia, rigidity, and hyperthermia.

**Bottom Line:** Respiratory acidosis results from diverse conditions affecting ventilatory drive, neuromuscular function, chest wall mechanics, pulmonary function, or metabolic CO2 production. Understanding the specific cause guides targeted treatment beyond supportive ventilation. Categorizing the etiology as central, neuromuscular, chest wall, pulmonary, or metabolic helps direct appropriate interventions.

## 5.5. PHYSIOLOGICAL CONSEQUENCES

**Respiratory acidosis has significant physiological effects on multiple organ systems, with some potentially beneficial and others harmful.**

* **Cardiovascular Effects:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Direct Effects of Hypercapnia:**
	* Myocardial depression at severe levels
	  * Direct negative inotropic effect with pH <7.1
	  * Reduced contractility
	* Vasodilation of systemic vessels
	  * Direct effect of CO2 on vascular smooth muscle
	  * Contributes to hypotension in severe cases
	* Pulmonary vasoconstriction (can worsen pulmonary hypertension)
	  * Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction augmented by acidosis
	  * Increases right ventricular afterload
  * **Indirect Effects via Sympathetic Stimulation:**
	* Increased catecholamine release
	  * Stimulation of adrenal medulla
	  * Enhanced peripheral sympathetic activity
	* Increased heart rate and cardiac output
	  * Tachycardia
	  * Augmented stroke volume
	* Elevated systemic blood pressure
	  * Counteracts direct vasodilatory effects
	  * Net effect depends on severity
  * **Arrhythmogenic Potential:**
	* Increased risk of arrhythmias, especially with electrolyte abnormalities
	  * Atrial and ventricular ectopy
	  * Atrial fibrillation
	* Decreased threshold for ventricular fibrillation
	  * Enhanced automaticity
	  * Decreased fibrillation threshold
* **Cerebral Effects:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Cerebral Blood Flow:**
	* Potent cerebral vasodilation (4% increase per mmHg rise in PaCO2)
	  * Linear relationship between PaCO2 and CBF
	  * Mediated by local H+ concentration
	* Increased intracranial pressure
	  * Due to increased cerebral blood volume
	  * Particularly problematic in patients with space-occupying lesions
	* Potential cerebral edema with severe acute hypercapnia
	  * Breakdown of blood-brain barrier
  * **Neurological Symptoms:**
	* Mild: Anxiety, confusion, headache
	  * Earliest manifestations
	* Moderate: Somnolence, disorientation
	  * PaCO2 >60-70 mmHg
	* Severe: Coma, papilledema, seizures
	  * PaCO2 >100 mmHg or rapid increase
  * **Cerebrospinal Fluid:**
	* pH decreases more slowly than blood in acute respiratory acidosis
	  * Limited permeability of blood-brain barrier to H+
	* Fully equilibrates in chronic respiratory acidosis
	  * Explains adaptation to chronic hypercapnia
* **Respiratory Effects:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Increased work of breathing (compensatory)
	* Use of accessory muscles
	* Increased minute ventilation
  * Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (worsened by acidosis)
	* Increases pulmonary vascular resistance
	* Worsens ventilation-perfusion matching
  * Blunted response to hypoxia in chronic hypercapnia
	* Reset chemoreceptor sensitivity
	* Risks respiratory arrest with oxygen therapy ("hypoxic drive")
  * Bronchodilation (potentially beneficial in obstructive disease)
	* Direct relaxant effect on bronchial smooth muscle
	* Counteracts bronchoconstriction from other causes
* **Metabolic Effects:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Enhanced oxygen delivery to tissues (right shift of oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve)
	* Bohr effect
	* Facilitates tissue oxygen unloading
  * Hyperkalemia due to transcellular shift
	* H+ enters cells, K+ exits
	* Approximately 0.5 mEq/L rise in K+ for each 0.1 unit fall in pH
  * Impaired enzyme function in severe acidosis
	* pH-sensitive enzymes
	* Metabolic pathways disruption
  * Increased anaerobic metabolism with severe acidosis
	* Tissue hypoxia
	* Lactic acid production
* **Renal Effects:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Enhanced renal blood flow (acute phase)
	* Vasodilation of renal vasculature
  * Increased H+ excretion and HCO3reabsorption (compensatory)
	* Enhanced ammoniagenesis
	* Increased proximal tubular bicarbonate reabsorption
  * Mild natriuresis and kaluresis
	* Enhanced distal tubular Na+/K+/H+ exchange

⚠️ **Safety Warning:** Severe hypercapnia (PaCO2 >80-100 mmHg) with acidemia can cause significant hemodynamic instability, including arrhythmias and myocardial depression, particularly in patients with pre-existing cardiac disease. Acute increases in PaCO2 can cause dangerous elevations in intracranial pressure in patients with intracranial pathology.

**Bottom Line:** Respiratory acidosis affects multiple organ systems, with notable effects including sympathetic stimulation, cerebral vasodilation, increased intracranial pressure, and enhanced oxygen delivery to tissues. Severe acute hypercapnia can cause significant neurological and cardiovascular compromise requiring prompt intervention. Chronic hypercapnia is generally better tolerated due to adaptation of central chemoreceptors and renal compensation.

## 5.6. TREATMENT AND MANAGEMENT

**Management of respiratory acidosis focuses on improving ventilation while addressing the underlying cause.**

* **General Principles:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Treat the underlying cause
  * Support ventilation as needed
  * Consider the acuity of the process in determining urgency and targets
  * Avoid rapid normalization in chronic respiratory acidosis
  * Address complications (e.g., hypoxemia, hypotension)
* **Specific Treatments by Cause:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * **Central Nervous System Depression:**
	* Reversal agents when appropriate (e.g., naloxone for opioids, flumazenil for benzodiazepines)
	  * Naloxone: 0.04-0.4 mg IV, titrate to effect
	  * Flumazenil: 0.2 mg IV, repeat to maximum 1 mg
	* Careful dose titration to avoid withdrawal or seizures
	* Discontinue or reduce dosage of offending medications
  * **Airway Obstruction:**
	* Bronchodilators for bronchospasm
	  * β2-agonists (albuterol/salbutamol)
	  * Anticholinergics (ipratropium)
	  * Systemic corticosteroids
	* Racemic epinephrine for upper airway edema
	* Airway clearance techniques for secretions
	* Mechanical intervention for foreign body
  * **Neuromuscular Weakness:**
	* Specific treatments for reversible causes (e.g., pyridostigmine for myasthenia gravis)
	* Ventilatory support for irreversible or progressing conditions
	* Correction of electrolyte abnormalities (K+, Mg2+, phosphate)
  * **COPD Exacerbation:**
	* Bronchodilators, corticosteroids, antibiotics when indicated
	* Carefully titrated oxygen therapy (target SpO2 88-92%)
	* Non-invasive or invasive ventilation based on severity
	* Address associated factors (secretions, bronchospasm, infection)
* **Ventilatory Support Strategies:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * **Non-invasive Ventilation (NIV):**
	* First-line for many causes (especially COPD exacerbation)
	* CPAP: Primarily improves oxygenation
	* BiPAP: Improves both ventilation and oxygenation
	  * Higher inspiratory pressure (IPAP) reduces work of breathing
	  * Higher pressure differential (IPAP-EPAP) improves ventilation
	* Contraindicated in obtunded patients, excessive secretions, facial trauma
	* Typical settings:
	  * IPAP: 10-20 cmH2O
	  * EPAP: 4-8 cmH2O
  * **Invasive Mechanical Ventilation:**
	* For severe respiratory acidosis or NIV failure
	* Ventilator settings should target gradual normalization of pH rather than PCO2
	  * Mode: Typically volume-controlled or pressure support
	  * Tidal volume: 6-8 mL/kg IBW
	  * Rate: Adjust to achieve desired minute ventilation
	  * I:E ratio: Consider prolonged expiratory time in obstructive disease
	* Consider lung-protective strategies in ARDS and other parenchymal disease
	* Permissive hypercapnia may be appropriate in certain conditions (ARDS, asthma)
* **Special Considerations:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * **Chronic Respiratory Acidosis:**
	* ⚠️ Avoid rapid normalization of PaCO2 (risk of post-hypercapnic alkalosis)
	* Target PaCO2 reduction of 5-10 mmHg per day
	* Monitor for and treat electrolyte abnormalities
	* Consider baseline PCO2 when setting ventilation targets
  * **Intracranial Hypertension:**
	* More aggressive PCO2 normalization may be necessary despite chronic hypercapnia
	* Balance risks of alkalosis against benefits of reducing intracranial pressure
	* Consider ICP monitoring in severe cases
  * **Perioperative Management:**
	* Avoid hyperventilation in patients with compensated chronic respiratory acidosis
	* Target pH rather than absolute PCO2 values
	* Consider multimodal analgesia or regional techniques to minimize opioid requirements
	* ⚠️ Phrenic nerve palsy from interscalene or supraclavicular blocks may worsen respiratory status
	* Extended monitoring in PACU for at-risk patients
* **Controversial Therapies:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * **Bicarbonate Therapy:**
	* Limited evidence supporting use in respiratory acidosis
	* May worsen intracellular acidosis due to CO2 generation
	* Consider only in severe cases with hemodynamic compromise
	* If used, administer slowly and monitor closely
  * **Extracorporeal CO2 Removal (ECCO2R):**
	* Emerging therapy for severe respiratory acidosis
	* Consider in refractory cases where mechanical ventilation is inadequate or contraindicated
	* Lower flow rates than full ECMO
	* Primarily removes CO2 rather than providing oxygenation
  * **Helium-Oxygen Mixtures (Heliox):**
	* Reduces work of breathing in severe airway obstruction
	* Temporary measure while treating underlying cause
	* Limited evidence for long-term benefits
* **Long-term Management:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * COPD: Bronchodilators, inhaled corticosteroids, smoking cessation
  * Obesity hypoventilation: Weight loss, CPAP/BiPAP, treat sleep apnea
  * Neuromuscular disease: Nocturnal NIV, secretion management, pneumonia prevention
  * Chronic respiratory support for irreversible conditions

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** In COPD exacerbation with respiratory acidosis, non-invasive ventilation (NIV) can reduce intubation rates, mortality, and hospital length of stay. Early initiation is key, with careful monitoring for response within the first 1-2 hours. If pH improves by even 0.05-0.1 units in this timeframe, continue NIV; if not, consider escalation to invasive ventilation.

**Bottom Line:** Management of respiratory acidosis focuses on treating the underlying cause and supporting ventilation as needed. Treatment strategies vary by etiology, with attention to the acute versus chronic nature of the condition. In chronic respiratory acidosis, avoid rapid normalization of PCO2 to prevent post-hypercapnic alkalosis and associated complications. The approach should be individualized based on patient factors, underlying cause, and clinical severity.

# 6. RESPIRATORY ALKALOSIS




## 6.2. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY
**Respiratory alkalosis results from excessive elimination of carbon dioxide relative to production, leading to hypocapnia and alkalemia.**

* **Definition:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Primary decrease in PaCO2 (<35 mmHg)
  * Associated alkalemia (pH >7.45) without primary metabolic disorder
  * Secondary decrease in bicarbonate from renal compensation (in chronic cases)
  * Also referred to as hypocapnia or hypocapnic alkalosis
* **Pathophysiological Mechanism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Alveolar hyperventilation relative to metabolic CO2 production
	* Increased respiratory rate and/or tidal volume
	* Elimination of CO2 exceeds production
  * Minute ventilation exceeds what is necessary to eliminate CO2
	* Disproportionate increase in alveolar ventilation
  * Reduced carbonic acid formation → decreased H+ concentration → increased pH
	* CO2 + H2O ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ H+ + HCO3-
	* Shift of equilibrium toward reduced H+ concentration
  * Most common acute acid-base disturbance (especially in hospitalized patients)
* **Compensatory Mechanisms:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Acute (minutes to hours):**
	* Chemical buffering
	  * H+ release from intracellular buffers
	  * Hemoglobin, proteins, phosphates
	* Limited HCO3decrease (2 mEq/L for each 10 mmHg PCO2 decrease)
	  * Example: PCO2 falls from 40 to 20 mmHg → HCO3falls from 24 to 20 mEq/L
  * **Chronic (3-5 days):**
	* Renal compensation with decreased H+ excretion and HCO3reabsorption
	  * Reduced renal ammoniagenesis
	  * Decreased proximal tubular HCO3reabsorption
	* More significant HCO3decrease (5 mEq/L for each 10 mmHg PCO2 decrease)
	  * Example: PCO2 falls from 40 to 20 mmHg → HCO3falls from 24 to 14 mEq/L
	* New steady state with near-normalized pH but decreased PCO2 and HCO3-
* **Relationship to Oxygenation:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Often develops in response to hypoxemia (hypoxic stimulation of peripheral chemoreceptors)
	* Increased respiratory drive due to low PaO2
	* Represents Type 1 respiratory failure with hyperventilation
  * Can occur independently of hypoxemia
	* Direct stimulation of respiratory center
	* Mechanical hyperventilation
  * Left-shift of oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve
	* Impairs oxygen unloading to tissues
	* May worsen tissue hypoxia despite normal PaO2
* **Quantitative Aspects:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Each 1 mmHg decrease in PaCO2 increases pH by approximately 0.008 units
  * Acute hypocapnia causes more significant pH changes than chronic
  * Degree of alkalemia proportional to degree of hypocapnia in acute states

**Bottom Line:** Respiratory alkalosis occurs when CO2 elimination exceeds production due to hyperventilation, leading to hypocapnia and alkalemia. Compensatory mechanisms include immediate chemical buffering and delayed renal bicarbonate reduction, with more complete compensation in chronic states. This disorder often represents the body's response to hypoxemia or direct respiratory center stimulation and can have significant physiological consequences through alterations in pH and oxygen delivery.

## 6.3. ACUTE VS. CHRONIC

**Distinguishing between acute and chronic respiratory alkalosis is clinically important for interpretation of compensation and management.**

* **Acute Respiratory Alkalosis:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Timeframe:** Develops over minutes to hours
  * **Compensation:** Limited
	* HCO3decreases by approximately 2 mEq/L for each 10 mmHg fall in PCO2
	* Primarily relies on chemical buffering
	* Example: PCO2 falls from 40 to 20 mmHg → HCO3falls from 24 to 20 mEq/L
  * **Presentation:**
	* More pronounced symptoms due to rapid pH change
	* Significant alkalemia despite modest PCO2 reduction
	* More dramatic neurological and cardiovascular effects
	* Neuromuscular irritability, tetany, perioral numbness
  * **Clinical Examples:**
	* Acute anxiety/panic attack
	* Acute pain
	* Early sepsis
	* Acute hypoxemia
	* Mechanical hyperventilation
	* Pulmonary embolism
* **Chronic Respiratory Alkalosis:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Timeframe:** Develops over days to weeks
  * **Compensation:** More complete
	* HCO3decreases by 5 mEq/L for each 10 mmHg fall in PCO2
	* Renal adaptation with decreased H+ excretion and HCO3reabsorption
	* Example: PCO2 falls from 40 to 20 mmHg → HCO3falls from 24 to 14 mEq/L
  * **Presentation:**
	* Better tolerated despite similar PCO2 levels
	* Near-normal pH due to effective renal compensation
	* Fewer acute neurological symptoms
	* Adaptation to chronic state
  * **Clinical Examples:**
	* Chronic liver disease
	* Pregnancy
	* High altitude adaptation
	* Chronic hypoxemic lung disease
	* Central neurogenic hyperventilation
	* Salicylate intoxication
* **Distinguishing Features on ABG:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Acute:** Marked alkalemia with modest HCO3reduction
	* pH >7.45, mildly decreased HCO3-
	* PCO2 decreased with minimal HCO3compensation
  * **Chronic:** Near-normal pH with significantly reduced HCO3-
	* pH 7.40-7.45, significantly decreased HCO3-
	* Both PCO2 and HCO3decreased proportionally
  * **Acute-on-Chronic:** Worsening alkalemia from baseline with already reduced HCO3-
	* pH >7.45, HCO3already low from chronic compensation
	* Further decrease in PCO2 beyond baseline chronic level
* **Clinical Significance:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Affects interpretation of seemingly normal pH values
	* Chronic respiratory alkalosis with appropriate compensation may have near-normal pH
	* Underlying disorder still requires recognition and management
  * Influences management decisions and expected symptoms
	* Acute: More symptomatic, may require immediate intervention
	* Chronic: Better tolerated, more conservative management
  * Determines appropriate ventilation targets when mechanical ventilation is needed
	* Avoid abrupt changes in chronic states
	* May need to maintain mild hypocapnia in chronic conditions
* **Physiological Adaptation in Chronic States:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Reset central chemoreceptor sensitivity
  * Altered cerebrospinal fluid pH regulation
  * Renal adaptation with decreased bicarbonate reabsorption
  * Cellular adaptation to alkalotic state

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** In pregnant women, chronic respiratory alkalosis is physiological (progesterone-induced respiratory stimulation) with PCO2 typically 28-32 mmHg and compensatory reduction in bicarbonate. This is important to recognize when interpreting ABGs in pregnancy, as what might appear abnormal is actually the normal physiological adaptation to pregnancy.

**Bottom Line:** Acute respiratory alkalosis presents with significant alkalemia and limited compensation, while chronic respiratory alkalosis shows substantial renal compensation with near-normal pH despite persistent hypocapnia. This distinction aids in diagnostic interpretation and guides appropriate management. Recognition of physiological chronic respiratory alkalosis in conditions like pregnancy and high-altitude exposure prevents unnecessary interventions.

## 6.4. CLINICAL CAUSES

**Respiratory alkalosis has diverse etiologies categorized by their pathophysiological mechanisms.**

* **Response to Hypoxemia (Type 1 Respiratory Failure):** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Pulmonary Disease:**
	* Pneumonia
	  * Ventilation-perfusion mismatch
	  * Stimulation of peripheral chemoreceptors
	* Pulmonary edema
	  * Cardiogenic or non-cardiogenic
	  * Stimulation of J-receptors
	* Interstitial lung disease
	  * Decreased lung compliance
	  * Stimulation of stretch receptors
	* Asthma (early phase)
	  * Anxiety
	  * Increased work of breathing
  * **Pulmonary Vascular Disease:**
	* Pulmonary embolism
	  * Increased dead space
	  * Hypoxemia
	  * Stimulation of J-receptors
	* Pulmonary hypertension
	  * V/Q mismatch
	  * Right heart strain
  * **Cardiac Disease:**
	* Right-to-left shunts
	  * Cyanotic congenital heart disease
	* Congestive heart failure
	  * Pulmonary congestion
	  * Stimulation of J-receptors
* **Central Respiratory Stimulation:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Physiological:**
	* Pregnancy (progesterone effect)
	  * Direct stimulation of respiratory center
	  * Usually PaCO2 28-32 mmHg
	* High altitude (hypoxic drive)
	  * Compensation for decreased atmospheric oxygen
	  * Part of acclimatization process
	* Exercise
	  * Anticipatory response to increased CO2 production
	  * Usually transient
  * **Pathological CNS Stimulation:**
	* Fever or sepsis
	  * Direct effect on respiratory center
	  * Cytokine-mediated
	* CNS infection (meningitis, encephalitis)
	  * Meningeal irritation
	  * Increased ICP
	* CNS tumors or lesions
	  * Central neurogenic hyperventilation
	  * Typically involves midbrain/pons
	* Cerebrovascular accident
	  * Especially brainstem lesions
	* Traumatic brain injury
	  * Often part of Cushing's triad
  * **Metabolic/Hormonal:**
	* Hyperthyroidism
	  * Increased metabolic rate
	  * Direct stimulation of respiratory center
	* Hepatic failure
	  * Hyperammonemia
	  * Altered neurotransmitter metabolism
	* Pregnancy
	  * Progesterone effect
	* Salicylate intoxication
	  * Direct stimulation of respiratory center
	  * Initial phase before metabolic acidosis dominates
	* Recovery from metabolic acidosis
	  * Persistent respiratory compensation
	  * "Post-hypercapnic alkalosis"
* **Psychological/Voluntary:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Anxiety/panic disorders
	* Most common cause in emergency department
	* Characteristic pattern of deep sighing respirations
  * Voluntary hyperventilation
	* Often for demonstration purposes
  * Pain
	* Direct stimulation of respiratory center
	* Part of sympathetic response
  * Stress
	* Part of "fight or flight" response
	* Often subtle hyperventilation
* **Iatrogenic/Mechanical:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Excessive mechanical ventilation
	* Common in intubated patients
	* Usually due to inappropriate ventilator settings
  * Inappropriate ventilator settings
	* Excessive minute ventilation
	* Failure to adjust for patient needs
  * Overzealous bag-mask ventilation
	* During emergency situations
	* During anesthesia induction
  * Anesthesia-induced (light plane)
	* Response to surgical stimulation
	* Inadequate anesthetic depth
* **Drugs and Toxins:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Salicylates
	* Direct stimulation of respiratory center
	* Later phase develops metabolic acidosis
  * Nicotine
	* Carotid body stimulation
  * Xanthines (e.g., caffeine, theophylline)
	* Respiratory stimulants
	* Lowered threshold for CO2 response
  * Catecholamines
	* Sympathetic stimulation
  * Progesterone
	* Similar to pregnancy effect
  * Analeptics
	* Doxapram
	* Direct respiratory stimulants
* **Mixed Disorders:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Respiratory alkalosis with concurrent metabolic acidosis
	* Common in sepsis
	* Salicylate toxicity
	* Liver failure
  * Respiratory alkalosis masking metabolic acidosis
	* May show normal or near-normal pH

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Consider pulmonary embolism in patients with unexplained respiratory alkalosis, particularly when accompanied by hypoxemia disproportionate to lung examination findings. The hyperventilation often precedes the development of tachycardia and hypotension. Unexplained hyperventilation may be the earliest sign of sepsis, even before fever or leukocytosis.

⚠️ **Safety Warning:** In mechanically ventilated patients, iatrogenic respiratory alkalosis from excessive ventilation can cause cerebral vasoconstriction, decreased cerebral blood flow, and potential tissue hypoxia despite normal arterial oxygen levels. Adjust ventilator settings to achieve appropriate PCO2 for the clinical context.

**Bottom Line:** Respiratory alkalosis results from diverse causes including hypoxemia, central respiratory stimulation, psychological factors, iatrogenic hyperventilation, and drug effects. Categorizing by mechanism helps guide diagnostic evaluation and targeted treatment. The most common causes in clinical practice are anxiety/panic disorders, pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, and iatrogenic hyperventilation in mechanically ventilated patients.

## 6.5. PHYSIOLOGICAL CONSEQUENCES

**Respiratory alkalosis has significant physiological effects on multiple organ systems, many of which are clinically important.**

* **Cardiovascular Effects:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Coronary artery vasoconstriction → potentially reduced coronary blood flow
	* May precipitate angina in susceptible individuals
	* Decreased myocardial oxygen delivery despite normal PaO2
  * Systemic vasoconstriction → increased peripheral resistance
	* Partly counteracted by increased cardiac output
	* Net effect depends on severity
  * Potential for cardiac arrhythmias (especially with electrolyte abnormalities)
	* Ventricular ectopy
	* Supraventricular tachycardias
	* Enhanced automaticity
  * Tachycardia and increased cardiac output initially
	* Sympathetic stimulation
	* Direct chronotropic effect
  * Chest pain/discomfort (may mimic angina)
	* Coronary vasoconstriction
	* Musculoskeletal (respiratory muscle fatigue)
* **Neurological Effects:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Cerebral Blood Flow:**
	* Potent cerebral vasoconstriction (2% decrease per mmHg fall in PaCO2)
	  * Linear relationship in range of 20-60 mmHg
	  * Mediated by perivascular pH
	* Decreased cerebral blood flow and oxygen delivery
	  * May reach critical levels below PaCO2 of 20-25 mmHg
	  * Particularly problematic in vascular insufficiency
	* Potential cerebral ischemia with severe hypocapnia
	  * Tissue hypoxia despite normal arterial PO2
	  * Reduced cerebral metabolic rate
  * **Neurological Symptoms:**
	* Mild: Light-headedness, dizziness, paresthesias
	  * Earliest and most common symptoms
	  * Circumoral and distal extremity paresthesias
	* Moderate: Tetany, carpopedal spasm, perioral numbness
	  * Due to decreased ionized calcium
	  * Trousseau's sign may be positive
	* Severe: Seizures, altered consciousness, syncope
	  * Cerebral hypoperfusion
	  * Neuronal hyperexcitability
  * **Neuromuscular Irritability:**
	* Decreased ionized calcium due to increased protein binding
	  * Total calcium unchanged
	  * pH-dependent protein binding
	* Enhanced nerve and muscle excitability
	  * Decreased threshold for depolarization
	  * Spontaneous firing
	* Positive Chvostek's and Trousseau's signs
	  * Clinical indicators of hypocalcemic tetany
* **Respiratory Effects:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Bronchoconstriction (potentially problematic in asthma)
	* Direct effect on bronchial smooth muscle
	* May worsen reactive airway disease
  * Left shift of the oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve → impaired oxygen unloading to tissues
	* Alkalosis increases hemoglobin-oxygen affinity
	* Decreased P50
	* Tissue hypoxia despite normal PaO2
  * Increased ventilatory work and potential respiratory muscle fatigue
	* Particularly in prolonged hyperventilation
	* May lead to respiratory failure
* **Metabolic Effects:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Electrolyte Disturbances:**
	* Hypokalemia (due to transcellular shift)
	  * H+ exits cells, K+ enters
	  * ~0.4 mEq/L decrease in K+ for each 0.1 unit increase in pH
	* Hypocalcemia (decreased ionized fraction)
	  * Increased protein binding in alkalosis
	  * Total calcium unchanged
	* Hypophosphatemia
	  * Increased cellular uptake
	  * Increased renal excretion
  * **Other Metabolic Effects:**
	* Increased anaerobic metabolism with severe cerebral vasoconstriction
	  * Paradoxical lactic acidosis
	* Increased lactate production
	  * Enhanced glycolysis
	  * Catecholamine effect
	* Inhibition of glycolysis
	  * pH-sensitive enzymes (phosphofructokinase)
	* Increased hepatic glucose production
	  * Catecholamine effect
	  * May worsen hyperglycemia in diabetics
* **Respiratory Muscle Effects:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Respiratory muscle fatigue with prolonged hyperventilation
	* Increased work of breathing
	* May eventually lead to respiratory failure
  * Potential for post-hyperventilation apnea
	* After voluntary hyperventilation
	* Transient hypoxemia during recovery

⚠️ **Safety Warning:** Severe respiratory alkalosis may cause coronary artery spasm, cardiac arrhythmias, seizures, and tetany. In patients with coronary artery disease, the decreased coronary blood flow can precipitate myocardial ischemia. In patients with cerebrovascular disease, the cerebral vasoconstriction may cause ischemic symptoms.

**Bottom Line:** Respiratory alkalosis affects multiple organ systems with notable effects including cerebral vasoconstriction, decreased tissue oxygen delivery, neuromuscular irritability, and electrolyte abnormalities. These physiological consequences can cause significant symptoms and influence clinical management decisions. The cerebral vasoconstriction and impaired tissue oxygen delivery are particularly important in patients with underlying coronary or cerebrovascular disease.

## 6.6. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS

**While usually pathological, respiratory alkalosis has specific therapeutic applications in clinical practice.**

* **Neurosurgical Applications:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] `[B:S3:4]` [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * **Intentional Hypocapnia:**
	* Reduces cerebral blood flow and cerebral blood volume
	  * Linear relationship: ~2% reduction in CBF per 1 mmHg decrease in PaCO2
	  * Effective between PaCO2 of 25-60 mmHg
	* Reduces intracranial pressure (ICP)
	  * Potentially life-saving in severe intracranial hypertension
	  * Bridges to definitive treatment
	* Facilitates surgical exposure
	  * "Slack brain" for neurosurgical procedures
	  * Reduces bleeding
  * **Clinical Contexts:**
	* Acute intracranial hypertension
	  * TBI with elevated ICP
	  * Large ischemic stroke with edema
	  * Intracerebral hemorrhage
	* During neurosurgical procedures
	  * Aneurysm clipping
	  * Tumor resection
	* Temporary measure for cerebral herniation
	  * Emergency bridge to definitive treatment
	  * Often combined with osmotic therapy
  * **Limitations:**
	* Temporary effect (CSF bicarbonate levels normalize with time)
	  * Effect diminishes after 24-36 hours
	  * Adaptation occurs via CSF bicarbonate reduction
	* Reduces cerebral perfusion (potentially harmful)
	  * May worsen ischemia in vulnerable regions
	  * Balance between ICP reduction and perfusion maintenance
	* Less commonly used with modern management options
	  * Osmotic agents (mannitol, hypertonic saline)
	  * Sedation, therapeutic hypothermia, craniectomy
* **Management of Specific Conditions:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * **Acute Mountain Sickness:**
	* Physiological response to high altitude
	  * Compensatory hyperventilation to hypoxia
	  * Central chemoreceptor adaptation
	* Respiratory alkalosis compensates for hypoxemia
	  * Increases alveolar PO2
	  * Counteracts hypoxic effects
	* Acetazolamide therapy works partly by inducing metabolic acidosis, which stimulates ventilation
	  * Accelerates acclimatization
	  * Prevents symptoms of altitude illness
  * **Pulmonary Hypertension:**
	* Mild respiratory alkalosis may reduce pulmonary vascular resistance
	  * Counteracts hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction
	  * Reduces right ventricular afterload
	* Used in specific clinical scenarios (e.g., high-altitude pulmonary edema)
	  * Often combined with supplemental oxygen
	  * Temporary measure during descent/evacuation
  * **Neonatal Persistent Pulmonary Hypertension:**
	* Hyperventilation historically used to reduce pulmonary vascular resistance
	  * Induced respiratory alkalosis
	  * Combined with supplemental oxygen
	* Now largely replaced by inhaled nitric oxide and other therapies
	  * Selective pulmonary vasodilation
	  * Avoids systemic effects of alkalosis
  * **Salicylate Toxicity:**
	* Deliberate hyperventilation may enhance salicylate excretion
	  * Increases proportion of ionized form
	  * Reduces CNS penetration
	* Urinary alkalinization is more commonly used
	  * Traps salicylate in tubular lumen
	  * Prevents reabsorption
* **Recognition and Management of Iatrogenic Causes:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * **Mechanical Ventilation:**
	* Adjustment of ventilator parameters (tidal volume, respiratory rate)
	  * Reduce minute ventilation to normalize PCO2
	  * Target appropriate PCO2 for clinical context
	* Selection of appropriate PCO2 targets based on clinical context
	  * Neurological patients: may target normocapnia
	  * ARDS: may permit hypercapnia (lung-protective)
	  * Post-cardiac arrest: avoid hypocapnia
  * **Anesthesia-Related:**
	* Recognition of light anesthesia causing hyperventilation
	  * Response to surgical stimulation
	  * Sign of inadequate anesthetic depth
	* Appropriate depth of anesthesia
	  * Adequate analgesia
	  * Appropriate hypnotic component
	* Balanced analgesia to prevent pain-induced hyperventilation
	  * Multimodal approach
	  * Regional techniques when appropriate
* **Therapeutic Approach to Pathological Respiratory Alkalosis:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Treat underlying cause (anxiety, sepsis, pain, hypoxemia)
  * For anxiety-induced hyperventilation:
	* Rebreathing techniques (controversial)
	* Breathing control exercises
	* Anxiolytics when appropriate
  * For iatrogenic causes:
	* Adjust ventilator settings
	* Increase sedation if patient fighting ventilator
  * Monitor electrolytes and replace as needed
  * Avoid abrupt correction in chronic states

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** While hyperventilation can rapidly reduce elevated ICP, the effect is temporary (24-48 hours) as CSF bicarbonate levels equilibrate. Modern neurocritical care relies more on osmotic agents, surgical decompression, and targeted temperature management for sustained ICP control. When hyperventilation is needed, target PaCO2 25-30 mmHg for brief periods only.

⚠️ **Safety Warning:** Deliberate hyperventilation for ICP control should typically aim for PaCO2 of 30-35 mmHg. More aggressive hyperventilation (PaCO2 <30 mmHg) may cause cerebral ischemia due to excessive vasoconstriction, particularly in traumatic brain injury. Current guidelines recommend hyperventilation only as a temporizing measure for impending herniation.

**Bottom Line:** While respiratory alkalosis is typically pathological, it has specific therapeutic applications in neurosurgery and management of certain conditions. However, its clinical use has diminished with the development of more targeted therapies with fewer adverse effects. When deliberately induced, careful monitoring for complications and appropriate targets are essential. Modern practice favors less aggressive hyperventilation and shorter durations when used therapeutically.

# 7. MIXED ACID-BASE DISORDERS




## 7.2. RECOGNITION AND APPROACH

**Mixed acid-base disorders involve two or more primary acid-base disturbances occurring simultaneously.**

* **Definition and Concept:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * Simultaneous presence of two or more primary acid-base disorders
  * Not simply a primary disorder with its expected compensation
  * Result of multiple pathophysiological processes affecting acid-base balance
  * Common in critically ill patients with multisystem disease
* **Clues to Mixed Disorders:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **pH normal with abnormal PCO2 and HCO3-:**
	* Suggests mixed respiratory and metabolic disorders working in opposite directions
	* Example: Respiratory acidosis + metabolic alkalosis or respiratory alkalosis + metabolic acidosis
	* Opposing effects on pH cancel each other
  * **Compensation outside expected range:**
	* Inadequate compensation suggests additional primary acidosis
	* Excessive compensation suggests additional primary alkalosis
	* Reference expected compensation formulas (see section 2.4)
  * **Severe derangements in pH (<7.20 or >7.60):**
	* May indicate combined disorders working in same direction
	* Example: Respiratory acidosis + metabolic acidosis or respiratory alkalosis + metabolic alkalosis
	* Additive effects on pH
  * **Clinical context inconsistent with single disorder:**
	* Patients with multiple comorbidities or acute on chronic conditions
	* Discrepancy between clinical presentation and laboratory values
* **Systematic Approach to Recognition:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  1. **Calculate the expected compensation:** Apply formulas for primary disorder
	 * For respiratory acidosis (acute): HCO3increases by 1 mEq/L per 10 mmHg rise in PCO2
	 * For respiratory acidosis (chronic): HCO3increases by 4 mEq/L per 10 mmHg rise in PCO2
	 * For respiratory alkalosis (acute): HCO3decreases by 2 mEq/L per 10 mmHg fall in PCO2
	 * For respiratory alkalosis (chronic): HCO3decreases by 5 mEq/L per 10 mmHg fall in PCO2
	 * For metabolic acidosis: PCO2 = 1.5 × HCO3- + 8 (±2) (Winter's formula)
	 * For metabolic alkalosis: PCO2 = 0.7 × HCO3- + 21 (±2)
  2. **Compare observed with expected values:** Significant deviation suggests mixed disorder
	 * More than expected compensation suggests additional disorder in same direction as primary
	 * Less than expected compensation suggests additional disorder in opposite direction
  3. **Analyze anion gap:** Elevated AG with normal or increased chloride suggests combined HAGMA and metabolic alkalosis
	 * Calculate AG = [Na+] - ([Cl-] + [HCO3-])
	 * Correct for albumin if hypoalbuminemic
  4. **Calculate delta ratio:** Values <0.8 or >1.2 suggest mixed metabolic disorders
	 * Delta ratio = (Measured AG - Normal AG) / (Normal HCO3- - Measured HCO3-)
  5. **Consider clinical context:** Patient history, medications, and organ function often suggest potential mixed disorders
	 * Vomiting in DKA (HAGMA + metabolic alkalosis)
	 * COPD patient on diuretics (respiratory acidosis + metabolic alkalosis)
* **Common Combinations and Recognition Patterns:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Respiratory Acidosis + Metabolic Alkalosis:**
	* HCO3higher than expected for degree of hypercapnia
	* Example: COPD patient on diuretics
	* pH may be normal or slightly acidotic/alkalotic
	* Clue: Elevated HCO3out of proportion to PCO2
  * **Respiratory Alkalosis + Metabolic Acidosis:**
	* HCO3lower than expected for degree of hypocapnia
	* Example: Sepsis with both hypocapnia (early sepsis) and lactic acidosis
	* pH may be normal or slightly acidotic/alkalotic
	* Clue: Decreased HCO3out of proportion to PCO2
  * **Metabolic Acidosis + Metabolic Alkalosis:**
	* Normal or near-normal pH with abnormal HCO3and elevated anion gap
	* Urine chloride helps differentiate (low in vomiting, high in diuretic use)
	* Example: Vomiting in diabetic ketoacidosis
	* Clue: Elevated anion gap with normal or near-normal HCO3-
  * **Respiratory Acidosis + Metabolic Acidosis:**
	* Severe acidemia (pH typically <7.20)
	* Example: Cardiopulmonary arrest with both respiratory failure and lactic acidosis
	* Clue: Acidemia more severe than expected for either disorder alone
  * **Respiratory Alkalosis + Metabolic Alkalosis:**
	* Severe alkalemia (pH typically >7.60)
	* Example: Excessive mechanical ventilation in patient with nasogastric suction
	* Clue: Alkalemia more severe than expected for either disorder alone
* **Triple Acid-Base Disorders:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Rare but possible
  * Require careful analysis and often specialized testing
  * Example: COPD patient (respiratory acidosis) who develops sepsis (respiratory alkalosis + metabolic acidosis)
  * Clinical context crucial for recognition

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** In critically ill patients, mixed acid-base disorders are common rather than exceptional. Always consider the possibility of a mixed disorder, especially when the clinical context involves multiple organ systems or when pH derangement is severe. A normal pH in a critically ill patient should raise suspicion of a mixed disorder, not reassurance.

**Bottom Line:** Recognition of mixed acid-base disorders requires systematic analysis comparing observed with expected compensation, calculation of anion gap and delta ratio, and integration with clinical context. Multiple patterns exist, with the most common being respiratory alkalosis with metabolic acidosis in sepsis and respiratory acidosis with metabolic alkalosis in COPD patients on diuretics. A stepwise approach to ABG interpretation helps identify these complex disorders.

## 7.3. COMMON MIXED DISORDERS

**Certain clinical scenarios commonly produce specific patterns of mixed acid-base disorders.**

* **Respiratory Acidosis + Metabolic Alkalosis:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Clinical Scenarios:**
	* COPD with diuretic therapy
	  * Chronic hypercapnia from obstructive disease
	  * Diuretic-induced contraction alkalosis
	* COPD with vomiting or nasogastric suction
	  * Chronic CO2 retention
	  * Loss of gastric acid (HCl)
	* Intentional permissive hypercapnia in mechanically ventilated patients with metabolic alkalosis
	  * Strategy to avoid severe alkalemia
	  * Common in critical care
  * **Diagnostic Clues:**
	* Higher HCO3than expected for PCO2 level
	  * Example: PCO2 60 mmHg with HCO3- >38 mEq/L
	* Near-normal pH despite significant hypercapnia
	* Hypokalemia and hypochloremia typical
	* History of diuretic use, vomiting, or NG suction
  * **Clinical Significance:**
	* Challenging ventilator weaning due to blunted respiratory drive
	  * High HCO3buffers CO2 stimulus to breathe
	  * May require gradual reduction in ventilatory support
	* May mask severity of respiratory failure due to near-normal pH
	  * Creates false reassurance
	  * pH-driven therapy may be inadequate
* **Respiratory Alkalosis + Metabolic Acidosis:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Clinical Scenarios:**
	* Sepsis (respiratory alkalosis from fever/cytokines + lactic acidosis)
	  * Early hyperventilation from inflammatory mediators
	  * Later development of lactic acidosis from tissue hypoperfusion
	* Salicylate overdose (direct stimulation of respiratory center + metabolic acidosis)
	  * Early phase: direct respiratory stimulation
	  * Later phase: accumulation of salicylic acid and ketoacids
	* Liver failure (hyperventilation + lactic acidosis)
	  * Central stimulation from altered neurotransmitters
	  * Impaired lactate clearance
	* Early diabetic ketoacidosis with respiratory compensation
	  * Kussmaul breathing as compensation
	  * Additional direct respiratory stimulation
	* Pulmonary edema with hypoxemic respiratory drive and lactic acidosis
	  * Hypoxemia triggers hyperventilation
	  * Cardiogenic shock causes lactic acidosis
  * **Diagnostic Clues:**
	* Lower HCO3than expected for PCO2 level
	  * Example: PCO2 25 mmHg with HCO3- <16 mEq/L
	* May present with normal or near-normal pH
	* Anion gap typically elevated
	* Clinical picture with features of both disorders
  * **Clinical Significance:**
	* Normal pH may mask severity of underlying disorders
	  * Creates false reassurance
	  * Each disorder requires specific treatment
	* Respiratory alkalosis may partly compensate for severe metabolic acidosis
	  * Beneficial effect
	  * May worsen with sedation or mechanical ventilation
* **Metabolic Acidosis + Metabolic Alkalosis:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Clinical Scenarios:**
	* Vomiting in diabetic ketoacidosis
	  * Ketoacidosis (HAGMA)
	  * Loss of gastric acid (metabolic alkalosis)
	* Diuretic use with renal failure
	  * Diuretic-induced metabolic alkalosis
	  * Renal failure causing acid retention
	* Lactic acidosis with volume contraction
	  * Shock causing lactic acidosis
	  * Volume depletion causing contraction alkalosis
	* Post-hypercapnic alkalosis with new-onset lactic acidosis
	  * Rapid correction of chronic respiratory acidosis
	  * Tissue hypoperfusion from critical illness
  * **Diagnostic Clues:**
	* High anion gap with high, normal, or slightly low HCO3-
	* Delta ratio typically <0.8 or >1.2
	* Urine chloride helps distinguish cause of metabolic alkalosis
	  * Low (<20 mmol/L) in vomiting
	  * High (>20 mmol/L) in diuretic use
	* History often reveals both processes
  * **Clinical Significance:**
	* May present with normal pH despite significant acid-base derangements
	  * Delayed recognition
	  * Each disorder requires specific treatment
	* Treatment complexity due to opposing disorders
	  * Treating one may unmask or worsen the other
	  * Careful monitoring required
* **Respiratory Acidosis + Metabolic Acidosis:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Clinical Scenarios:**
	* Cardiopulmonary arrest
	  * Respiratory failure
	  * Tissue hypoperfusion
	* Severe pneumonia with sepsis
	  * Ventilation-perfusion mismatch
	  * Septic shock with lactic acidosis
	* Advanced COPD with renal failure
	  * Chronic CO2 retention
	  * Impaired acid excretion
	* Combined respiratory failure and shock
	  * Ventilatory failure
	  * Circulatory failure
	* Opioid overdose with aspiration and hypoperfusion
	  * Central respiratory depression
	  * Aspiration pneumonitis and shock
  * **Diagnostic Clues:**
	* Severe acidemia (pH typically <7.20)
	* HCO3lower than expected for degree of hypercapnia
	* Often elevated anion gap
	* Clinical features of both respiratory failure and shock
  * **Clinical Significance:**
	* High mortality due to profound acidemia
	  * Cumulative negative effects on cardiovascular function
	  * Resistance to catecholamines
	* Cardiorespiratory collapse if not promptly addressed
	  * Decreased myocardial contractility
	  * Vasodilation
	  * Arrhythmias
	* May require multifaceted intervention (mechanical ventilation, volume resuscitation, possibly dialysis)
	  * Multiple simultaneous interventions needed
	  * Prioritize based on clinical context
* **Respiratory Alkalosis + Metabolic Alkalosis:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Clinical Scenarios:**
	* Mechanical hyperventilation in patient with diuretic use
	  * Iatrogenic hyperventilation
	  * Diuretic-induced alkalosis
	* Hyperventilation (anxiety, pain) in patient with vomiting
	  * Psychological hyperventilation
	  * Loss of gastric acid
	* Liver disease with diuretic therapy
	  * Hyperventilation from hepatic encephalopathy
	  * Diuretic treatment for ascites
  * **Diagnostic Clues:**
	* Severe alkalemia (pH typically >7.60)
	* HCO3higher than expected for degree of hypocapnia
	* Often associated with profound hypokalemia
	* Clinical history reveals both processes
  * **Clinical Significance:**
	* Increased risk of arrhythmias, seizures, and tetany
	  * Cardiac effects of alkalemia and hypokalemia
	  * Neuromuscular effects of decreased ionized calcium
	* Decreased tissue oxygen delivery
	  * Left-shift of oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve
	  * Cerebral vasoconstriction
	* Requires careful approach to avoid worsening either component
	  * Correction of one may unmask or worsen the other
	  * Gradual correction preferred
* **Triple Acid-Base Disorders:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Clinical Scenarios:**
	* Chronic respiratory acidosis + acute respiratory alkalosis + metabolic acidosis
	  * Example: COPD patient with pneumonia and sepsis
	  * Baseline hypercapnia with superimposed sepsis-induced hyperventilation and lactic acidosis
	* Respiratory alkalosis + metabolic alkalosis + metabolic acidosis
	  * Example: Vomiting with salicylate overdose
	  * Vomiting (metabolic alkalosis), salicylate-induced hyperventilation, and salicylate-induced metabolic acidosis
  * **Diagnostic Clues:**
	* Complex pattern requiring structured analysis
	* History and clinical context crucial
	* Often requires arterial and venous blood gases, electrolytes, and urinary studies
	* May require serial measurements to identify components
  * **Clinical Significance:**
	* Challenging to diagnose and manage
	* Different components may emerge as treatment progresses
	* Requires careful monitoring and sequential correction

⚠️ **Safety Warning:** Mixed disorders may mask each other's presence, leading to underappreciation of disease severity. A normal pH with abnormal PCO2 and HCO3should always trigger evaluation for mixed disorders. Treating one component of a mixed disorder may unmask the other, potentially leading to dangerous swings in pH.

**Bottom Line:** Common patterns of mixed acid-base disorders occur in specific clinical scenarios and require careful analysis beyond basic ABG interpretation. Recognition of these patterns helps guide diagnostic evaluation and treatment planning. The most severe combinations (respiratory and metabolic acidosis, or respiratory and metabolic alkalosis) produce extreme pH derangements with significant clinical consequences. Understanding these patterns allows for anticipatory management and more effective treatment.

## 7.4. DIAGNOSTIC CHALLENGES

**Mixed acid-base disorders present unique diagnostic challenges requiring a structured approach and comprehensive evaluation.**

* **Analytical Challenges:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Compensatory vs. Mixed Disorders:**
	* Difficult to distinguish exceptional compensation from additional primary disorder
	* Requires precise application of compensation formulas
	* Confounded by individual variation in compensatory responses
  * **Chronic vs. Acute Respiratory Disorders:**
	* Different expected compensations based on chronicity
	* Patient history crucial for interpretation
	* Limited information about baseline status
  * **Multiple Metabolic Disorders:**
	* May be masked when HAGMA and metabolic alkalosis coexist
	* Requires careful delta ratio analysis
	* Normal anion gap may hide underlying processes
* **Diagnostic Approach to Complex Cases:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  1. **Blood Gas Analysis:**
	 * Arterial blood gas for primary analysis
	 * Consider venous blood gas for trend monitoring
	 * Evaluate pH, PaCO2, HCO3-, and BE/BD
	 * Compare with previous results when available
  
  2. **Electrolyte Panel:**
	 * Sodium, potassium, chloride, bicarbonate
	 * Calculate anion gap: [Na+] - ([Cl-] + [HCO3-])
	 * Correct anion gap for albumin if hypoalbuminemic
	 * Assess for patterns (hyperchloremia, hypochloremia)
  
  3. **Additional Laboratory Tests:**
	 * Lactate levels
	 * Ketones (blood and urine)
	 * Renal function (BUN, creatinine)
	 * Urinary electrolytes (particularly chloride)
	 * Toxicology screen when indicated
	 * Plasma osmolality (measured and calculated)
  
  4. **Structured Analysis Framework:**
	 * Start with pH - acidemia or alkalemia?
	 * Identify the primary disorder most likely responsible for the pH change
	 * Calculate expected compensation
	 * Evaluate for additional disorders if compensation inappropriate
	 * Calculate and analyze delta ratio if metabolic acidosis present
	 * Consider clinical context and potential etiologies
* **Pitfalls and Common Errors:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Normal pH Misconception:**
	* Normal pH does not exclude significant acid-base disorders
	* May represent multiple offsetting disorders
	* Abnormal PCO2 and HCO3with normal pH strongly suggests mixed disorder
  * **Compensation Misinterpretation:**
	* Mistaking a second primary disorder for compensation
	* Failing to recognize inadequate or excessive compensation
	* Applying wrong compensation formulas based on acuity
  * **Laboratory Errors:**
	* Pre-analytical: Improper sample handling, air bubbles, delayed analysis
	* Analytical: Analyzer calibration issues, interference
	* Post-analytical: Transcription errors, incorrect units
	* Failure to repeat unusual or inconsistent results
  * **Clinical Context Neglect:**
	* Failing to integrate patient history, medications, and clinical status
	* Isolated interpretation of laboratory values without clinical correlation
	* Not considering temporal relationship between disorders
* **Advanced Diagnostic Tools:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]]
  * **Urinary Anion Gap:** [Na+] + [K+] - [Cl-]
	* Helps distinguish renal from non-renal causes in NAGMA
	* Negative in diarrhea (renal response appropriate)
	* Positive in RTA (renal response inappropriate)
  * **Osmolar Gap:** Measured - calculated osmolality
	* Elevated in toxic alcohol ingestions
	* Normal <10 mOsm/L
	* Calculated: 2[Na+] + [glucose mmol/L] + [urea mmol/L]
  * **Stewart Approach (Strong Ion Difference):**
	* Alternative analysis framework based on physicochemical principles
	* Useful in complex cases, especially with hypoalbuminemia
	* Considers [Na+], [Cl-], albumin, phosphate, and lactate
	* Helps identify mixed disorders missed by traditional approach
  * **Arterial-Venous PCO2 Difference:**
	* Can help assess tissue perfusion and metabolism
	* Widened in shock states
	* Normal: 4-6 mmHg
* **Serial Measurements:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Track changes over time
  * Observe response to therapeutic interventions
  * May reveal emerging components of mixed disorders
  * Particularly valuable in critically ill patients
* **Specialized Consultation:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Nephrology input for complex renal tubular disorders
  * Toxicology for suspected poisonings
  * Critical care for multisystem involvement
  * Endocrinology for complex metabolic disorders

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** In complex acid-base disorders, consider serial measurements to track changes over time. The temporal evolution of acid-base parameters often provides valuable diagnostic clues and helps assess response to therapy. The pattern of change can reveal components of mixed disorders that were initially masked.

**Bottom Line:** Diagnosing mixed acid-base disorders requires a systematic approach integrating blood gas analysis, electrolyte panels, additional laboratory tests, and clinical context. Common challenges include distinguishing compensation from mixed disorders and recognizing multiple metabolic disturbances. A structured analytical framework and awareness of potential pitfalls improve diagnostic accuracy. Advanced tools like urinary anion gap, osmolar gap, and the Stewart approach can help resolve complex cases.

## 8.2. TEMPERATURE EFFECTS ON PH

**Blood pH changes with temperature due to altered equilibrium constants and gas solubility.**

* **Basic Principle:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Water dissociation is temperature-dependent: H2O ⇌ H+ + OH-
  * Lower temperatures shift equilibrium left, reducing free H+ concentration
  * Higher temperatures shift equilibrium right, increasing free H+ concentration
  * Affects interpretation of blood gases in hypothermic or hyperthermic patients
* **Quantitative Relationship:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Blood pH increases by approximately 0.015 units for every 1°C decrease in temperature
  * Blood pH decreases by approximately 0.015 units for every 1°C increase in temperature
  * Example: pH measured as 7.40 at 37°C would be approximately 7.55 at 27°C in vivo
* **Effects on Blood Gas Measurements:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Blood gas analyzers operate at 37°C
  * For hypothermic patients, measured values differ from actual in vivo values:
	* Measured pH appears lower than actual in vivo pH
	* Measured PCO2 appears higher than actual in vivo PCO2
	* Measured PO2 appears higher than actual in vivo PO2
  * Mathematical correction can be applied to determine actual values at patient's temperature
  * Example: Patient temperature 27°C with measured pH 7.40, PCO2 40 mmHg
	* Corrected pH: 7.40 + (0.015 × 10) = 7.55
	* Corrected PCO2: 40 × 0.83 = 33.2 mmHg
* **Alpha-stat vs. pH-stat Management:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **pH-stat Method:**
	* Maintains temperature-corrected pH at normal values
	* Adds CO2 to maintain normal pH when corrected to patient's temperature
	* Potential benefits:
	  * Counteracts leftward shift of oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve
	  * May improve oxygen delivery during hypothermia
	* Potential risks:
	  * Causes cerebral vasodilation
	  * Increases cerebral blood flow
	  * May increase embolic load and neurological complications
	  * Abolishes cerebral autoregulation
  * **Alpha-stat Method:**
	* Maintains 37°C-measured values in normal range
	* Permits alkaline drift with hypothermia
	* Targets normal pH at 37°C (not corrected for temperature)
	* Accepts that actual in vivo pH will be alkalotic
	* Potential benefits:
	  * Maintains cellular electrochemical neutrality
	  * Preserves enzyme function
	  * Maintains cerebral autoregulation
	* Current practice in most centers
* **Clinical Relevance:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Hypothermic Cardiopulmonary Bypass:**
	* Choice between alpha-stat and pH-stat affects cerebral blood flow
	* May impact neurological outcomes
	* Remains controversial which approach is superior
  * **Post-cardiac Arrest Therapeutic Hypothermia:**
	* Typically use alpha-stat approach
	* Avoids excessive CO2 administration
  * **Severe Accidental Hypothermia:**
	* Focus on rewarming rather than pH correction
	* Monitor trends rather than absolute values
  * **Malignant Hyperthermia:**
	* Hypermetabolic state with elevated temperature
	* Measured pH appears higher than in vivo pH
	* Focus on treating underlying disorder
* **Practical Considerations:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Most clinical decisions based on uncorrected (37°C) values
  * Use correction only in moderate to severe hypothermia (<32°C)
  * Consider trends more important than absolute values in temperature extremes
  * Document temperature when reporting blood gas values
  * Specify whether values are corrected or uncorrected

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** During deep hypothermic circulatory arrest procedures, the choice between alpha-stat and pH-stat management may vary by surgical phase. Some centers use pH-stat during cooling (to maximize cerebral blood flow and cooling) and alpha-stat during rewarming (to maintain cerebral autoregulation).

**Bottom Line:** Blood pH increases as temperature decreases due to altered dissociation constants. This has significant implications for blood gas interpretation in hypothermic patients and for management during cardiopulmonary bypass. The choice between pH-stat and alpha-stat approaches remains controversial, with each offering potential advantages in different clinical scenarios. Most centers currently favor the alpha-stat approach for routine cardiac surgery.

## 8.3. THE STEWART APPROACH TO ACID-BASE PHYSIOLOGY

**The Stewart approach provides an alternative physicochemical framework for understanding and analyzing acid-base disorders.**

* **Conceptual Framework:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Developed by Canadian physiologist Peter Stewart in the 1980s
  * Based on physicochemical principles rather than descriptive approach
  * Focuses on independent variables that determine pH
  * More mathematically rigorous than traditional approach
  * Recently simplified for clinical use (Stewart-Fencl-Story approach)
* **Independent Variables:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Three primary factors determine acid-base status:
	* **Strong Ion Difference (SID):**
	  * Difference between strong cations and strong anions
	  * SID = [Na+] + [K+] + [Ca2+] + [Mg2+] - [Cl-] - [Lactate-]
	  * Normal: 40-44 mEq/L
	  * Decreased SID → acidosis
	  * Increased SID → alkalosis
	* **Partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PCO2):**
	  * Independent variable regulated by respiratory system
	  * Increased PCO2 → acidosis
	  * Decreased PCO2 → alkalosis
	* **Total weak acids (ATOT):**
	  * Primarily albumin and phosphate
	  * Decreased ATOT (e.g., hypoalbuminemia) → alkalosis
	  * Increased ATOT (e.g., hyperphosphatemia) → acidosis
* **Dependent Variables:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * Hydrogen ion concentration [H+]
  * Bicarbonate concentration [HCO3-]
  * These are determined by the independent variables
  * Not primary controllers of acid-base status
* **Simplified Stewart-Fencl-Story Approach:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Acid Base Physiology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]]
  * **Base Deficit/Excess for Sodium-Chloride Effect:**
	* BDENaCl = [Na+] - [Cl-] - 38
	* Quantifies metabolic acidosis/alkalosis from sodium-chloride imbalance
	* BDENaCl > 0: alkalosis
	* BDENaCl < 0: acidosis
  * **Base Deficit/Excess for Albumin Effect:**
	* BDEAlb = 0.25 × (42 - [albumin in g/L])
	* Quantifies metabolic alkalosis from hypoalbuminemia
	* Always positive in hypoalbuminemia (alkalinizing effect)
  * **Clinical Example:**
	* Patient with sepsis: Na+ 147 mmol/L, Cl115 mmol/L, albumin 12 g/L
	* BDENaCl = 147 - 115 - 38 = -6 mEq/L (acidosis)
	* BDEAlb = 0.25 × (42 - 12) = 7.5 mEq/L (alkalosis)
	* Net effect: Mixed disorder with near-normal pH
* **Clinical Applications:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * **Hyperchloremic Metabolic Acidosis:**
	* Traditional: Normal anion gap metabolic acidosis
	* Stewart: Decreased SID due to increased [Cl-] relative to [Na+]
  * **Dilutional Acidosis:**
	* Traditional: Difficult to explain
	* Stewart: Decreased SID from proportionally greater dilution of Na+ than Cl-
  * **Hypoalbuminemic Alkalosis:**
	* Traditional: Often overlooked
	* Stewart: Decreased ATOT causing alkalinizing effect
  * **Complex Critical Care Cases:**
	* Traditional: Difficult to unravel mixed disorders
	* Stewart: Can identify components based on SID, ATOT, and PCO2
* **Advantages of Stewart Approach:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Better explains complex acid-base disturbances in critical care
  * Accounts for effect of hypoalbuminemia
  * Explains dilutional acidosis from normal saline
  * Identifies mixed disorders that may be missed by traditional approach
  * Provides quantitative assessment of each component
* **Limitations of Stewart Approach:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Mathematically complex in its original form
  * Requires more laboratory measurements
  * Limited evidence for improved clinical outcomes
  * Not widely taught in medical education
  * Simplified versions lose some physicochemical rigor
* **Reconciliation with Traditional Approach:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S1 Critical Care Medicine|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Both approaches valid but with different perspectives
  * Traditional: Descriptive, clinically intuitive
  * Stewart: Mechanistic, physicochemical
  * Similar therapeutic implications in most cases
  * Stewart may provide additional insights in complex cases

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The Stewart approach is particularly valuable in critically ill patients with hypoalbuminemia, where traditional approaches may miss the alkalinizing effect of low albumin. Correcting the anion gap for albumin (AG + 2.5 × [(40-albumin g/L)/10]) or applying the Stewart equations can reveal hidden acidosis masked by hypoalbuminemia.

**Bottom Line:** The Stewart approach provides an alternative physicochemical framework for acid-base analysis based on strong ion difference (SID), PCO2, and total weak acids (ATOT). It offers advantages in explaining complex disorders in critical care, particularly in patients with hypoalbuminemia or after large volume resuscitation. Simplified versions like the Stewart-Fencl-Story approach make the concepts more accessible for clinical use while retaining the key insights of the original theory.
