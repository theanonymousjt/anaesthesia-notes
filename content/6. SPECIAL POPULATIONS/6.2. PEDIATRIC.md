# 1. PEDIATRIC PHYSIOLOGY AND DEVELOPMENT


## 1.1. AGE CLASSIFICATIONS AND DEFINITIONS


**Exam Weighting:** Medium

The pediatric population is divided into specific age categories:

* **Neonate:** Birth to 1 month of age `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1. PHYSIOLOGY (~32% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]`
* **Infant:** 1 month to 1 year of age `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1. PHYSIOLOGY (~32% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]`
* **Child:** 1 year to 16 years of age `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1. PHYSIOLOGY (~32% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]`
   * **Adolescent:** 13 years to 16 years of age `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1. PHYSIOLOGY (~32% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]`
* **Preterm**: Born before 37 weeks

### 1.1.2. ENHANCED TERMINOLOGY FOR PREMATURE NEONATES [INTEGRATION]

**AAP Definitions for Premature Neonates:** `[PREM:2]`

| Term | Definition | Example (24w GA, 16w old) |
|------|------------|---------------------------|
| **Gestational Age (GA)** | Time D1 of LMP to birth<br>Defines prematurity (<37wks GA) | 24 weeks at birth |
| **Chronological Age** | Weeks since birth | 16 weeks old |
| **Postmenstrual Age (PMA)** | GA at birth + chronological age | 24 + 16 = 40 weeks |
| **Corrected Age (CA)** | Chronological age – # weeks born before 40 weeks GA<br>• Only for premature infants up to 3 years corrected age<br>• Chronological age − (40 − GA at birth) | 16 − (40 − 24) = 0 weeks |

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Understanding these age definitions is crucial for determining anesthetic risk, particularly for postoperative apnea in ex-premature infants.

**Bottom Line:** Age classification is essential for accurate dosing of medications, equipment selection, and physiological understanding throughout pediatric anesthetic care.

## 1.2. CARDIOVASCULAR SYSTEM


**Exam Weighting:** High

### 1.2.2. FETAL CIRCULATION

* **Parallel circulatory system** characterized by both ventricles pumping most output into systemic circulation `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]` 
* **Key features:**
   * Less than 10% of combined cardiac output goes through fetal lungs due to high pulmonary vascular resistance `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]` 
   * Placenta provides oxygenated blood into the ductus venosus, inferior vena cava, and right atrium `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]` 
   * **Three important shunts:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]`
	  * **Ductus venosus:** Allows blood to bypass the liver
	  * **Foramen ovale:** Allows blood to flow from right atrium to left atrium
	  * **Ductus arteriosus:** Connects pulmonary artery to descending aorta

![Schematic representation of fetal circulation](PEDIATRIC_PEDIATRIC_image.png_28a451.png_c4b9e0.png)

* **Physiological details:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]` 
   * In the right atrium, most oxygenated blood primarily flows through the foramen ovale into the left atrium, bypassing the right ventricle and pulmonary circulation `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]` 
   * Preferential flow across the foramen occurs because of lower pressure in the left atrium compared to the right atrium `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]` 
   * Pulmonary vascular resistance is high in utero due to alveolar collapse, compression of blood vessels, low PaO₂, and low pH `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]` 

### 1.2.3. CHANGES AT BIRTH

* **Dramatic circulatory changes occur at birth:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]`
   * Elimination of placental shunt
   * Closure of ductus venosus
   * Expansion of lungs initiates decreased pulmonary vascular resistance and increased pulmonary blood flow `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]`
   * **Foramen ovale** functionally closes within first hour as left atrial pressure exceeds right atrial pressure `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]` 
	  * Anatomic closure occurs in first year but remains probe-patent in 10-20% of adults `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]` 
   * **Ductus arteriosus** begins closing in first day and usually functionally closed by second day `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]` 
	  * Closure triggered when PaO₂ rises to about 50 mmHg `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]` 
	  * Preterm infants have less responsive ductus to increased oxygen `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]` 
* **Additional transitional details:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]` 
   * Left ventricle now pumps blood exclusively into higher-pressure systemic circulation `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]` 
   * Although initial changes are dramatic, it typically takes 3-4 days for pulmonary vascular resistance to decrease to normal levels `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]` 
   * In utero, ductal patency was maintained by combined relaxant effects of low oxygen tension and endogenously produced prostaglandins, especially prostaglandin E2 `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.1 Cardiovascular System|ⓘ]]`

### 1.2.4. MYOCARDIAL FUNCTION

* **Neonatal myocardium has structural/functional differences:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` 
   * Fewer myofibril elements that are less organized, making them less efficient `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` 
   * Less mature sarcoplasmic reticulum with decreased Ca²⁺-ATPase activity `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` 
   * Limited ability to generate force and relatively noncompliant `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` 
   * Poor tolerance of afterload increases `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` 
   * Rapid myocyte growth post-birth, especially in left ventricle `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` 
* **Cardiac output differences:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]`
   * Neonate: Up to 400 mL/kg/min
   * Infant: Around 200 mL/kg/min
   * Adolescence: 100 mL/kg/min
   * Adult: 70-80 mL/kg/min
* **Autonomic influences:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` 
   * Parasympathetic system more mature than sympathetic in first 3 months `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` 
   * Reduced response to inotropic support compared to older children `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` 
   * Maturational changes in β-adrenergic receptor function `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` 
   * Increased glycogen stores and anaerobic glycolysis rates (provides relative resistance to hypoxia) `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` 
* **Frank-Starling relationship present but less effective:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` 
   * Preload changes can increase stroke volume but not as effectively as in older children `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` 
   * Cardiac output poorly maintained during bradycardia `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` 
* **Immature baroreceptors:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` 
   * Limited ability to compensate for hypotension with increased heart rate `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` 
   * Baroresponse more depressed by anesthesia than in adults `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` 
* **Clinical implications:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` 
   * Resting cardiac output in neonates is close to maximal output, providing limited reserve `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` 
   * Mature heart can increase cardiac output by 300%, while immature heart can only increase by 30-40% `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` 
   * Higher resting cardiac output relative to body weight compared to adults `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.2 Respiratory System|ⓘ]]` 

### 1.2.5. NORMAL CARDIOVASCULAR PARAMETERS

* **Heart rate norms:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * 0-3 months: 100-150 beats/min
   * 3-6 months: 90-120 beats/min
   * 6-12 months: 80-120 beats/min
   * 1-3 years: 70-110 beats/min
   * 3-6 years: 65-110 beats/min
   * 6-12 years: 60-95 beats/min
   * > 12 years: 55-85 beats/min
* **Blood pressure norms:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * 0-3 months: 65-85/45-55 mmHg
   * 3-6 months: 70-90/50-65 mmHg
   * 6-12 months: 80-100/55-65 mmHg
   * 1-3 years: 90-105/55-70 mmHg
   * 3-6 years: 95-110/60-75 mmHg
   * 6-12 years: 100-120/60-75 mmHg
   * > 12 years: 110-135/65-85 mmHg
* **Clinical estimation formulas for children 1-10 years:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * 5th percentile systolic BP: 70 + (2 × age in years) mmHg
   * 50th percentile systolic BP: 90 + (2 × age in years) mmHg
   * 5th percentile is cutoff for hypotensive shock in PALS algorithms
* **Volume status:** Children compensate well for blood loss, often maintaining normal BP until 20% of blood volume is lost `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`

**Bottom Line:** The pediatric cardiovascular system undergoes dramatic changes from fetal life through childhood. Limited cardiac reserve, heart rate dependency for cardiac output, and different regulatory mechanisms require careful anesthetic management, particularly in neonates and infants.

   * **Pierre Robin sequence:**
	 * Micrognathia, airway distress in first 24-48 hours, glossoptosis
	 * Often improves with age
	 * Severe cases may require surgical airway
   * **Treacher Collins syndrome:**
	 * Hypoplasia of zygomatic complex, cheekbones, jaw, palate and mouth
	 * Downward slanting palpebral fissures
	 * Becomes progressively more difficult with age
	 * Difficulties with both mask ventilation and tracheal intubation
   * **Down syndrome:**
	 * Large tongue can make mask ventilation difficult
   * **Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome:**
	 * Large tongue can make mask ventilation difficult
   * **Crouzon syndrome and Apert syndrome:**
	 * Facial anomalies can make mask ventilation difficult

**Bottom Line:** Understanding the numerous anatomical differences in the pediatric airway is essential for safe airway management. The cephalad position of the larynx, large tongue, omega-shaped epiglottis, anterior-slanting vocal cords, and narrowest point at the cricoid ring all contribute to the challenges in pediatric airway management.

# 2. PEDIATRIC ANESTHESIA PHARMACOLOGY


## 2.1. DEVELOPMENTAL PHARMACOLOGY


**Exam Weighting:** High

* **Protein binding:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]`
   * Two major binding proteins synthesized in liver:
	 * **Albumin:** Binds acidic drugs
	   * Reduced at birth
	   * Also reduced in liver disease, cancer, nephropathy, and malnutrition
	 * **α1-acid glycoprotein:** Binds basic drugs (e.g., lidocaine)
	   * Reduced at birth
	   * Increases with age, stress, and inflammation
   * Reduced plasma concentrations of both proteins in neonates/infants leads to:
	 * Increased free fraction of drugs
	 * Greater dose reaching effect site
	 * Enhanced physiologic and potential toxic effects
	 * Example: Free fraction of lidocaine is greater in young infants than older children
* **Hepatic metabolism:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]`
   * Termination of most IV drug action depends on:
	 * Phase 1 reactions (hydroxylation, oxidation) - primarily via cytochrome P450 system
	 * Phase 2 reactions (glucuronidation)
   * Enzyme system maturation varies widely among individuals
   * Genetic polymorphisms affect activity of many enzymes
   * Clinical example: codeine metabolism
	 * "Rapid metabolizers" can convert codeine to morphine too quickly, causing excessive sedation/death
	 * "Slow metabolizers" get little effect
	 * No longer recommended for children, removed from many formularies
	 * FDA black box warning (2017): Codeine and tramadol not recommended for children <12 years
* **Enzyme maturation:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]`
   * Phase 1 reactions (cytochrome P450):
	 * Mature from birth
	 * Reach adult activity by 1-5 years
   * Phase 2 enzymes (conjugation):
	 * Immature at birth (why bilirubin can reach toxic levels in neonates)
	 * Mature quickly with age
* **Elimination pathways:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]`
   * Drug action termination via:
	 * Redistribution from effect site to vessel-rich organs or muscle
	 * Metabolism in liver
	 * Excretion by kidneys
   * Glomerular filtration rate:
	 * Markedly reduced in neonates/young infants
	 * Matures throughout childhood
	 * Reaches adult rates by 5-15 years
* **Physiological factors altering pharmacokinetics:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]` 
   * **Volume of distribution:** Total body water represents greater proportion of body weight in neonates, increasing necessary dose for water-soluble medications `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]` 
   * **Protein binding:** Decreased protein leads to increased free drug levels, heightening activity and potential toxicity of protein-bound drugs `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]` 
   * **Fat/muscle content:** Decreased fat and muscle mass leads to greater levels of drugs primarily redistributed to these tissues `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]` 
   * **Organ function:** Decreased renal and hepatic function predisposes neonates to increased blood levels from standard doses `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]` 

**Bottom Line:** Developmental differences in drug disposition significantly impact pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics in pediatric patients. Reduced protein binding, immature enzyme systems, and developing elimination pathways can lead to unpredictable drug effects, requiring age-appropriate dosing strategies.

## 2.2. INHALATIONAL ANESTHETICS

### 2.2.2. GENERAL CHARACTERISTICS

* **Advantages in pediatrics:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]`
   * Rapid onset and offset of action
   * Limited adverse effects on cardiovascular and respiratory systems when dosed appropriately
   * Caution needed in congenital heart disease and mitochondrial disorders
* **Agent selection considerations:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]`
   * Sevoflurane has replaced halothane as induction agent of choice in North America
   * Desflurane not recommended for induction due to airway irritability
   * ⚠️ Desflurane is a potent greenhouse gas due to atmospheric persistence - environmental concerns suggest avoiding use `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]`
* **Historical perspective:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]` 
   * Halothane still commonly used in developing countries but not in US or other developed countries `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]` 
   * Long history and ease of use still make halothane desirable in some settings `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]` 

### 2.2.3. PHARMACOKINETICS

* **Wash-in determinants:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]`
   * **Delivery to lungs:**
	 * Inspired concentration (FI)
	 * Alveolar ventilation
	 * Functional residual capacity (FRC)
   * **Removal from lungs:**
	 * Cardiac output
	 * Solubility
	 * Alveolar-to-venous anesthetic partial pressure gradient
* **Wash-in ratio (FA/FI):** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]`
   * Increases from 0 toward 1 in exponential curve for all agents
   * Inversely related to blood solubility - lower solubility results in more rapid wash-in
   * Described by equation: FA/FI = 1 - e^(-kt)
	 * k = 1/time constant (τ)
	 * τ = ratio of organ volume to blood flow
	 * Smaller time constant means more rapid equilibration
   * Time constants for most organs in children are less than in adults
* **Factors explaining more rapid wash-in in infants:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]`
   * Greater alveolar ventilation compared to adults
   * Greater distribution of cardiac output to vessel-rich group
   * Reduced tissue solubility of inhaled agents
   * Reduced blood solubility of inhaled agents
* **Physiological considerations:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]`
   * Higher alveolar ventilation-to-FRC ratio speeds equilibration of inspired to alveolar concentrations
   * Greater cardiac output should theoretically slow FA/FI increase, but actually speeds equilibration in neonates/infants due to:
	 * High perfusion of vessel-rich group (VRG)
	 * VRG comprises 18% of body weight in infants vs. only 8% in adults
   * Reduced tissue solubility (brain, muscle, heart) of all inhalational agents in infants
   * Reduced blood solubility speeds wash-in
* **Modern agents:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1. PHYSIOLOGY (~32% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]`
   * Less soluble agents (sevoflurane, desflurane) affected less by changes in ventilation and cardiac output
   * Only marginally more rapid wash-in in infants than adults
* **Safety considerations during induction:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.3 Renal, Cellular, Body Fluids and Electrolytes, Acid Base|ⓘ]]`
   * 💡 Maintaining spontaneous ventilation during inhalational induction is safer
   * As depth increases, ventilation decreases and slows further uptake
   * Controlled ventilation with high vaporizer settings can rapidly increase anesthetic depth with cardiac depressant effects
   * Noted cause of cardiac arrest in Pediatric Perioperative Cardiac Arrest (POCA) registry, especially with halothane
   * Sevoflurane at 8% is ~4× MAC, whereas halothane at 5% is ~7× MAC
   * Right-to-left shunts make maintaining adequate anesthesia depth with sevoflurane difficult - supplemental IV anesthesia often required

### 2.2.4. PHARMACODYNAMICS

* **MAC variation with age:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1. PHYSIOLOGY (~32% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]`
   * MAC varies substantially with age in children
   * Increases as fetus matures and reaches term
   * Peaks in infants 1-6 months of age
   * Decreases steadily thereafter with increasing age
   * 25% less in children with cognitive dysfunction, especially those taking antiseizure drugs

### 2.2.5. SYSTEM-SPECIFIC EFFECTS

* **Respiratory effects:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1. PHYSIOLOGY (~32% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]`
   * Airway responses depend on concentration and specific agent
   * Halothane and sevoflurane infrequently trigger reflex responses
   * Isoflurane and desflurane irritate airways - not recommended for inhalational induction
   * Sevoflurane preferable in children with asthma
   * Inhalational agents effective in treating refractory status asthmaticus
* **Cardiovascular effects:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1. PHYSIOLOGY (~32% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]`
   * Dose-dependent myocardial depression in children and adults
   * Halothane particularly prone to causing brady-dysrhythmias (often given with atropine)
   * Halothane associated with ventricular dysrhythmias, especially with circulating epinephrine
* **Central nervous system effects:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1. PHYSIOLOGY (~32% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]`
   * Autoregulation of cerebral blood flow similar to adults but occurs at greater flow rates
   * Lower limit of autoregulation appears similar (mean arterial pressure ~60 mmHg)
* **Renal effects:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1. PHYSIOLOGY (~32% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]`
   * No substantive effects except through metabolism
   * Kidneys serve as degradation site
   * Nephrotoxicity from inorganic fluoride was concern with older agents (methoxyflurane) but not with modern agents
* **Hepatic effects:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1. PHYSIOLOGY (~32% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]`
   * Limited data specific to children
   * Isolated cases of hepatic dysfunction reported with all inhalational agents
   * Most children recover without treatment
   * Halothane hepatitis associated with antibodies to hepatic cell membrane antigens
   * Sevoflurane, isoflurane, and desflurane generally not associated with hepatic dysfunction

### 2.2.6. IN VITRO METABOLISM

* **Carbon dioxide absorbent interactions:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1. PHYSIOLOGY (~32% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]`
   * Sevoflurane degradation via Cannizzaro reaction in CO₂ absorbents
   * Reaction accelerated by:
	 * Increased temperature
	 * Barium hydroxide
	 * Strong bases (potassium hydroxide)
	 * Very low fresh gas flow
	 * Large sevoflurane concentrations
	 * Desiccated absorbent
   * Releases five compounds, with compound A being most common
   * Nephrotoxic concentrations of compound A are >100 ppm
   * In children, 1 MAC sevoflurane produces 16 ppm after 5.6 MAC hours (2 L/min fresh gas flow)
   * No instances of compound A-induced nephrotoxicity reported in children
* **Specific volatile agents:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
   * **Halothane:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
	 * Weak muscle relaxant properties facilitate induction and intubation without relaxants `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
	 * Potent bronchodilator, may reduce airway reflexes with intubation `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]`

	 * Sensitizes myocardium to cardiac dysrhythmias `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
   
   * **Isoflurane:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
	 * Common maintenance agent, pungent odor prevents use for mask induction `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
	 * Greater potentiation of muscle relaxation - reduce relaxant doses accordingly `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
	 * Less myocardial depression than halothane in neonates `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
   
   * **Sevoflurane:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
	 * Advantages include rapid induction and awakening `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
	 * Less pungent smell than isoflurane, ideal for mask induction `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
	 * Reasonable safety profile in neonates and children `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
	 * Fewer hemodynamic changes in congenital heart disease than isoflurane `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
	 * Less myocardial depression but greater respiratory depression vs. halothane `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
	 * Decreased minute ventilation, respiratory frequency, and thoracoabdominal asynchrony `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
   
   * **Desflurane:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
	 * Low partition coefficient similar to nitrous oxide enabling rapid uptake `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]`

**Bottom Line:** Inhalational anesthetics have unique pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiles in pediatric patients. ### 2.3.3. INDUCTION AGENTS

#### 2.3.3.1. PROPOFOL

* **Characteristics:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1. PHYSIOLOGY (~32% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1. PHYSIOLOGY (~32% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
   * Most commonly used IV induction agent in children
   * Highly lipophilic with rapid distribution to VRG
   * Action terminated by redistribution, followed by hepatic and extrahepatic metabolism
* **Dosing requirements:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1. PHYSIOLOGY (~32% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Children require 50% greater induction and infusion dosing compared to adults
   * ~3 mcg/mL blood concentration required for general anesthesia
   * ED50 and ED90 for LMA insertion: 3.5 and 5.4 mg/kg respectively
   * Dose for tracheal intubation during sevoflurane anesthesia: 1-2 mg/kg
* **Total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA):** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1. PHYSIOLOGY (~32% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]`
   * For painless medical/radiologic procedures:
	 * Initial infusion rate: 15 mg/kg/hr (250 mcg/kg/min) after induction
	 * May need increases to stop spontaneous movement (especially in neurocognitive impairment or younger patients)
	 * Can reduce in older children
   * For prolonged surgery:
	 * Stepwise decrease in infusion rate based on pharmacokinetic modeling
	 * Target ~3 mcg/mL blood concentration
   * Target-controlled infusions (TCIs) used in Europe but not US
* **Clinical considerations:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1. PHYSIOLOGY (~32% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]`
   * Injection pain in ≥70% of patients, worse in small veins
	 * Lessened by simultaneous inhalational agent or IV lidocaine 0.5-1 mg/kg
	 * Mini-Bier block technique useful
   * Profound airway/respiratory effects:
	 * Transient apnea after induction dose, followed by return of spontaneous respiration
	 * Airway obstruction effectively managed with jaw thrust
	 * Relaxes upper pharyngeal muscles, facilitating LMA acceptance
   * Effective antiemetic properties - useful for PONV history and emetogenic surgeries
* **Propofol infusion syndrome (Prise):** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1. PHYSIOLOGY (~32% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
   * First reported in 1992 - metabolic acidosis and fatal myocardial failure
   * Five patients with respiratory illness developed worsening metabolic acidosis, bradycardia, and myocardial dysfunction within 5 days
   * Not recommended as long-term sedative in pediatric patients
   * Many institutions limit propofol infusions to 24 hours
   * ⚠️ Patients with mitochondrial disorders particularly sensitive to Prise at lower doses/durations
   * Recommendation: only single bolus for mitochondrial disorder patients, use alternative agents for maintenance
   * Variability in elimination with longer elimination times in neonates and preterm infants `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
   * Linked to increased risk of mortality in critically ill children `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
* **Hemodynamic effects:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
   * Maintains adequate hemodynamics in neonates when properly dosed `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
   * Randomized trial showed comparable stability to atropine/succinylcholine/morphine combination `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
   * Most commonly used IV induction agent in the United States `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 

#### 2.3.3.2. KETAMINE

* **Characteristics:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1. PHYSIOLOGY (~32% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
   * Phencyclidine derivative with great clinical flexibility
   * Clearance reduced in neonates, reaches adult levels by 6 months
   * NMDA antagonist used for induction, especially with cardiovascular instability `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
* **Administration routes and doses:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1. PHYSIOLOGY (~32% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * **Oral:** 5-6 mg/kg for premedication
	 * May cause postoperative nausea/vomiting
	 * Nightmares uncommon by this route
   * **Intranasal:** 3-6 mg/kg
   * **Rectal:** 6-10 mg/kg
	 * May result in prolonged recovery due to larger dose requirement
   * **Intramuscular:** 2-5 mg/kg
	 * Sedates uncooperative child in 3-5 minutes
	 * Duration 30-40 minutes
   * **Intravenous:** 1-2 mg/kg for induction
	 * Useful in cyanotic heart disease, septic shock, and when spontaneous respiration preservation needed
	 * Can be given as continuous infusion
	 * Occasionally used for perioperative analgesia
	 * Useful as opioid avoidance strategy in children with obstructive sleep apnea
* **Side effects:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1. PHYSIOLOGY (~32% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
   * Increased secretions
   * Nystagmus
   * Nausea/vomiting (up to 33% of children)
   * Nightmares and hallucinations (infrequent)
	 * May be reduced by coadministration of midazolam

#### 2.3.4.4. REVERSAL AGENTS

##### 2.3.4.4.1. NEOSTIGMINE

* **Mechanism and dosing:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1. PHYSIOLOGY (~32% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
   * Anticholinesterase preventing acetylcholine breakdown at neuromuscular junction
   * Most widely used agent for nondepolarizing blockade antagonism
   * Given with anticholinergic (usually glycopyrrolate) to balance bradycardia
   * Typical pediatric dose: 0.05 mg/kg neostigmine and 0.01 mg/kg glycopyrrolate (or 0.02 mg/kg atropine)

   * Doses can be repeated if full reversal not achieved
   * ⚠️ Avoid exceeding 0.1 mg/kg neostigmine (acetylcholine-associated weakness may occur)
   * ⚠️ Administration without anticholinergic is most common drug error leading to perioperative cardiac arrest in pediatric population `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
   * Takes 10 minutes for 90% reversal of neuromuscular block `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 

##### 2.3.4.4.2. SUGAMMADEX

* **Characteristics and mechanism:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1. PHYSIOLOGY (~32% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
   * γ-cyclodextrin compound that binds rocuronium (and to lesser extent vecuronium)
   * Eliminates neuromuscular blocking activity
   * Can be administered during moderate to profound blockade
   * Direct intervention for "cannot ventilate, cannot intubate" situation
   * Rocuronium/sugammadex complex excreted unchanged by kidneys
   * Restricted to off-label indications for children by FDA `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
   * Encapsulates and binds aminosteroid neuromuscular blocking agents (rocuronium, vecuronium) `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]`

   * 16 mg/kg for successful reversal 3 minutes after intubating dose of rocuronium
   * 2 mg/kg or more after partial recovery (two TOF twitches) yields TOF ratio of 0.9 in ~2 minutes
   * Significantly increased pediatric experience in recent years
   * Now default reversal agent at many US pediatric institutions
   * Retrospective study of 1,946 pediatric patients (including neonates) showed shorter time from reversal to OR exit compared to neostigmine (4,136 patients) with no differences in adverse effects `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]`

   * Despite effectiveness, monitoring for residual weakness still recommended
   * Partially reversed patients have increased risk of postoperative respiratory events
   * Monitoring of neuromuscular blockade remains essential
* **Special considerations:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1. PHYSIOLOGY (~32% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]`
   * Reduces effectiveness of birth control medications for up to one week
   * Usual practice to disclose this possibility to patients taking or who could be taking birth control

* **Medical history considerations:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Gestational age at birth
   * Chronological age
   * Birth weight
   * Current weight and trend
   * Delivery complications
   * Apgar scores
   * Respiratory status including:
	 * Need for supplemental oxygen
	 * Prior mechanical ventilation
	 * Surfactant administration
   * Prior surgeries and anesthetics
   * Presence of congenital anomalies, especially cardiac
   * Maternal diabetes (risk for neonatal hypoglycemia)
   * Maternal drug use
* **Physical examination focus:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Airway assessment - particularly important in syndromic neonates
   * Hydration status
   * Cardiorespiratory examination
   * Current medications
   * Recent laboratory values:
	 * Hemoglobin
	 * Electrolytes
	 * Glucose
	 * Additional tests as indicated by history and examination
* **Additional testing:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Consider echocardiogram for suspected congenital heart disease
   * Chest radiograph if respiratory concerns
   * Cranial ultrasound if preterm or other neurologic concerns
* **NPO status:** `[B:10-11]`
   * Special considerations for exclusively breastfed or formula-fed infants
   * Generally, clear liquids up to 2 hours before anesthesia
   * Breast milk up to 4 hours
   * Formula up to 6 hours
   * Balance between fasting requirements and risk of hypoglycemia
   * May require maintenance fluids with dextrose during prolonged fasting
* **Anesthesia plan considerations:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Review timing of surgery (elective vs. emergent)
   * Consider awake vs. asleep regional techniques when appropriate
   * Review concomitant medical conditions requiring perioperative management
   * Anticipate postoperative ventilation needs
   * Coordinate postoperative disposition (NICU/PICU vs. ward)
* **Overarching principle:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` 
   * Understanding physiologic changes during transition from fetal to neonatal life is crucial to anesthetic management `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` 
   * First 24 hours of life defined as newborn period `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` 
   * First 28 days of life defined as neonatal period `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` 
   * First 72 hours especially significant for cardiovascular, pulmonary, and renal systems `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` 

**Bottom Line:** Thorough preoperative assessment is critical in neonatal anesthesia, with special attention to gestational and chronological age, respiratory status, congenital anomalies, and metabolic considerations. NPO guidelines must balance aspiration risk against hypoglycemia risk, especially in premature or medically complex neonates.

## 3.2. INTRAOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT


**Exam Weighting:** High

### 3.2.2. ANESTHETIC TECHNIQUE SELECTION

* **Factors influencing anesthetic technique:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Surgical requirements
   * Need for postoperative ventilation
   * Cardiovascular stability
   * Anticipated method of postoperative pain control
* **Uptake and distribution considerations:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Increased alveolar ventilation-to-FRC ratio speeds inhalational induction
   * Decreased protein binding increases free fraction of IV agents
   * Decreased hepatic metabolism prolongs effect of many drugs
   * Immature blood-brain barrier increases CNS effects of medications
* **Dose adjustments:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Most anesthetic agents require reduced dosing
   * Clearance of many drugs reduced due to immature liver and kidney function
   * Increased volume of distribution may require weight-based calculation adjustments
* **Key physiologic factors:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` 
   * Dramatic growth and maturation during first year of life - body weight changes by factor of three `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` 
   * Interrelated changes in cardiovascular, pulmonary, and renal systems mean disease in one system can rapidly alter maturation of others `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` 

### 3.2.3. REGIONAL ANESTHESIA

* **Benefits in neonates:** `[B:11-12]`
   * Can reduce or eliminate need for opioids and general anesthetics
   * May allow for earlier extubation and recovery
   * Particularly useful for lower abdominal and lower extremity surgeries
* **Common techniques:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Caudal blocks
   * Spinal anesthesia
   * Peripheral nerve blocks increasingly utilized with ultrasound guidance
* **Considerations:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Reduced local anesthetic doses compared to older children
   * Higher risk of local anesthetic toxicity due to reduced protein binding
   * Careful attention to total dosing, especially with multiple blocks or continuous infusions

### 3.2.4. POSTOPERATIVE PAIN MANAGEMENT

* **Multimodal approach:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Combinations of regional techniques, acetaminophen, and minimal opioids when possible
   * Judicious use of opioids with careful monitoring for respiratory depression
* **Opioid considerations:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Increased sensitivity due to immature blood-brain barrier
   * Reduced clearance prolongs effect
   * Higher risk of respiratory depression, especially in preterm infants
   * Stringent monitoring requirements, especially if ex-preterm with history of apnea
* **Non-opioid options:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Acetaminophen (IV, rectal, or oral)
   * Regional anesthesia
   * NSAIDs used cautiously due to renal and platelet considerations

### 3.2.5. POSTOPERATIVE VENTILATION

* **Indications for continued ventilation:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Prematurity, especially with history of apnea
   * Major surgical procedures
   * Pre-existing pulmonary disease
   * Cardiovascular instability
   * Hypothermia
   * Significant fluid shifts or blood loss
* **Extubation considerations:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Adequate reversal of neuromuscular blockade
   * Normothermia
   * Cardiovascular stability
   * Adequate pain control
   * Appropriate postoperative monitoring capability

**Bottom Line:** Intraoperative management of neonates requires understanding of their unique physiology and pharmacology. Dose adjustments for most medications, careful temperature management, judicious fluid administration, and consideration of regional techniques where appropriate can improve outcomes. Decisions regarding postoperative ventilation should consider multiple factors beyond the surgical procedure itself.

## 3.3. SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS


**Exam Weighting:** High

### 3.3.2. MATERNAL DRUG USE DURING PREGNANCY

* **Considerations:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
   * Maternal medications can affect neonatal physiology and drug responses
   * Opioid exposure may lead to neonatal abstinence syndrome
   * β-blockers can cause neonatal hypoglycemia and bradycardia
   * Carefully review all maternal medications during preoperative assessment

### 3.3.3. TEMPERATURE CONTROL AND THERMOGENESIS

* **Neonatal thermal regulation:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
   * Neonates at high risk for hypothermia due to:
	 * Higher body surface area to weight ratio
	 * Limited subcutaneous fat
	 * Immature thermoregulatory mechanisms
	 * Inability to shiver effectively
* **Consequences of hypothermia:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
   * Metabolic acidosis
   * Increased oxygen consumption
   * Pulmonary hypertension
   * Delayed drug metabolism
   * Delayed emergence
   * Apnea and respiratory depression
   * Coagulopathy
* **Prevention strategies:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
   * Increased ambient temperature in operating room (>26°C/78.8°F)
   * Warmed IV fluids
   * Forced-air warming devices
   * Humidified and warmed respiratory gases
   * Plastic wrap or bags for very small infants
   * Minimizing exposed surface area
   * Warming surgical prep solutions

### 3.3.4. RESPIRATORY DISTRESS SYNDROME

* **Pathophysiology:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
   * Insufficient surfactant production, especially in premature infants
   * Alveolar collapse leading to atelectasis and poor oxygenation
   * Increased work of breathing
* **Management implications:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
   * May require continued mechanical ventilation postoperatively
   * Need for higher PEEP to maintain alveolar recruitment
   * Judicious fluid management to avoid pulmonary edema
   * Consider timing of surgery relative to surfactant administration
   * Higher risk of barotrauma and pneumothorax

### 3.3.5. POSTOPERATIVE APNEA

* **Risk factors:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` `[B:12-13]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
   * Prematurity (highest risk in ex-premature infants <60 weeks post-conceptual age)
   * History of apnea of prematurity
   * Anemia (Hgb <10 g/dL)
   * Neurological disorders
   * Hypothermia
   * Sepsis
   * Recent general anesthesia
* **Prevention and management:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
   * Continuous respiratory and oxygen saturation monitoring for 12-24 hours postoperatively
   * Consider overnight admission for high-risk outpatients
   * Maintain normothermia
   * Judicious use of opioids and sedatives
   * Consider caffeine citrate (10 mg/kg loading, 5 mg/kg/day maintenance) for high-risk infants
   * Regional anesthesia where feasible to minimize general anesthetic exposure

### 3.3.6. RETINOPATHY OF PREMATURITY

* **Pathophysiology:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
   * Abnormal retinal vascular development in premature infants
   * Risk factors include:
	 * Extreme prematurity (<32 weeks gestational age)
	 * Low birth weight (<1,500 g)
	 * Prolonged oxygen exposure
	 * Fluctuating oxygen levels
* **Anesthetic considerations:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
   * Careful oxygen titration to maintain saturations appropriate for gestational age
   * Avoid hyperoxia and wide SpO₂ fluctuations
   * Coordination with neonatology for target oxygen saturations
   * Consider lower acceptable SpO₂ ranges (85-92%) in extremely premature infants
   * Balance oxygen needs for surgical conditions against ROP risk

### 3.3.7. NEURODEVELOPMENTAL EFFECTS OF ANESTHETIC AGENTS

* **Current understanding:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
   * Animal studies suggest most general anesthetics and sedatives acting on NMDA or GABA receptors may cause apoptosis in developing brains
   * Only α-2 agonists, opioids, muscle relaxants, and possibly xenon appear to lack this effect
   * FDA statement (2016) recommended limiting anesthesia exposure in children ≤3 years
* **Human evidence:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
   * GAS study (randomized controlled trial) compared general vs. spinal anesthesia in infants <60 weeks postmenstrual age
   * No difference in neurocognitive outcomes at 2 and 5 years
   * Comparison of siblings discordant for anesthesia exposure at <36 months showed no neurocognitive differences
   * Brief single exposures appear relatively safe based on current human data
   * More research needed for multiple or prolonged exposures
* **Clinical practice implications:** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2. PHARMACOLOGY + BIOSTATISTICS (~33% of Total Weightage)|ⓘ]]` 
   * Balance risks of anesthesia against risks of delaying necessary procedures
   * Consider regional anesthesia where appropriate
   * Minimize anesthesia duration and depth when possible
   * Ensure adequate analgesia despite concerns
   * Discuss current evidence with parents during informed consent

**Bottom Line:** Neonatal anesthesia requires attention to multiple unique considerations including temperature regulation, postoperative apnea risk, retinopathy concerns in premature infants, and potential neurodevelopmental effects. Careful preoperative assessment, appropriate monitoring, and multidisciplinary planning are essential for optimizing outcomes in this vulnerable population.

## 3.4. SURGICAL PROCEDURES IN NEONATES


**Exam Weighting:** Medium

### 3.4.2. FIRST WEEK OF LIFE PROCEDURES

#### 3.4.2.1. TRACHEOESOPHAGEAL FISTULA (TEF) REPAIR

* **Preoperative assessment:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` `[B:S3:5]`
   * Evaluate for associated anomalies (VACTERL - Vertebral, Anorectal, Cardiac, Tracheal, Esophageal, Renal, Limb)
   * Cardiac evaluation essential (30-50% have congenital heart disease)
   * Assess for pneumonia from aspiration
   * Renal function evaluation
* **Anesthetic management:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` `[B:S3:5]`
   * ⚠️ Avoid mask ventilation if possible to prevent gastric distention
   * Consider awake intubation in unstable neonates
   * Position endotracheal tube above fistula but below vocal cords
   * May require bronchoscopy to identify fistula location
   * Arterial line for monitoring during one-lung ventilation
   * Anticipate significant surgical stimulation during thoracotomy
   * Consider epidural or paravertebral catheter for postoperative analgesia
   * Prepare for postoperative ventilation

#### 3.4.2.2. CONGENITAL DIAPHRAGMATIC HERNIA (CDH) REPAIR

* **Pathophysiology:** `[B:13-14]` `[B:S3:5-6]`
   * Herniation of abdominal contents into chest during embryonic development
   * Pulmonary hypoplasia on affected side and often contralateral side
   * Pulmonary hypertension is primary determinant of outcome
   * Leftshift of mediastinum and cardiac structures
* **Preoperative preparation:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` `[B:S3:5-6]`
   * Avoid mask ventilation after birth
   * Immediate intubation and gentle ventilation
   * Placement of oro/nasogastric tube to decompress stomach
   * Echocardiogram to assess cardiac function and pulmonary hypertension
   * Preoperative stabilization critical - surgery usually not emergent
   * Medical management of pulmonary hypertension may include:
	 * Inhaled nitric oxide
	 * ECMO in severe cases
	 * High-frequency ventilation
	 * Permissive hypercapnia strategy
* **Anesthetic considerations:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` `[B:S3:6]`
   * High risk for pneumothorax on ventilated side during repair
   * Arterial line essential
   * Central venous access often needed
   * Avoid nitrous oxide
   * Minimize peak inspiratory pressures
   * Maintain normocapnia or mild hypercapnia
   * Be prepared to treat pulmonary hypertensive crisis:
	 * Increase FiO₂
	 * Hyperventilation (temporarily)
	 * Deepening anesthesia
	 * Inhaled nitric oxide if available
   * Consider postoperative ventilation with delayed extubation

#### 3.4.2.3. OMPHALOCELE/GASTROSCHISIS REPAIR

* **Distinguishing features:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` `[B:S3:6]`
   * **Omphalocele:**
	 * Herniation of abdominal contents into umbilical cord
	 * Covered by peritoneum
	 * Higher association with other congenital anomalies (cardiac, chromosomal)
   * **Gastroschisis:**
	 * Paraumbilical defect with evisceration of bowel
	 * No protective membrane
	 * Bowel often thickened, matted, and foreshortened
* **Preoperative considerations:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` `[B:S3:6]`
   * Fluid resuscitation (significant third-space losses)
   * Evaluate for associated anomalies
   * Prevent hypothermia (large exposed surface area)
   * Assess bowel viability
   * Determine if primary or staged closure possible
* **Anesthetic management:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` `[B:S3:6]`
   * Large-bore IV access
   * Arterial line monitoring
   * Anticipate large fluid requirements (10-20 mL/kg/hr)
   * Blood products available
   * ⚠️ Monitor for abdominal compartment syndrome during primary closure:
	 * Increased peak inspiratory pressures
	 * Decreased urine output
	 * Lower extremity venous congestion
   * Staged closure (silo placement) may be needed if primary closure causes:
	 * Respiratory compromise
	 * Cardiovascular instability
	 * Inadequate perfusion to lower extremities or viscera

### 3.4.3. FIRST MONTH OF LIFE PROCEDURES

#### 3.4.3.1. NECROTIZING ENTEROCOLITIS (NEC)

* **Pathophysiology:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` `[B:S3:6-7]`
   * Inflammatory bowel condition primarily affecting premature infants
   * Characterized by intestinal inflammation, pneumatosis intestinalis, and potential perforation
   * Multifactorial etiology including prematurity, enteral feeding, and infection
* **Preoperative preparation:** `[B:14-15]` `[B:S3:7]`
   * Often critically ill, requiring significant resuscitation
   * Metabolic acidosis common
   * Frequently on mechanical ventilation
   * Review coagulation studies (often abnormal)
   * Thrombocytopenia common
* **Anesthetic management:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` `[B:S3:7]`
   * Arterial and central venous access
   * Blood products available (including platelets and plasma)
   * Anticipate significant fluid shifts
   * Consider vasopressor support
   * Monitor for sepsis and cardiovascular instability
   * Surgical options range from peritoneal drain to extensive bowel resection
   * Postoperative ventilation usually required

#### 3.4.3.2. PYLORIC STENOSIS

* **Preoperative assessment:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` `[B:S3:7]`
   * Characteristic metabolic alkalosis with hypokalemia and hypochloremia
   * Dehydration from vomiting
   * Delayed gastric emptying
* **Preoperative preparation:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` `[B:S3:7]`
   * Correction of fluid and electrolyte abnormalities (goal: Cl⁻ >100 mEq/L)
   * Rehydration with isotonic fluid containing potassium chloride
   * Nasogastric tube placement to decompress stomach
* **Anesthetic management:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` `[B:S3:7]`
   * Consider rapid sequence induction
   * Opioid-sparing technique with regional anesthesia (rectus sheath or wound infiltration)
   * Often amenable to early extubation
   * Consider antiemetics for postoperative nausea/vomiting prophylaxis
* **General principle:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` 
   * True surgical emergencies are uncommon in the neonatal period `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` 
   * Thorough preoperative evaluation and stabilization is critical for conditions with significant comorbidities `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]` 

**Bottom Line:** Neonatal surgical procedures present unique challenges requiring thorough understanding of the underlying pathophysiology and its anesthetic implications. Careful preoperative preparation, appropriate monitoring, and anticipation of potential complications are essential for successful management. Multidisciplinary coordination between surgery, anesthesia, and neonatology teams optimizes outcomes for these vulnerable patients.

# 4. PEDIATRIC ANESTHESIA PRACTICE


## 4.1. PREOPERATIVE ASSESSMENT AND PREPARATION


**Exam Weighting:** High

### 4.1.2. INFORMED CONSENT, ASSENT, AND PARENTAL PERMISSION

* **Process overview:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Fundamental to ethical medical practice
   * More complex in pediatrics due to varying decision-making capacity
   * Obtaining permission from parents/legal guardians required
   * Seeking assent from children based on developmental level
* **Age-appropriate approaches:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Infants and toddlers: Focus on parental permission and preparation
   * School-age children: Include in discussion at appropriate level, seek informal assent
   * Adolescents: Increased involvement in decision-making, formal assent process
   * Mature minors: May have decision-making capacity approaching that of adults
* **Special situations:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Emergencies without parental presence
   * Parental religious objections to treatment
   * Adolescent confidentiality and reproductive health
   * Conflicts between parents or between child and parents

### 4.1.3. FASTING GUIDELINES

* **Standard recommendations:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Clear liquids: 2 hours
   * Breast milk: 4 hours
   * Formula, non-human milk, light meal: 6 hours
   * Heavy meal or meal with fat: 8 hours
* **Special considerations:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Infants and young children at higher risk for hypoglycemia during prolonged fasting
   * Balance between aspiration risk and hypoglycemia risk
   * Consider IV fluids with dextrose for prolonged fasting in infants
   * Recent trend toward more liberal clear fluid policies (up to 1 hour pre-induction) in some institutions

### 4.1.4. LABORATORY TESTING

* **Evidence-based approach:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Routine laboratory testing not recommended for healthy children
   * Tests should be ordered based on history, physical examination, and planned procedure
   * Targeted testing based on specific concerns more valuable than blanket policies
* **Common indications:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Hemoglobin/hematocrit:
	 * History of anemia or bleeding disorder
	 * Major surgery with significant blood loss anticipated
	 * Severe cardiac disease
   * Coagulation studies:
	 * Personal or family history of bleeding disorder
	 * Liver disease
	 * Anticoagulant therapy
   * Electrolytes:
	 * Renal disease
	 * Receiving diuretics or other medications affecting electrolytes
	 * Significant failure to thrive
   * Blood glucose:
	 * History of diabetes
	 * Receiving steroids or parenteral nutrition
	 * Suspected metabolic or endocrine disorder

### 4.1.5. UPPER RESPIRATORY TRACT INFECTION (URI)

* **Risk assessment:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Increased risk of perioperative respiratory adverse events (PRAEs)
   * Risk factors for complications:
	 * Current or recent URI (within 2-4 weeks)
	 * Age <1 year
	 * History of prematurity or bronchopulmonary dysplasia
	 * Reactive airway disease
	 * Passive smoke exposure
	 * Airway surgery
	 * Endotracheal intubation (versus mask or LMA)
* **Decision-making guidelines:** `[B:33-34]`
   * Elective procedures may be postponed 2-4 weeks after URI symptoms resolve
   * Consider proceeding if:
	 * Symptoms limited to rhinorrhea without fever
	 * No signs of lower respiratory involvement
	 * Child is otherwise well
	 * Procedure is minor or outpatient
   * Consider postponing if:
	 * Fever, toxic appearance, or productive cough
	 * Lower respiratory symptoms (wheezing, rales)
	 * Major procedure requiring postoperative ventilation
	 * Child has significant comorbidities
* **Management if proceeding with anesthesia:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Consider avoiding endotracheal intubation if possible
   * Deep extubation may reduce coughing
   * Use of anticholinergics may help reduce secretions
   * Consider intraoperative bronchodilators for reactive airways
   * Plan for appropriate postoperative monitoring

### 4.1.6. ASTHMA

* **Preoperative assessment:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Current symptoms and control
   * Recent exacerbations or emergency visits
   * Current medications and adherence
   * Exercise tolerance
   * Prior anesthetic experiences
   * Signs of active bronchospasm
* **Optimization strategies:** `[B:35-36]`
   * Continue all controller medications through day of surgery
   * Administer rescue inhaler (albuterol) before induction
   * Consider short course of oral steroids for poorly controlled asthma
   * Wait until well-controlled if elective procedure (no wheezing, normal peak flows)
   * Preoperative pulmonary function testing in selected cases
* **Intraoperative management:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Avoid airway irritants (desflurane, tracheal intubation if possible)
   * Consider IV induction if moderate to severe asthma
   * Sevoflurane preferred for inhalational induction
   * Deep extubation to avoid bronchospasm if appropriate
   * Be prepared to treat intraoperative bronchospasm:
	 * Deepen anesthesia
	 * Bronchodilators via circuit
	 * Consider IV agents (magnesium, epinephrine, ketamine) for severe cases

### 4.1.7. OBSTRUCTIVE SLEEP APNEA (OSA)

* **Screening and diagnosis:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Ask about snoring, observed apneas, difficulty breathing during sleep
   * Consider validated screening tools (e.g., BEARS, STOP-BANG)
   * Polysomnography is gold standard but often not available pre-surgery
   * Physical findings: tonsillar hypertrophy, obesity, craniofacial abnormalities
* **Risk stratification:** `[B:37-38]`
   * Low risk: Occasional snoring, no witnessed apneas, no daytime symptoms
   * Moderate risk: Regular snoring with occasional apneas or daytime symptoms
   * High risk: Severe snoring, frequent apneas, significant daytime symptoms, or abnormal polysomnography
* **Perioperative management:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Consider opioid-sparing or opioid-free anesthesia techniques
   * Regional anesthesia where appropriate
   * Multimodal analgesia (acetaminophen, NSAIDs, dexmedetomidine)
   * Staged extubation when fully awake
   * Extended postoperative monitoring for high-risk patients
   * Consider postoperative CPAP/BiPAP if used preoperatively

### 4.1.8. PREMATURITY

* **Definitions and risk assessment:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Preterm: <37 weeks gestational age
   * Very preterm: <32 weeks gestational age
   * Extremely preterm: <28 weeks gestational age
   * Post-conceptual age (PCA) = gestational age at birth + chronological age
   * Post-menstrual age (PMA) = gestational age at birth + chronological age
* **Major considerations:** `[B:39-40]`
   * Risk of postoperative apnea until 60 weeks PCA (primarily ex-preterm <56 weeks PCA)
   * Risk factors: younger gestational age at birth, anemia, neurological injury
   * Respiratory considerations from bronchopulmonary dysplasia
   * Potential for retinopathy of prematurity (oxygen management)
   * Increased risk for neurodevelopmental issues
   * Potential for intraventricular hemorrhage in very preterm infants
* **Management strategies:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Consider delaying elective procedures until >60 weeks PCA
   * Overnight admission for observation if <60 weeks PCA
   * Regional techniques to minimize general anesthesia exposure
   * Consider caffeine prophylaxis (10 mg/kg loading, 5 mg/kg/day maintenance)
   * Careful oxygen management with specific SpO₂ targets
   * Vigilant temperature management
   * Gentle ventilation strategies for those with BPD

### 4.1.9. MALIGNANT HYPERTHERMIA (MH)

* **Risk assessment:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Personal or family history of MH reaction
   * Family history of unexplained anesthesia-related deaths
   * Certain myopathies (central core disease, King-Denborough syndrome)
   * History of exertional rhabdomyolysis warrants caution
* **Preoperative preparation:** `[B:41-42]`
   * Prepare MH-safe anesthesia machine:
	 * Fully clean according to manufacturer guidelines
	 * Replace breathing circuit, carbon dioxide absorbent, and reservoir bag
	 * Remove vaporizers if possible
	 * Flush with high flow oxygen for appropriate time (varies by machine)
   * Prepare alternative ventilation system if needed
   * Ensure dantrolene immediately available (at least 36 vials or 720 mg)
   * Verify operation of cooling equipment, ice availability
   * Create emergency contact list (MH Hotline: 1-800-644-9737)
* **Safe anesthetic technique:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Avoid all triggering agents:
	 * Volatile anesthetics (sevoflurane, isoflurane, desflurane)
	 * Succinylcholine
   * Safe agents include:
	 * Propofol, ketamine, etomidate, dexmedetomidine
	 * All opioids
	 * All non-depolarizing muscle relaxants
	 * All local anesthetics
	 * Nitrous oxide (though often avoided to simplify technique)

### 4.1.10. MYOPATHIES

* **Duchenne muscular dystrophy:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Progressive muscle weakness and cardiomyopathy
   * Increased risk of rhabdomyolysis with volatile agents and succinylcholine
   * Associated with malignant hyperthermia-like reactions
   * Assess cardiac and respiratory function preoperatively
   * Total intravenous anesthesia preferred
   * Monitor for respiratory complications postoperatively
* **Myotonic dystrophy:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Risk of prolonged myotonia with succinylcholine
   * Increased sensitivity to sedatives and anesthetics
   * Risk of aspiration due to poor airway protection
   * Cardiac conduction abnormalities common
   * Consider avoiding anticholinesterases due to potential myotonia
   * Maintain normothermia (cold can trigger myotonia)
* **Other myopathies:** `[B:43-44]`
   * Individualized approach based on specific disorder
   * Common considerations:
	 * Cardiac evaluation
	 * Assessment of respiratory function
	 * Risk of rhabdomyolysis with certain agents
	 * Sensitivity to neuromuscular blocking agents
	 * Postoperative ventilatory support needs

### 4.1.11. MITOCHONDRIAL DISEASE

* **Anesthetic considerations:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Heterogeneous group of disorders affecting cellular energy production
   * Preoperative evaluation of cardiac, neurologic, and metabolic status
   * Maintain glucose homeostasis (avoid hypoglycemia)
   * ⚠️ High sensitivity to propofol (avoid continuous infusions)
   * Increased risk of malignant hyperthermia-like reactions
   * Preference for regional anesthesia when possible
   * Close monitoring of acid-base status
   * Careful titration of all anesthetic agents

### 4.1.12. SICKLE CELL DISEASE

* **Preoperative preparation:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Review baseline hematological status
   * Assess history of crises, acute chest syndrome, strokes
   * Consider preoperative transfusion for major surgery
   * Simple transfusion to hemoglobin ~10 g/dL often sufficient
   * Exchange transfusion may be needed for high-risk procedures
* **Intraoperative management:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Avoid triggers for sickling:
	 * Hypoxemia
	 * Hypothermia
	 * Dehydration
	 * Acidosis
	 * Vascular stasis
   * Generous hydration
   * Maintain normothermia
   * Supplemental oxygen
   * Adequate analgesia
   * Consider regional techniques for postoperative pain

### 4.1.13. OTHER HEMATOLOGIC DISORDERS

* **Hemophilia and von Willebrand disease:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Coordination with hematology for factor replacement
   * Target factor levels depend on surgical procedure
   * Avoid intramuscular injections
   * Regional anesthesia generally contraindicated unless factor levels normalized
* **Thrombocytopenia:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Consider platelet transfusion for counts <50,000/μL before major surgery
   * Higher thresholds (80,000-100,000/μL) for neurosurgery or ophthalmology
   * Avoid regional techniques if platelet count <80,000/μL

### 4.1.14. ANTERIOR MEDIASTINAL MASS

* **Risk assessment:** `[B:46-47]`
   * High-risk features:
	 * Symptoms of positional dyspnea or orthopnea
	 * Stridor, wheezing, or upper airway obstruction
	 * Superior vena cava syndrome
	 * Pericardial effusion
	 * Tracheal compression >50% on imaging
* **Preoperative evaluation:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * CT scan to assess airway and vascular compression
   * Flow-volume loops if available
   * Echocardiogram to assess cardiac compression
   * Consider arterial blood gas
   * Multidisciplinary planning with surgeons and oncology
* **Management strategies:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Consider local anesthesia or awake procedures if feasible
   * Maintain spontaneous ventilation
   * Avoid muscle relaxants until airway secured and ventilation confirmed
   * Position patient semi-upright or lateral if tolerated
   * Prepare for rigid bronchoscopy and cardiopulmonary bypass
   * Consider radiation therapy before diagnostic procedures in severe cases

### 4.1.15. SUBACUTE BACTERIAL ENDOCARDITIS PROPHYLAXIS

* **Current recommendations:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Much more limited than historical practice
   * Prophylaxis indicated only for highest risk cardiac conditions:
	 * Prosthetic cardiac valve
	 * Previous endocarditis
	 * Unrepaired cyanotic congenital heart disease
	 * Completely repaired CHD with prosthetic material (first 6 months)
	 * Repaired CHD with residual defects adjacent to prosthetic material
	 * Cardiac transplant with valvulopathy
* **Procedures requiring prophylaxis:** `[B:47-48]`
   * Dental procedures involving manipulation of gingival tissue or periapical region
   * Procedures involving respiratory tract mucosa
   * Procedures on infected skin or musculoskeletal tissue
   * GI or GU procedures generally do not require prophylaxis
* **Antibiotic regimens:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Amoxicillin: 50 mg/kg (max 2g) orally 1 hour before procedure
   * Alternatives for penicillin allergy:
	 * Clindamycin: 20 mg/kg (max 600mg)
	 * Cephalexin: 50 mg/kg (max 2g)
	 * Azithromycin: 15 mg/kg (max 500mg)

### 4.1.16. OBESITY

* **Assessment and risk stratification:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Body mass index (BMI) percentile for age
   * Assess for comorbidities:
	 * Obstructive sleep apnea
	 * Hypertension
	 * Type 2 diabetes
	 * Asthma
	 * Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease
* **Perioperative considerations:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Potentially difficult airway management
   * Increased risk for aspiration
   * Reduced functional residual capacity
   * Increased risk for perioperative respiratory adverse events
   * Altered pharmacokinetics of many anesthetic agents
   * Consider regional techniques where appropriate
   * Extended PACU monitoring for respiratory complications

### 4.1.17. DIABETES MELLITUS

* **Preoperative evaluation:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Type 1 vs. Type 2 diabetes
   * Current insulin regimen or oral medications
   * Recent glucose control (HbA1c if available)
   * Presence of microor macrovascular complications
   * History of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) or hypoglycemia
* **Perioperative management:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Type 1:
	 * Continue basal insulin at 80-100% of usual dose
	 * Hold rapid-acting insulin while fasting
	 * Check glucose before induction and every 1-2 hours intraoperatively
	 * IV insulin infusion for major surgeries or poor control
	 * Target glucose 100-180 mg/dL (5.6-10 mmol/L)
   * Type 2:
	 * Hold oral agents day of surgery
	 * Consider reducing insulin doses
	 * Similar glucose monitoring strategy to Type 1
	 * Resume regular regimen when eating normally

### 4.1.18. AUTISM

* **Preoperative preparation:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Detailed history of triggers, calming techniques, communication style
   * Consider involving caregivers in planning
   * Minimize waiting time and transitions
   * Consider pre-visit to familiarize with environment
   * Visual schedules or social stories may help
* **Perioperative approach:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Individualized premedication plan
   * Consider separate quiet induction area
   * Allow comfort items (toys, tablets, headphones)
   * Minimize sensory stimulation
   * Involve caregivers in emergence planning
   * Consider early PACU discharge or reunion with caregivers

**Bottom Line:** Comprehensive preoperative assessment in pediatric anesthesia requires consideration of multiple factors beyond the planned procedure. Understanding developmental, physiological, pharmacological, and psychological differences in children is essential for optimizing outcomes. A systematic approach to specific conditions, tailored communication with children and families, and appropriate preparation can minimize complications and improve the perioperative experience.

## 4.2. INDUCTION AND AIRWAY MANAGEMENT


**Exam Weighting:** High

### 4.2.2. INDUCTION TECHNIQUES

* **Inhalational induction:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Most common approach in younger children without IV access
   * Advantages:
	 * Avoids anxiety of needle placement while awake
	 * Preserves spontaneous respiration
	 * Easily titratable
	 * Smooth transition to maintenance
   * Technique:
	 * Sevoflurane preferred agent (least airway irritation)
	 * Start with lower concentration (0.5-2%) and gradually increase
	 * Single-breath technique in cooperative older children
	 * Consider circuit priming for faster induction
	 * Maintain mask seal while allowing some spontaneous movement
	 * Support airway with appropriate maneuvers
* **Intravenous induction:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Preferred for older children, emergencies, rapid sequence induction
   * Common agents:
	 * Propofol: 2.5-3.5 mg/kg (higher in younger children)
	 * Ketamine: 1-2 mg/kg
	 * Etomidate: 0.2-0.3 mg/kg (hemodynamic stability)
	 * Thiopental: 3-5 mg/kg (where available)
   * Techniques for IV placement:
	 * Use of topical anesthetics (EMLA, LMX)
	 * Distraction techniques
	 * Comfort positioning
	 * Child life specialist involvement
	 * Consider nitrous oxide administration during IV placement
* **Combining techniques:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Mask induction followed by IV placement under anesthesia
   * IV placement with sedation followed by IV induction
   * Consideration of anxiety level, procedure type, and patient factors

### 4.2.3. MONITORING DURING INDUCTION

* **Standard monitoring:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Pulse oximetry
   * ECG
   * Non-invasive blood pressure
   * Capnography (once airway established)
   * Temperature
* **Placement considerations:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Apply monitors in least threatening order:
	 * Pulse oximetry first (can be presented as "special light")
	 * Blood pressure cuff (can be called "arm hug")
	 * ECG leads
   * Consider applying ECG leads after induction in anxious children
   * Apply at least pulse oximetry and have other monitors ready before induction

### 4.2.4. AIRWAY EQUIPMENT SELECTION

* **Endotracheal tubes:** `[B:52-53]`
   * **Size estimation:**
	 * Uncuffed: Age/4 + 4 (internal diameter in mm)
	 * Cuffed: Age/4 + 3 (internal diameter in mm)
	 * Newborns: 3.0-3.5 mm ID
	 * 1-6 months: 3.5 mm ID
	 * 6-18 months: 4.0 mm ID
	 * Alternative formula for cuffed ETT: (Age in years + 3)/4
   * **Length estimation:**
	 * Oral: (Age/2) + 12 cm
	 * Nasal: (Age/2) + 15 cm
   * Always have one size smaller and larger available
   * Confirmation of placement:
	 * End-tidal CO2
	 * Bilateral breath sounds
	 * Appropriate depth marking at teeth/gums
	 * Symmetric chest rise
* **Laryngeal mask airways:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Sizing based on patient weight:
	 * Size 1: <5 kg
	 * Size 1.5: 5-10 kg
	 * Size 2: 10-20 kg
	 * Size 2.5: 20-30 kg
	 * Size 3: 30-50 kg
	 * Size 4: 50-70 kg
	 * Size 5: >70 kg
   * Consider second-generation devices with gastric port for higher-risk patients
   * Appropriate for many procedures in spontaneously breathing children
   * Contraindicated in high-risk aspiration scenarios
* **Face masks:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Various shapes available (anatomic, circular)
   * Size should cover from bridge of nose to mental cleft
   * Clear masks allow visualization of secretions, cyanosis
   * Soft cushion provides better seal
   * Consider oral airway for difficult mask ventilation
* **Ventilation strategies:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Age-appropriate settings:
	 * Tidal volume: 5-8 mL/kg ideal body weight
	 * Respiratory rate by age:
	   * Infants: 25-30 breaths/min
	   * Toddlers: 20-25 breaths/min
	   * School-age: 16-20 breaths/min
	   * Adolescents: 12-16 breaths/min
	 * PEEP: 3-5 cmH2O standard, higher for obese or at risk for atelectasis
   * Pressure vs. volume control:
	 * Pressure control often preferred in children <20 kg
	 * Volume control with pressure limiting in older children
   * Minimize peak inspiratory pressures

### 4.2.5. DIFFICULT AIRWAY MANAGEMENT

* **Prediction of difficult airway:** `[B:54-55]`
   * Syndromic features:
	 * Micrognathia (Pierre Robin, Treacher Collins)
	 * Midface hypoplasia (Apert, Crouzon)
	 * Macroglossia (Beckwith-Wiedemann, Down syndrome)
	 * Cervical spine limitations (Klippel-Feil, arthrogryposis)
   * History of previous difficult intubation
   * Limited mouth opening or neck mobility
   * Airway masses or trauma
* **Difficult airway approach:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Differs from adult approach - awake intubation rarely feasible
   * Maintain spontaneous ventilation when possible
   * Consider video laryngoscopy for first attempt
   * Have multiple backup plans:
	 * Different blade types and sizes
	 * Supraglottic airways
	 * Optical stylets
	 * Fiberoptic devices
	 * Surgical airway equipment (appropriate sizes)
   * Multidisciplinary planning for known difficult airways
   * Clear communication of airway handoff between providers
* **Cannot intubate, cannot ventilate scenario:** `[B:55-56]`
   * Optimize positioning
   * Two-person mask ventilation with oral/nasal airways
   * LMA or other supraglottic airway
   * Call for help early
   * Consider needle cricothyroidotomy with jet ventilation
   * Surgical airway as last resort (challenging in children <8 years)

### 4.2.6. SPECIAL AIRWAY CONSIDERATIONS

* **Full stomach and rapid sequence induction:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Indications:
	 * Recent meal
	 * Trauma
	 * Intestinal obstruction
	 * Symptomatic reflux
	 * Emergency surgery
   * Modified approach for children:
	 * Preoxygenation
	 * Application of cricoid pressure (less emphasis than historically)
	 * IV induction agent followed immediately by muscle relaxant
	 * Intubation without bag-mask ventilation
	 * Consider gentle mask ventilation if desaturation occurs
	 * Muscle relaxant options include rocuronium (preferred) or succinylcholine
* **Epiglottitis and other airway infections:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Much less common since Haemophilus influenzae vaccination
   * Avoid agitating child; allow to maintain position of comfort
   * Consider transfer to specialized center if time allows
   * Inhalational induction maintaining spontaneous ventilation
   * Equipment for emergency surgical airway must be available
   * Consider intensivist and ENT presence for induction
   * Secure airway in operating room when possible
* **Foreign body aspiration:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Maintain spontaneous ventilation
   * Avoid positive pressure ventilation if possible
   * Coordinate closely with surgeon
   * Prepare for:
	 * Hypoxemia
	 * Bronchospasm
	 * Complete obstruction
	 * Pneumothorax
   * Rigid bronchoscopy by surgeon or anesthesiologist may be needed

**Bottom Line:** Pediatric induction and airway management require age-appropriate techniques, equipment selection, and preparation for potential challenges. Understanding the anatomical and physiological differences in children's airways is essential for safe management. While difficult airways may be predicted in many cases, contingency planning and early recognition of problems are critical for all pediatric anesthetics.

## 4.3. MAINTENANCE AND FLUID MANAGEMENT


**Exam Weighting:** High

### 4.3.2. MAINTENANCE TECHNIQUES

* **Inhalational anesthesia:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Most common technique in children
   * Age-specific MAC values:
	 * Higher in infants 1-6 months (peak at 6 months)
	 * Gradually decrease throughout childhood
	 * Lower in premature infants
   * Appropriate end-tidal concentrations:
	 * Sevoflurane: 2-3% for surgical anesthesia
	 * Isoflurane: 1-1.5% for surgical anesthesia
	 * May require higher concentrations for stimulating procedures
   * Options for airway management:
	 * Face mask for brief procedures
	 * LMA for intermediate procedures without significant aspiration risk
	 * Endotracheal tube for longer procedures or when airway protection needed
* **Total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA):** `[B:57-58]`
   * Increasing use in pediatrics
   * Indications:
	 * MH susceptibility
	 * Procedures where depth monitoring needed (e.g., neurophysiologic monitoring)
	 * Avoidance of pollution where scavenging inadequate
	 * Malignant hyperthermia susceptibility
	 * Transport or remote location anesthesia
	 * Patient preference
   * Common regimens:
	 * Propofol (100-300 mcg/kg/min) + remifentanil (0.05-0.3 mcg/kg/min)
	 * Propofol + fentanyl or hydromorphone boluses
	 * Ketamine (1-2 mg/kg/hr) for hemodynamic instability
   * Challenges:
	 * Reliable IV access essential
	 * Limited target-controlled infusion models for young children
	 * Higher weight-based propofol doses in young children
	 * Concerns about Prise with prolonged infusions
* **Balanced techniques:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Combination of inhalational agent and IV medications
   * Reduces MAC requirements
   * Benefits:
	 * Lower concentrations of each agent may reduce side effects
	 * Improved hemodynamic stability
	 * Better postoperative analgesia
	 * Potentially reduced volatile agent pollution
   * Common combinations:
	 * Low-dose volatile agent + intermittent opioid boluses
	 * Low-dose volatile agent + dexmedetomidine infusion
	 * Low-dose volatile agent + ketamine boluses
	 * Volatile agent + regional anesthesia

### 4.3.3. FLUID MANAGEMENT

* **Maintenance fluids:** `[B:58-59]`
   * Holliday-Segar formula:
	 * 4 mL/kg/hr for first 10 kg
	 * 2 mL/kg/hr for next 10 kg
	 * 1 mL/kg/hr for each additional kg
   * Simplified calculation:
	 * 0-10 kg: 100 mL/kg/day (4 mL/kg/hr)
	 * 10-20 kg: 1000 mL + 50 mL/kg/day for each kg >10 kg
	 * > 20 kg: 1500 mL + 20 mL/kg/day for each kg >20 kg
   * Fluid composition:
	 * Isotonic solutions (normal saline, Plasma-Lyte, lactated Ringer's) now preferred
	 * Hypotonic solutions should be avoided due to risk of hyponatremia
	 * Dextrose considerations:
	   * Infants <6 months: consider D5 or D2.5 solutions
	   * Consider dextrose for prolonged procedures
	   * Monitor blood glucose in at-risk patients
* **Deficit replacement:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Calculation: hourly maintenance × hours NPO
   * Traditional replacement schedule:
	 * 50% in first hour
	 * 25% in second hour
	 * 25% in third hour
   * Modern approach more individualized based on clinical assessment
   * Signs of significant deficit:
	 * Tachycardia
	 * Hypotension
	 * Decreased urine output
	 * Poor skin turgor
	 * Dry mucous membranes
* **Third-space losses:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Varies by surgical procedure:
	 * Minimal (0-2 mL/kg/hr): superficial procedures
	 * Moderate (2-4 mL/kg/hr): thoracotomy, intermediate abdominal surgery
	 * Severe (4-8 mL/kg/hr): major abdominal surgery, major trauma
   * Replace with isotonic crystalloid or colloid
   * Goal-directed approach based on clinical signs and monitoring
* **Blood loss replacement:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Crystalloid replacement: 3:1 ratio to blood loss
   * Colloid replacement: 1:1 ratio to blood loss
   * Blood transfusion triggers:
	 * Hemodynamic instability despite crystalloid/colloid
	 * Acute blood loss >10-15% of estimated blood volume
	 * Hemoglobin <7-8 g/dL in healthy children
	 * Higher triggers in children with cardiac or respiratory disease
   * Guidelines for packed red blood cells:
	 * 10-15 mL/kg will raise hemoglobin by approximately 2-3 g/dL
	 * Consider blood warmers for large volume transfusions
	 * Monitor calcium with multiple units of citrated blood products

### 4.3.4. BLOOD TRANSFUSION THERAPY

* **Estimated blood volume:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Premature neonates: 90-100 mL/kg
   * Term neonates: 80-90 mL/kg
   * Infants: 75-80 mL/kg
   * Children: 70-75 mL/kg
   * Adolescents: 65-70 mL/kg
* **Blood component therapy:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * **Packed red blood cells:**
	 * Dose: 10-15 mL/kg
	 * Indications:
	   * Symptomatic anemia
	   * Acute blood loss with hemodynamic compromise
	   * Hemoglobin <7 g/dL in most children
	   * Higher thresholds for cardiac, respiratory disease
   * **Fresh frozen plasma:**
	 * Dose: 10-15 mL/kg
	 * Indications:
	   * Active bleeding with coagulopathy
	   * Correction of multiple factor deficiencies
	   * Warfarin reversal
	   * Not for volume expansion
   * **Platelets:**
	 * Dose: 5-10 mL/kg or 1 unit/10 kg
	 * Indications:
	   * <50,000/μL with active bleeding
	   * <20,000/μL prophylactically
	   * Higher thresholds for neurosurgery
   * **Cryoprecipitate:**
	 * Dose: 1 unit/5-10 kg
	 * Indications:
	   * Fibrinogen <100 mg/dL
	   * Von Willebrand disease or hemophilia A
	   * Factor XIII deficiency
* **Massive transfusion:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Definition: Replacement of >50% of blood volume in 3 hours or >100% in 24 hours
   * Protocol considerations:
	 * Fixed ratio of PRBC:FFP:platelets (often 1:1:1)
	 * Calcium chloride/gluconate for citrate toxicity
	 * Temperature management
	 * Point-of-care coagulation testing if available
	 * Antifibrinolytic therapy (tranexamic acid)

### 4.3.5. PROPHYLAXIS FOR POSTOPERATIVE VOMITING

* **Risk factors:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Age >3 years (peak at 6-10 years)
   * Personal or family history of PONV/motion sickness
   * Emetogenic surgeries (strabismus, tonsillectomy, otoplasty)
   * Postoperative opioid use
   * Prolonged surgery
   * Female gender (postpubertal)
* **Prevention strategies:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Multimodal approach based on risk:
	 * Low risk (0-1 factors): Consider single-agent prophylaxis
	 * Moderate risk (2 factors): Two-agent prophylaxis
	 * High risk (≥3 factors): Three-agent prophylaxis plus TIVA
   * Common agents:
	 * Dexamethasone: 0.15 mg/kg (max 4 mg)
	 * Ondansetron: 0.1 mg/kg (max 4 mg)
	 * Propofol induction and/or TIVA
	 * Avoidance of nitrous oxide
	 * Adequate hydration
	 * Minimizing opioids
	 * Midazolam premedication

**Bottom Line:** Maintenance of anesthesia in pediatric patients can be accomplished through inhalational techniques, TIVA, or balanced approaches, with selection based on patient and procedural factors. Fluid management requires understanding of age-specific requirements, careful deficit calculation, and assessment of ongoing losses. Blood component therapy should be guided by physiologic triggers rather than arbitrary thresholds, and multimodal PONV prophylaxis should be tailored to the patient's risk profile.

## 4.4. REGIONAL ANESTHESIA


**Exam Weighting:** Medium

### 4.4.2. GENERAL CONSIDERATIONS

* **Benefits of regional anesthesia:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Reduced general anesthetic requirements
   * Excellent postoperative analgesia
   * Opioid-sparing or opioid-free anesthesia
   * Reduced stress response
   * Earlier mobilization
   * Potentially decreased incidence of chronic pain
   * May reduce risk in high-risk populations (OSA, pulmonary disease)
* **Medical decision making:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Regional as primary anesthetic vs. supplement to general anesthesia
   * Consideration of:
	 * Patient factors (age, anxiety, ability to cooperate)
	 * Surgical procedure and duration
	 * Comorbidities
	 * Coagulation status
	 * Expected pain intensity
	 * Outpatient vs. inpatient setting
   * Discussion with family about risks/benefits
   * Most commonly performed after induction of general anesthesia in children
* **Safety and complications:** `[B:61-62]`
   * Large-scale studies show excellent safety profile in children
   * Pediatric Regional Anesthesia Network (PRAN) data:
	 * > 100,000 blocks with no permanent neurologic complications
	 * Incidence of temporary neurologic symptoms <0.1%
	 * Local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST) <0.1%
   * Risk-reduction strategies:
	 * Ultrasound guidance
	 * Appropriate dosing
	 * Test dose for large-volume blocks
	 * Meticulous attention to sterile technique
	 * Availability of lipid emulsion for LAST treatment

### 4.4.3. PHARMACOLOGY AND DOSING

* **Local anesthetic selection:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * **Bupivacaine:**
	 * Most commonly used
	 * Typical concentrations: 0.125-0.25% for surgical blocks
	 * Maximum single dose: 2-2.5 mg/kg
	 * For continuous infusions: 0.1-0.125%, 0.2 mg/kg/hr
   * **Ropivacaine:**
	 * Less cardiotoxic than bupivacaine
	 * Potentially less motor block
	 * Typical concentrations: 0.2% for surgical blocks
	 * Maximum single dose: 2-3 mg/kg
	 * For continuous infusions: 0.2%, 0.2 mg/kg/hr
   * **Chloroprocaine:**
	 * Ultra-short acting ester local anesthetic
	 * Rapid metabolism by plasma esterases
	 * Useful for short procedures or test doses
	 * 3% concentration standard
* **Adjuvants:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * **Clonidine:**
	 * Prolongs block duration by 30-50%
	 * Dose: 1-2 mcg/kg
	 * Side effects: sedation, hypotension, bradycardia
   * **Dexmedetomidine:**
	 * Similar to clonidine but more potent
	 * Dose: 0.5-1 mcg/kg
	 * Prolongs sensory and motor block
   * **Epinephrine:**
	 * Vasoconstrictor to reduce systemic absorption
	 * Test dose marker (heart rate increase with intravascular injection)
	 * 1:200,000 to 1:400,000 concentration (2.5-5 mcg/mL)
   * **Narcotics:**
	 * Limited evidence for peripheral benefit
	 * Used in neuraxial techniques (preservative-free)
	 * Morphine: 25-50 mcg/kg (caudal/epidural)
	 * Hydromorphone: 6-10 mcg/kg (caudal/epidural)
	 * Risk of delayed respiratory depression with neuraxial opioids

### 4.4.4. REGIONAL TECHNIQUES—NEURAXIAL

* **Caudal block:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Most common pediatric regional technique
   * Technically easier than lumbar epidural in children
   * Anatomic landmarks:
	 * Sacral hiatus between sacral cornua
	 * Sacrococcygeal membrane
   * Procedure:
	 * Lateral or prone position
	 * Needle insertion perpendicular to skin initially, then redirect 20-30° cephalad
	 * Loss of resistance on penetration of sacrococcygeal membrane
	 * Aspiration test and test dose
	 * Single-shot or catheter technique
   * Dosing:
	 * 0.5-1 mL/kg for sacral dermatomes
	 * 1-1.25 mL/kg for lower thoracic spread
	 * Maximum single dose: 20-25 mL
   * Complications:
	 * Intravascular injection
	 * Dural puncture
	 * Block failure (5-10%)
	 * Infection (rare with single-shot)
* **Lumbar/thoracic epidural:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Indications:
	 * Major abdominal, thoracic, or lower extremity surgery
	 * Procedures above T10 dermatome
	 * When caudal approach inadequate
   * Technique:
	 * Same as adult epidural with pediatric equipment
	 * Loss of resistance technique (saline preferred to air)
	 * Caution with distance from skin to epidural space (may be only 1-2 cm in infants)
	 * Catheter insertion 2-3 cm into epidural space
   * Dosing:
	 * Bolus: 0.3-0.5 mL/kg (less than caudal due to smaller epidural space)
	 * Infusion: 0.2 mg/kg/hr bupivacaine or equivalent
   * Epidurography to confirm catheter position may be useful
* **Spinal anesthesia:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Indications:
	 * Alternative to general anesthesia in high-risk infants
	 * Lower abdominal or lower extremity procedures of short duration
	 * Combined with general anesthesia for postoperative analgesia
   * Technique:
	 * Sitting or lateral position
	 * Midline approach at L3-L5 interspace
	 * Immediate CSF return expected (unlike adults)
	 * Rapid injection due to short duration of cooperation
   * Dosing:
	 * Bupivacaine 0.5%: 0.5-1 mg/kg
	 * Tetracaine 0.5%: 0.4-0.8 mg/kg
   * Limitations:
	 * Short duration (60-90 minutes)
	 * High failure rate in some series (up to 20%)
	 * Limited utility in procedures above umbilicus

### 4.4.5. REGIONAL TECHNIQUES—PERIPHERAL

* **Upper extremity blocks:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * **Brachial plexus approaches:**
	 * Ultrasound-guided interscalene:
	   * Suitable for shoulder and proximal humerus procedures
	   * Risk of phrenic nerve block (contraindicated in respiratory compromise)
	 * Supraclavicular:
	   * Provides anesthesia to entire upper extremity except shoulder
	   * Good option for elbow and forearm procedures
	   * Risk of pneumothorax lower with ultrasound guidance
	 * Infraclavicular:
	   * Good for procedures below mid-humerus
	   * Lower risk of phrenic nerve involvement
	 * Axillary:
	   * Easiest approach in children
	   * Suitable for elbow, forearm, and hand procedures
	   * Lower risk of complications
   * Dosing:
	 * 0.3-0.5 mL/kg of local anesthetic per block site
	 * Total dose must stay within maximum limits
* **Lower extremity blocks:** `[B:64-65]`
   * **Femoral nerve block:**
	 * Provides analgesia to anterior thigh and knee
	 * Useful for femur fractures, knee surgery
	 * May cause quadriceps weakness and falls
   * **Fascia iliaca block:**
	 * Similar coverage to femoral block but higher success blocking lateral femoral cutaneous nerve
	 * Easier to perform in children
	 * Requires larger volume (0.5-0.7 mL/kg)
   * **Sciatic nerve block:**
	 * Multiple approaches (subgluteal, popliteal)
	 * Provides analgesia to posterior thigh and majority of lower leg/foot
	 * Combined with femoral or saphenous block for complete lower extremity coverage
   * **Popliteal block:**
	 * Excellent for foot and ankle procedures
	 * Preserves proximal sciatic function
* **Truncal blocks:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * **Transversus abdominis plane (TAP) block:**
	 * Provides somatic analgesia to anterolateral abdominal wall
	 * Useful for inguinal hernia repair, appendectomy, laparoscopic procedures
	 * Does not cover visceral pain
	 * Dose: 0.3-0.5 mL/kg per side
   * **Rectus sheath block:**
	 * Analgesia for midline incisions
	 * Commonly used for umbilical hernia repairs
	 * Dose: 0.1-0.2 mL/kg per side
   * **Ilioinguinal/iliohypogastric block:**
	 * Traditional technique for inguinal hernia repair
	 * Risk of femoral nerve block with blind technique
	 * Ultrasound guidance significantly improves safety
	 * Dose: 0.1-0.2 mL/kg
   * **Paravertebral block:**
	 * Alternative to epidural analgesia
	 * Useful for thoracic and upper abdominal procedures
	 * Lower risk of hypotension than epidural
	 * Dose: 0.3-0.5 mL/kg per level
* **Catheter techniques:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Indications:
	 * Procedures with expected moderate-severe pain >12-24 hours
	 * Major orthopedic, thoracic, or abdominal surgery
   * Considerations:
	 * Requires institutional support for follow-up
	 * Patient/family education
	 * Infusion management
   * Typical regimens:
	 * 0.1-0.125% bupivacaine or 0.1-0.2% ropivacaine
	 * Rates: 0.1-0.3 mL/kg/hr (single catheter)
	 * Divided rates for bilateral catheters

**Bottom Line:** Regional anesthesia techniques provide excellent analgesia and can significantly reduce general anesthetic and opioid requirements in pediatric patients. With appropriate training, equipment, and patient selection, they have an excellent safety profile. Ultrasound guidance has improved success rates and safety for most blocks. Understanding age-appropriate dosing, anatomical considerations, and technique selection is essential for integrating regional anesthesia into a comprehensive perioperative plan.

## 4.5. EMERGENCE AND RECOVERY


**Exam Weighting:** High

### 4.5.2. EMERGENCE FROM ANESTHESIA

* **Tracheal extubation:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * **Timing options:**
	 * **Awake extubation:**
	   * Patient responding to commands or making purposeful movements
	   * Return of protective airway reflexes
	   * Regular respiratory pattern
	   * Adequate tidal volume and respiratory rate
	   * Preferred for patients with full stomach, difficult airway, or high aspiration risk
	 * **Deep extubation:**
	   * Removal of tube while still in surgical plane of anesthesia
	   * Advantages: smoother emergence, less coughing, reduced stridor risk
	   * Best candidates: easy airway, minimal secretions, non-reactive airway
	   * Contraindications: full stomach, difficult airway, reactive airway disease, obstructive sleep apnea
   
   * **Technique considerations:** `[B:66-67]`
	 * Complete reversal of neuromuscular blockade
	 * Adequate analgesia
	 * Administration of anti-emetics if indicated
	 * Consider deep extubation in children at risk for croup
	 * Positioning (lateral vs. supine)
	 * Suctioning of oropharynx
	 * 100% oxygen administration
* **LMA removal:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * **Options:**
	 * Deep removal: less airway stimulation, lower risk of laryngospasm
	 * Awake removal: better airway protection but more stimulating
   * Most practitioners favor deep removal when appropriate
   * Same contraindications as deep extubation
   * Ensure pharynx is suctioned before removal
* **Delayed emergence:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * **Causes:**
	 * Residual anesthetic effects
	 * Residual neuromuscular blockade
	 * Hypothermia
	 * Hypoglycemia
	 * Electrolyte disturbances
	 * Central anticholinergic syndrome
	 * Neurological injury
   * **Assessment:**
	 * Confirm adequate reversal of neuromuscular blockade
	 * Check temperature, glucose, electrolytes
	 * Consider naloxone if opioid effect suspected
	 * Consider flumazenil if benzodiazepine effect suspected
	 * Neurodiagnostic imaging if persistent without other explanation

### 4.5.3. TRANSPORT TO PACU

* **Preparation for transport:** `[B:67-68]`
   * Ensure adequate oxygenation
   * Confirm stable vital signs
   * Appropriate monitoring during transport
   * Supplemental oxygen source
   * Positioning to maintain airway patency
   * Emergency equipment readily available
* **Monitoring during transport:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Continuous pulse oximetry
   * Visual monitoring of respiratory effort
   * Consider ECG monitoring for high-risk patients
   * Portable oxygen source and delivery system
* **PACU handoff:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * Structured, comprehensive communication
   * Patient demographics and procedure
   * Anesthetic technique and airway management
   * Intraoperative medications, especially analgesics and antiemetics
   * Fluid balance and blood loss
   * Special considerations or concerns
   * Pain management plan
   * Expected postoperative course

### 4.5.4. PACU COMPLICATIONS

* **Respiratory complications:** `[B:68-69]`
   * **Laryngospasm:**
	 * Sudden, complete glottic closure
	 * Characterized by high-pitched stridor or silent chest with respiratory effort
	 * Management:
	   * 100% oxygen with positive pressure ventilation
	   * Jaw thrust
	   * Consider propofol 0.5-1 mg/kg IV
	   * Succinylcholine 0.1-0.5 mg/kg IV if persistent
	   * Avoid stimulation during light planes of anesthesia
   
   * **Postoperative apnea:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
	 * Highest risk in:
	   * Ex-premature infants <60 weeks post-conceptual age
	   * Full-term infants <44 weeks post-conceptual age
	   * History of apnea of prematurity
	   * Anemia (Hgb <10 g/dL)
	   * Neurological disorders
	 * Prevention/management:
	   * Continuous monitoring for at least 12-24 hours post-anesthesia
	   * Consider caffeine citrate prophylaxis
	   * Minimal opioid use
	   * Appropriate NICU/PICU disposition
   
   * **Postoperative stridor:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
	 * Risk factors:
	   * Young age (<3 years)
	   * Long duration of intubation
	   * Multiple intubation attempts
	   * Coughing on endotracheal tube
	   * History of recent URI
	 * Management:
	   * Humidified oxygen
	   * Head elevation
	   * Nebulized racemic epinephrine (0.05 mL/kg of 2.25% solution, max 0.5 mL)
	   * Consider dexamethasone (0.5-0.6 mg/kg, max 10 mg)
   * **Negative pressure pulmonary edema:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
	 * Usually follows relief of severe upper airway obstruction (post-laryngospasm)
	 * Pink, frothy secretions from airways
	 * Decreased oxygen saturation, bilateral rales
	 * Management:
	   * Supplemental oxygen, possibly CPAP
	   * Mild diuresis
	   * Usually resolves within 24-48 hours
	   * May require reintubation in severe cases
* **Emergence delirium:** `[B:69-70]`
   * **Characteristics:**
	 * Agitation, restlessness, inconsolability
	 * Lack of awareness of surroundings
	 * Disorientation, thrashing movements
	 * Peak incidence: 3-7 years of age
	 * Most common with sevoflurane
   
   * **Risk factors:**
	 * Preschool age
	 * Preoperative anxiety
	 * Pain
	 * Rapid emergence
	 * ENT procedures
	 * Previous history of emergence delirium
   
   * **Prevention strategies:**
	 * Preoperative anxiety reduction
	 * Parental presence during induction and/or recovery
	 * Propofol at end of case (1 mg/kg)
	 * Dexmedetomidine (0.3-0.5 mcg/kg) before emergence
	 * Adequate pain control
	 * Fentanyl (1-2 mcg/kg) near end of case
   
   * **Management:**
	 * Exclude pain, hypoxemia, bladder distension
	 * Provide calm, quiet environment
	 * Consider small doses of:
	   * Dexmedetomidine (0.3-0.5 mcg/kg)
	   * Propofol (0.5-1 mg/kg)
	   * Fentanyl (0.5-1 mcg/kg) if pain may be contributing
* **Postoperative nausea and vomiting:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * **Treatment options:**
	 * Ondansetron 0.1 mg/kg (max 4 mg) if not given intraoperatively
	 * Dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg (max 4 mg) if not given intraoperatively
	 * Metoclopramide 0.1 mg/kg
	 * Diphenhydramine 0.5 mg/kg
	 * Propofol 0.5 mg/kg (rescue dosing)
   
   * **Supportive measures:**
	 * IV hydration
	 * Delay oral intake until nausea subsides
	 * Avoid movement during peak nausea
	 * Consider underlying causes (opioids, pain, hypotension)
* **Pain:** `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S4 Paediatric Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]`
   * **Assessment tools:**
	 * FLACC scale (Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability) for preverbal children
	 * Wong-Baker FACES or numerical scale for verbal children
	 * Behavioral observation in nonverbal/cognitively impaired children
   
   * **Management approach:**
	 * Multimodal techniques
	 * Regional anesthesia when appropriate
	 * Scheduled acetaminophen (15 mg/kg)
	 * NSAIDs if not contraindicated (ketorolac 0.5 mg/kg, ibuprofen 10 mg/kg)
	 * Opioids for moderate-severe pain (morphine 0.05-0.1 mg/kg)
	 * Non-pharmacologic techniques (distraction, positioning, parental presence)
	 * Consider adjuvants (dexmedetomidine, ketamine) for opioid-sparing effect

**Bottom Line:** Emergence and recovery represent critical transition periods in pediatric anesthesia. Tailoring the extubation strategy to the individual patient, maintaining appropriate monitoring during transport, and anticipating common PACU complications are essential for safety. A systematic approach to respiratory events, emergence delirium, PONV, and pain management improves outcomes and patient satisfaction.

  * Consider preoperative thyroid function tests (TFTs)

#### 5.1.8.2. HEMATOLOGICAL CONSIDERATIONS [INTEGRATION]

* **Associated with acute lymphoblastic leukemia and acute myeloid leukemia (1.5%)** `[T21:13]`
  * Consider workup if abnormal cell counts noted preoperatively or red flag symptoms present

#### 5.1.8.3. MUSCULOSKELETAL SYSTEM [INTEGRATION]

* **Ligamentous laxity predisposes to joint dislocation** `[T21:13]`
  * Careful handling of patient and positioning perioperatively required

#### 5.1.8.4. SENSORY SYSTEMS [INTEGRATION]

* **Ocular and otorhinolaryngologic disorders leading to impaired vision and hearing** `[T21:13]`

#### 5.1.8.5. IMMUNE SYSTEM [INTEGRATION]

* **Impaired cellular immunity** `[T21:13]`
  * Risk of infectious disease (especially pneumonia) 12× that of the general population

#### 5.1.8.6. OTHER CONSIDERATIONS [INTEGRATION]

* **Obesity** `[T21:13]`
* **Difficult peripheral venous access** `[T21:13]`
* **Dental issues** `[T21:13]`
* **Informed consent: assess capacity and involve legal guardians if needed** `[T21:13]`

### 5.1.9. PREOPERATIVE EVALUATION [INTEGRATION]

**Comprehensive assessment requirements:** `[T21:14]`

* **Thorough history and physical examination, focus on airway, cardiac, and neurological systems** `[T21:14]`
* **Baseline oxygen saturation and sleep history** `[T21:14]`
* **Echocardiography if congenital heart disease suspected or unclear** `[T21:14]`
* **Cervical spine assessment if symptoms suggest instability** `[T21:14]`

* **Multidisciplinary involvement and optimization of patient for surgery** `[T21:14]`

### 5.1.10. INTRAOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT [INTEGRATION]

**Key management strategies:** `[T21:15]`

* **Consider premedication** `[T21:15]`
* **Consider inhalational induction especially given potential difficult venous access and patient cooperation with IV placement** `[T21:15]`
* **Choose appropriate-sized airway devices and standby advanced airway equipment for anticipated difficult airway:**
  * Airway adjuncts `[T21:15]`
  * Video laryngoscopy, fiberoptic bronchoscopy `[T21:15]`
  * Standby 1-2 sizes smaller endotracheal tubes `[T21:15]`
  * LMA if no aspiration risk and procedure allows `[T21:15]`
* **Gentle handling of neck and positioning of patient** `[T21:15]`
* **Maintain normothermia and normovolemia** `[T21:15]`
* **Careful fluid management and drug titration** `[T21:15]`
* **Opioid-sparing analgesia if possible, including regional anesthesia** `[T21:15]`
* **Extubate fully awake with full paralysis reversal** `[T21:15]`

### 5.1.11. POSTOPERATIVE CARE [INTEGRATION]

#### 5.1.11.1. EXTENDED MONITORING [INTEGRATION]

**Extended monitoring in PACU required:** `[T21:16]`

* **⚠️ Beware of airway obstruction, apnea, and hypoventilation** `[T21:16]`
* **⚠️ Beware of suboptimal pain management** (increased risk of inadequate pain control in this group of patients) `[T21:16]`

#### 5.1.11.2. POSTOPERATIVE DISPOSITION [INTEGRATION]

* **HDU/ICU as postoperative disposition depending on severity of patient's comorbidities and nature of surgery** `[T21:16]`

### 5.1.12. SUMMARY AND KEY POINTS [INTEGRATION]

**Essential principles for managing Trisomy 21 patients:** `[T21:17]`

* **Trisomy 21 affects multiple organ systems relevant to anesthesia** `[T21:17]`
* **Anticipate and plan for potential challenges especially airway, respiratory, cardiac, gastrointestinal, and neurological** `[T21:17]`
* **Preoperative assessment must be thorough** `[T21:17]`
* **Tailored anesthetic technique required** `[T21:17]`
* **Vigilant perioperative monitoring essential** `[T21:17]`

## 5.2. PREMATURE NEONATE ANESTHETIC CONSIDERATIONS [INTEGRATION]


### 5.2.1. DEFINITIONS AND TERMINOLOGY [INTEGRATION]

Refer to enhanced terminology in Section 1.1.2 for comprehensive age definitions critical to premature neonate management.

**Exam Weighting:** High

### 5.2.2. NEONATAL AIRWAY CONSIDERATIONS [INTEGRATION]

#### 5.2.2.1. ANATOMICAL CHALLENGES [INTEGRATION]

**Anatomically difficult features:** `[PREM:4]`

* **Difficult bag-mask ventilation:**
  * **Bigger occiput** (neck is in flexion, might need shoulder roll to "open neck up"/C donut) `[PREM:4]`
  * **Prone to upper airway obstruction** – large tongue & pliable neck tissue (early use of oral airway, gentle bag-mask ventilation, finger at bony prominence, decompress stomach) `[PREM:4]`

* **Difficult laryngoscopy:**
  * **Cephalad angled vocal cords** (anterior cords, increased traumatic intubation) `[PREM:4]`
  * **Floppy epiglottis** (straight blade preferred but caution with vagal response) `[PREM:4]`

* **Difficult intubation:**
  * **Funnel-shaped larynx with narrow cricoid and subglottic area** (risk of subglottic trauma, uncuffed tube/microcuff tube, permissive leak, minimize cuff pressure) `[PREM:4]`
  * **Smaller airway** (DAS Pediatrics guidelines – limit number of attempts; multiple ETT sizes, small ETT easily blocked/bent/kinked) `[PREM:4]`
  * **Short trachea 3-4 cm** (compared to adult 10-12 cm) (risk of endobronchial intubation) `[PREM:4]`

**Physiologically difficult (rapid desaturation):** `[PREM:4]`
* **Increased oxygen consumption 2× adults** (6-10 mL/kg/min compared to 3 mL/kg/min) `[PREM:4]`
* **Closing capacity > FRC due to stiff lungs** `[PREM:4]`
* **Shunt due to PDA which closes functionally day 3, anatomically day 21** (decreased oxygen reserves) `[PREM:4]`

#### 5.2.2.2. PREMATURE AIRWAY COMPLICATIONS [INTEGRATION]

**Early complications of prematurity:** `[PREM:5]`

* **Even more prone to obstruction:**
  * Compliant soft tissues `[PREM:5]`
  * Poorly coordinated pharyngeal muscle groups `[PREM:5]`
  * Congenital or acquired laryngo- and tracheomalacia, tracheal stenosis or subglottic stenosis `[PREM:5]`

**Late complications of prematurity:** `[PREM:5]`

* **Prolonged intubation complications**
* **Acquired tracheal/subglottic stenosis** (requiring downsizing of ETT) `[PREM:5]`

#### 5.2.2.3. AIRWAY EQUIPMENT IN NEONATES [INTEGRATION]

**ANZCOR Guidelines for equipment selection:** `[PREM:6]`

**1. Laryngoscope Blades:**
* **Straight blade preferred:** size 1 (10 cm) for term infants and larger preterm infants, size 0 (7.5 cm) for preterm infants <32 weeks' gestation, or size 00 (6 cm) for extremely low birth weight infants `[PREM:6]`

**2. ETT Sizing:**
* **Age-based:** GA (weeks) / 10 (e.g., 25 weeks = 2.5 mm ID) `[PREM:6]`
* **Weight-based:** `[PREM:6]`
  * <1 kg = 2.5 mm
  * 1-2 kg = 3.0 mm
  * 2-3 kg = 3.5 mm
  * ≥3 kg = 3.5 mm or larger

**3. Depth of ETT:**
* **Oral intubation:** Weight (kg) + 6 cm (to middle of upper lip) `[PREM:6]`
* **Nasal intubation:** Genoa Formula: 2 × weight (kg) + 5.5 cm `[PREM:6]`

#### 5.2.2.4. PEDIATRIC ENDOTRACHEAL TUBES [INTEGRATION]

**Cuffed vs. Uncuffed debate:** `[PREM:7]`

**Benefits of cuffed tubes:** `[PREM:7]`
* Better lung recruitment
* Lower fresh gas flow requirements
* Reduced aspiration risk
* More accurate monitoring

**Microcuff tube differences:** `[PREM:7]`
* **High volume, low pressure cuff** - ideally use lower cuff pressure <15 cmH2O to form complete seal `[PREM:7]`
* **Polyurethane construction** compared to PVC of normal ETT - less irritative material, thinner walled ETT (external diameter smaller) `[PREM:7]`
* **Very thin cuff (10 μm)** - no folds between the cuff and the tracheal wall `[PREM:7]`
* **Short cuff** - when inflated, remains below vocal cords & doesn't herniate to ETT opening `[PREM:7]`
* **Distally placed cuff** (at expense of no Murphy eye) - can be inflated & not abut vocal cords, avoiding injury to subglottic area `[PREM:7]`
* **Black line & depth marking for accurate placement** `[PREM:7]`

### 5.2.3. NEONATAL RESPIRATORY SYSTEM [INTEGRATION]

#### 5.2.3.1. BASIC RESPIRATORY PHYSIOLOGY [INTEGRATION]

**Key physiological differences:** `[PREM:8]`

1. **Immature control of respiration with risk of apnea** - prone to apneas (~45% at PCA<48 weeks, 1% at PCA >60 weeks; defer elective surgery until PCA>60 weeks or provide close postoperative monitoring) `[PREM:8]`
   * **Biphasic response to hypoxia** – initial increased respiratory rate followed by apnea + bradycardia `[PREM:8]`
   * **Worsened by:** hypoxia, hypoglycemia, hypo-/hyperthermia, anemia, sedative drugs, neurohormonal response to surgery, and postoperative pain `[PREM:8]`

2. **Inefficient lung mechanics** - minute ventilation dependent on respiratory rate and diaphragmatic excursion; abdominal splinting decreases tidal volume significantly `[PREM:8]`
   * **Ribs aligned horizontally** - bucket handle action not possible, difficult to increase vital capacity `[PREM:8]`
   * **Diaphragm less dome-shaped** `[PREM:8]`

3. **Increased dead space but absolute tidal volume lower** (risk of alveolar hypoventilation, reduce dead space with pediatric circuits) `[PREM:8]`
   * **Anatomical dead space increased** (3.0 mL/kg compared to 2.2 mL/kg) `[PREM:8]`

4. **Terminal bronchioles open into single alveoli** (risk of pneumothorax is high) `[PREM:8]`
   * **Alveolar system matures only at 8 years** `[PREM:8]`

#### 5.2.3.2. PREMATURE RESPIRATORY COMPLICATIONS [INTEGRATION]

**Early complications of prematurity:** `[PREM:9]`

1. **Respiratory Distress Syndrome** (decreased lung compliance, increased lung collapse) `[PREM:9]`
   * **Inadequate surfactant at GA <34 weeks** `[PREM:9]`
   * **Management:** CPAP/intubation/HFOV, surfactant `[PREM:9]`

2. **Pulmonary hypertension** (increased PVR may precipitate return to transitional circulation & shunting) `[PREM:9]`

3. **Immature structural lung development** (prone to VILI, permissive hypercapnia if PIP >14 cmH2O) `[PREM:9]`
   * **Reduced lung volumes compared to term infants** `[PREM:9]`

4. **High risk of postoperative apneas** - multifactorial: immature respiratory centers, obstructive apnea, apnea of prematurity `[PREM:9]`

5. **Increased risk of oxygen toxicity** (discuss oxygen targets, use PEEP to decrease oxygen requirements) `[PREM:9]`
   * **Hyperoxia complications:** retinopathy of prematurity (ROP), ciliary dysfunction, development of bronchopulmonary dysplasia `[PREM:9]`

**⚠️ Special consideration:** If a patient is particularly small and/or requires special ventilation, consider transferring and operating on NICU ventilator `[PREM:9]`

**Late complications of prematurity:** `[PREM:9]`

1. **Bronchopulmonary Dysplasia** (increased airway reactivity, increased secretions, increased obstruction, chronic hypoxic/hypercarbic respiratory failure, pulmonary hypertension) `[PREM:9]`

**Diagnosis:** 28 days of >21% oxygen or continued need for supplemental oxygen at ≥36 weeks PCA `[PREM:9]`

**Treatment:** `[PREM:9]`
   * Diuretics → monitor fluid/electrolyte status
   * Steroids → provide stress dose steroids
   * Bronchodilators → continue perioperatively
   * Long-term oxygen/ventilation → plan extubation strategy

#### 5.2.3.3. APNEA MANAGEMENT [INTEGRATION]

**Definition:** Pause >20 seconds/loss of effective breathing + bradycardia `[PREM:10]`

**Triggers:** `[PREM:10]`
* Hypoxia/sepsis/intraventricular hemorrhage
* Heart failure/prostaglandins
* Opioids/anesthetic agents

**Predisposing factors:** `[PREM:10]`
* Anemia/neurological disease/lung pathology

**Treatment:** `[PREM:10]`
* Stimulation, positive pressure ventilation
* Removal of triggers
* Caffeine (for apnea of prematurity)

### 5.2.4. PRETERM NEONATE – GENERAL VS REGIONAL ANESTHESIA [INTEGRATION]

#### 5.2.4.1. EVIDENCE BASE [INTEGRATION]

**Meta-analysis findings:** `[PREM:11]`

* **No statistically significant difference** in risk of postoperative apnea/bradycardia/postoperative desaturations/use of postoperative analgesics/postoperative respiratory support `[PREM:11]`
* **BUT when infants receiving preoperative sedatives were excluded,** meta-analysis supported reduction in postoperative apnea in the spinal anesthesia group `[PREM:11]`
* **Infants receiving spinal anesthesia had increased risk of anesthetic placement failure** (borderline statistical significance) `[PREM:11]`
* **Infants with no history of apnea in preoperative period had reduced risk of postoperative apnea** `[PREM:11]`

**Conclusion:** SOME evidence to suggest spinal anesthesia without addition of sedative drugs to assist in keeping baby still/provide pain relief may be safer than general anesthesia `[PREM:11]`

#### 5.2.4.2. CONSIDERATIONS FOR REGIONAL ANESTHESIA [INTEGRATION]

**Benefits of regional anesthesia:** `[PREM:11]`
* Avoid airway management & associated trauma
* Reduce neurodevelopmental insult by avoiding volatile agents
* Provide perioperative analgesia

**Limitations:** `[PREM:11]`
* Difficult to assess success of block & complications
* Complication risk – increased hematoma risk especially if coagulopathic, infection risk in immunocompromised
* Doesn't 100% prevent apneas – may need to rescue airway under drapes

### 5.2.5. NEONATAL CARDIOVASCULAR SYSTEM [INTEGRATION]

#### 5.2.5.1. PHYSIOLOGICAL CHARACTERISTICS [INTEGRATION]

**Key cardiovascular features:** `[PREM:12]`

1. **Stiff ventricles** (heart rate & rhythm dependent, limited tolerance to fluid loading, reduced contractile capacity) `[PREM:12]`

2. **Immature sympathetic nervous system** (increased vagal response e.g., to laryngoscopy/hypoxia) `[PREM:12]`
   * **SNS matures at 4-6 months** `[PREM:12]`

3. **Immature baroreceptors** (decreased tachycardic response to hypovolemia) `[PREM:12]`

4. **Transitional circulation** (maintain PVR:SVR) `[PREM:12]`
   * **PDA:** closes by day 3 (functional), day 21 (anatomical) → reopens in hypoxia, acidosis, hypercarbia `[PREM:12]`

#### 5.2.5.2. PREMATURE CARDIOVASCULAR COMPLICATIONS [INTEGRATION]

**Early complications of prematurity:** `[PREM:13]`

1. **Association with congenital heart defects** (hemodynamic goals) `[PREM:13]`
   * **Preterm infants 2.4× more likely to have cardiovascular defects** `[PREM:13]`

2. **Persistent PDA** `[PREM:13]`
   * **Meticulous de-airing** (paradoxical air embolism) `[PREM:13]`
   * **Maintain PVR:SVR:**
     * Increased PVR: R>L shunt → systemic hypoxia `[PREM:13]`
     * Increased SVR: L>R shunt → acute pulmonary edema, congestive cardiac failure, systemic steal (NEC, acute kidney injury, intraventricular hemorrhage) `[PREM:13]`
   * **Cautious fluid balance:**
     * Hypervolemia = acute pulmonary edema `[PREM:13]`
     * Hypovolemia = distal ischemia `[PREM:13]`

3. **Immature sarcoplasmic reticulum** (replace calcium) `[PREM:13]`
   * **Poor calcium reserves,** reliant on extracellular calcium rather than intracellular calcium for myocardial function `[PREM:13]`

4. **Blood pressure & MAP targets still debatable** (consider NIRS monitoring) `[PREM:13]`
   * **PALS:** systolic BP >60 mmHg `[PREM:13]`
   * **Association of Pediatric Anesthesia:** systolic BP 45-50 mmHg `[PREM:13]`
   * **British Association of Perinatal Medicine:** MAP ≥ neonatal age (weeks) `[PREM:13]`
   * **Within 20% of baseline** `[PREM:13]`

### 5.2.6. NEONATAL NEUROLOGICAL SYSTEM [INTEGRATION]

#### 5.2.6.1. DEVELOPING BRAIN CONSIDERATIONS [INTEGRATION]

**Key considerations:** `[PREM:14]`

1. **Neurotoxicity concerns** `[PREM:14]`
2. **Anesthetic requirements** `[PREM:14]`
3. **Presence of pain pathways**
   * **Pain receptors develop by 20 weeks GA** `[PREM:14]`
   * **Pain pathways develop by 26 weeks** `[PREM:14]`

#### 5.2.6.2. PREMATURE NEUROLOGICAL COMPLICATIONS [INTEGRATION]

**Early complications of prematurity:** `[PREM:15]`

**Brain Injury:**
1. **Inherent fragile vascular germinal matrix** (risk of intraventricular hemorrhage especially if GA <32 weeks & first 4 days of life) `[PREM:15]`
   * **Precipitating factors:** hypoxia/hypercarbia/blood pressure swings/increased CVP/aggressive fluid resuscitation/hypertonic solutions/thrombocytopenia `[PREM:15]`

2. **Immature cerebral autoregulation** (risk cerebral ischemia) `[PREM:15]`
   * **Manifests as periventricular leukomalacia** `[PREM:15]`

3. **Retinopathy of Prematurity** (minimum FiO2 to SpO2 90-95%) `[PREM:15]`

**Late complications of prematurity:** `[PREM:15]`
1. **Cerebral Palsy** - up to 50% of ex-premature infants `[PREM:15]`
2. **Developmental Delay** `[PREM:15]`
3. **Epilepsy** `[PREM:15]`

**⚠️ Critical concept:** Linear increase in cerebral blood flow with MAP in premature infants (lack of autoregulation) `[PREM:16]`

#### 5.2.6.3. NEUROTOXICITY CONSIDERATIONS [INTEGRATION]

**Supporting claims of neurotoxicity:** `[PREM:17]`

1. **Observational studies** (fetal effects of maternal anesthesia during pregnancy, 2021) - in-utero exposure to general anesthesia during maternal antenatal surgical procedures and subsequent behavioral problems at 10 years old `[PREM:17]`

2. **Animal studies** - neuronal apoptosis in monkeys, but anesthetic doses much higher than children are exposed to `[PREM:17]`

3. **2016 FDA warning** - possible harmful effects in <3-year-old patients receiving general anesthesia for >3 hours `[PREM:17]`

**Against claims of neurotoxicity:** `[PREM:17]`

1. **GAS 2016** - first published multicenter RCT of 700 infants undergoing inguinal hernia repair randomized to spinal-awake vs sevoflurane-based GA (1 hour duration). No difference in cognitive development at 2 years and 5 years. Operation <1 hour in children <60 weeks PCA shows minimal difference `[PREM:17]`

2. **MASK Study** - exposure before age 3 years was not associated with deficits in primary outcome of general intelligence. Multiple exposures may be associated with behavioral and learning difficulties `[PREM:17]`

3. **PANDA 2019** - compared sibling pairs where one sibling had exposure to anesthesia for inguinal hernia repair before 36 months of age. No difference in developmental domains `[PREM:17]`

**Clinical recommendation:** Inconclusive evidence, difficult to prove causation. Delay elective surgery to >3 years (FDA & Editorial NEJM recommendation) `[PREM:17]`

### 5.2.7. NEONATAL HEMATOLOGICAL SYSTEM [INTEGRATION]

#### 5.2.7.1. BLOOD VOLUME AND HEMOGLOBIN [INTEGRATION]

**Blood volume characteristics:** `[PREM:18]`

* **Blood volume larger but absolute amount less** (modest blood loss = hypovolemia, calculate allowable blood loss) `[PREM:18]`
  * **Extreme preterm:** 100-110 mL/kg `[PREM:18]`
  * **Term:** 90 mL/kg `[PREM:18]`
  * **Child:** 70-80 mL/kg `[PREM:18]`
  * **Adult:** 70 mL/kg `[PREM:18]`

**Hemoglobin considerations:** `[PREM:18]`

* **Higher hemoglobin targets, increased transfusion requirements** `[PREM:18]`
* **Fetal hemoglobin = left shift of the OHDC** (increased affinity for oxygen, less offloading of oxygen to tissues) `[PREM:18]`
* **Increased oxygen demand by tissues** `[PREM:18]`

#### 5.2.7.2. COAGULATION ISSUES [INTEGRATION]

**Coagulopathy & Thrombocytopenia** (increased bleeding risk) `[PREM:18]`

* **Deficient in clotting factors** (usually given vitamin K) `[PREM:18]`
* **Quantitative and/or qualitative platelet dysfunction** `[PREM:18]`

**Physiological anemia** – nadir at 8-12 weeks (~Hgb 9-11) `[PREM:18]`

#### 5.2.7.3. TRANSFUSION CONSIDERATIONS [INTEGRATION]

**Platelet transfusion:** `[PREM:18]`
* **Use leukoreduced platelets** (decreased CMV infection, HLA alloimmunization, febrile reactions) `[PREM:18]`
* **Avoid repeated transfusions with different platelet bags** `[PREM:18]`
* **Choose apheresed platelets > pooled platelets** (decreased alloimmunization of baby - complicates future transfusions) `[PREM:18]`

#### 5.2.7.4. PREMATURE HEMATOLOGICAL COMPLICATIONS [INTEGRATION]

**Early complications of prematurity:** `[PREM:19]`

1. **Anemia of prematurity** - baseline lower hemoglobin compared to term neonates (Hgb 13-15 compared to 18-20) `[PREM:19]`
2. **Coagulopathy & Vitamin K deficiency** `[PREM:19]`
3. **Thrombocytopenia** `[PREM:19]`

### 5.2.8. PRETERM ENDOCRINE/GIT SYSTEM [INTEGRATION]

#### 5.2.8.1. GLUCOSE MANAGEMENT [INTEGRATION]

**Critical glucose considerations:** `[PREM:20]`

1. **Limited glycogen stores: prone to hypoglycemia** (regular glucose monitoring, limit fasting, glucose supplementation in maintenance drip BUT not for resuscitation) `[PREM:20]`
   * **Hypoglycemia management:** 2 mL/kg D10% followed by infusion `[PREM:20]`
   * **Glucose infusion rates:** full-term infants are 4-6 mg/kg/min, while rates for premature infants may be 6-8 mg/kg/min `[PREM:20]`

#### 5.2.8.2. GASTROINTESTINAL COMPLICATIONS [INTEGRATION]

1. **NEC & Sepsis risk** `[PREM:20]`
2. **GERD increases perioperative pulmonary complications** - neurological immaturity, underdeveloped lower esophageal sphincter `[PREM:20]`

### 5.2.9. PRETERM RENAL SYSTEM [INTEGRATION]

#### 5.2.9.1. FLUID AND ELECTROLYTE MANAGEMENT [INTEGRATION]

**Fluid management challenges:** `[PREM:21]`

* **Impaired ability to concentrate urine** - poor tolerance of under/over hydration `[PREM:21]`
* **Sodium handling** (use isotonic fluids perioperatively) `[PREM:21]`
  * **Poor retention sodium until 32 weeks GA** - prone to hyponatremia `[PREM:21]`
  * **Easily dehydrated from insensate losses** from thin skin and large surface area - prone to hypernatremia `[PREM:21]`
* **Immature acid-base handling** - may have metabolic acidosis → increases calcium renal excretion `[PREM:21]`

### 5.2.10. PRETERM PHYSICAL CONCERNS [INTEGRATION]

#### 5.2.10.1. PHYSICAL CHALLENGES [INTEGRATION]

**Key physical considerations:** `[PREM:22]`

1. **Fragile vessels** (difficult lines, risk vs. benefit of invasive lines) `[PREM:22]`
2. **Equipment sizing** `[PREM:22]`
3. **Labile temperature control** (core temperature monitoring, warming/cooling methods) `[PREM:22]`
   * **Increased hypothermia risk:** Large surface area/thin skin, inability to shiver (increased apnea, increased basal metabolic rate from brown fat metabolism) `[PREM:22]`
   * **Increased hyperthermia:** Large surface area/thin skin (increased evaporative losses, increased oxygen consumption, increased CBF & IVH) `[PREM:22]`
4. **Pressure injuries** - caution in positioning, tapes `[PREM:22]`

#### 5.2.10.2. TEMPERATURE MONITORING METHODS [INTEGRATION]

**Temperature monitoring options and considerations:** `[PREM:23]`

* **Pulmonary artery:** Gold standard, continuous reading (invasive)
* **Skin:** Easy placement (correlates poorly with core body temperature)
* **Skin overlying temporal artery:** Easy placement (limited data available)
* **Tympanic membrane:** Correlates with core body temperature (contraindications: basilar skull fractures, major anatomic defects, coagulopathy)
* **Nasopharynx:** Correlates with core body temperature, easy placement, continuous reading (contraindications: high fresh-gas flows, mask anesthesia, uncuffed endotracheal tube)
* **Esophagus:** Correlates with core body temperature, easy placement, continuous reading (requires proper seating, distal third of esophagus)
* **Rectum:** Correlates with core body temperature, easy placement, continuous reading (requires proper seating, contraindications: neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, inflammatory bowel disease)
* **Axilla:** Correlates with core body temperature, easy placement, continuous reading (requires proper seating, above axillary artery with tight adduction of arm)
* **Bladder:** Correlates with core body temperature, continuous reading, integrated in urinary catheter (requires bladder catheterization)

### 5.2.11. PRETERM PHARMACOLOGICAL CONCERNS [INTEGRATION]

#### 5.2.11.1. PHARMACOKINETIC DIFFERENCES [INTEGRATION]

**Key pharmacological considerations:** `[PREM:24]`

* **Weight-based dosing** (prone to drug errors) `[PREM:24]`
* **Variable pharmacodynamic effect:** `[PREM:24]`
  * **Increased total body water** (increased volume of distribution for hydrophilic drugs) `[PREM:24]`
  * **Increased end-organ sensitivity** (increased decreased acetylcholine, hence decreased ED95 for nondepolarizing NMBA & increased ED95 for depolarizing NMBA) `[PREM:24]`
  * **Reduced protein binding,** increased free drug fraction `[PREM:24]`
* **Variable pharmacokinetics:** `[PREM:24]`
  * **Increased cardiac output,** increased drug delivery to vessel-rich group (increased onset of action) `[PREM:24]`
  * **Immature hepatorenal function** (decreased clearance, prolonged duration of action) `[PREM:24]`

### 5.2.12. OTHER CONSIDERATIONS [INTEGRATION]

#### 5.2.12.1. CONSENT AND ASSESSMENT [INTEGRATION]

**Additional considerations:** `[PREM:25]`

1. **Consent** (increased perioperative risk, postoperative intubation, cardiac event, vascular access, transfusions) `[PREM:25]`
2. **Reason for prematurity** (need to assess other associated systems/dysmorphism if presence of congenital defects) `[PREM:25]`

**Bottom Line:** Premature neonates represent one of the highest-risk populations for anesthesia, with immature organ systems affecting every aspect of perioperative care. Understanding the developmental physiology, anticipating complications, and using appropriate monitoring and techniques are essential for safe outcomes.
