## 1.1. DEFINITION AND ROLE OF TRAUMA ANESTHESIA

**Trauma anesthesia encompasses the perioperative resuscitation and management of acutely injured, hemorrhaging patients requiring both timely operative control and well-balanced resuscitation.**

* Trauma is the leading cause of death in young Americans and the leading cause of years-of-life lost
* Surgical correction of hemorrhage is of no utility in a poorly resuscitated patient
* An elegant resuscitation in a patient in whom bleeding is not controlled is equally useless
* The role of the anesthesiologist in trauma is to guide the resuscitation of the trauma patient in the operating room (and increasingly in the interventional radiology suite)
* The trauma anesthesiologist serves as a **resuscitation consultant** - equal parts teammate, clinical pharmacologist, applied physiologist, procedural expert, and respectful skeptic
* Effective resuscitation prepares the patient for the next step on the continuum of care, often in the intensive care unit (ICU)
* **Damage Control Surgery**
* Always reevaluate patients in EOT
	* Auscultate lungs to ensure ETT placement
	* Check patency of all lines
	* Check pupils (esp. If intubated and sedated)
	* Check vitals (as last vitals likely taken more than 10minute)
* Is rapid resuscitation needed?
	* Level 1/Belmont to be primed 
	* MTP to continue 
	* ROTEM
	* Avoid crystalloids/collide as much as possible (best product is whole blood)

## 1.2. TRAINING AND CLINICAL BENEFITS

* Defining the training and quantifying the clinical benefit of trauma anesthesia can be challenging
* When trauma resuscitation practices were compared between critical care trained anesthesiologists and noncritical care anesthesiologists:
  * Critical care trained anesthesiologists administered more crystalloid, less albumin, fewer vasopressors, and more fresh frozen plasma (FFP)
  * However, mortality was no different between the two groups

## 1.3. STANDARDIZATION AND CHECKLISTS

* Standardization of care can offer benefit and checklists can contribute to that standardization
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Checklists decrease inpatient complications and death, especially useful in emergencies but require leadership to implement
* Checklists offer the opportunity to "plug holes" in resuscitation with a methodical approach to step-wise processes
* Failure can be conceptualized as a "Swiss cheese" model where several smaller errors must align to enable a serious error
* The trajectory for failure is mathematically described as a Bethe Lattice [Nk = z(z − 1)k − 1 for k > 0]

### EMERGENCY MANUALS AND COGNITIVE AIDS

* Stanford University developed an emergency manual of cognitive aids for crisis resource management in anesthesiology (available at <http://emergencymanual.stanford.edu>)
* Specific checklists for trauma anesthesia have been developed
* Tobin et al. developed a template for care in critically injured patients that places impactful stages of resuscitation in a logical order throughout the continuum of care from induction, through anesthetic management to ICU transition
* A cognitive aid adapted the Tobin checklist into a visually appealing multicolored format

**Bottom Line:** Trauma anesthesia requires a systematic, multidisciplinary approach combining resuscitation expertise, procedural skills, and standardized protocols to optimize outcomes in critically injured patients.

## 2.1. MEDICATIONS FOR EMERGENCY RESUSCITATION

**Essential medications for emergency anesthetic resuscitation during trauma management.**

### SEDATIVE/HYPNOTICS

* **Propofol:** 1-3 mg/kg IV
  * Mechanism: GABA-A agonist
  * ⚠️ **Warning:** Can drop SVR (systemic vascular resistance)
  
* **Ketamine:** 1-2 mg/kg IV
  * Mechanism: NMDA antagonist
  * 💡 **Advantage:** Maintains airway reflexes and SVR
  
* **Etomidate:** 0.6 mg/kg IV
  * Mechanism: GABA-A agonist
  * ⚠️ **Caution:** Single doses for induction can suppress hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis

### NEUROMUSCULAR BLOCKING AGENTS

* **Succinylcholine:** 1-2 mg/kg IV
  * Depolarizing neuromuscular relaxant
  * ⚠️ **Contraindication:** Can cause life-threatening hyperkalemia in burns and spinal cord injury
  
* **Rocuronium:** 0.6-1.2 mg/kg IV
  * Nondepolarizing muscle relaxant
  * Can be effectively used in rapid sequence induction
  
* **Cis-Atracurium:** 0.1-0.2 mg/kg IV
  * Eliminated by Hofmann degradation
  * 💡 **Advantage:** Useful in renal insufficiency/failure
  
* **Vecuronium:** 0.1 mg/kg IV
  * Longer duration neuromuscular blockade

### ADJUNCT MEDICATIONS

* **Tranexamic Acid:** 1 g over 10 minutes, then 1 g over 8 hours
  * Synthetic lysine derivative/antifibrinolytic
  * 💡 **Benefit:** Improvement in mortality when given within 3 hours of injury
  
* **Vasopressin:** 5-20 IU
  * Potent vasoconstrictor
  * Shunts blood to cerebral, cardiac, and pulmonary vascular beds
  
* **Calcium Chloride:** 1 g
  * Facilitates smooth muscle contraction
  * Used to restore low calcium levels during massive transfusion
  
* **Hydrocortisone:** 100 mg
  * Potent mineralocorticoid
  * Treats adrenal suppression seen in critical illness

## 2.2. RAPID SEQUENCE INDUCTION AND SAFETY

* **Rapid sequence induction (RSI) and direct laryngoscopy are safe in trauma**
* Analysis of 6,088 patients over 10 years at a level I trauma center showed:
  * 98.6% were successfully intubated via the orotracheal route
  * Only 0.3% required a surgical airway
* Norwegian prehospital critical care system: prehospital anesthesiologists successfully intubated 99.2% of patients

### GUM ELASTIC BOUGIE TECHNIQUE

* Can be inserted into the glottic opening during laryngoscopy
* Followed with a "tube over stylet" technique for challenging airways
* Randomized control trial of 1,102 patients found no difference between bougie and stylet approaches regarding:
  * Successful first-attempt intubation
  * Severe hypoxemia
  * Esophageal intubation
  * Pneumothorax
  * Injury to oropharyngeal structures

## 2.3. MANUAL IN-LINE STABILIZATION (MILS)
<!-- EPA-5:K1:cervical_spine EPA-5:S2:airway EPA-5:EQ1:ga_induction_precautions -->

* **MILS is frequently undertaken during emergency airway management in trauma to stabilize the cervical spine during intubation**
* ⚠️ **Important:** Cervical spine injury during intubation is not common
* Study of 81 patients with vertebral body fractures requiring airway management:
  * 26 required airway management, 22 were intubated orotracheally
  * **No subsequent neurologic deficit in any patients**
* Study of 69 patients with documented high spinal cord injury:
  * 29 required tracheal intubation, 26 were intubated orotracheally
  * **No worsening of neurologic deficit in any patients**

### MILS EFFECTIVENESS

* Investigation of subluxation, angulation, and distraction in cadaveric model found MILS did not have significant impact on maximal motion using direct laryngoscopy (DL) or Bullard laryngoscope
* Contradictory cadaver model showed MILS produced significantly less movement than a cervical spine collar at C5-6 disk space
* 💡 **Clinical Use:** When used as part of good communication within intubating team, MILS is an easy, low-cost technique to remind laryngoscopist to manipulate cervical spine minimally

## 2.4. CERVICAL SPINE ASSESSMENT
<!-- EPA-5:K1:cervical_spine EPA-5:EQ1:cervical_spine_clearance -->

### IMPORTANCE OF EARLY EVALUATION

* Ruling out cervical spine injury as soon as clinically feasible is important in early trauma airway management
* Patients in cervical collar immobilization for 48 hours can show signs of ligamentous edema on MRI, even without initial spine injury
* ⚠️ **Clinical Impact:** Unnecessary cervical collar use may condemn patients to 6 weeks or longer of unneeded cervical immobilization

### CANADIAN C-SPINE RULE

**100% sensitive and 45% specific in identifying cervical spine injuries**

Assessment questions:
1. Are there any high-risk factors necessitating radiography (age over 65 years, dangerous mechanism, paresthesias)?
2. Are there any low-risk factors allowing safe assessment of range-of-motion (simple rear-end motor vehicle accident, sitting in emergency department, ambulation since accident, delayed onset of neck pain, absence of mid-line tenderness)?
3. Can the patient actively rotate the head 45 degrees to the left and right?

### NEXUS CRITERIA

**Study of 34,069 patients with blunt trauma undergoing cervical spine radiography**

Five criteria (99% sensitive in detecting cervical spine injury, negative predictive value 99.8%):
1. No midline tenderness
2. No focal neurologic deficit
3. Normal alertness
4. No intoxication
5. No distracting injury

**Bottom Line:** Airway management in trauma patients is generally safe using RSI with MILS, and systematic cervical spine assessment can prevent unnecessary prolonged immobilization.

## 2.5. VIDEO LARYNGOSCOPY

**Video laryngoscopy (VL) affords an improved view of vocal cords but clinical advantages over direct laryngoscopy (DL) remain questionable.**

### RESEARCH FINDINGS

* Meta-analysis of 17 trials (2,000 patients): no difference in successful first attempts at intubation or time to intubation between VL and DL
* Non-expert study (medical students, paramedic students, nurses) intubating 200 patients:
  * VL had greater overall success and faster time to intubation
  * No difference by the time the fifth patient was intubated

### TRAUMA-SPECIFIC STUDIES

* **Study of 623 trauma patients randomized to DL or VL:**
  * No difference in mortality
  * Higher median intubation duration in VL group
  * Subgroup analysis found higher mortality in patients with traumatic brain injury (TBI)
  * More frequent oxygen saturation <80% in TBI patients with VL

* **Intensive care setting study:**
  * First-attempt success no different between VL (67.7%) and DL (70.3%) groups
  * Median time to successful intubation: 3 minutes in both groups
  * ⚠️ **Concern:** Severe life-threatening complications higher in VL group (9.5% vs. 2.8%)

### PREHOSPITAL EXPERIENCE

* Study of 326 prehospital physician intubations:
  * First-attempt success higher with DL (96.2% vs. 61.9%)
  * Second-attempt crossover: 95.3% success with DL vs. 66.7% with VL
  * Failure factors: difficulty advancing endotracheal tube into trachea, impaired visualization due to blood

### COCHRANE REVIEW CONCLUSIONS

* **Cochrane report evaluating 64 studies concluded:**
  * No difference in first-attempt success
  * No reduction in hypoxia or mortality
  * No evidence that VL "reduces the number of intubation attempts or the incidence of hypoxia or respiratory complications… [or] affects time required for intubation"

**Bottom Line:** Video laryngoscopy provides improved vocal cord visualization but has not demonstrated superior clinical outcomes compared to direct laryngoscopy in trauma patients.

## 2.6. SUPRAGLOTTIC AIRWAYS

**Supraglottic airways (SGAs) have limited but specific applications in trauma settings.**

### CLINICAL APPLICATIONS

* Used in 15 trauma patients who were entrapped and unable to be intubated
* Successfully used in 57 trauma patients either as rescue device or primary device for less experienced providers
* Intubating SGA airway used with success in morbidly obese patient with chest trauma

### LIMITATIONS IN TRAUMA

⚠️ **Substantial limitations:**
* SGA does not definitively secure the airway
* Offers limited protection against aspiration risk
* Provides challenges to positive pressure ventilation in noncompliant lungs
* Case report of ventilation following drowning: peak pressure adequate for ventilation unable to be generated, resulting in air insufflation into stomach

**Bottom Line:** SGAs may serve as rescue devices in trauma but have significant limitations regarding airway protection and ventilation effectiveness.

## 2.7. CRICOTHYROIDOTOMY
<!-- EPA-5:S2:fona EPA-5:K1:emergency_airway -->

**In "can't intubate, can't ventilate" situations, cricothyroidotomy may be the remaining option to secure the airway.**

### CLINICAL OUTCOMES

* **Prospective study of 282 trauma patients requiring airway management in combat setting:**
  * 17 patients required cricothyroidotomy
  * **Failure rate: 23.5% (4 of 17 cricothyroidotomies failed)**
  * Physicians had highest success rates and lowest complication rates compared to medics

### PREHOSPITAL EXPERIENCE

* Prehospital cricothyroidotomy failed to cannulate trachea in **26% of cases**
* ⚠️ **Mortality:** 66% of patients undergoing percutaneous cricothyroidotomy died
* 82% of patients requiring cricothyroidotomy had gunshot wounds to face, neck, or head
* More highly trained providers (physicians, physician assistants) had lower failure rates than medics (15% vs. 33%)

### TECHNIQUE

* ⚠️ **Critical:** When indicated, cricothyroidotomy should be committed to and promptly undertaken
* **"Scalpel-finger-tube" approach** has been described
* 💡 **Improvement:** Use of bougie rather than finger may aid swift and efficient passage of endotracheal tube and is recommended by Difficult Airway Society

**Bottom Line:** Cricothyroidotomy is a high-risk procedure with significant failure rates, but remains essential for "can't intubate, can't ventilate" situations in trauma.

## 3.1. VENTILATORY STRATEGIES

**Ventilatory strategies can be broadly categorized as decelerating flow patterns (pressure control) and constant flow patterns (volume target/volume control).**

* Mathematical integral of flow is typically used to calculate volumes due to challenges in accurately measuring dynamic ventilator volumes in clinical setting
* **Low lung volume ventilation is widely acknowledged strategy to limit lung injury in critically ill patients**

## 3.2. LOW TIDAL VOLUME VENTILATION

### ARDS NET TRIAL RESULTS

* **Adult Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS) Net trial demonstrated mortality benefit:**
  * Lower tidal volumes (6 mL/kg): 31% mortality
  * Traditional volumes: 39% mortality (P = .007)
  * More ventilator-free days: 12 days vs. 10 days (P = .007)

* Lower tidal volume ventilation used as part of approach allowing:
  * Permissive hypercarbia
  * Ladder of escalating fraction of inspired oxygen (FIO2)
  * Positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP)

### INTRAOPERATIVE APPLICATIONS

* Low tidal volume ventilation initiated in operating room before lung injury was evaluated in 20 reports (over 2,800 patients)
* **Benefits of low lung volume ventilation:**
  * Decreased lung injury development (RR 0.33, CI 0.23-0.47)
  * Decreased mortality (RR 0.64, CI 0.46-0.89)
  * Lower incidence of pulmonary infection
  * Lower mean hospital length of stay (LOS)

## 3.3. LUNG-PROTECTIVE VENTILATION

### RECRUITMENT MANEUVERS

* **Traditional alveolar recruitment:** Sustained Valsalva maneuver (PEEP ~30 cm H2O for ~30 seconds)
  * Tends to divert gas to healthy compliant tissue
  * Does not recruit atelectatic lung tissue

* **💡 More effective strategy:** Cycles increasing levels of PEEP over tidal volume ventilation in stepwise pattern
  * PEEP changes of 10-15-20-15-10 over 3 tidal volume breaths each
  * Applies low lung volume ventilation to healthy lungs in operating room
  * Objective: avoid lung injury and/or alveolar recruitment before it occurs

### COMPARATIVE STUDIES

* **Serpa Neto et al. comparison of protective vs. conventional ventilation:**
  * 15 randomized controlled trials reviewed
  * Lung-protective ventilation with lower tidal volumes associated with fewer postoperative pulmonary complications (adjusted RR 0.64, CI 0.46-0.88, P < .01)
  * **Important finding:** No benefit when comparing low tidal volume + low PEEP to low tidal volume + high PEEP (adjusted RR 0.93, CI 0.64-1.37, P = .72)
  * Complications related to tidal volume, not PEEP

**Bottom Line:** Lung-protective ventilation with low tidal volumes (6 mL/kg) reduces mortality and pulmonary complications in both ARDS patients and intraoperative settings.

## 3.4. TRANSFUSION-ASSOCIATED CIRCULATORY OVERLOAD (TACO)

**TACO can occur following massive transfusion and presents challenges to ventilatory management in trauma patients.**

### PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND PRESENTATION

* **Mechanism:** Transudative flow of fluid to interstitium contributes to decreased lung compliance
* **Definition:** Respiratory distress and hydrostatic pulmonary edema within 6 hours of blood transfusion
* **Clinical presentation:**
  * Volume overload
  * Lung crackles
  * Generalized sequelae of positive fluid balance

### RISK FACTORS

* History of hemorrhagic shock
* Chronic renal failure
* Heart failure
* Positive fluid balance per hour

### MANAGEMENT

* TACO may respond to diuresis
* ⚠️ **Caution:** Any diuretic therapy in hemorrhagic shock must be carefully evaluated in context of volume loss from bleeding

## 3.5. TRANSFUSION-RELATED ACUTE LUNG INJURY (TRALI)

**TACO and TRALI can present with similar clinical pictures of respiratory embarrassment following blood transfusion.**

### DIFFERENTIAL CHARACTERISTICS

* **TACO:** Generally presents with hydrostatic pulmonary edema
* **TRALI:** Presents more commonly as pulmonary permeability edema related to inflammatory-immune response

### DIAGNOSTIC TOOLS

* Brain natriuretic peptide and leukocyte antibody testing may offer technique to distinguish TACO and TRALI
* ⚠️ **Research needed:** More work needed to elucidate pathogenesis of these two entities

**Bottom Line:** Lung-protective ventilation strategies are essential in trauma patients, and clinicians must distinguish between TACO and TRALI when respiratory compromise follows transfusion.

## 4.1. VASOPRESSOR USE IN TRAUMA

**⚠️ It is appealing to correct low blood pressure from blood loss with vasopressor support, but this has not been shown to improve outcomes in trauma patients.**

### CLINICAL EVIDENCE AGAINST EARLY VASOPRESSOR USE

* **Study of 1,349 trauma patients (351 received vasopressors):**
  * Vasopressors used: dopamine, epinephrine, phenylephrine, norepinephrine, or vasopressin
  * **Mortality significantly worse in vasopressor group: 43.6% vs. 4.2% (P < .01)**

* **Study of 921 severely injured blunt trauma patients:**
  * Survived 48 hours, treated with norepinephrine, vasopressin, dopamine, or phenylephrine
  * **Early vasopressor use associated with 80% higher risk of mortality (hazard ratio 1.81, CI 1.1-2.9, P = .013)**
  * Exception: 95% confidence interval for hazard ratio crossed unity in vasopressin group

### PATHOPHYSIOLOGIC RATIONALE

* **Blood pressure vs. tissue perfusion:** The approach underestimates the role that tissue perfusion plays in hemorrhagic shock
* Tissue perfusion likely more closely linked to blood flow than to blood pressure
* Current computer modeling tools may offer approach to describe complex interactions between tissue beds mathematically

## 4.2. VASOPRESSIN IN HEMORRHAGIC SHOCK

**Vasopressin is a potent vasoconstrictor with unique properties that may benefit trauma patients.**

### PHYSIOLOGIC PROPERTIES

* **Mechanism:** Acts on most vascular distribution with notable exception of cerebral, pulmonary, and cardiac vascular beds
* Amplifies neuroendocrine response
* More potent than norepinephrine or angiotensin II
* **💡 Key advantage:** Maintains efficacy in hypoxia and severe acidosis

### ANIMAL MODEL EVIDENCE

**Porcine liver injury model (vasopressin vs. placebo vs. crystalloid):**
* Mean arterial pressure (MAP) higher in vasopressin group (58 mmHg vs. 32 mmHg, P < .05)
* Vasopressin improved abdominal blood flow but did not cause further blood loss
* **All vasopressin animals survived until bleeding control plus additional 60 minutes**
* All animals in other treatment arms died

**Second porcine model (vasopressin vs. epinephrine vs. placebo):**
* MAP higher in vasopressin group at 2.5 minutes (82 mmHg vs. 23 mmHg vs. 11 mmHg, P < .001)
* MAP higher in vasopressin group at 10 minutes (42 mmHg vs. 10 mmHg vs. 6 mmHg, P < .001)
* Hepatic and renal artery blood flow higher in vasopressin group
* **All (7/7) animals in vasopressin group survived to bleeding control plus 60 minutes, compared to none (0/14) in other groups**

### HUMAN CLINICAL EXPERIENCE

**Case report:** 41-year-old woman with multiple trauma
* Fall from 15 m height, multiple blunt trauma injuries and hemorrhagic shock
* Transfused with packed red blood cells (PRBCs) and fresh frozen plasma (FFP)
* Remained hemodynamically unstable with IV fluids and norepinephrine
* **Vasopressin infusion with interventional radiology coiling stabilized patient**
* Discharged from ICU after 39 days with no neurologic deficit

### AVERT TRIAL RESULTS

**Arginine Vasopressin during Early Resuscitation of Traumatic Shock (AVERT) trial:**
* Trauma patients with hemorrhagic shock who received 6 units PRBCs
* Randomized to vasopressin bolus (4 units) followed by vasopressin infusion or placebo
* **Results:**
  * Vasopressin group required fewer blood products (1.4 L vs. 2.9 L, P = .01)
  * No difference in crystalloid or vasopressor use
  * Similar mortality and complications
  * **Fewer DVTs in vasopressin group (5/44 vs. 16/47, P = .02)**

## 4.3. ALTERNATIVE VASOPRESSOR AGENTS

### METHYLENE BLUE

* Used to restore blood pressure in vasoplegic patients (cardiopulmonary bypass, burns)
* **Mechanism:** Inhibits guanylyl cyclase, interrupts cGMP pathways, antagonizes vasodilatory effect of nitric oxide (NO)
* **⚠️ Clinical concern:** Retrospective study of 226 vasoplegic patients following cardiopulmonary bypass showed methylene blue associated with worse mortality, renal failure, and hyperbilirubinemia
* May be useful as rescue therapy but not supported as first-choice agent

**Bottom Line:** Vasopressor use in early trauma resuscitation is associated with increased mortality, with the possible exception of vasopressin which may have unique benefits in hemorrhagic shock.

## 4.4. HYPOTENSIVE RESUSCITATION
<!-- EPA-5:K3:damage_control_resuscitation EPA-5:EQ2:fluid_management -->

**During hypotensive resuscitation, low blood pressure is tolerated until surgical control of bleeding is undertaken, based on the concept that higher blood pressure will exacerbate blood loss ("pop the clot").**

### HISTORICAL BACKGROUND

* Described by Cowell over one hundred years ago
* Based on concept that higher or even normal blood pressure will exacerbate blood loss in patients where bleeding has not been controlled

### CLINICAL EVIDENCE

**Challenging to demonstrate clear benefit:**
* **Prospective investigation of 110 trauma patients:**
  * Randomized to target systolic blood pressure of 100 mmHg or 70 mmHg
  * **Identical mortality in each group (4/55)**

* **Larger multicenter study (1,216 patients with ISS >15):**
  * Compared >500 mL vs. <500 mL prehospital crystalloid
  * **In normotensive patients:** Larger crystalloid infusion increased mortality risk (HR 2.5, CI 1.3-4.9, P < .01) and worsened coagulopathy (OR 2.2, CI 1.01-4.9, P = .04)
  * **In hypotensive patients:** Larger crystalloid infusion raised emergency department systolic blood pressure
  * **For each 1 mmHg increase in systolic blood pressure, survival increased by 2% (OR 1.02, CI 1.01-1.03, P < .01)**

## 4.5. DEFINITION OF HYPOTENSION

**The definition of hypotension can be challenging to quantify.**

### EMPIRICAL DEFINITION STUDY

* **Walsh et al. evaluated 33,330 noncardiac surgery patients**
* Found risk of acute kidney injury and myocardial injury increased with **MAP less than 55 mmHg**
* **Acute kidney injury:** Defined as creatinine elevation to 1.5 times baseline
* **Myocardial injury:** Defined as elevations in troponin or CK-MB isoenzymes
* 💡 **Clinical insight:** Longer durations of lower blood pressure increased risk for end-organ dysfunction

**Bottom Line:** Hypotensive resuscitation remains controversial, with benefits potentially dependent on patient hemodynamic status, and MAP <55 mmHg representing a threshold for end-organ dysfunction risk.

## 5.1. SECONDARY BRAIN INJURY PREVENTION

**⚠️ Neurologic injury is a leading cause of death in trauma. Prevention of secondary injury caused by hypotension and hypoxemia is important for improvement of long-term outcome following traumatic brain injury.**

### IMPACT OF HYPOTENSION AND HYPOXEMIA

* **Analysis of 717 patients with Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) <8:**
  * Systolic blood pressure <90 mmHg OR partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2) <60 mmHg **increased mortality by 150%**
  * Mortality increase ascribed largely to hypotension
* In TBI patients, maintaining sufficient cerebral-perfusion pressure (CPP) to support metabolic energetics is critical
* **Clinical challenge:** Potential conflict between abdominal trauma surgeon requesting lower MAP and neurotrauma surgeon requesting higher MAP
* 💡 **Role of trauma anesthesiologist:** Serves as focal point for discussion of cooperative management of complex multitrauma patients

## 5.2. CEREBRAL PERFUSION PRESSURE MANAGEMENT

**Maintaining adequate cerebral perfusion pressure is critically important in TBI patients.**

### PHYSIOLOGIC PRINCIPLES

* **CPP = MAP - ICP (intracranial pressure)**
* Prevention of secondary brain injury requires maintaining cerebral perfusion and oxygenation
* Hypotension and hypoxemia have detrimental effects following TBI

## 5.3. DECOMPRESSIVE CRANIECTOMY

**Decompressive craniectomy can be used to lower intracranial pressure in patients with severe TBI unresponsive to standard ICP therapies.**

### CLINICAL OUTCOMES

* **Study results:**
  * Similar rates of death at 6 months (19% vs. 18%)
  * Despite resulting in fewer interventions for elevated ICP, shorter ICU LOS, and less time with elevated ICP
  * **⚠️ Worse functional outcomes:**
	* Decompressive craniectomy group had worse Glasgow Outcome Scale scores (OR 1.84, CI 1.05-3.24, P = .03)
	* Higher odds of unfavorable outcome (OR 2.21, 95% CI 1.14-4.26, P = .02)

### MONITORING CONSIDERATIONS

* Placement of ventriculostomy catheter suggested to help with TBI decision-making
* When compared to computed tomography (CT) imaging, no differences in functional outcomes were reported

## 5.4. STEROID THERAPY IN TBI

**⚠️ Steroids in TBI are associated with increased mortality and should be avoided.**

### MAJOR CLINICAL TRIAL EVIDENCE

* **Investigation of over 10,000 patients with TBI and GCS <14 within 8 hours of injury:**
  * Patients receiving 48 hours of methylprednisolone (compared to placebo) had **higher relative risk of death within 2 weeks and death/disability at 6 months (RR 1.18, CI 1.09-1.27, P = .001)**

### COCHRANE REVIEW

* **Review of steroid treatment in TBI (20 trials, over 12,000 patients):**
  * Found significant heterogeneity, unable to calculate pooled result estimate of death risk
  * **Largest trial (80% of randomized patients) found increased risk of death with steroid use (RR 1.15, CI 1.07-1.24)**
  * No significant increased risk of infection or gastrointestinal bleeding

**Bottom Line:** Steroids are contraindicated in TBI management due to increased mortality risk.

## 5.5. HYPEROSMOLAR THERAPY

**Hyperosmolar therapy with mannitol or hypertonic saline can be used as osmotic diuretic to decrease ICP following TBI.**

### MECHANISM OF ACTION

* Facilitates osmotic shift from brain tissue into vasculature
* Fluid then eliminated via urine

### COMPARATIVE STUDIES

**Animal studies:**
* 10% sodium chloride lowered ICP and increased CPP compared to 20% mannitol

**Pediatric study (200 ICU patients with raised ICP):**
* Treated with mannitol or 3% saline
* Using MAP as surrogate for ICP:
  * **3% saline lowered MAP and decreased coma hours compared to mannitol**
  * Use of hypertonic saline avoided risk of renal failure sometimes seen with mannitol

### CLINICAL CONSIDERATIONS

* **Mannitol risks:** Risk of renal failure, possibly related to hypovolemia
* **Hypertonic saline:** Should be administered via central venous access
* **💡 Brain Trauma Foundation recommendation:** References mannitol use

### AVAILABLE AGENTS

* **Mannitol:** 20% solution
* **Hypertonic saline:** 3% saline preferred over higher concentrations due to safety profile

**Bottom Line:** Both mannitol and hypertonic saline are effective for ICP reduction, but 3% saline may offer advantages including reduced renal failure risk.

## 5.6. POST-TRAUMATIC SEIZURE PROPHYLAXIS

**Post-traumatic seizure prophylaxis in the first 7 days following injury is advocated by the Brain Trauma Foundation.**

### PHENYTOIN

* **Traditional agent:** Sodium channel blocker
* Investigated thoroughly over past several decades for prevention of post-traumatic seizures
* Requires frequent medication level checking

### LEVETIRACETAM

* **Mechanism:** Binds to synaptic vesicle protein SV2A
* **💡 Advantages:**
  * Offers comparable efficacy to phenytoin
  * Does not require frequent medication level checking
  * Preferred for ease of management

**Bottom Line:** Prevention of secondary brain injury through maintenance of cerebral perfusion and oxygenation is critically important, while avoiding harmful interventions like steroids and optimizing ICP management strategies.

## 6.1. TRANSFUSION PRINCIPLES

**💡 The cornerstone of trauma resuscitation is "hemostatic" blood products administration with emphasis on plasma:erythrocyte (RBC) ratios, as well as platelet:RBC ratios greater than 1:2, preferably closer to 1:1.**

### DEFINITION OF MASSIVE TRANSFUSION

**Massive transfusion defined as:**
* Transfusion of more than 10 units of whole blood in 24 hours, OR
* Estimated blood loss of greater than 150 mL/hr, OR
* Transfusion of greater than 5 units of whole blood in 3 hours

### MILITARY EXPERIENCE FOUNDATION

* In military conflicts of first two decades of 21st century, empiric administration of FFP employed in trauma resuscitation
* Often used in ratio of 1 unit PRBC to 1 unit FFP
* **Retrospective review of 246 patients at Combat Support Hospital:**
  * **Mortality improved with ratios closer to unity:**
	* 1:8 ratio = 65% mortality
	* 1:2.5 ratio = 34% mortality
	* 1:1.4 ratio = 19% mortality (P < .001)

### CIVILIAN TRAUMA EXPERIENCE

* Subsequent civilian trauma reports showed similarly improved survival with transfusion ratios for both FFP:PRBC and platelet:PRBC approaching unity
* **Ratios closer to 1:1 associated with:**
  * Decreased truncal hemorrhage
  * Increased 6-hour, 24-hour, and 30-day survival
  * Increased ICU-free days, ventilator-free days, and hospital-free days

## 6.2. MASSIVE TRANSFUSION PROTOCOLS
<!-- EPA-5:K4:mtp_activation EPA-5:EQ2:mtp_definition_complications -->

### SURVIVOR BIAS CONSIDERATIONS

* **⚠️ Important confound:** Improvement in survival with ratios closer to 1:1 potentially confounded by survivor bias
* Patients who received FFP had lived long enough to receive the thawed blood product (potentially as long as 45 minutes)
* In observational studies, early deaths may have been disproportionately over-represented by low FFP:PRBC ratio cohort

### OPTIMAL TRANSFUSION RATIOS

**Davenport et al. findings:**
* Maximal hemostatic effect at FFP:PRBC ratios of **1:2-3:4**
* FFP:PRBC transfusion ratios >1:1 conferred no benefit
* Hemostatic effect of balanced FFP to PRBC ratios seemed limited to coagulopathic patients

### PROPPR STUDY RESULTS

**Pragmatic Randomized Optimum Platelet and Plasma Ratio study (PROPPR):**
* Designed to better define benefit of 1:1 transfusion ratios
* Compared plasma-to-platelet-to-PRBC ratios of 1:1:1 and 1:1:2
* Patients who received >10 units PRBC in first 24 hours after injury
* **Results:**
  * **No difference in 24-hour or 30-day mortality**
  * In 1:1:1 group (more plasma): exsanguination significantly decreased and more patients achieved hemostasis
  * No difference in ARDS, multiorgan failure, thromboembolic events, or sepsis between groups

### PAMPER STUDY

**Prehospital plasma safety and efficacy:**
* 501 patients randomized to plasma or standard care groups
* **Plasma group experienced lower mortality (23.2% vs. 33%, CI -18.6 to -1, P = .03)**
* Suggests benefit to early administration of blood products
* No difference in complications including multiorgan failure, acute lung injury, ARDS, infection, allergic reaction, or transfusion-related reaction

### INTERNATIONAL EXPERIENCE

**European military experience:**
* **French military:** pH-buffered, freeze-dried plasma product pooled from 8-10 donors
  * 2-year shelf-life
  * No adverse events encountered
* **German military:** "LyoPlas" single donor product
  * Shelf life of 18 months
  * Type-specific product requiring reconstitution with buffering solution

**Bottom Line:** Balanced transfusion with plasma:RBC ratios approaching 1:1 improves hemostasis and reduces exsanguination, though mortality benefits remain debated.

## 6.3. WHOLE BLOOD RESUSCITATION

**💡 Group O whole blood with low hemagglutinin (anti-A and anti-B) titer offers logistical advantages over blood component therapy and has been increasingly used during initial trauma resuscitation.**

### HISTORICAL CONTEXT AND CURRENT USE

* **Historical use:** WB resuscitation traditionally used in military/combat trauma settings as "fresh" WB from "walking blood bank"
* **Current trend:** Many level-1 trauma centers across United States incorporating low-titer group O whole blood (LTOWB) for initial trauma resuscitation
* **Fresh WB concerns:** Carries risk of infectious disease transmission and is not FDA-approved

### LTOWB ADVANTAGES

**LTOWB characteristics:**
* Low hemagglutinin (anti-A and anti-B) titers
* Cold storage increases shelf-life to 21-35 days
* **Safety profile:** Group O red blood cells compatible with all blood types, eliminating major acute hemolytic transfusion reactions

### SAFETY CONSIDERATIONS

**Hemolytic reaction concerns:**
* Concerns remain for minor hemolytic transfusion reactions between donor anti-A and anti-B hemagglutinin and recipient red cells
* **Mitigation:** Use only donors with low titer (usually defined as <200 to 256 dilution)
* Some institutions set predefined maximum transfusion units of WB

**Clinical safety data:**
* Numerous studies in military and civilian settings report **no evidence of increased hemolytic reactions** for WB recipients when compared to component therapy or LTOWB
* No increased reactions between group O and non-group O recipients of LTOWB
* **Conclusion:** WB has similar safety profile to component therapy

### PRACTICAL ADVANTAGES

**LTOWB benefits:**
* **Logistical:** Single larger bag vs. multiple component bags
* **Concentrated:** More balanced hemostatic product
* **Time-efficient:** Requires less time to prepare

### CLINICAL OUTCOMES

* **⚠️ Survival data:** WB has **not shown significant difference in survival** compared to individual components
* Systematic reviews note large heterogeneity in study methodology
* **Research need:** Large randomized controlled trial needed

### SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS FOR LTOWB

**Female patients:**
* **⚠️ Critical:** LTOWB use in females, especially <50 years old, requires selecting only Rh-negative WB
* **Risk:** Avoid Rh alloimmunization (anti-D conversion) in Rh-negative recipients
* **Consequence:** Potential hemolytic disease in future newborns

**Volume considerations:**
* Total volumes of WB usually larger than PRBCs (approximately 500 vs. 250-350 mL)
* **TACO risk mitigation:** Frequently reassess resuscitation status to avoid volume overload

**Processing considerations:**
* **Leukoreduction:** May be applied to decrease platelet concentration and aggregation
* **⚠️ Time limitation:** WB older than 2 weeks may have reduced and variable hemostatic function, necessitating additional component therapy

**Bottom Line:** LTOWB offers practical advantages and appears safe, but has not demonstrated survival superiority over component therapy and requires special considerations for female patients and volume management.

## 6.4. TRANSFUSION ADJUNCTS

### TRANEXAMIC ACID

**Tranexamic acid is a synthetic antifibrinolytic that inhibits lysine binding sites on plasminogen, decreasing plasmin and limiting degradation of fibrin clot.**

#### CRASH-2 TRIAL

* **Multicenter, prospective, randomized trial (>20,000 patients)**
* **Results:**
  * Administration decreased all-cause mortality and death due to bleeding
  * No increase in vascular occlusive events
  * **💡 Benefit seen when administered within 3 hours of injury**
  * Interestingly, no difference in blood transfusions

#### MATTERS STUDY

* 896 consecutive trauma admissions, 293 received tranexamic acid
* Patients receiving tranexamic acid were more severely injured but experienced **lower mortality**
* **Greatest benefit in patients receiving >10 units PRBCs in 24-hour period**

#### CAUTIONS AND CONTRAINDICATIONS

* **⚠️ Use questioned:** Impaired fibrinolysis can potentially worsen mortality
* **Study of 180 trauma patients:**
  * Fibrinolytic shutdown identified in 64% (mortality 17%)
  * Physiologic fibrinolysis in 18% (mortality 3%)
  * Hyperfibrinolysis in 18% (mortality 44%)
  * **Recommendation:** Careful selection of patients who may benefit rather than universal use

#### CRASH-3 IN TBI

* Evaluated antifibrinolytics in >12,000 TBI patients without major extracranial bleeding
* **In mild-to-moderate TBI:** Tranexamic acid lowered risk of TBI-related death (RR 0.78, CI 0.64-0.95)
* **Patients with reactive pupils:** Decreased risk of TBI-related death (RR 0.87, CI 0.77-0.98)
* **Severe TBI:** No beneficial effect apparent
* No difference in vascular occlusive events and seizures

### CALCIUM

**Calcium is a divalent cation required as cofactor in multiple bioenergetic pathways.**

#### CLINICAL IMPACT

* **Mechanism:** Citrate preservative in blood products can chelate calcium, lowering concentration and contributing to hypotension
* **Trauma patient presentation:** Frequently hypocalcemic on admission (before transfusion)
* **Study of 212 severely injured patients (mean ISS = 34):**
  * Nearly 75% were hypocalcemic (ionized calcium <1.15 mmol/L) or severely hypocalcemic (ionized calcium <0.9 mmol/L) prior to transfusion
  * Survival not significantly different from similarly injured normocalcemic patients

#### MORTALITY ASSOCIATION

* **Study of 591 trauma patients:**
  * Lower mortality in low calcium group (15.5%) vs. high calcium group (8.7%)
  * Multiple transfusions and massive transfusion elevated in low calcium group
  * **Ionized calcium <1 mmol/L independently predictive of massive transfusion need (OR 2.2, CI 1.05-5)**

### PROTHROMBIN COMPLEX CONCENTRATE (PCC)

**PCC offers hemostatic effect with lower volume than FFP.**

#### TYPES AND COMPOSITION

* **3-factor PCC (3-PCC):** Factors II, IX, and X
* **4-factor PCC (4-PCC):** Factors II, VII, IX, and X
* Contains variable amounts of protein C, protein S, and heparin

#### ADVANTAGES

* **Volume benefits:** Decrease administered volume, decrease TACO risk, limit hydrostatic pulmonary edema risk
* **Preparation:** Lyophilized PCC allows reconstitution rather than thawing
* **Concentration:** Provides 25 times the concentration of clotting factors
* **Dosing:** 25 units/kg targeted to reverse coagulopathy

### FIBRINOGEN

**Available in FFP, cryoprecipitate, and fibrinogen concentrate.**
* **Benefits:** Can decrease blood product use, correct hypofibrinogenemia due to dilutional coagulopathy, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and liver failure
* **⚠️ Risks:** Prospective study of 63 patients noted three venous and one arterial nonfatal thrombotic events

### OTHER ADJUNCTS

#### ESTROGEN

* **Use:** Correct uremic platelet dysfunction and gastrointestinal bleeding
* **Mechanism (uncertain):** Appears to decrease L-arginine production, resulting in decreases in NO, guanylyl cyclase, and cGMP
* **Effects:** May increase thromboxane A2 and ADP, promote platelet plug formation
* **Additional effects:** May decrease antithrombin III (ATIII) and protein S, increase factor VII

#### DESMOPRESSIN

* **Use:** Treat uremic platelet dysfunction
* **Mechanism:** Releases factor VII from storage sites, minimizing effects of dysfunctional von Willebrand Factor (vWF)

**Bottom Line:** Multiple adjuncts can enhance trauma resuscitation, with tranexamic acid showing mortality benefit when used within 3 hours, while calcium, PCC, and fibrinogen products address specific coagulation deficits.

## 6.5. STEROID THERAPY

**Adrenal insufficiency in critical illness is well described; hydrocortisone can improve catecholamine sensitivity, decrease inflammatory markers, and decrease vasopressor requirements.**

### CORTICUS TRIAL

* **Study design:** 251 sepsis patients randomized to hydrocortisone 50 mg
* **Result:** **No mortality benefit found**

### ADRENAL INSUFFICIENCY IN TRAUMA

#### PREVALENCE AND ASSESSMENT

* **Study of 34 trauma patients receiving cosyntropin immediately following injury:**
  * 47% were nonresponders (defined as cortisol increase <9 mcg/dL)
  * 24% remained nonresponders after 1 week
  * **Mortality similar in both groups**
  * **Nonresponders had longer duration of norepinephrine infusion**

#### PHYSIOLOGIC EFFECTS

* **Hydrocortisone study:** Stepwise phenylephrine dosing before and after 50 mg hydrocortisone administration
  * **Hydrocortisone decreased ED50 by 37%**
  * **Increased sensitivity to phenylephrine in trauma patients at end of resuscitation period**

### RANDOMIZED CONTROLLED TRIAL RESULTS

**150 trauma patients treated with hydrocortisone (200 mg × 5 days, 100 mg × 1 day, 50 mg × 1 day):**
* **Mortality:** No difference (absolute difference 3%, 95% CI -5 to 11, P = .44)
* **Hospital-acquired pneumonia:** Less frequent (HR 0.51, 95% CI 0.3-0.83, P = .007)
* **Ventilator-free days:** Increased by 4 days in hydrocortisone group (95% CI 2-7, P = .001)

**Bottom Line:** Steroid therapy in trauma may improve some secondary outcomes like pneumonia rates and ventilator-free days, but does not demonstrate mortality benefit.

## 6.6. CRYSTALLOID THERAPY

**💡 Blood is the ideal fluid for resuscitating trauma patients in hemorrhagic shock, but understanding crystalloid effects is important for small-quantity use.**

### HYPERCHLOREMIC METABOLIC ACIDOSIS

**Chloride-rich solutions can contribute to hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis and worsen acid-base status.**

#### COMPARATIVE STUDY

* **Prospective, open-label study of critically ill patients:**
  * **Chloride-rich solutions:** 0.9% saline, 4% succinylated gelatin, 4% albumin
  * **Chloride-poor solutions:** Lactated solution, Plasma-Lyte, chloride-poor 20% albumin
  * **Results:** Lower chloride content solutions associated with higher rate of renal injury and higher renal replacement therapy (RRT) use
  * **No difference:** Hospital mortality, ICU/hospital LOS, or RRT need following discharge

### META-ANALYSIS RESULTS

* **Meta-analysis of low chloride content solutions in critically ill patients:**
  * **No statistically significant difference in mortality or RRT use**

### CLINICAL TRIAL COMPARISONS

#### PLASMA-LYTE VS. NORMAL SALINE

**Randomized controlled trial in first 24 hours following trauma:**
* **Plasma-Lyte associated with:**
  * Improved base excess
  * Improved pH
  * Lower serum chloride
* **No significant difference:** Mortality, resource utilization, or urine output

#### BALANCED MULTIELECTROLYTE SOLUTION STUDY

**Critically ill patients comparison:**
* **No difference between balanced solution and 0.9% saline:**
  * Mortality
  * New need for RRT
  * Serum creatinine
  * Adverse events

### CLINICAL RECOMMENDATIONS

**Data suggest either:**
* No difference in outcome between chloride-rich solutions (0.9% saline) vs. balanced salt solutions (Plasma-Lyte), OR
* Potential benefit to balanced salt solutions

**💡 Key principle:** No physiologic advantage to chloride-rich solutions  
**⚠️ Critical reminder:** Ideal resuscitative fluid in hemorrhagic shock is blood

**Bottom Line:** While balanced salt solutions may offer slight advantages over normal saline, blood remains the optimal resuscitation fluid for hemorrhagic shock.

## 6.7. BICARBONATE ADMINISTRATION

**Lactic acidosis is a common feature of hemorrhagic shock, but bicarbonate treatment may be counterproductive.**

### MECHANISM AND CONCERNS

* **⚠️ Paradoxical effect:** Treatment with sodium bicarbonate may actually **worsen intracellular acidosis** despite improving serum pH
* **Evidence gap:** No controlled studies have shown hemodynamic improvement attributable to sodium bicarbonate

### PHYSIOLOGIC REQUIREMENTS

* **Renal dependence:** Bicarbonate use dependent upon adequate renal function
* **Mechanism:** Bicarbonate helps primarily in elimination of hydrogen ion (and potassium) via urine

### CLINICAL RECOMMENDATION

**💡 No convincing evidence to support use of bicarbonate in trauma patients with lactic acidosis**

**Bottom Line:** Bicarbonate therapy in trauma-associated lactic acidosis is not supported by evidence and may be harmful by worsening intracellular acidosis.

## 7.1. URINE OUTPUT AS RESUSCITATION MARKER

**💡 Urine output is one of the most sensitive, though nonspecific measures of resuscitation in the operating room.**

### NORMAL PARAMETERS

* **Target urine output:** 0.5 to 1 mL/kg/hr suggests adequate renal perfusion
* **Clinical utility:** Sensitive indicator of resuscitation adequacy
* **⚠️ Limitation:** Nonspecific measure - can be affected by multiple factors

## 7.2. ACUTE KIDNEY INJURY

**In trauma, hypovolemia can lead to prerenal syndrome due to hypoperfusion and acute tubular necrosis, resulting in decreased urine output.**

### PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

#### TUBULAR OBSTRUCTION MECHANISMS

* **Tubular obstruction by necrotic cells** at nephron's proximal tubule and Loop of Henle
* **Intratubular casts** can precipitate when myoglobin reacts with Tamm-Horsfall tubular protein

#### CONTRIBUTING FACTORS

* **Hypovolemia:** Leading to prerenal syndrome
* **Hypoperfusion:** Causing acute tubular necrosis
* **Rhabdomyolysis:** Can contribute to AKI following trauma

### TREATMENT APPROACH

* **Best treatment:** Acute tubular necrosis best treated with **prompt fluid administration**
* **Effectiveness:** Can decrease incidence of post-traumatic AKI
* **⚠️ Important:** Serum myoglobin alone is **not an indication for renal replacement therapy (RRT)**

## 7.3. RHABDOMYOLYSIS

**Rhabdomyolysis following trauma can contribute to acute kidney injury.**

### PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

* **Mechanism:** Myoglobin release from damaged muscle tissue
* **Renal impact:** Myoglobin reacts with Tamm-Horsfall tubular protein to form intratubular casts
* **Clinical assessment:** Serum myoglobin levels helpful for diagnosis but not treatment indication

### MANAGEMENT CONSIDERATIONS

* **RRT indication:** Based on standard AKI criteria, not myoglobin levels alone
* **Prevention:** Early aggressive fluid resuscitation most important intervention

## 7.4. RENAL PROTECTIVE STRATEGIES

### LOW-DOSE DOPAMINE

#### THEORETICAL BENEFITS

* **Mechanism:** Can increase renal and mesenteric blood flow in experimental models
* **Historical use:** "Renal dose dopamine" to treat renal failure

#### CLINICAL EVIDENCE

* **⚠️ Lack of benefit:** Use of renal dose dopamine has **not been correlated with improvement** in markers for renal function
* **Potential harms:**
  * Can worsen splanchnic oxygenation
  * May impair gastrointestinal function
  * Can impair endocrine function
  * May impair immunologic function
* **Meta-analysis findings:** Low-dose dopamine **did not prevent mortality, onset of acute renal failure, or need for RRT**

### DIURETIC THERAPY

#### FUROSEMIDE CONSIDERATIONS

* **⚠️ Masking effect:** Diuretic therapy may **mask ongoing AKI** by facilitating urine output at expense of renal cellular dysfunction
* **Clinical principle:** AKI determines response to furosemide, rather than furosemide defining degree of AKI
* **Potential complications:**
  * Can result in alkalosis
  * Can contribute to hypovolemia and hypokalemia
  * May lead to dysrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation

### HISTORICAL APPROACHES

#### BIC/MAN THERAPY

* **Combination:** Bicarbonate and mannitol (BIC/MAN) used historically to treat renal failure
* **Study results:** Review of 2,083 trauma patients
  * **No difference in RRT or mortality** in patients with creatine kinase >5,000 U/L who received BIC/MAN therapy

### RECOMMENDED APPROACH

**💡 Best approach to treat low urine output in hemorrhagic shock:**
* **Primary:** Fluid resuscitation
* **Secondary:** RRT as indicated by RIFLE criteria
* **Avoid:** Potentially harmful interventions like low-dose dopamine or inappropriate diuretic use

### RIFLE CRITERIA

**Standard criteria for RRT indication in AKI:**
* **R**isk, **I**njury, **F**ailure, **L**oss, **E**nd-stage kidney disease
* **Evidence-based:** Provides standardized approach to RRT timing

**Bottom Line:** Urine output is a sensitive but nonspecific marker of resuscitation adequacy; the best approach to oliguria in trauma is aggressive fluid resuscitation and RRT based on standard criteria, while avoiding potentially harmful interventions like low-dose dopamine.

## 8.1. MEASURES OF RESUSCITATION ADEQUACY

**Evaluation of adequacy of resuscitation during ongoing hemorrhagic shock presents challenges and requires multiple complementary approaches.**

## 8.2. ASSESSMENT OF BLOOD CONSUMPTION (ABC) SCORE

**💡 The ABC score can be used to predict the need for massive transfusion using four quickly assessable variables.**

### ABC SCORE COMPONENTS

![ABC Score Table](https://via.placeholder.com/600x200)

**Four variables assessed quickly, noninvasively, without laboratory support:**
1. **Penetrating mechanism of injury** (yes/no)
2. **Systolic blood pressure <90 mmHg** (yes/no)
3. **Heart rate >120 beats per minute** (yes/no)
4. **Positive FAST exam** (yes/no)

### SCORING AND INTERPRETATION

* **Activation criteria:** If 2 or more YES answers, activate Massive Transfusion Protocol
* **Performance characteristics:**
  * **75% sensitive** for predicting need for massive transfusion
  * **86% specific** for predicting need for massive transfusion
* **💡 Clinical advantage:** Rapid assessment without waiting for laboratory results

## 8.3. END-TIDAL CO2 MONITORING

**💡 Sudden drops in end-tidal carbon dioxide (ETCO2) suggest an increase in dead-space ventilation and are an early indicator of impending cardiovascular collapse.**

### PATHOPHYSIOLOGY IN HEMORRHAGIC SHOCK

* **Mechanism:** In hemorrhagic shock, precipitous drops in ETCO2 represent substantial perfusion deficit
* **Clinical significance:** Early indicator of impending cardiovascular collapse
* **Physiologic basis:** Decreased pulmonary perfusion leads to increased dead space ventilation

### PREDICTIVE VALUE FOR ROSC

**Mathematical modeling study of cardiac arrest patients:**
* **Higher peak ETCO2** correlated positively with return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC)
* **Larger area under ETCO2 curve** correlated positively with ROSC
* **Rising ETCO2 slope** correlated positively with ROSC
* **Specific threshold:** Cumulative maximum ETCO2 >20 mmHg between 5-10 minutes post-intubation predicted ROSC
  * **Sensitivity:** 0.88
  * **Specificity:** 0.77 (P < 0.001)

### CLINICAL APPLICATION IN TRAUMA

**Study of 106 trauma patients with arterial blood gas samples in operating room:**
* **ETCO2 ≥27 mmHg associated with 5% mortality**
* **⚠️ ETCO2 <24 mmHg associated with 68% mortality**
* **Clinical utility:** Real-time, continuous, non-invasive monitoring

## 8.4. BASE DEFICIT

**💡 Base deficit serves as a rapidly obtainable and dynamic measure of resuscitation that accurately reflects the state of resuscitation in trauma patients, and is superior to pH at assessing effectiveness of resuscitation.**

### DEFINITION AND CALCULATION

* **Base deficit:** Amount of base required to bring 1 L of whole blood at 37°C and PCO2 of 40 mmHg to pH of 7.4
* **Clinical advantage:** Attempts to isolate metabolic contribution to acid-base status
* **💡 Dynamic interpretation:** Single value less important than trend during resuscitation

### CLINICAL CORRELATIONS

**Study of 1,191 trauma patients:**
* **Base deficit associated with need for blood transfusion (OR 0.8, P < .001)**
* **Worsening base deficit associated with:**
  * Lower blood pressure
  * Higher injury severity score (ISS)
  * Increased transfusion requirements

### CLASSIFICATION SYSTEM

**Base deficit classification more predictive than traditional ATLS classification:**
* **Class I shock:** Base deficit <2
* **Class II shock:** Base deficit 2-6
* **Class III shock:** Base deficit 6-10
* **Class IV shock:** Base deficit >10

**⚠️ Severely deranged base deficit (<-10 mmHg) associated with significantly worse mortality than normal (2 to -2), mild (-3 to -5), or moderate (-6 to -9) base deficit derangement**

## 8.5. STROKE VOLUME VARIABILITY

**💡 Stroke volume variability (SVV) evaluates hemodynamic responsiveness and can guide fluid resuscitation.**

### CALCULATION AND METHODOLOGY

* **SVV formula:** SVV = [SVmax - SVmin]/SVmean
* **Calculation period:** Floating average over 30 seconds
* **Data source:** Stroke volume calculated from area under arterial line waveform function

### CLINICAL PERFORMANCE

**Study evaluating SVV as measure of resuscitation adequacy:**
* **SVV >9.5% predicted 5% increase in stroke volume** with 100 mL fluid bolus administration
* **Fluid responders correlated with SVV** but not heart rate or central venous pressure (CVP)
* **Area under ROC curves:**
  * **SVV:** 0.87
  * **Heart rate:** 0.59
  * **CVP:** 0.49

💡 **Clinical insight:** Area under ROC of 0.5 suggests no correlation between measured metrics

### COMPARISON TO CVP

**CVP has poor correlation with fluid responsiveness:**
* **Marik et al. meta-analysis investigating CVP as fluid therapy guide:**
  * **Area under ROC for CVP correlation with cardiac performance change:** 0.56 for all studies
  * **ICU-only studies:** 0.56
  * **Operating room studies:** 0.56
* **⚠️ Clinical conclusion:** Little evidence to support CVP use as metric for fluid responsiveness and/or resuscitation

**Bottom Line:** Multiple monitoring modalities including ABC score, ETCO2, base deficit, and SVV provide complementary information for resuscitation guidance, while traditional metrics like CVP have limited utility.

## 9.1. TRAUMA-INDUCED COAGULOPATHY
<!-- EPA-5:K4:tic_coagulopathy EPA-5:K3:coagulopathy_management -->

**The complex pathophysiology and temporal dynamics of trauma-induced coagulopathy (TIC) call for tests that yield timely and specific results for diagnosis, monitoring, and therapy guidance.**

### TRADITIONAL COAGULATION TESTING

#### CONVENTIONAL COAGULATION TESTS (CCTS)

**Standard tests include:**
* **Prothrombin time (PT)**
* **International normalized ratio (INR)**
* **Activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT)**
* **Clauss fibrinogen level**
* **Platelet counts**

#### TIC DEFINITION AND LIMITATIONS

* **Common TIC definition:** INR >1.2 (or PT >18 seconds) - though no standard definition exists
* **Associated outcomes:** Worsened hemorrhage and mortality
* **⚠️ Recent recognition:** INR or PT may be prolonged in samples with adequate coagulation factor activity levels
* **Current understanding:** INR increasingly recognized as **biomarker for injury severity rather than dysfunctional hemostasis**

## 9.2. CONVENTIONAL VS. VISCOELASTIC TESTING

### POINT-OF-CARE COAGULATION TESTING

**💡 Point-of-care (POC) coagulation tests are increasingly utilized for trauma resuscitation due to rapid turnaround time.**

![POC Testing Diagram](https://via.placeholder.com/800x400)

### VISCOELASTIC HEMOSTATIC ASSAYS (VHA)

#### COMMON CHARACTERISTICS

* **POC capability:** Available at bedside
* **Mechanism:** Quantify increasing viscosity of whole blood as clot forms
* **Manufacturers:** Multiple devices available with different reagents, test mechanics, and result parameters

#### FOUR PHASES OF CLOT KINETICS

**VHA tracings represent:**
1. **Initiation**
2. **Propagation** 
3. **Clot strength**
4. **Fibrinolysis**

![VHA Tracings](https://via.placeholder.com/800x600)

#### MOST COMMON VHA DEVICES

**Thromboelastography (TEG) and Rotational Thromboelastometry (ROTEM):**
* **TEG parameters:** R (reaction time), K (time to 20mm amplitude), MA (maximum amplitude), LY30 (lysis at 30 min)
* **ROTEM parameters:** CT (coagulation time), A5/A10 (amplitude at 5/10 minutes), MCF (maximum clot firmness), Li30 (lysis index at 30 min)

### VHA ADVANTAGES OVER CCT

#### RAPID RESULTS

* **Complete test time:** 15-45 minutes
* **💡 Actionable results available as early as 5 minutes**
* **Strong correlation:** Early clot amplitudes correlate with maximum clot firmness and massive transfusion

#### ENHANCED DIAGNOSTIC CAPABILITIES

* **Hyperfibrinolysis detection:** CCT cannot reliably identify, VHA can identify both hyperfibrinolysis and fibrinolytic shutdown
* **Clinical significance:** Both conditions associated with increased mortality and complications
  * **Hyperfibrinolysis:** Associated with early TIC and hemorrhage
  * **Fibrinolytic shutdown:** Occurs hours later, associated with thrombosis

#### COMPREHENSIVE HEMOSTASIS ASSESSMENT

* **Platelet function:** VHA reflects both quantity and function of platelets and fibrinogen
* **Clot strength contribution:** Platelets account for 70-80% of peak resistance curve
* **Specialized assays:** Functional fibrinogen TEG and FIBTEM can distinguish fibrinogen effects from platelet effects
* **Strong correlation:** Both tests correlate well with gold-standard Clauss method

## 9.3. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS AND EVIDENCE

### SURVIVAL BENEFIT QUESTIONS

**⚠️ Limited evidence for survival benefit:**
* **Two earlier systematic reviews:** Limited trauma-specific studies found little to no evidence supporting VHA association with survival
* **Recent reviews:** Cochrane and Eastern Association for Surgery of Trauma (EAST) recommended VHA for potential benefits of reduced blood product transfusions and mortality
* **Study limitations:** Low level of evidence, populations not limited to trauma

#### ITACTIC STUDY RESULTS

**Most recent multicenter trial (VHA-guided vs. CCT-guided resuscitation):**
* **Primary finding:** **No difference in clinical outcomes** including massive transfusion rate or mortality
* **Study limitations:**
  * TIC prevalence only 25%
  * Overall massive transfusion rate low (27%)
* **⚠️ Concern:** Population may not have been coagulopathic enough to identify VHA benefit

### EMERGENCY HEMORRHAGE PANEL ALTERNATIVE

* Some institutions utilize rapid emergency hemorrhage panel as alternative to POC testing
* **Logistical optimization:** Immediate sample movement, limited hemolysis checks, rapid centrifugation
* **Result:** Can reduce CCT turnaround times to comparable to VHA

## 9.4. VHA LIMITATIONS

### COST AND LEARNING CURVE

* **Cost effectiveness:** Retrospective study showed POC INR testing (costing less than one-tenth of VHA) correlated well with TEG values and blood product transfusion
* **Training requirements:** Steep learning curve for performing and interpreting VHA tests
* **Operator variability:** Older devices have high intraand inter-operator variability
* **💡 Newer devices:** Cartridge-based and automated with lower variability

### TECHNICAL LIMITATIONS

#### HEMOSTASIS COVERAGE

* **Secondary hemostasis only:** VHA tests measure only secondary hemostasis
* **Cannot detect:** Primary hemostasis pathology (e.g., von Willebrand disease)
* **Missing component:** Cannot depict vascular endothelium function on in vivo hemostasis

#### ENVIRONMENTAL FACTORS

* **Sample conditioning:** Samples heated to body temperature and recalcified
* **⚠️ Masking effects:** Masks effects of hypothermia and hypocalcemia on hemostasis

#### DOAC DETECTION

* **Limited specificity:** VHA may detect coagulation abnormality from direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs)
* **Cannot differentiate:** These assays cannot distinguish DOAC effects from TIC

### COMPARISON TABLE: CCT VS. VHA

| Parameter | Conventional Coagulation Test | Viscoelastic Test |
|-----------|-------------------------------|-------------------|
| **Turnaround time** | 15-60 min* | 20-40 min** |
| **Costs** | Less | More |
| **Perform on** | Citrated plasma | Whole blood |
| **Detect hyperfibrinolysis** | No | Yes |
| **Detect hypercoagulation** | No | Yes |
| **Detect Warfarin** | Yes | Unreliable |
| **Detect DOAC** | No | Nonspecific |
| **Detect primary hemostasis abnormality** | No | No |

*With emergency hemorrhage panel  
**Actionable results within 5-10 min

**Bottom Line:** VHA provides comprehensive, rapid assessment of coagulation with advantages over conventional testing, but survival benefits remain unproven and significant limitations exist including cost, complexity, and inability to detect primary hemostasis disorders.

## 10.1. FAST EXAMINATION
<!-- EPA-5:K5:fast_efast_examination -->

**💡 Point-of-Care ultrasound (PoCUS) is commonly defined as the use of ultrasonography at the bedside to guide procedures or rapidly answer specific goal-oriented diagnostic clinical questions.**

### IMPORTANCE IN TRAUMA ANESTHESIA

* **Key competency:** Diagnostic use of ultrasound is key competency in trauma anesthesia practice
* **Educational recognition:** Reflected in recent American Board of Anesthesiology (ABA) content guidelines and American Society of Anesthesiologists educational programs
* **ATLS integration:** FAST has long been part of Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) adjuncts to primary trauma survey

### FAST EXAMINATION COMPONENTS

![FAST Exam Views](https://via.placeholder.com/800x600)

**Primary goal:** Evaluate presence of pathologic free fluid (anechoic) in pericardial, peritoneal, and pleural cavities

#### STANDARD FAST VIEWS (FOUR VIEWS)

1. **Subxiphoid (pericardial):** Evaluate for pericardial effusion
2. **Right flank (hepato-renal/Morrison's pouch):** Assess for free fluid in right upper quadrant
3. **Left flank (perisplenic):** Evaluate for free fluid in left upper quadrant  
4. **Suprapubic (pelvic):** Check for free fluid in pelvis

#### EXTENDED FAST (E-FAST)

* **Additional view:** Anterior thoracic view to identify pneumothoraces
* **Enhanced capability:** Combines traditional FAST with thoracic assessment

### CLINICAL PERFORMANCE AND OUTCOMES

#### IMPACT ON CLINICAL CARE

**Large randomized study demonstrated FAST:**
* **Reduced time to surgery**
* **Reduced CT imaging use** 
* **Reduced hospital stay**

#### DIAGNOSTIC ACCURACY

**Blunt thoracoabdominal trauma:**
* **Sensitivity:** 74-82%
* **Specificity:** 96-99%

**Penetrating trauma performance:**
* **Sensitivity:** As low as 28% (less sensitive than blunt trauma)
* **Specificity:** 94-100% (remains high)

### LIMITATIONS OF FAST

⚠️ **Important limitations:**
* **Poor detection of:**
  * Bowel injuries
  * Retroperitoneal injuries  
  * Diaphragmatic injuries
  * Pancreatic injuries
* **Clinical implication:** Further investigation typically using CT scan needed to rule out specific injuries when FAST negative and clinical suspicion high

**Bottom Line:** FAST is a rapid, accurate screening tool for detecting free fluid in trauma patients, but has important limitations requiring complementary imaging when clinical suspicion persists.

## 10.2. AIRWAY ULTRASOUND

**💡 Ultrasound has been increasingly studied and used to assist with airway management in trauma care.**

![Airway Ultrasound](https://via.placeholder.com/600x400)

### CLINICAL APPLICATIONS

#### PRE-INTUBATION ASSESSMENT

* **Difficult airway prediction:** Ultrasound can predict challenging intubations
* **Cricothyroid membrane identification:** Facilitates rapid front-of-neck access planning
* **Expected difficult airway preparation:** Enables preparation for surgical airway if needed

## 

* **Endotracheal vs. esophageal tube position confirmation**
* **💡 Advantage in low cardiac output:** Especially valuable when capnography less reliable due to poor circulation
* **Real-time verification:** Immediate confirmation of proper tube placement

#### ULTRASOUND FINDINGS

**Esophageal intubation appearance:**
* **Trachea:** Hypoechoic acoustic shadow below hyperechoic horseshoe-shaped tracheal cartilage
* **Esophagus:** Seen as dilated structure with hyperechoic curved line surrounded by thin muscular layer
* **"Double trachea sign":** Characteristic appearance of esophageal intubation

### CLINICAL ADVANTAGES

* **Immediate results:** Real-time confirmation capability
* **Low cardiac output utility:** More reliable than capnography in shock states
* **Procedural guidance:** Assists with emergency surgical airway procedures
* **Non-invasive:** No additional risk to critically ill patients

**Bottom Line:** Airway ultrasound provides valuable real-time guidance for both pre-intubation planning and post-intubation confirmation, especially valuable in low cardiac output states.

## 10.3. PNEUMOTHORAX DETECTION

**💡 PoCUS for detection of pneumothorax (PTX) has superior sensitivity and specificity compared to chest x-ray in trauma patients.**

### DIAGNOSTIC PERFORMANCE

#### META-ANALYSIS RESULTS

**Thoracic PoCUS for pneumothorax in trauma:**
* **Sensitivity:** 91% 
* **Specificity:** 99%

**Chest x-ray comparison:**
* **Sensitivity:** 47%
* **Specificity:** 100%

**Clinical significance:** Ultrasound significantly more sensitive than chest x-ray for pneumothorax detection

### TECHNIQUE AND EQUIPMENT

* **Probe:** High-frequency linear probe
* **Orientation:** Parasagittal orientation
* **Location:** Least dependent portion of lungs (anterior chest wall in supine patients)

### NORMAL ULTRASOUND FINDINGS

![Lung Ultrasound](https://via.placeholder.com/800x600)

**Normal image characteristics:**
* **Rib shadows:** Visible rib shadowing
* **Pleural line:** Bright hyperechoic line between and underneath ribs
* **Lung sliding sign:** Dynamic "shimmering" horizontal movement along visceral and parietal pleural surface during breathing

### PNEUMOTHORAX FINDINGS

**Findings suggestive of PTX:**
* **Absence of lung sliding**
* **"Barcode sign":** Examined in M-mode ultrasound, shows loss of normal variation
* **Lung transition point:** Between presence and absence of lung sliding

### PLEURAL EFFUSION/HEMOTHORAX DETECTION

* **Location:** Most dependent portion of thorax (mid-to-posterior axillary line in supine patients)
* **Appearance:** Anechoic collection between visceral and parietal pleura or diaphragm
* **Specific sign:** "Extension of thoracic spine" sign - spine visualized above diaphragm
* **Performance:** Meta-analysis reported 67% sensitivity and 99% specificity for hemothorax detection

**Bottom Line:** Ultrasound is significantly more sensitive than chest x-ray for pneumothorax detection and provides rapid, bedside assessment of thoracic pathology in trauma patients.

## 10.4. FOCUSED CARDIAC ULTRASOUND (FOCUS) 

**💡 FOCUS is a time-sensitive, concise echocardiographic examination done in conjunction with other hemodynamic parameters, emerging as the "21st century stethoscope."**

### CLINICAL APPLICATIONS

#### PRIMARY ASSESSMENT PARAMETERS

**FOCUS primarily assesses:**
* **Pericardial effusion:** Detection of pericardial fluid collections
* **Intravascular status:** Volume status assessment  
* **Ventricular size:** Left and right ventricular dimensions
* **Ventricular function:** Systolic function evaluation

#### IMAGING MODALITIES

* **Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE):** Surface probe application
* **Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE):** Intraoperative preference
  * **Advantages:** Less surgical field interference, better image quality
  * **Additional capability:** Assessment of blunt traumatic aortic injury with high sensitivity and specificity

### CLINICAL IMPACT

#### RESCUE TEE IN UNSTABLE PATIENTS

**Case series of rescue TEE in unstable trauma patients:**
* **Management changes:** TEE findings changed management in **49% of patients**
* **Most common interventions:**
  * Fluid challenge
  * Initiation of inotropic support
* **Real-time guidance:** Immediate impact on resuscitation decisions

### SAFETY CONSIDERATIONS

#### CONTRAINDICATIONS

* **⚠️ Absolute contraindication:** Esophageal injury (for TEE)
* **⚠️ Relative contraindications (use extreme caution):**
  * Severe coagulopathy
  * Cervical spine pathology

#### RISK-BENEFIT ASSESSMENT

* **High-risk patients:** Careful evaluation needed in coagulopathic patients
* **Cervical spine:** Extra precaution required with suspected C-spine injury
* **Clinical judgment:** Benefits vs. risks assessment essential

### INTEGRATION WITH OTHER MONITORING

* **Hemodynamic correlation:** Used in conjunction with other hemodynamic parameters
* **Real-time decision making:** Provides immediate diagnostic information for resuscitation management
* **Complement to FAST:** Enhances overall ultrasound assessment in trauma

**Bottom Line:** FOCUS provides rapid, valuable hemodynamic information in trauma patients, with TEE offering superior intraoperative imaging quality while requiring careful attention to contraindications in high-risk patients.

## 11.3. IMMUNOTHERAPY

**As the role of inflammation in trauma becomes more clearly defined, the trauma anesthesiologist must understand immune implications of severe injury and potential therapeutic options.**

### INFLAMMATORY-IMMUNE RESPONSE IN TRAUMA

* **Opportunity for intervention:** The inflammatory-immune response to trauma stress may offer opportunity to influence development and progression of end-organ injury
* **Complex pathophysiology:** Understanding of immune implications essential for trauma anesthesiologists

### POTENTIAL FUTURE THERAPIES

#### IMMUNOMODULATING INTERVENTIONS

* **Immunomodulating diets:** May decrease infection rates
* **Anti-high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) antibody therapy:** Potential role in acute lung injury during hemorrhage
* **FFP endothelial protection:** Refined understanding of FFP role in protecting endothelium from inflammation

#### ALM (ADENOSINE, LIDOCAINE, MAGNESIUM) THERAPY

**💡 ALM therapy represents novel approach to attenuating inflammatory response and potentially improving hemorrhagic shock outcomes.**

##### COMPONENT MECHANISMS

* **Adenosine:** Endogenous nucleoside important in supply/demand relationship of bioenergetics
* **Lidocaine:** Class IB antiarrhythmic
* **Magnesium:** Involved in ionic regulation and cellular bioenergetics

##### PHYSIOLOGIC EFFECTS

* **Polarizing cardioplegia:** Creates -80 mV polarization without cardiac arrest (vs. potassium-rich depolarizing cardioplegia at -50 mV)
* **"Natural hibernation" hypothesis:** Decreases action potential duration through:
  * Opening potassium channels with adenosine
  * Inhibiting sodium fast channels in phase 0 with lidocaine  
  * Protecting from ischemia-reperfusion and arrhythmia with magnesium

##### COAGULATION EFFECTS

* **Hyperfibrinolysis correction:** ALM corrects hyperfibrinolysis in rats
* **Endothelial mechanism:** Acts at endothelial thrombomodulin-thrombin complex
* **Pathway shift:** Shifts thrombin substrate from Protein C pathway toward Thrombin Activatable Fibrinolysis Inhibitor (TAFI) pathway

### PRECLINICAL EVIDENCE

#### TBI MODEL RESULTS

**Rat model of TBI (ALM vs. saline, sham, or no treatment):**
* **Survival:** All ALM subjects survived
* **Cardiac function:** Improved cardiac function
* **Cerebral blood flow:** Enhanced cerebral blood flow  
* **Anti-inflammatory effects:**
  * Decreased proinflammatory cytokines (IL1β, TNF-α)
  * Increased anti-inflammatory cytokines in brain (IL10, IL4)
  * Decreased brain injury markers (neuron-specific enolase, HMGB1)

## 

**Rat liver injury model (noncompressible hemorrhage):**
* **Inflammatory response:** ALM subjects had:
  * Undetectable levels of IL1α
  * Below baseline levels of IL1β, IL2, IL6, TNF-α during bolus phase
  * Biomarker levels similar to sham group during infusion phase
* **Coagulation improvement:**
  * Improved ADP-platelet aggregation
  * Improved collagen platelet aggregation
  * Maintained fibrinogen levels
  * Preserved coagulation function
* **Comparison group:** Non-ALM subjects unable to aggregate platelets, decreased fibrinogen, hypocoagulable

### THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK

* **"Permissive environment":** ALM may create environment for healing without overexpression of inflammatory response
* **IL1 release:** Controlled induction and amplification of inflammation for healing
* **Preventing overexpression:** Avoid excessive inflammation that can result in multiorgan failure

### CURRENT STATUS

* **⚠️ Human trials:** ALM has **not been demonstrated in any human trials** to improve outcome
* **Research stage:** Encouraging preclinical results require human validation
* **Future potential:** Represents novel approach to trauma resuscitation

**Bottom Line:** Immunotherapy represents an emerging frontier in trauma care, with ALM therapy showing promising preclinical results in attenuating inflammatory response and improving coagulation, though human validation is still needed.

## 12.1. PEDIATRIC TRAUMA

**💡 Injury is the leading cause of death among children and adolescents in the United States, accounting for years of potential life loss that surpass other major diseases.**

### EPIDEMIOLOGY AND PATTERN DIFFERENCES

#### INJURY PATTERNS

* **Blunt injuries predominance:** Approximately **90% of all pediatric trauma are blunt injuries**
* **Common mechanisms:**
  * Motor vehicle crashes (most common)
  * Falls
* **Traumatic brain injury (TBI) association:** Nearly **75% of pediatric trauma patients associated with TBI**
* **Mortality factor:** TBI accounts for **70% of mortality** in pediatric trauma

### UNIQUE ANATOMICAL CONSIDERATIONS

#### AIRWAY DIFFERENCES

* **Challenging anatomy features:**
  * Smaller oral cavity
  * Relatively large tongue and soft tissue
  * More anterior larynx
* **Positioning issues:** Larger head-to-body ratios and prominent occiput cause neck flexion in supine position, potentiating airway obstruction
* **Respiratory reserve:** Reduced due to:
  * Increased oxygen consumption
  * Reduced functional residual capacity (FRC)
  * **Clinical consequence:** Children prone to rapid hypoxia

#### GASTRIC CONSIDERATIONS

* **⚠️ Full stomach assumption:** Full stomach should be assumed in **all pediatric trauma patients regardless of reported fasting time**
* **Aspiration risk:** Higher risk due to delayed gastric emptying and stress response

#### SPINAL CONSIDERATIONS

* **SCIWORA:** Up to **35% of spinal cord injury in children** can present as spinal cord injury without radiographic abnormality
* **Airway management:** Preferred method is RSI with restriction of cervical motion (similar to adults)

### EQUIPMENT AND DOSING CONSIDERATIONS

#### WEIGHT-BASED CALCULATIONS

* **Critical factors:** Child's weight and age guide:
  * Airway equipment sizes
  * Drug doses  
  * Fluid and blood volumes
* **💡 Broselow tape:** Length-based resuscitation tape determines appropriate:
  * Patient weight
  * Medication doses
  * Equipment sizes

#### EMERGENCY AIRWAY MANAGEMENT

**"Cannot intubate, cannot ventilate" (CICV) situations:**
* **Age consideration:** Children younger than 10-12 years
* **Preferred approach:** Needle cricothyroidotomy preferred over surgical cricothyroidotomy
* **Rationale:** Smaller airway size and proximity to vocal cords result in less potential damage to surrounding structures

### CARDIOVASCULAR PHYSIOLOGY DIFFERENCES

#### PHYSIOLOGIC COMPENSATION

* **Reserve capacity:** Child's physiologic reserve can maintain normal blood pressures even with **30-40% circulatory volume drop**
* **💡 Early shock indicators:** More sensitive indicators of early hypovolemic shock:
  * Tachycardia  
  * Capillary refill
  * Level of consciousness

#### AGE-SPECIFIC NORMAL VALUES

**Critical knowledge for assessment:**
* **Mean systolic blood pressure (SBP):** Approximately **90 mmHg + (2 × age in years)**
* **Lower limit SBP (5th percentile):** Approximately **70 mmHg + (2 × age in years)**

#### BLOOD VOLUME BY AGE

**Total blood volume varies across age groups:**
* **Full-term neonate:** 90 mL/kg
* **1-6 months:** 85 mL/kg  
* **6 months-5 years:** 75 mL/kg
* **5 years and older:** 70 mL/kg

### VASCULAR ACCESS AND TEMPERATURE

#### VASCULAR ACCESS STRATEGY

* **Initial attempts:** Percutaneous peripheral venous catheter insertion
* **⚠️ Backup plan:** If unsuccessful after **two attempts**, consider intraosseous needle placement
* **Preferred IO sites in pediatrics:**
  * Anteromedial tibia
  * Distal femur

## 

* **High risk:** Low body mass-to-body surface area ratios cause children to develop hypothermia precipitously
* **⚠️ Clinical importance:** Prevention of hypothermia and associated coagulation abnormality crucial in this population

### EVIDENCE-BASED TREATMENT CONSIDERATIONS

#### ADULT EVIDENCE EXTRAPOLATION LIMITATIONS

**⚠️ Many adult trauma strategies still under debate for pediatric populations:**

* **Damage control resuscitation (1:1:1 ratio):** Has **not shown benefit in children**
* **Tranexamic acid:** Has **only shown survival benefit in children suffering injury in combat setting**
  * **Clinical question:** Applicability questionable among majority of blunt pediatric trauma patients
* **Viscoelastic tests:** Limited evidence in injured children has **not shown survival benefit**

#### HYPOTENSIVE RESUSCITATION CONCERNS

* **⚠️ Potentially harmful:** Evidence suggests hypotensive resuscitation might be harmful in children
* **Physiologic rationale:** Small gap between baseline BP and cerebral autoregulatory limit in children
* **Clinical implication:** Aggressive normotensive resuscitation may be more appropriate in pediatric patients

**Bottom Line:** Pediatric trauma requires specialized knowledge of anatomical, physiological, and pharmacological differences, with many adult evidence-based practices not applicable to children.

## 12.2. BURNS
<!-- EPA-8:K1:burns_injury EPA-8:K3:burns_centre EPA-8:K4:burns_anaesthesia -->

**💡 Burns are a significant public health problem, with 11 million people suffering new burns each year globally and an estimated 180,000 deaths.**

### SCOPE AND IMPACT

* **Global burden:** 11 million new burns annually worldwide
* **Mortality:** Estimated 180,000 deaths annually
* **Anesthesia involvement:** Trauma anesthesia involves many facets of burn care:
  * Airway and pain management
  * Resuscitation
  * Intraoperative management

### PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND TEMPORAL PHASES

#### TWO-PHASE RESPONSE

**💡 Major burns result in multiorgan systemic pathophysiologic effects correlated with burn injury severity, occurring in two temporal phases:**

#### PHASE 1: BURN SHOCK (EBB PHASE)

**Timeline:** 24-48 hours after injury
* **Pathophysiology:** Intravascular (plasma) volume loss into both injured and noninjured tissue
* **Circulatory effects:**
  * Reduced circulatory volume
  * Hemoconcentration  
  * **Cardiac output can fall up to 60% from baseline**
  * Increased systemic vascular resistance
* **Clinical consequences:** Impaired tissue and organ perfusion

#### PHASE 2: HYPERMETABOLIC STATE (FLOW PHASE)

**Timeline:** 48-72 hours after injury
* **Drivers:** Inflammatory cytokines and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs)
* **Systemic effects:**
  * Systemic vasodilation
  * Hyperdynamic circulation
  * Increased basal body temperature
  * Increased oxygen consumption  
  * Increased muscle protein catabolism
* **Duration:** Effects can last **up to years after burn injury**
* **Clinical importance:** Highlights essence of nutritional support in burn care

### BURN CLASSIFICATION

#### BY DEPTH (SKIN DESTRUCTION)

* **Superficial burns:** Epidermis only
* **Partial-thickness burns:** Part of dermis
  * **Superficial partial-thickness:** Less healing time, usually no scarring/functional impairment
  * **Deep partial-thickness:** Requires surgical debridement and grafting
* **Full-thickness burns:** Entire dermis destroyed

### INHALATION INJURY

#### EPIDEMIOLOGY AND IMPACT

* **Prevalence:** Present in **2-14% of admitted burn patients**
* **⚠️ Critical impact:** **Significantly increases mortality** above that predicted by extent of skin burn alone

#### THREE DISTINCT TYPES (CAN OCCUR CONCURRENTLY)

##### TYPE 1: UPPER AIRWAY THERMAL/CHEMICAL INJURY

* **Location:** Supraglottic structures
* **Mechanism:** Thermal and chemical injury to upper airway
* **Pathophysiology:** Due to efficient heat exchange and protective reflexes, **true thermal injuries usually limited to upper airway**
* **Clinical effects:**
  * Mucosal edema and swelling
  * **Ultimate risk:** Upper airway obstruction
  * **Progressive nature:** Edema can progress over early hours of active fluid resuscitation

## 

* **Location:** Tracheobronchial-alveolar
* **Mechanism:** Noxious chemical injury from combustion byproducts
* **Pathophysiology:**
  * Loss of surfactant
  * Airway ciliary dysfunction  
  * Sloughing of mucosal epithelium
  * Release of inflammatory mediators causing bronchoconstriction
  * Increased exudative loss into interstitium
  * Cast formation
* **Complications:** Loss of hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, increased risks of:
  * Bronchospasm
  * Lower airway obstruction
  * Atelectasis  
  * Pneumonia

##### TYPE 3: SYSTEMIC TOXICITY

* **Mechanism:** Toxic gas exposure (e.g., carbon monoxide, hydrogen cyanide)
* **Effects:** Systemic toxicity from absorbed toxic gases

### DIAGNOSIS OF INHALATION INJURY

#### CLINICAL ASSESSMENT

* **⚠️ No standard diagnostic criteria** exist for inhalation injury
* **Approach:** Combination of clinical findings and additional testing most commonly used

#### RISK FACTORS

* **High likelihood indicators:**
  * Burns in enclosed space
  * Patient unconscious at scene
  * Larger cutaneous burns

## 

* **Head/neck:** Deep facial burns, soot on/swelling of oro/nasopharynx
* **Respiratory:** Carbonaceous sputum, hoarse voice, inability to swallow

#### DIAGNOSTIC MODALITIES

##### FLEXIBLE BRONCHOSCOPY (FB)

* **Primary tool:** Most common diagnostic modality
* **High accuracy:** Found to have high accuracy for diagnosis and severity stratification
* **Prognostic value:** FB diagnosis useful for predicting mortality and ICU length of stay

##### ADDITIONAL TESTS

* **Imaging:** Chest CT, virtual bronchoscopy
* **Functional:** Xenon ventilation-perfusion scan, pulmonary function tests
* **⚠️ Limitation:** Initial chest x-ray **lacks reliability** to detect inhalation injury

#### CLINICAL MONITORING REQUIREMENTS

* **High index of suspicion:** Essential given progressive nature
* **Repeated monitoring:** Crucial for diagnosis and management
* **Airway patency:** Particularly important for airway obstruction risk
* **⚠️ Important:** Patients with inhalation injury can present **fully conscious with patent airway**

**Bottom Line:** Burns require understanding of biphasic pathophysiology with early shock phase followed by hypermetabolic phase, while inhalation injury significantly increases mortality and requires high clinical suspicion with aggressive monitoring for progressive airway compromise.

## 13.1. PRINCIPLES OF PATIENT TRANSFER

**💡 Transfer should not be undertaken unless the risks of continuing care in the patient's current location are greater than the potential benefits of transfer.**

### TYPES OF TRANSFER

* **Primary transfer**: From injury/illness scene to initial receiving facility
* **Extended primary transfer**: From scene directly to specialist trauma centre, bypassing local hospitals
* **Secondary transfer (interhospital)**: Movement between hospital facilities for specialist care or capacity
* **Intrahospital transfer**: Movement within same hospital for investigations, procedures, or ICU admission
* **Repatriation**: Return to referring hospital or facility closer to patient's home

### INDICATIONS FOR INTERHOSPITAL TRANSFER

**Clinical transfers:**
* Specialist surgical intervention unavailable (neurosurgery, cardiothoracic, vascular, spinal)
* Specialist medical services (ECMO, burns unit, trauma centre)
* Higher level ICU care required
* Interventional radiology services

**Capacity transfers:**
* ICU beds unavailable at current facility
* Specialist services temporarily unavailable
* ⚠️ **Important**: Capacity transfers should only occur during working hours when additional staffing support available

### DECISION-MAKING FRAMEWORK

* **Senior consultant authority**: Both referring and receiving consultants must agree to transfer
* **Risk-benefit analysis**: Potential benefit must outweigh transfer risks
* **Timing considerations**:
  * Daytime transfers are safer (more staff available, reduced fatigue)
  * Night transfers and poor weather significantly increase risk
  * ⚠️ **Balance speed against thorough preparation** - never rush an inadequately prepared transfer

**Bottom Line:** Transfer decisions require senior consultant agreement from both institutions, with careful risk-benefit analysis weighing potential benefits against the inherent risks of moving critically ill patients.

## 13.2. INTRAHOSPITAL VS. INTERHOSPITAL TRANSFER

**Critically ill patients are in a dynamic and often precarious physiological state; transfer potentially exposes them to additional harm and instability.**

### INTRAHOSPITAL TRANSFER

**Common purposes:**
* CT scanning and other imaging
* Interventional radiology procedures
* Operative theatre
* Transfer to/from ICU

**Key considerations:**
* **Incidence of complications**: Up to two-thirds of intrahospital transfers involve difficulty or complication
* Often first transfer experience for trainees under distant supervision
* Same systematic preparation as interhospital transfer required
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: ICS Guideline Recommendation 17 states similar process should be followed whether moving within or to another hospital

### INTERHOSPITAL TRANSFER

**Additional challenges:**
* Transfer team isolation from institutional resources
* Small teams often unfamiliar with each other and patient
* One-third of interhospital complications result in adverse outcomes
* Road/air platform considerations apply
* Longer duration increases exposure to environmental hazards

### ADVERSE OUTCOMES FROM POOR TRANSFER

| Outcome Category | Incidence |
|------------------|-----------|
| Major physiological derangement | 15% |
| Patient/relative dissatisfaction | 7% |
| Prolonged hospital stay | 4% |
| Physical/psychological injury | 3% |
| Death | 2% |

**⚠️ Critical insight**: **52-91% of these incidents are preventable** with proper patient selection, transfer planning, and equipment preparation

**Bottom Line:** Both intrahospital and interhospital transfers require the same systematic approach, with the majority of adverse events being preventable through careful preparation and standardized protocols.

## 13.3. PRE-TRANSFER STABILISATION (ABCDE APPROACH)

**💡 Systematic pre-transfer assessment and stabilization using the ABCDE framework is essential to prevent preventable complications during patient movement.**

### AIRWAY

* **Secure before transfer**: Intubation during transfer is suboptimal and high-risk
* **Anticipate deterioration**: Consider elective intubation for at-risk patients
* **Confirm placement**: Radiological confirmation and documentation of ETT position
* **Secondary equipment**: Nasogastric tube to prevent aspiration
* ⚠️ **Warning**: Once airway secured, equipment for emergency reintubation must accompany patient

### BREATHING

* **Pre-transfer optimisation**:
  * Verify adequate ventilation via arterial blood gases
  * Correct hypoxia and hypercarbia before departure
  * Set ventilator parameters that maintain stability
* **Monitoring requirements**:
  * Reliable pulse oximetry (ear/adhesive probes preferred over pegboard)
  * **Mandatory end-tidal CO2 monitoring** for all intubated patients
* **Pneumothorax management**: Identify and drain before transfer
* **Oxygen calculation**: 2 × (patient consumption + ventilator consumption) for duration
* **Backup ventilation**: Bag-valve mask assembly essential

### CIRCULATION

* **Complete resuscitation before departure**
* **Vascular access**:
  * Minimum two accessible, well-secured IV sites
  * Avoid femoral central lines (kinking risk during positioning)
  * Prefer internal jugular or subclavian central access
* **Haemodynamic monitoring**:
  * Arterial line with non-invasive BP as backup
  * All lines properly secured with visible insertion sites
* **Vasopressor/inotrope considerations**:
  * Establish infusions with easy titration capability
  * Calculate pump rates for dose adjustments during transfer
* **Blood products**: Transport if ongoing transfusion; return unused units to referring laboratory

### DISABILITY

* **Sedation and analgesia**: Adequate levels (typically propofol + opioid combination)
* **Neuromuscular blockade**: Paralysis often advantageous to prevent fighting ventilator
* **Blood glucose monitoring**: Check before departure; recheck during prolonged transfers if insulin running
* **Seizure control**: Ensure seizures controlled before transfer
* **ICP management**: Agree strategy with receiving neurosurgical centre before departure

### EXPOSURE AND PACKAGING

* **Fracture stabilisation**: Immobilize cervical spine, pelvis, and long bone fractures
* **Line and drain security**: Secure all tubes, drains, and catheters
* **Thermoregulation**:
  * Prevent hypothermia (vacuum mattresses, warm blankets, active warming)
  * Continuous temperature monitoring
  * Limit patient exposure during preparation
* **Patient positioning**:
  * Secure patient to transfer trolley
  * Vacuum mattress patients secured within mattress first
  * ⚠️ **Avoid spinal boards** for interhospital transfer (pressure injury risk)

**Bottom Line:** Systematic ABCDE preparation with particular attention to securing the airway before departure, optimizing ventilation, completing resuscitation, ensuring adequate sedation, and preventing hypothermia is essential for safe patient transfer.

## 13.4. PERSONNEL REQUIREMENTS

**💡 Personnel undertaking transfers should have the appropriate competencies, qualifications, and experience, with attendance at a suitable transfer course highly desirable. [AAGBI/ICS Guidelines]**

### TRANSFER TEAM COMPOSITION

**UK Standard (Secondary/Critical Care Transfer):**
* Minimum: Doctor (anaesthetic/critical care specialty trainee or consultant) + trained assistant
* Support: Paramedic crew from ambulance service

**Competency requirements:**
* Appropriate qualifications and experience for patient acuity
* Supervised transfer experience before independent practice
* Regular training including technical and non-technical skills
* Team familiarity through joint training when possible

### LEVEL-BASED STAFFING REQUIREMENTS

| Patient Level | Clinical Status | Personnel Required |
|---------------|-----------------|---------------------|
| **Level 0** | Ward patients, stable | No specialized accompaniment |
| **Level 1** | At risk of deterioration, requires additional monitoring | Trained nurse or paramedic |
| **Level 2** | Single organ system failure, requiring invasive monitoring | Trained competent personnel (nurse + paramedic minimum) |
| **Level 3** | Advanced respiratory support, multi-organ failure | Physician + critical care nurse + paramedic |

### TRAINING STANDARDS

**Essential competencies:**
* Advanced airway management including RSI and difficult airway
* Mechanical ventilation and troubleshooting
* Cardiovascular resuscitation and vasopressor management
* Interpretation and response to monitoring
* Familiarity with transfer equipment
* Non-technical skills: communication, teamwork, decision-making

**Recommended courses:**
* Safe Transfer and Retrieval (STaR) course
* Critical Care Safe Transfer Training (CCSTT)
* Advanced Life Support certification

### SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS

* **Trainee supervision**: Transfer team composition dictates supervision scope; ensure appropriate backup arrangements
* **Single doctor transfers**: Higher risk; ensure robust communication and escalation plans
* **Fatigue management**: Consider return journey requirements when planning staffing

**Bottom Line:** Transfer team composition should be appropriate to patient acuity, with all personnel appropriately trained and experienced, and clear governance structures for supervision of trainees.

## 13.5. EQUIPMENT AND MONITORING

**💡 Dedicated equipment must be designed for transfer and appropriate for use aboard the selected transfer platform. A hospital critical care department has large teams with experts and equipment on hand, but these are not available in-transit.**

### CORE EQUIPMENT REQUIREMENTS

**Transfer trolley:**
* Purpose-designed with docks for monitoring, pumps, ventilator, oxygen
* Adequate battery backup for all electrical equipment
* Lockable wheels and secure patient restraints
* Vehicle inverter connection capability

**Monitoring equipment:**
* Cardiac monitor/defibrillator with pacing capability
* Pulse oximetry
* Non-invasive blood pressure
* Invasive blood pressure (if indicated)
* End-tidal CO2 capnography (mandatory for intubated patients)
* Temperature monitoring

**Respiratory equipment:**
* Transfer ventilator familiar to team
* Oxygen cylinders with calculated reserves
* Bag-valve mask assembly
* Suction with portable power source
* Airway management equipment (intubation kit, surgical airway kit)

**Infusion equipment:**
* Multiple syringe pumps
* Volumetric pump for fluids
* Spare batteries for all pumps

### OXYGEN REQUIREMENTS CALCULATION

**Formula for oxygen reserve:**
* Duration = Available oxygen (L) ÷ Flow rate (L/min)
* **Safety margin**: Carry 2× anticipated requirement

**Example calculation:**
* E-cylinder contains ~660 L when full
* Patient requires 8 L/min, ventilator consumes 4 L/min
* Total consumption = 12 L/min
* Duration for single cylinder = 55 minutes
* For 60-minute transfer: require minimum 2 cylinders

### EMERGENCY MEDICATION PREPARATION

**Pre-drawn medications:**
* Vasopressors in appropriate concentrations
* Sedatives (propofol, midazolam)
* Opioids (fentanyl, morphine)
* Neuromuscular blocking agents
* Emergency drugs (adrenaline, atropine, calcium, sodium bicarbonate)

**Labelling requirements:**
* Clear labels on all syringes
* Pre-calculated doses for high-risk patients
* Diluting solutions and spare syringes/lines available

### EQUIPMENT CHECKLISTS

**Pre-departure checklist categories:**
1. Patient identification and documentation
2. Monitoring functional and attached
3. Oxygen supply adequate
4. Ventilator settings confirmed and functioning
5. IV access secure and medications running
6. Emergency medications prepared
7. Communication devices functional
8. Personnel equipped (warm clothing, identification, contact numbers)

⚠️ **Critical**: Equipment-related problems account for up to one-third of transfer mishaps

**Bottom Line:** Equipment must be purpose-designed for transfer, carefully checked before departure using standardized checklists, with particular attention to adequate oxygen reserves, functional monitoring, and prepared emergency medications.

## 13.6. MONITORING DURING TRANSFER

**💡 Vital signs should be recorded every 5 minutes per AAGBI standards during patient transfer.**

### CONTINUOUS MONITORING

**Mandatory parameters:**
* Cardiac rhythm (ECG)
* Pulse oximetry (SpO2)
* End-tidal CO2 (EtCO2) - mandatory for intubated patients
* Blood pressure (invasive with non-invasive backup)

**Additional monitoring as indicated:**
* Temperature
* Central venous pressure
* Intracranial pressure (neurosurgical patients)
* Urine output (for prolonged transfers)

### ADVERSE PHYSIOLOGICAL CHANGES

**Common complications during transfer:**

| Category | Findings | Incidence |
|----------|----------|-----------|
| Cardiocirculatory | BP changes >20 mmHg, arrhythmias | 40-47% |
| Respiratory | Desaturation, hypercapnia/hypocapnia | 2-17% |
| Equipment-related | Malfunction, disconnection | Up to 33% |
| Neurological | ICP elevation, pupil changes | Variable |

### ENVIRONMENTAL FACTORS AFFECTING PHYSIOLOGY

**Vibration and motion:**
* May cause discomfort and nausea
* Risk of tube/line dislodgement
* Interference with monitoring accuracy

**Noise:**
* Impairs communication
* Prevents auscultation
* Limits alarm audibility

**Acceleration/deceleration:**
* Transient hypertension and dysrhythmias
* Patient movement on stretcher
* Fluid shifts

### ALTITUDE EFFECTS (AIR TRANSPORT)

**Physiological considerations:**
* Hypobaric hypoxia reduces inspired oxygen
* **Gas expansion**: Boyle's law affects pneumothorax, bowel distension, ETT cuff pressure
* Third-space fluid shifts causing edema and hypovolemia
* Reduced cabin temperature and humidity

**Mitigation strategies:**
* Increase FiO2 before ascent
* Drain pneumothorax before air transfer
* Replace air in ETT cuff with saline
* Active warming measures

### DOCUMENTATION REQUIREMENTS

**Essential recording:**
* Vital signs every 5 minutes
* All significant events and interventions
* Drug administration with times and doses
* Patient identifiers and transfer details
* Referring and receiving consultant names
* Transfer team details and timings

**Standards**: Standardized documentation across networks for both inter- and intrahospital transport; copies for referring hospital, receiving hospital, and audit purposes

**Bottom Line:** Continuous monitoring with documented vital signs every 5 minutes, awareness of environmental physiological effects, and comprehensive documentation are essential for safe patient transfer.

## 13.7. COMMUNICATION AND HANDOVER

**💡 Clinical handover is one of the most critical steps in a patient's journey. Performed well, clinical handover should ensure that lapses in continuity of patient care, errors, and harm are reduced.**

### ISBAR STRUCTURED COMMUNICATION

| Component | Content |
|-----------|---------|
| **I** - Identification | Your name, role, location; patient name and identifiers |
| **S** - Situation | Current clinical status, reason for transfer, acute issues |

### MULTI-STAGE HANDOVER PROCESS

**Stage 1: Consultant-to-consultant discussion**
* Agreement to transfer between senior clinicians

**Stage 2: Referring team to transfer team**

**Stage 3: Transfer team to receiving team**
* Structured verbal handover on arrival

* Patient demographics and clinical summary

* Expected time of arrival

### DOCUMENTATION FOR TRANSFER

**Essential documents to accompany patient:**
* Clinical summary/referral letter
* Relevant investigation results and imaging
* Drug chart and recent medication administration times
* Transfer documentation template
* Patient property and valuables list

### BARRIERS TO EFFECTIVE HANDOVER

**Common problems:**
* Interruptions and distractions during handover
* Omission of critical information
* Inclusion of irrelevant detail
* Power differential between specialties
* Time pressure

**Solutions:**
* Structured ISBAR approach reduces variation
* Protected handover time without interruptions
* Written documentation to supplement verbal handover
* Active confirmation of understanding

**Bottom Line:** Structured handover using ISBAR at multiple stages of the transfer process reduces communication errors and ensures critical information is transmitted between clinical teams.

## 13.8. TRANSPORT MODALITIES

* Lower level monitoring and intervention capability

**Advanced life-support ambulance:**
* Life-threatening conditions requiring intervention capability

* Purpose-built critical care ambulance

* Direct door-to-door transfer
* Easier to stop for interventions
* More space for procedures

* Traffic delays unpredictable

### AIR TRANSPORT

**Rotor-wing (helicopter):**
* Optimal for distances ~80-240 km
* Direct access to hospital helipads
* Faster than ground for medium distances

* Distances >240 km

* Weather conditions
* Receiving facility access

### PLATFORM-SPECIFIC CONSIDERATIONS

* Cramped conditions limit intervention capability
* Position equipment for accessibility

**Noise and vibration:**
* Impairs monitoring and communication
* Motion sickness prevention for team
* Ear protection may be required

**Temperature control:**
* Variable cabin temperatures
* Active warming essential
* Monitor patient temperature continuously

**Electrical interference:**
* May affect monitoring equipment
* Ensure compatibility with aircraft systems

**Bottom Line:** Selection of transport modality depends on distance, patient condition, weather, and receiving facility access, with each platform presenting specific advantages and challenges requiring appropriate preparation.

# 14. DAMAGE CONTROL SURGERY AND RESUSCITATION

<!-- EPA-5:K6:damage_control EPA-5:S3:hemorrhage_control -->

## 14.1. OVERVIEW AND DEFINITIONS

**Damage Control Surgery (DCS)** is a systematic approach to the critically injured patient involving abbreviated surgical intervention focused on hemorrhage control and contamination prevention, followed by ICU resuscitation, with delayed definitive repair.

**Damage Control Resuscitation (DCR)** comprises resuscitation strategies that address the "lethal triad" of coagulopathy, hypothermia, and acidosis through permissive hypotension, early balanced blood product transfusion, and hemostatic adjuncts.

**Historical Context:**
* Term "damage control" originated from US Navy First World War protocols for keeping damaged ships combat-capable until definitive repair
* Adapted to trauma surgery by Rotondo and Schwab (1993)
* Foundational principles trace to Lucas and Ledgerwood's 1976 work on liver packing

**Fundamental Paradigm Shift:**
* Traditional surgery: Anatomical restoration in single operation
* Damage control: **Physiological restoration** prioritized over anatomical repair
* Recognition that patient physiology determines survival more than injury pattern

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Damage control principles apply to approximately 3-8% of severely injured patients who are physiologically exhausted and would not survive prolonged definitive surgery.

---

## 14.2. THE LETHAL TRIAD

**The three interconnected physiological derangements that drive mortality:**

| Component | Mechanism | Threshold | Effect on Coagulation |
|-----------|-----------|-----------|----------------------|
| **Coagulopathy** | Tissue injury, hypoperfusion, acidosis, hemodilution | INR >1.5, PTT >60s | Primary driver - thrombin generation impaired |
| **Hypothermia** | Heat loss, cold fluids, exposed body cavities | <35°C | Each 1°C drop reduces clotting efficiency by ~10% |
| **Acidosis** | Tissue hypoperfusion, lactate accumulation | pH <7.2 | Impairs coagulation factor function, especially fibrinogen |

**Key Interactions:**
* Hypothermia → inhibits coagulation enzyme function → worsens coagulopathy
* Acidosis → impairs clotting factor activity → worsens coagulopathy
* Coagulopathy → ongoing hemorrhage → worsens hypothermia and acidosis
* **Vicious cycle becomes irreversible without intervention**

⚠️ **Safety Warning:** Once established, the lethal triad becomes increasingly difficult to reverse. Early recognition and aggressive intervention is critical.

---

## 14.3. INDICATIONS FOR DAMAGE CONTROL SURGERY

### 14.3.1. PHYSIOLOGICAL TRIGGERS

**Classic Triggers (any one indicates DCS):**
* Core temperature <35°C
* pH <7.2
* Base deficit >15 mEq/L
* Lactate >4 mmol/L
* PT >16 seconds or INR >1.5
* PTT >60 seconds

**Clinical Triggers:**
* >10 units PRBC anticipated or received
* Intraoperative cardiac arrest
* Massive hemorrhage with hemodynamic instability
* Complex injury pattern requiring >90 minutes surgery
* Multiple body regions requiring surgical intervention
* Non-mechanical bleeding observed intraoperatively

### 14.3.2. INJURY PATTERNS FAVOURING DCS

| Injury Type | DCS Indication |
|-------------|----------------|
| Hepatic | Major venous injury, bilobar parenchymal destruction |
| Pancreatic | Combined pancreaticoduodenal injury |
| Vascular | Multiple major vessel injuries, inaccessible bleeding |
| Pelvic | Open pelvic fracture with massive hemorrhage |
| Multiple body regions | Competing priorities (abdomen + thorax + extremity) |

🔑 **High-Yield:** The decision for DCS should be made **EARLY** - ideally within 30 minutes of laparotomy, before the lethal triad becomes established.

---

## 14.4. THREE PHASES OF DAMAGE CONTROL SURGERY

### 14.4.1. PHASE 1: ABBREVIATED SURGERY (0-2 HOURS)

**Objectives:**
1. **Stop hemorrhage** - packing, ligation, shunting
2. **Control contamination** - stapling, drainage (NOT anastomosis)
3. **Temporary closure** - avoid forced closure

**Specific Techniques by Organ System:**

**Abdominal:**
* Four-quadrant packing
* Perihepatic packing for hepatic hemorrhage
* Splenectomy (NOT splenorrhaphy)
* Bowel stapling without anastomosis
* Damage control laparostomy with negative pressure dressing

**Vascular:**
* Temporary intravascular shunts (TIVS) - maintain perfusion
* Ligation of non-essential vessels
* Balloon catheter tamponade for inaccessible hemorrhage

**Thoracic:**
* Pulmonary tractotomy for through-and-through injuries
* Temporary stapling of lung parenchyma
* Packing for non-anatomic bleeding

**Orthopedic:**
* External fixation of long bone fractures
* Pelvic binder or external fixator for unstable pelvis
* Fasciotomy for compartment syndrome risk

**Temporary Abdominal Closure Options:**

| Technique | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|-----------|------------|---------------|
| **Negative pressure dressing** | Removes fluid, maintains domain, tension on fascia | Requires equipment |
| **Bogota bag** | Simple, inexpensive | No fluid removal, risk of ACS |
| **Skin-only closure** | Quick, simple | Risk of ACS, loss of domain |
| **Towel clips** | Fastest | High ACS risk, temporary only |

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT) is the preferred temporary closure method - reduces fascial retraction and facilitates delayed primary closure.

### 14.4.2. PHASE 2: ICU RESUSCITATION (12-48 HOURS)

**Objectives:**
1. **Rewarming** to normothermia (>36°C)
2. **Correction of coagulopathy** - blood products, coagulation factors
3. **Correction of acidosis** - restore perfusion, support ventilation
4. **Hemodynamic optimization** - adequate volume, appropriate vasopressors

**Rewarming Strategies:**
* Warm IV fluids (39-40°C)
* Forced air warming blankets
* Increase ambient temperature
* Continuous renal replacement therapy (if indicated)
* Warm peritoneal lavage (if abdomen open)

**Coagulopathy Correction:**
* Goal-directed transfusion using TEG/ROTEM
* Fibrinogen replacement if <1.5 g/L
* Platelet transfusion if <100 × 10⁹/L
* FFP for factor replacement
* Avoid excessive crystalloid (dilutional coagulopathy)

**Acidosis Correction:**
* Restore tissue perfusion (primary goal)
* Optimize oxygen delivery
* Mechanical ventilation support
* Consider bicarbonate only if pH <7.1 and refractory

**Endpoints for Phase 2 Completion:**
* Temperature >36°C
* pH >7.25
* Lactate normalizing (clearance >20%/hour)
* Coagulopathy corrected (INR <1.5)
* Hemodynamically stable on minimal support

### 14.4.3. PHASE 3: DEFINITIVE SURGERY (24-72 HOURS)

**Objectives:**
1. Remove packing (incremental, watch for rebleeding)
2. Perform definitive repairs
3. Restore gastrointestinal continuity
4. Definitive wound/fascial closure

**Timing Considerations:**
* Too early (<24 hours): Risk of recurrent coagulopathy
* Too late (>72 hours): Increased adhesions, infection risk, fascial retraction
* Optimal window: 24-48 hours post-Phase 1

**Open Abdomen Management:**
* Aim for primary fascial closure within 7-10 days
* Delayed closure mortality 22% vs forced primary closure mortality 85%
* Monitor for abdominal compartment syndrome (IAP >20 mmHg)

---

## 14.5. DAMAGE CONTROL RESUSCITATION PRINCIPLES

### 14.5.1. PERMISSIVE HYPOTENSION

**Definition:** Deliberately accepting lower-than-normal blood pressure until surgical hemorrhage control achieved.

**Rationale:**
* Higher BP disrupts immature clots
* Increased bleeding with aggressive fluid resuscitation
* Crystalloid hemodilution worsens coagulopathy

**Target Blood Pressure by Clinical Scenario:**

| Scenario | SBP Target | MAP Target |
|----------|------------|------------|
| **Penetrating torso trauma** | 80-90 mmHg | 50-60 mmHg |
| **Blunt trauma (no TBI)** | 90-100 mmHg | 60-65 mmHg |
| **Traumatic brain injury** | >100 mmHg | >80 mmHg |
| **Spinal cord injury** | >90 mmHg | >85 mmHg |
| **Extended transport (>60 min)** | 100 mmHg | - |

⚠️ **Safety Warning:** Permissive hypotension is CONTRAINDICATED in traumatic brain injury - higher cerebral perfusion pressure required to prevent secondary brain injury.

### 14.5.2. BLOOD PRODUCT RATIOS

**Target: 1:1:1 Ratio (PRBC:FFP:Platelets)**

**Evidence Base:**
* PROPPR trial: 1:1:1 achieved hemostasis faster than 1:1:2
* No difference in 24-hour or 30-day mortality
* Reduced death from exsanguination at 24 hours with 1:1:1

**Massive Transfusion Protocol (MTP):**
* Predefined packages (e.g., 6 PRBC : 6 FFP : 1 Platelet apheresis)
* Blood bank notified early
* Coolers delivered to OR at regular intervals
* Goal-directed adjustment based on TEG/ROTEM

**Whole Blood:**
* Low-titer O-negative whole blood (LTOWB) increasingly used
* Contains all components in physiological ratios
* Reduced component wastage
* Logistically simpler
* Military experience shows potential survival advantage

### 14.5.3. TRANEXAMIC ACID (TXA)

**Mechanism:** Antifibrinolytic - inhibits plasminogen activation

**CRASH-2 Protocol:**
* 1g IV over 10 minutes
* Then 1g IV over 8 hours
* Alternative: 2g IV bolus (prehospital)

**Critical Timing:**
* **<1 hour**: Greatest survival benefit
* **1-3 hours**: Still beneficial
* **>3 hours**: May INCREASE mortality - CONTRAINDICATED

🔑 **High-Yield:** TXA benefit decreases by 10% for every 15 minutes of delay. Give as early as possible, never after 3 hours.

### 14.5.4. CALCIUM REPLACEMENT

**Rationale:** Citrate in blood products chelates ionized calcium

**Protocol:**
* 1g calcium gluconate (or 300mg calcium chloride) after first unit
* Repeat every 4 units of blood products
* Monitor ionized calcium if available
* Target iCa >1.0 mmol/L

**Calcium Chloride vs Gluconate:**
* Chloride: 3× more ionized calcium per volume, but caustic
* Gluconate: Safer peripheral administration
* No outcome difference in clinical trials

### 14.5.5. GOAL-DIRECTED COAGULATION MANAGEMENT

**Viscoelastic Testing (TEG/ROTEM):**
* Point-of-care coagulation assessment
* Guides targeted blood product administration
* Reduces transfusion volume
* Identifies specific coagulation defects

**TEG Parameters and Treatment:**

| Parameter | Defect | Treatment |
|-----------|--------|-----------|
| Prolonged R time | Clotting factor deficiency | FFP, PCC |
| Reduced MA | Platelet dysfunction/low fibrinogen | Platelets, Cryoprecipitate |
| Reduced α-angle | Low fibrinogen | Cryoprecipitate, Fibrinogen concentrate |
| Increased LY30 | Hyperfibrinolysis | TXA |

---

## 14.6. RESUSCITATIVE ENDOVASCULAR BALLOON OCCLUSION OF AORTA (REBOA)

**Definition:** Endovascular placement of occlusion balloon in aorta to control non-compressible torso hemorrhage.

**Zones of Deployment:**

| Zone | Location | Indication |
|------|----------|------------|
| **Zone I** | Descending thoracic aorta (diaphragm to celiac axis) | Abdominal/junctional hemorrhage |
| **Zone II** | Paravisceral aorta | NO DEPLOYMENT - organ ischemia |
| **Zone III** | Infrarenal aorta | Pelvic/junctional hemorrhage |

**Advantages:**
* Less invasive than resuscitative thoracotomy
* Bridge to definitive surgery
* Can be placed in ED or OR

**Complications:**
* Distal ischemia (limit occlusion time)
* Vascular injury
* Reperfusion injury on deflation
* Balloon rupture

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** REBOA is a BRIDGE, not a destination. Maximum occlusion time before complications is approximately 60-90 minutes.

---

## 14.7. ANAESTHETIC CONSIDERATIONS IN DAMAGE CONTROL

### 14.7.1. INDUCTION AND AIRWAY

**Dose Modification:**
* Reduce induction agent doses by 50-75% in hemorrhagic shock
* Consider ketamine (hemodynamic stability)
* Avoid propofol bolus in severe hypovolemia
* Etomidate suitable but adrenal suppression concern
* Full paralytic dose for rapid sequence

**Recommended Agents:**

| Agent | Dose Modification | Notes |
|-------|-------------------|-------|
| Ketamine | 0.5-1 mg/kg | Maintains SVR, may be first choice |
| Etomidate | 0.2-0.3 mg/kg | Hemodynamically neutral |
| Midazolam | 0.05-0.1 mg/kg | Less cardiodepressant |
| Fentanyl | 1-2 mcg/kg | Reduces induction agent requirement |
| Rocuronium | Full dose 1.2 mg/kg | Rapid paralysis critical |

### 14.7.2. MAINTENANCE

**Key Principles:**
* Avoid volatile agents in severe shock (vasodilation)
* Consider ketamine-based TIVA
* Minimize crystalloid - use blood products
* Aggressive temperature management
* Prepare vasopressors early

**Vasopressor Use:**
* Norepinephrine first-line if volume-replete but hypotensive
* Vasopressin useful in refractory vasodilatory shock
* Avoid dopamine (arrhythmogenic)

### 14.7.3. MONITORING

**Essential:**
* Arterial line (beat-to-beat BP)
* Large-bore IV access ×2 (minimum 16G)
* Urinary catheter
* Core temperature (continuous)
* Point-of-care coagulation (TEG/ROTEM)

**Consider:**
* Central venous access (large-bore introducer for RIC)
* Cardiac output monitoring in prolonged cases
* ABG for lactate, base deficit monitoring

### 14.7.4. COMMUNICATION

**Critical Elements:**
* Clear handover from trauma team
* Continuous communication with surgeon about:
  * Blood loss estimates
  * Expected duration
  * Decision for damage control vs definitive
* Blood bank liaison for MTP management
* ICU preparation during Phase 1

---

## 14.8. OUTCOMES AND EVIDENCE

**Benefits of DCS/DCR Approach:**
* Improved survival in physiologically exhausted patients
* Earlier abdominal closure
* Decreased length of ICU stay
* Reduced healthcare costs
* Allows borderline patients to undergo early definitive surgery

**Key Studies:**

| Study | Finding |
|-------|---------|
| PROPPR (2015) | 1:1:1 ratio achieved hemostasis faster; trend to reduced mortality |
| CRASH-2 (2010) | TXA reduces death from bleeding by 28% when given <1 hour |
| PAMPer (2018) | Prehospital plasma reduces 30-day mortality (23% vs 33%) |

**Contraindications to DCS:**
* Patient can tolerate definitive repair (physiology stable)
* Injury severity does not require abbreviated approach
* Limited surgical resources (DCS requires planned return)

**Bottom Line:** Damage control surgery is a life-saving approach for the 3-8% of severely injured patients with physiological exhaustion. The paradigm prioritizes physiological restoration over anatomical repair through abbreviated surgery, ICU resuscitation, and delayed definitive repair. Key principles include early recognition of the lethal triad, permissive hypotension, balanced transfusion ratios (1:1:1), early TXA, aggressive rewarming, and goal-directed coagulation management. The anaesthetist plays a critical role in resuscitation, hemostatic adjunct administration, and communication with the surgical team.

# 15. ACUTE COMPARTMENT SYNDROME

<!-- EPA-5:K7:compartment_syndrome EPA-5:S4:fasciotomy_recognition -->

## 15.1. OVERVIEW AND PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

**Definition:** Acute compartment syndrome (ACS) occurs when interstitial pressure within a closed osseo-fascial space rises above capillary perfusion pressure, causing muscle and nerve ischemia.

**Pathophysiology:**
* Tissue perfusion = Capillary perfusion pressure (CPP) - Interstitial fluid pressure
* Increased compartment pressure → venous collapse → capillary collapse → arterial compromise
* Cascade: Ischemia → inflammation → edema → further pressure increase
* **Vicious cycle** within non-expandable fascial compartment

**Normal Values:**
* Resting compartment pressure: 0-8 mmHg
* With exertion: up to 10 mmHg
* Critical threshold: >30 mmHg

⚠️ **Safety Warning:** ACS is a surgical EMERGENCY. Delayed diagnosis leads to irreversible muscle necrosis, contracture, amputation, and death. One of the most litigated conditions in orthopaedic surgery.

---

## 15.2. ETIOLOGY AND RISK FACTORS

### 15.2.1. CAUSES

**Traumatic (75% of cases):**
* Tibial shaft fractures (36%) - most common cause
* Soft tissue injuries (23%)
* Forearm/supracondylar fractures (9%)
* Crush injuries
* High-energy trauma

**Non-Traumatic:**
* Reperfusion injury (vascular surgery, embolectomy)
* Burns (circumferential)
* Snake envenomation
* Tight casts/dressings/splints
* Extravasation of IV fluids
* Bleeding disorders/anticoagulation
* Prolonged immobilization (lithotomy position)
* Exercise-induced (chronic/exertional)

### 15.2.2. RISK FACTORS

| Risk Factor | Notes |
|-------------|-------|
| Male gender | 10× higher incidence |
| Age ≤35 years | Most common demographic |
| Anticoagulation | Increased bleeding into compartment |
| Hypotension | Reduced perfusion pressure |
| Peripheral vascular disease | Impaired tissue tolerance |
| Double-level tibial fracture | High-risk injury pattern |

---

## 15.3. ANATOMICAL CONSIDERATIONS

### 15.3.1. LOWER LEG COMPARTMENTS (MOST COMMON SITE)

| Compartment | Contents | Nerve | Clinical Signs if Affected |
|-------------|----------|-------|---------------------------|
| **Anterior** | Tibialis anterior, EHL, EDL | Deep peroneal | Foot drop, first web space numbness |
| **Lateral** | Fibularis longus/brevis | Superficial peroneal | Weak foot eversion, dorsum numbness |
| **Superficial Posterior** | Gastrocnemius, soleus | Tibial (sural) | Weak plantar flexion |
| **Deep Posterior** | Tibialis posterior, FHL, FDL | Tibial | Weak toe flexion, sole numbness |

**Anterior compartment** is most commonly affected (tightest fascial boundaries).

### 15.3.2. OTHER COMMON SITES

* **Forearm**: Volar and dorsal compartments
* **Thigh**: Anterior, posterior, medial compartments
* **Hand**: Interosseous compartments
* **Foot**: 9 compartments (medial, lateral, superficial, 4 interosseous, calcaneal)
* **Buttock/Gluteal**: Following prolonged immobilization

---

## 15.4. CLINICAL PRESENTATION - THE 6 Ps

### 15.4.1. CLINICAL SIGNS

| Sign | Description | Timing | Reliability |
|------|-------------|--------|-------------|
| **Pain** | Out of proportion to injury; increased with passive stretch | EARLIEST | Most reliable |
| **Pressure** | Tense, swollen compartment on palpation | Early | Moderate |
| **Paresthesia** | Sensory deficit in nerve distribution | Within 30 minutes | High |
| **Paralysis** | Motor weakness/loss | Within 4 hours | Late sign |
| **Pallor** | Pale/mottled skin | Late | Low |
| **Pulselessness** | Absent distal pulses | Very late | Low (arterial collapse) |

🔑 **High-Yield:** Do NOT wait for all 6 Ps! **Pain out of proportion** and **pain with passive stretch** are the EARLIEST and MOST RELIABLE signs. Pulselessness indicates arterial occlusion and is a LATE finding.

### 15.4.2. CLINICAL EXAMINATION

**Key Tests:**
* **Passive stretch test**: Gently dorsiflex the toes/ankle (for anterior compartment) - exquisite pain indicates ACS
* **Compartment palpation**: Tense, firm compartment (compare to unaffected limb)
* **Neurological examination**: Sensory testing in nerve distributions
* **Vascular examination**: Pulses may be present until late stages

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Pain that is refractory to adequate analgesia (increasing opioid requirement) should raise immediate suspicion for compartment syndrome.

---

## 15.5. DIAGNOSIS

### 15.5.1. CLINICAL DIAGNOSIS

**ACS is primarily a CLINICAL diagnosis** - do not delay treatment for confirmatory testing if clinical suspicion is high.

**High-risk scenarios requiring heightened vigilance:**
* Tibial fractures (especially high-energy)
* Unconscious/intubated patients
* Patients with regional anaesthesia
* Post-revascularization (reperfusion injury)
* Polytrauma patients

### 15.5.2. COMPARTMENT PRESSURE MEASUREMENT

**Indications:**
* Equivocal clinical examination
* Unreliable patient (unconscious, intoxicated, head injury)
* Patients with regional anaesthesia
* Serial monitoring in high-risk patients

**Technique:**
* Stryker STIC Monitor (validated standard)
* Needle manometry systems
* Measure at level of fracture AND 5cm above/below
* Measure ALL at-risk compartments

**Diagnostic Thresholds:**

| Criterion | Threshold | Notes |
|-----------|-----------|-------|
| **Absolute pressure** | >30 mmHg | Traditional threshold |
| **Delta pressure (ΔP)** | ≤30 mmHg | ΔP = Diastolic BP - Compartment pressure; **PREFERRED** |

🔑 **High-Yield:** Delta pressure (ΔP) accounts for individual blood pressure variability and is the preferred diagnostic criterion. A ΔP ≤30 mmHg for >2 hours is diagnostic.

---

## 15.6. MANAGEMENT

### 15.6.1. IMMEDIATE MEASURES

**While awaiting definitive surgery:**
1. **Remove ALL external compression** - split casts to skin, remove dressings, loosen splints
2. **Position limb at HEART LEVEL** - NOT elevated (elevation reduces perfusion pressure)
3. **Maintain normotension** - avoid hypotension (reduces ΔP)
4. **Correct hypovolemia** - adequate perfusion pressure critical
5. **Supplemental oxygen**
6. **Analgesia** - but monitor for breakthrough pain
7. **Serial examinations** - every 30-60 minutes
8. **Prepare for emergency fasciotomy**

⚠️ **Safety Warning:** Do NOT elevate the limb above heart level - this reduces arterial perfusion pressure and worsens ischemia.

### 15.6.2. SURGICAL MANAGEMENT - FASCIOTOMY

**Indications:**
* Clinical diagnosis of ACS
* Compartment pressure >30 mmHg
* Delta pressure ≤30 mmHg

**Timing Critical:**

| Time to Fasciotomy | Functional Recovery |
|-------------------|---------------------|
| <6 hours | ~100% recovery |
| 6-12 hours | ~68% normal function |
| >12 hours | ~8% normal function |
| >8 hours | Irreversible myoneural damage begins |
| >36 hours | Fasciotomy NOT recommended (risk outweighs benefit) |

**Lower Leg Technique (Double-Incision, Four-Compartment):**
* **Anterolateral incision**: Releases anterior and lateral compartments
  * 15-20 cm incision, midway between tibial crest and fibula
  * Identify and protect superficial peroneal nerve
* **Posteromedial incision**: Releases superficial and deep posterior compartments
  * 15-20 cm incision, 2cm posterior to medial tibial border
  * Detach soleus from tibia to access deep posterior

**Principles:**
* Full-length incisions (inadequate length = inadequate decompression)
* Release ALL fascial layers
* Debride necrotic muscle (non-contractile, pale, non-bleeding)
* Leave wounds OPEN
* Plan re-look surgery at 48-72 hours

### 15.6.3. WOUND MANAGEMENT

* **Initial**: Leave fasciotomy wounds open
* **Negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT)**: Preferred - reduces edema, facilitates closure
* **Delayed primary closure**: At 7-10 days when edema resolved
* **Skin grafting**: If primary closure not possible
* **Repeat debridement**: At 48-72 hours to assess muscle viability

---

## 15.7. ANAESTHETIC CONSIDERATIONS

### 15.7.1. GENERAL ANAESTHESIA FOR FASCIOTOMY

**Considerations:**
* Often emergency/out-of-hours surgery
* May be polytrauma patient with other injuries
* Risk of rhabdomyolysis and hyperkalaemia
* Potential for significant blood loss
* Plan for multiple returns to theatre

**Monitoring:**
* Standard ASA monitoring
* Arterial line (if time permits)
* Core temperature (often hypothermic trauma patient)
* Urinary catheter (monitor for myoglobinuria)
* Consider TEG/ROTEM if coagulopathic

**Medications:**
* Avoid suxamethonium if rhabdomyolysis suspected (hyperkalaemia risk)
* Prepare calcium and insulin for hyperkalaemia treatment
* Aggressive fluid resuscitation to prevent renal injury
* Consider mannitol/bicarbonate for rhabdomyolysis prevention

### 15.7.2. REGIONAL ANAESTHESIA CONTROVERSY

**The Debate:**
* Traditional concern: RA may mask pain and delay diagnosis
* Counter-argument: Adequate RA allows more thorough examination of other injuries

**Evidence:**
* No case reports where peripheral nerve block alone delayed diagnosis
* 4 case reports where epidural analgesia associated with delayed diagnosis
* Overall evidence does NOT support absolute contraindication

**Recommendations for High-Risk Patients:**

| Practice Point | Rationale |
|----------------|-----------|
| Regional anaesthesia NOT absolutely contraindicated | No evidence of harm if vigilant monitoring |
| Use lower concentration local anaesthetic | Allows breakthrough pain detection |
| Avoid dense motor block | Preserves ability to assess paralysis |
| Serial clinical examinations mandatory | Cannot rely on pain alone |
| Low threshold for pressure monitoring | Objective data when clinical signs equivocal |
| Clear documentation and handover | Communication of risk to all team members |
| Patient and family education | Report new/worsening pain immediately |

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** If using regional anaesthesia in high-risk patients, ensure breakthrough pain can be detected by using dilute local anaesthetic concentrations and maintaining regular clinical examinations.

---

## 15.8. COMPLICATIONS

### 15.8.1. DELAYED/MISSED DIAGNOSIS COMPLICATIONS

* **Volkmann's ischemic contracture** - irreversible muscle fibrosis and shortening
* **Permanent nerve damage** - sensory loss, motor weakness
* **Muscle necrosis** and scarring
* **Chronic pain**
* **Amputation**
* **Rhabdomyolysis** → Acute kidney injury → Multi-organ failure → Death

### 15.8.2. POST-FASCIOTOMY COMPLICATIONS

| Complication | Incidence |
|--------------|-----------|
| Altered sensation | 77% |
| Tethered scars | 26% |
| Muscle herniation | 13% |
| Chronic venous insufficiency | Common |
| Wound complications | Common |
| Prolonged healing | Expected |

---

## 15.9. SPECIAL SCENARIOS

### 15.9.1. UNCONSCIOUS/INTUBATED PATIENT

* Cannot report pain
* **Must rely on:**
  * Tense compartment on palpation
  * Serial pressure measurements
  * Comparison with contralateral limb
  * High index of suspicion based on injury pattern

### 15.9.2. PAEDIATRIC PATIENTS

* "The 3 As": Anxiety, Agitation, increasing Analgesic requirements
* May not localize pain well
* Lower threshold for pressure measurement
* Smaller compartments = may develop ACS at lower pressures

### 15.9.3. CHRONIC EXERTIONAL COMPARTMENT SYNDROME

* Recurrent, exercise-induced symptoms
* Symptoms resolve with rest
* Diagnosis: Compartment pressure during/after exercise
* Treatment: Elective fasciotomy (non-emergency)

**Bottom Line:** Acute compartment syndrome is a surgical emergency where early recognition is critical for limb salvage. Pain out of proportion to injury and pain with passive stretch are the earliest and most reliable clinical signs. A delta pressure (diastolic BP - compartment pressure) ≤30 mmHg is the preferred diagnostic criterion. Fasciotomy within 6 hours achieves near-complete functional recovery; delay beyond 8 hours results in irreversible damage. Regional anaesthesia is not contraindicated but requires heightened vigilance with serial examinations and low threshold for pressure monitoring in high-risk patients.
