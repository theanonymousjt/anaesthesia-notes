# 1. INTRODUCTION TO ECHOCARDIOGRAPHY AND POCUS
<!-- EPA-6:K3:pocus EPA-6:K6:cardiac_output EPA-6:EQ6:shock_diagnosis -->

> **Syllabus**: [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Equipment & Monitoring|Part B: Equipment & Monitoring]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Procedural Competency|Part C: Procedural Skills]]

Echocardiography and point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) have become essential components of perioperative and peri-interventional anesthesia practice. Over the past four decades, these modalities have evolved from specialized tools used primarily in cardiac surgery to ubiquitous instruments employed across the spectrum of anesthesia practice.

## 1.1. APPLICATIONS OF ECHOCARDIOGRAPHY

Echocardiography serves multiple crucial functions in anesthesia practice:

* **Preoperative assessment:** Evaluation of cardiac structure and function
* **Perioperative monitoring:** Real-time assessment of hemodynamic status
* **Diagnostic tool:** Rapid identification of causes of hemodynamic instability
* **Procedural guidance:** Direction of interventions such as valve repairs, septal defect closures, and cannula placements
* **Post-intervention evaluation:** Assessment of surgical or interventional results

### 1.1.1. ASA/SCA PRACTICE GUIDELINES APPLICATIONS

The 2010 ASA/SCA Practice Guidelines define specific evidence-based applications for perioperative TEE:

* **Cardiac and thoracic aortic procedures:**
  * Detection of abnormalities with sensitivity/specificity ranges: valvular abnormalities, ventricular dysfunction, aortic dissection, atrial septal defects
  * Confirmation/refinement of preoperative diagnosis
  * Detection of new or unsuspected pathology
  * Assessment of surgical intervention results
* **Catheter-based intracardiac procedures:**
  * Guidance of device placement (occluder devices, percutaneous valvular procedures)
  * Detection of complications (tamponade, pericardial effusion)
  * Assessment when intracardiac ultrasound unavailable
* **Noncardiac surgery:**
  * Detection of venous air embolism and patent foramen ovale
  * Assessment of hemodynamic compromise
  * Evaluation of intracardiac emboli and wall motion abnormalities
* **Critical care:**
  * Diagnosis when other modalities inadequate or unavailable
  * Assessment of unexplained hemodynamic instability
  * Guidance of therapeutic interventions

## 1.2. ROLE IN PERIOPERATIVE CARE

Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) offer complementary approaches:

* **TEE advantages:** Ideal during surgery due to unimpeded imaging access
  * Provides superior visualization of posterior cardiac structures
  * Less affected by obesity, mechanical ventilation, or surgical drapes
  * Can be performed continuously during procedures
* **TTE advantages:** Non-invasive approach preferred outside the operating room
  * Avoids risks associated with esophageal probe placement
  * Better visualization of anterior structures (e.g., pulmonic valve)
  * Easier to perform serially in the pre/postoperative setting

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Basic (hemodynamic) TEE allows rapid identification of sources of hemodynamic instability, while advanced (diagnostic) TEE provides specific therapeutic/surgical recommendations.

⚠️ **Safety Consideration:** TEE is invasive with potential for serious complications including esophageal rupture and mediastinitis, mandating careful patient selection.

**Bottom Line:** Echocardiography and POCUS have transformed from specialized cardiac surgery tools to essential components of modern anesthesia practice across all perioperative settings, with each modality offering specific advantages and applications. The 2010 ASA/SCA Practice Guidelines provide evidence-based recommendations that emphasize risk-benefit analysis and appropriate patient selection for optimal clinical outcomes.

# 2. PHYSICS OF ULTRASOUND

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Ultrasound Physics|Part A: Ultrasound Physics]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Equipment & Monitoring|Part B: Equipment & Monitoring]]

## 2.1. CHARACTERISTICS OF SOUND WAVES

Ultrasound imaging utilizes sound waves with frequencies beyond the human audible range (>20 kHz), typically 2-10 MHz for diagnostic purposes. Understanding the physical principles of sound is essential for optimizing image acquisition:

* **Wave equation:** Velocity (V) = Frequency (f) × Wavelength (λ)
  * With velocity being relatively constant in soft tissues, frequency and wavelength are inversely related
  * Higher frequencies provide better image resolution but decreased tissue penetration
* **Pulse-echo principle:** Piezoelectric elements (PZEs) in the transducer:
  * Convert electrical energy to sound waves when activated
  * Emit brief pulses of ultrasound (few microseconds)
  * "Listen" between pulses for returning echoes
  * Convert returned sound waves back to electrical signals
* **Tissue velocity differences:** Sound travels at different speeds through various tissues:
  * Average soft tissue velocity = 1,540 m/s (used by ultrasound machines for calculations)
  * Air: 330 m/s
  * Lung: 600 m/s
  * Bone: 4,080 m/s
  * Muscle: 1,580 m/s
  * Fat: 1,450 m/s
  * Blood: 1,560 m/s

![Characteristics of a sound wave](ECHO_Untitled_1.png_f6a036.png)

## 2.2. REFLECTION

Image creation depends on sound waves reflecting from interfaces between tissues with different acoustic impedance:

* **Acoustic impedance:** Related to tissue density
* **Reflection principles:**
  * Greater difference in acoustic impedance = stronger reflection
  * Structures with high acoustic impedance (bone, calcification, metal) reflect most ultrasound
  * Homogeneous media (e.g., blood) reflect minimal ultrasound and appear echolucent (black)
* **Acoustic shadow:** Areas beyond highly reflective structures where ultrasound cannot penetrate
  * Common with prosthetic valves, calcifications, and bony structures
  * Limits visualization of deeper structures

![Acoustic shadow artifact](ECHO_Untitled_2.png_02c285.png)

## 2.3. RESOLUTION AND IMAGE OPTIMIZATION

### 2.3.1. TEMPORAL RESOLUTION

* **Frame rate:** Number of complete image refreshes per second
  * Affected by sector width and depth settings
  * Minimum of 25 fps typically needed for smooth motion perception
  * Higher frame rates provide better visualization of rapidly moving structures
* **M-mode (motion mode):** Plots a single scan line against time
  * Provides superior temporal resolution compared to 2D imaging
  * Useful for measuring rapidly moving structures (valve leaflets)
  * Optimal for precise timing of cardiac events

### 2.3.2. SPATIAL RESOLUTION

* **Axial resolution:** Ability to distinguish two structures along the path of the ultrasound beam
  * Determined by wavelength/frequency (higher frequency = better axial resolution)
  * 5 MHz transducer: ~0.3 mm resolution
  * 10 MHz transducer: ~0.15 mm resolution
* **Lateral resolution:** Ability to distinguish structures side by side
  * Worsens with increasing depth (approximately depth/50)
  * At 5 cm depth: ~1 mm resolution
  * At 10 cm depth: ~2 mm resolution
  * Can be optimized by adjusting focal point
* **Elevational resolution:** Ability to distinguish structures perpendicular to scan plane
  * Approximately depth/30
  * At 10 cm depth: ~3.3 mm resolution

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Structures closer to the probe are imaged with better resolution. When possible, position the structure of interest as close to the probe as feasible to optimize image quality.

## 2.4. KNOBOLOGY AND IMAGE OPTIMIZATION

Mastering the "knobology" of the ultrasound machine is critical for obtaining high-quality images.

### 2.4.1. BASIC CONTROLS (THE "KNOBS")

* **Power Button:** Platforms vary, but the basic functionality is consistent.
* **Trackpad/Trackball:** Used for navigating the screen and placing measurement calipers.
* **Freeze/Live:** Switch between real-time 2D imaging and a still image.
* **Save/Store:** Save clips (loops) or still images for documentation.

### 2.4.2. IMAGE OPTIMIZATION TECHNIQUES

1. **Depth:**
   * **Principle:** Sound waves attenuate (diminish) as they go deeper.
   * **Goal:** Visualize all relevant structures without "wasting space" at the bottom of the screen.
   * **Adjustment:** Decrease depth to magnify near-field structures; increase depth to see deeper structures.
   * **Trade-off:** ↑ Depth = ↓ Frame Rate (more time needed for sound to return).

2. **Gain:**
   * **Principle:** Amplification of returning sound waves (like volume on a radio).
   * **Overall Gain:** Increases brightness of the entire image.
       * **Too High:** Noise is amplified, "washed out" image.
       * **Too Low:** Image is too dark, details lost.
   * **Time Gain Compensation (TGC):** Adjusts gain at specific depths to correct for attenuation.
       * **Near Field:** Decrease gain (strong signals).
       * **Far Field:** Increase gain (weak signals due to attenuation).

3. **Frequency:**
   * **Principle:** Inverse relationship between resolution and penetration.
   * **Selection:**
       * **High Frequency (e.g., 6-13 MHz):** ↑ Resolution, ↓ Penetration. Used for superficial structures (vascular, nerve blocks).
       * **Low Frequency (e.g., 2-5 MHz):** ↓ Resolution, ↑ Penetration. Used for deep structures (cardiac, abdominal).

4. **Sector Size:**
   * **Principle:** Width of the scanning field.
   * **Optimization:** Narrowing the sector size increases frame rate and temporal resolution (useful for fast-moving valvular structures).

5. **Focus (Focal Zone):**
   * **Principle:** Narrowest part of the ultrasound beam has the best lateral resolution.
   * **Adjustment:** Move the focal marker to the depth of the structure of interest.

Selecting the correct transducer is the first step in any exam:

## 2.6. COMMON ARTIFACTS

Understanding artifacts is crucial for accurate image interpretation and avoiding pitfalls.

### 2.6.1. REVERBERATION AND MIRROR IMAGE
* **Reverberation:** "Back-and-forth" bouncing between parallel reflectors. Appears as equidistant parallel lines (e.g., A-lines in lung ultrasound).
* **Comet Tail (Ring Down):** A form of reverberation from air/fluid interfaces or metal, appearing as a vertical hyperechoic line fading into depth (e.g., B-lines in lung edema).
* **Mirror Image:** Reflection from a strong reflector (like the diaphragm) creates a duplicate image of a structure (e.g., liver appearing in the chest).

### 2.6.2. ACOUSTIC SHADOWING AND ENHANCEMENT
* **Acoustic Shadowing:** Sound cannot pass through a strong reflector (bone, stone, metal). Appears as a black (anechoic) shadow behind the object.
* **Posterior Acoustic Enhancement:** Sound travels through a fluid-filled structure with little attenuation, resulting in increased brightness (enhancement) behind it (e.g., behind the bladder or cysts).

### 2.6.3. SIDE LOBE ARTIFACT
* **Mechanism:** Minor off-axis beams encounter a strong reflector and map it to the main beam's path.
* **Appearance:** Debris-like echoes in anechoic structures (e.g., "pseudo-sludge" in the gallbladder or bladder).

![Reverberation and Mirror-Image artifact](ECHO_Untitled.png_b55fae.png)

**Bottom Line:** Master the "Knobology" (Depth, Gain, Frequency) to optimize every image. Recognize artifacts to distinguish true pathology from physics illusions.

**Bottom Line:** Understanding ultrasound physical principles is essential for optimizing image acquisition and accurately interpreting findings. Recognizing limitations in resolution and common artifacts helps prevent diagnostic errors.

# 3. DOPPLER ECHOCARDIOGRAPHY

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Doppler Principles|Part A: Doppler Principles]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Hemodynamic Monitoring|Part B: Hemodynamic Monitoring]]

## 3.1. THE DOPPLER PRINCIPLE

The Doppler principle forms the foundation for blood flow velocity measurement in echocardiography:

* **Basic concept:** Change in frequency of reflected sound waves from moving objects
  * Objects moving toward the transducer: Reflected wave has higher frequency
  * Objects moving away from the transducer: Reflected wave has lower frequency
* **Blood flow measurement:** Red blood cells serve as reflectors
  * Magnitude of frequency shift is proportional to velocity of moving blood cells
  * Only the component vector directly toward or away from the transducer is measured
* **Angle correction:** Accurate measurements require proper alignment
  * Doppler beam should be within 20° of blood flow direction
  * Misalignment of 20° = 6% underestimation of true velocity
  * Greater misalignment leads to significant measurement error

![The Doppler principle](ECHO_Untitled_1.png_f6a036.png)

## 3.2. DOPPLER IMAGING MODES

### 3.2.1. PULSED WAVE DOPPLER

* **Principle:** Measures velocity at a specific point (sample volume) in the cardiac structure
  * Transducer alternates between transmitting and receiving signals
  * Time gating allows measurement at a precise location
  * Velocity is plotted against time in a spectral display
* **Nyquist limit:** Maximum measurable velocity
  * Limited by sampling rate of the reflected signal
  * When velocity exceeds Nyquist limit, "aliasing" occurs
  * Aliasing displays velocities in the opposite direction
* **Applications:**
  * Transmitral and transtricuspid flow assessment
  * Pulmonary vein and hepatic vein flow patterns
  * LVOT velocity measurement

![Pulsed wave Doppler spectral display](ECHO_Untitled_1.png_f6a036.png)

### 3.2.2. COLOR DOPPLER IMAGING

* **Principle:** Modified form of pulsed wave Doppler displaying velocities as colors
  * Multiple sample volumes analyzed simultaneously within a region of interest (ROI)
  * Color map assigns specific colors to velocities and directions
* **Conventions:**
  * Flow toward the probe: Red shades ("BART" - Blue Away, Red Toward)
  * Flow away from the probe: Blue shades
  * Higher velocities: Brighter/lighter shades
  * Velocities exceeding Nyquist limit: Aliasing with color reversal
* **Applications:**
  * Rapid identification of regurgitant or stenotic jets
  * Assessment of flow direction and pattern
  * Identification of shunts

![Color Doppler imaging](ECHO_Untitled_1.png_f6a036.png)

### 3.2.3. TISSUE DOPPLER IMAGING

* **Principle:** Measures lower velocity, higher amplitude tissue movement
  * Similar methodology to pulsed wave Doppler
  * Filters detect tissue movement rather than blood flow
* **Applications:**
  * Assessment of myocardial movement and function
  * Mitral and tricuspid annular velocities (e', a', s')
  * Diastolic function assessment
  * Myocardial performance index calculation

![Tissue Doppler imaging](ECHO_Untitled_1.png_f6a036.png)

### 3.2.4. CONTINUOUS WAVE DOPPLER IMAGING

* **Principle:** Continuously emits and receives ultrasound along a scan line
  * Measures all velocities along the path simultaneously
  * No upper limit for velocity measurement (no aliasing)
  * Cannot determine the precise location of measured velocities ("range ambiguity")
* **Applications:**
  * High-velocity jet measurement (valvular stenosis)
  * Pressure gradient calculations across valves
  * Regurgitant jet assessment
  * Cardiac output calculation

![Continuous wave Doppler imaging](ECHO_Untitled_1.png_f6a036.png)

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** For optimal velocity measurement, obtain multiple windows and select the highest measured velocity, which indicates the best alignment with the direction of blood flow.

⚠️ **Important Consideration:** The same valve lesion assessed from different windows may yield significantly different velocity measurements due to alignment variations.

**Bottom Line:** Doppler echocardiography provides essential information about blood flow velocity, direction, and patterns, enabling quantitative hemodynamic assessment. Each Doppler mode has specific advantages and limitations that determine its appropriate clinical applications.

# 4. HEMODYNAMIC ASSESSMENT

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Cardiovascular Physiology|Part A: Cardiovascular Physiology]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Hemodynamic Monitoring|Part B: Hemodynamic Monitoring]]

## 4.1. VELOCITY

Blood flow velocity measurement forms the foundation of hemodynamic assessment:

* **Measurement methods:** Using Doppler imaging techniques
  * Spectral display: Velocity plotted against time
  * Color Doppler: Visual representation using color mapping
  * Requires careful alignment with direction of blood flow for accuracy
* **Clinical applications:**
  * Normal and abnormal flow patterns identification
  * Stenotic and regurgitant lesion assessment
  * Shunt evaluation
  * Cardiac output calculation

## 4.2. GRADIENT

Pressure gradients across cardiac structures can be calculated from velocity measurements:

* **Simplified Bernoulli equation:** Gradient = 4 × Velocity²
  * Fundamental equation for gradient calculation in clinical practice
  * Neglects viscous friction and acceleration components
  * Valid for high-velocity jets (>1 m/s)
* **Gradient types:**
  * Peak gradient: Using peak velocity (Vmax)
  * Mean gradient: Average of instantaneous gradients throughout flow period
  * Used for grading severity of stenotic and regurgitant lesions
* **Derived pressure calculations:**
  * RVSP or PASP = 4(VTR)² + RAP
  * MPAP = 4(Vearly PI)² + RAP
  * PADP = 4(Vend PI)² + RAP
  * LAP = SBP – 4(VMR)²
  * LVEDP = DBP – 4(Vend AI)²

![Calculation of gradients](ECHO_Untitled_1.png_f6a036.png)

## 4.3. STROKE DISTANCE, STROKE VOLUME, AND CARDIAC OUTPUT

Velocity measurements can be used to calculate stroke volume and cardiac output:

* **Velocity Time Integral (VTI):** Area under the velocity curve
  * Also called "stroke distance"
  * Represents the distance a blood cell travels during one cardiac cycle
  * Measured in centimeters
* **Stroke Volume calculation:** SV = VTI × Cross-sectional area
  * Cross-sectional area is calculated from measured diameter (π × r²)
  * Most commonly measured at LVOT
  * SV = LVOT VTI × LVOT area
* **Cardiac Output calculation:** CO = SV × Heart Rate
  * Serial measurements useful for tracking response to interventions
  * Assumes constant cross-sectional area between measurements

![Calculation of stroke volume](ECHO_Untitled_1.png_f6a036.png)

## 4.4. THE CONTINUITY EQUATION

Based on the principle of conservation of mass in fluid mechanics:

* **Basic principle:** Flow through sequential orifices must be equal (in absence of regurgitation or shunting)
  * VTI₁ × Area₁ = VTI₂ × Area₂
  * Used when one orifice area is unknown
* **Clinical applications:**
  * Calculation of stenotic valve areas (especially aortic valve)
  * Assessment of intracardiac shunts
  * Prosthetic valve function evaluation
* **LVOT-based calculations:**
  * Aortic valve area = (LVOT VTI × LVOT area) ÷ AV VTI
  * Dimensionless index = LVOT VTI ÷ AV VTI
  * Simplified approach useful for serial measurements

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Sequential VTI measurements can track changes in stroke volume without repeating diameter measurements, assuming the orifice size remains constant.

⚠️ **Important Consideration:** The continuity equation assumes no significant regurgitation or shunting between the measured areas, which may lead to inaccurate calculations if present.

**Bottom Line:** Doppler-derived hemodynamic assessment allows quantification of pressure gradients, flow volumes, and valve areas that are essential for diagnosing and grading cardiac conditions, monitoring therapy response, and guiding perioperative management.

# 5. PERIOPERATIVE ECHOCARDIOGRAPHY IMAGING

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Cardiac Anatomy|Part A: Cardiac Anatomy]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Perioperative Monitoring|Part B: Perioperative Monitoring]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Procedural Skills|Part C: Procedural Skills]]

## 5.1. INDICATIONS

Echocardiography has become an integral component of perioperative care across surgical specialties:

### 5.1.1. ASA/SCA EVIDENCE-BASED INDICATIONS

The 2010 ASA/SCA Practice Guidelines provide specific evidence-based recommendations for perioperative TEE use:

#### 5.1.1.1. CARDIAC AND THORACIC AORTIC PROCEDURES

* **Class I Recommendations (Should be used):**
  * **All open heart procedures** (e.g., valvular procedures)
  * **All thoracic aortic surgical procedures**
  * **Should be considered in CABG surgeries** for:
    1. Confirm and refine preoperative diagnosis
    2. Detect new or unsuspected pathology
    3. Adjust anesthetic and surgical plan accordingly
    4. Assess results of surgical intervention

* **Pediatric Considerations:**
  * In small children, use should be considered case-by-case due to unique risks (e.g., bronchial obstruction)

* **Evidence Base:**
  * Literature reports high sensitivity/specificity for detection of:
    * Mitral valve abnormalities (87-100% sensitivity)
    * Valvular abscesses (90-100% sensitivity)
    * Aortic dissection (67-100% sensitivity)
    * Prosthetic valve complications (92-100% sensitivity)

#### 5.1.1.2. CATHETER-BASED INTRACARDIAC PROCEDURES

* **TEE may be used for:**
  * Transcatheter intracardiac procedures when general anesthesia provided and intracardiac ultrasound unavailable
  * **Strong agreement for specific procedures:**
    * Septal defect closure
    * Atrial appendage obliteration
    * Catheter-based valve replacement and repair

#### 5.1.1.3. NONCARDIAC SURGERY

* **TEE may be used when:**
  * Nature of planned surgery or patient's cardiovascular pathology might result in severe hemodynamic, pulmonary, or neurologic compromise
  * **Should be used** when unexplained life-threatening circulatory instability persists despite corrective therapy (if equipment and expertise available)

* **Specific Clinical Scenarios (Strong Expert Agreement):**
  * Unexplained persistent hypotension
  * Persistent unexplained hypoxemia
  * Life-threatening hypotension anticipated
  * Known/suspected cardiovascular pathology with potential compromise

* **Surgical Procedures with Evidence Support:**
  * Lung transplantation
  * Major abdominal or thoracic trauma
  * Liver transplantation (consultant agreement)
  * Open abdominal aortic procedures (consultant agreement)

#### 5.1.1.4. CRITICAL CARE

* **Should be used when:**
  * Diagnostic information expected to alter management cannot be obtained by transthoracic echocardiography or other modalities in timely manner
  * Unexplained persistent hypotension in critical care patients
  * Persistent unexplained hypoxemia occurs

* **Evidence Base:**
  * High diagnostic yield for: regurgitant valvular lesions, aortic dissection, intracardiac masses, tamponade, ventricular failure, hypovolemia

### 5.1.2. TRADITIONAL INDICATIONS

* **Cardiovascular surgery applications:**
  * Preoperative diagnosis confirmation
  * Identification of additional pathology
  * Intraoperative hemodynamic monitoring
  * Procedural guidance
  * Surgical repair evaluation
  * Assessment of left heart deairing
* **Noncardiovascular surgery applications:**
  * Hemodynamic monitoring
  * Diagnosis of unexpected instability
  * Volume status assessment
  * Evaluation of treatment response
* **Critical care applications:**
  * Diagnosis in acute hemodynamic compromise
  * Ongoing monitoring of unstable patients
  * Guidance of interventions
  * Assessment of treatment efficacy
* **Resuscitation applications:**
  * Identification of cause of cardiac arrest
  * Evaluation of CPR adequacy
  * Monitoring response to resuscitative efforts

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The ASA/SCA Practice Guidelines emphasize that TEE recommendations are based on risk-benefit analysis for individual patients, and should not be performed when equipment/skilled examiners are unavailable or when the procedure cannot be performed safely.

## 5.2. TRANSESOPHAGEAL ECHOCARDIOGRAPHY (TEE)
<!-- EPA-10:K3:tee EPA-10:EQ2:echocardiography -->

### 5.2.1. COMPLICATIONS AND CONTRAINDICATIONS

TEE is a semi-invasive procedure with significant risks that must be weighed against benefits:

* **Complications:**
  * Minor: Odynophagia, sore throat, dental injuries (common)
  * Major: Esophageal perforation, bleeding, respiratory compromise (rare but potentially fatal)
  * Mortality: Up to 1 in 3,000 in cardiac surgery patients
  * Mucosal injury: Present in >80% of patients on endoscopy, though usually subclinical

#### 5.2.1.1. ASA/SCA PRACTICE GUIDELINES CONTRAINDICATIONS

Based on the 2010 ASA/SCA Practice Guidelines, contraindications are categorized by expert consensus:

* **Absolute Contraindications (Strong Expert Agreement):**
  * Esophageal stricture
  * Tracheoesophageal fistula
  * Post-esophageal surgery
  * Esophageal trauma
  * Previous esophagectomy or esophagogastrectomy

* **Relative Contraindications (Consultant Agreement):**
  * Esophageal varices (Expert consensus: equivocal)
  * Post-radiation therapy (Expert consensus: equivocal)
  * Previous bariatric surgery (Expert consensus: equivocal)
  * Zenker diverticulum (Consultant agreement, ASA member equivocal)

* **Generally NOT Contraindications (Expert Disagreement):**
  * Barrett esophagus
  * Hiatal hernia
  * Large descending aortic aneurysm
  * Unilateral vocal cord paralysis
  * Dysphagia (ASA members disagree, consultants equivocal)

#### 5.2.1.2. RISK-BENEFIT ASSESSMENT AND PRECAUTIONS

* **ASA/SCA Recommendations:**
  * TEE may be used for patients with oral, esophageal, or gastric disease if expected benefit outweighs potential risk
  * Appropriate precautions must be applied, including:
    * Considering alternative imaging modalities (e.g., epicardial echocardiography)
    * Obtaining gastroenterology consultation
    * Using smaller probe when available
    * Limiting examination duration
    * Avoiding unnecessary probe manipulation
    * Using most experienced operator available

⚠️ **Safety Warning:** TEE should only be performed when benefits outweigh risks, and operators must be trained to recognize and manage potential complications. The risk-benefit analysis should consider individual patient factors and availability of alternative diagnostic methods.

### 5.2.2. PROBE INSERTION AND MANIPULATION

Proper technique minimizes risk of complications:

* **Preparation:**
  * Disinfected probe with or without sheath (per institutional protocol)
  * Apply acoustic gel to TEE sheath to eliminate air interfaces
  * Check sheath tip for air bubbles which may impair imaging
* **Insertion technique:**
  * Anesthetized patient: Blind insertion or under laryngoscopy guidance
  * Mandible elevation: Using thumb inside mouth behind incisors or jaw thrust
  * Apply gentle, constant pressure during advancement
  * Abort if significant resistance encountered
  * Upper esophageal images appear at 25-30 cm depth in adults
* **Probe manipulation options:**
  * Advance/withdraw
  * Turn left/right
  * Anteflex/retroflex
  * Left/right lateral flex
  * Ultrasound plane rotation

![TEE probe positions](ECHO_Untitled_1.png_f6a036.png)

### 5.2.3. IMAGE ORIENTATION

Understanding spatial relationships is essential for image interpretation:

* **Standard orientation:**
  * Narrow apex of sector (top of screen): Posterior structures
  * Wide far edge of sector (bottom of screen): Anterior structures
  * Left side of screen (0° rotation): Right-sided cardiac structures
  * Right side of screen (0° rotation): Left-sided cardiac structures
* **Multiplane rotation effects:**
  * 0° rotation: Horizontal plane (transverse)
  * 90° rotation: Vertical plane with inferior structures on left, superior on right
  * 180° rotation: Mirror image of 0° view
  * Transgastric views: Probe below heart, so top of screen is inferior structures

## 5.3. PRINCIPAL TEE IMAGING PLANES

The comprehensive TEE examination includes 28 standard views, while a basic exam may include 12 key views:

### 5.3.1. MIDESOPHAGEAL FOUR CHAMBER VIEW (ME 4C)

* **Acquisition:**
  * Probe at 35-40 cm depth
  * 0° rotation with minor adjustments (0-20°)
  * Retroflexion to align with LV long axis and avoid foreshortening
* **Structures visualized:**
  * All four cardiac chambers
  * Mitral and tricuspid valves
  * Interatrial and interventricular septa
  * Papillary muscles
* **Clinical applications:**
  * Biventricular size and function
  * Valve morphology and function
  * Septal integrity
  * Volume status assessment

![Midesophageal four-chamber view](ECHO_Untitled_1.png_f6a036.png)

### 5.3.2. MIDESOPHAGEAL TWO CHAMBER VIEW (ME 2C)

* **Acquisition:**
  * From ME 4C, rotate to ~90°
  * Center left heart in image
  * Adjust to visualize true apex
* **Structures visualized:**
  * Left atrium and left ventricle
  * Mitral valve
  * Anterior and inferior LV walls
  * Left atrial appendage
  * Coronary sinus
* **Clinical applications:**
  * LV inferior and anterior wall function
  * Left atrial appendage thrombus assessment
  * Mitral valve assessment

![Midesophageal two-chamber view](ECHO_Untitled_1.png_f6a036.png)

### 5.3.3. MIDESOPHAGEAL LONG AXIS VIEW (ME LAX)

* **Acquisition:**
  * From ME 2C, rotate to 120-150°
  * Adjust to visualize LVOT and aortic valve
* **Structures visualized:**
  * LV, LVOT, aortic valve

## 5.4. FOCUSED TRANSTHORACIC ECHOCARDIOGRAPHY

A systematic approach to image acquisition ensures comprehensive assessment.

### 5.4.1. PROBE MANIPULATION TEHCNIQUES
* **Sliding:** Moving the probe’s footprint on the skin (e.g., sliding up/down an intercostal space).
* **Tilting (Fanning):** Angling the probe along its short axis (e.g., tilting the tail up/down) to sweep through a structure.
* **Rocking:** Angling the probe along its long axis.
* **Rotating:** Twisting the probe to change the axis of the beam (e.g., from Long Axis to Short Axis).

### 5.4.2. STANDARD WINDOWS AND VIEWS

#### 5.4.2.1. A. SUBCOSTAL WINDOW
* **Position:** Inferior to the xiphoid process, pressing down and angling up towards the left shoulder.
* **Views:**
    1.  **Subcostal 4-Chamber (SC4C):**
        *   **Anatomy:** Liver, RA, RV, LA, LV. Good for assessing global function and pericardial effusion.
        *   **Technique:** Overhand grip, probe parallel to bed.
    2.  **Subcostal IVC:**
        *   **Anatomy:** IVC entering the RA, hepatic veins.
        *   **Technique:** Rotate 90 degrees counter-clockwise from SC4C (indicator to head), sagittal plane. used for volume status.

#### 5.4.2.2. B. APICAL WINDOW
* **Position:** Point of Maximal Impulse (PMI), typically 5th intercostal space, mid-clavicular line. Patient in Left Lateral Decubitus (LLD) is ideal.
* **Views:**
    1.  **Apical 4-Chamber (A4C):**
        *   **Anatomy:** All four chambers, Mitral and Tricuspid valves. LV apex at top of screen.
        *   **Technique:** Indicator to patient's left (bed position 3 o'clock).
    2.  **Apical 5-Chamber (A5C):**
        *   **Technique:** Tilt tail down (beam angles anteriorly) from A4C. Allows visualization of LVOT and Aortic Valve.
    3.  **Apical 2-Chamber (A2C):**
        *   **Technique:** Rotate 60-90 degrees counter-clockwise from A4C. Shows LV anterior and inferior walls.

#### 5.4.2.3. C. PARASTERNAL WINDOW
* **Position:** Left sternal border, 2nd-4th intercostal space.
* **Views:**
    1.  **Parasternal Long Axis (PLAX):**
        *   **Anatomy:** RV, LV, LA, Aortic Root, MV, AV. Descending aorta seen deep to LA.
        *   **Technique:** Indicator towards Right Shoulder. "Home base" view.
    2.  **Parasternal Short Axis (PSAX):**
        *   **Technique:** Rotate 90 degrees clockwise from PLAX (indicator to Left Shoulder).
        *   **Levels:**
            *   **Aortic Valve:** Mercedes-Benz sign (AV in center), RA, RVOT, LA.
            *   **Mitral Valve:** Fish-mouth appearance.
            *   **Papillary Muscles:** Mid-ventricular level.

### 5.4.3. TROUBLESHOOTING
* **Poor Image:**
    *   **P**osition: Move patient (LLD usually better).
    *   **P**ressure: Apply firm pressure.
    *   **B**reathing: Ask patient to exhale/inhale to move lungs out of view.
    *   **G**el: Ensure adequate contact.
* **Advanced views:**
  * Modified parasternal views for RV
  * Parasternal aortic valve short axis
  * Apical five chamber
  * Apical two and three chamber
  * Suprasternal view
  * Transhepatic views
* **Probe manipulation techniques:**
  * Sliding: Moving toward or away from marker
  * Rocking: Angling along imaging plane axis
  * Tilting: Angling perpendicular to imaging plane
  * Rotation: Moving clockwise/counterclockwise

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** For critical hemodynamic assessment, the transgastric midpapillary short-axis view often provides the most rapid and comprehensive information about biventricular function, volume status, and septal position.

⚠️ **Safety Warning:** Maintain awareness of probe tip position at all times, especially during flexion and rotation maneuvers. Never advance against resistance, and unlock flexion wheels before withdrawal to avoid esophageal injury.

**Bottom Line:** Mastery of standardized imaging planes in both TEE and TTE enables systematic evaluation of cardiac structures and function. Proper probe manipulation technique and understanding of spatial relationships are essential for accurate image acquisition and interpretation.

# 6. THREE-DIMENSIONAL ECHOCARDIOGRAPHY



![3D TEE imaging in the en face view](ECHO_Untitled_1.png_f6a036.png)

## 6.1. ADVANTAGES AND LIMITATIONS

Understanding the technical aspects of 3D echocardiography helps optimize its use:

* **Advantages:**
  * Volume rendering creates depth perception
  * Virtual dissection allows focus on structures of interest
  * Realistic visualization from any spatial point of view
  * Reduced need for mental reconstruction of 3D anatomy
  * More accurate volumetric measurements
* **Limitations:**
  * Lower temporal resolution than 2D imaging
  * Frame rates below 25 fps appear jerky
  * Requires balance between sector size and temporal resolution
  * Learning curve for acquisition and interpretation
  * Limited penetration in challenging acoustic windows
* **Technical considerations:**
  * Shorter depth and narrower sectors increase temporal resolution
  * Multibeat, ECG-gated imaging improves frame rates for larger datasets
  * Careful breath-holding may be required for optimal image quality

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** 3D echocardiography is particularly valuable for mitral valve assessment, providing a surgeon's-eye view that enhances understanding of complex pathology and facilitates surgical planning.

**Bottom Line:** Three-dimensional echocardiography provides enhanced visualization of cardiac structures from any spatial perspective, improving assessment of complex pathology and facilitating interventional procedures, though it requires balancing sector size with temporal resolution.

### 7.1.1. GLOBAL SYSTOLIC FUNCTION

![LV Function Assessment](06-UltrasoundAdult-BasicLV-2025_image12.png)


Assessment of LV systolic function can be performed qualitatively or quantitatively:

* **Qualitative assessment:**
  * Visual estimation of wall thickening
  * Evaluation of cavity size reduction during systole
  * Useful for rapid assessment during hemodynamic instability
* **Quantitative measurements:**
  * **Fractional Shortening (FS):** Percentage change in LV basal diameter
	* Normal: >26-45%
	* Abnormal: <25%
	* Measured in TG mid SAX or PLAX views
  * **Fractional Area Change (FAC):** Percentage change in LV area at midpapillary level
	* Normal: >41-60%
	* Abnormal: <40%
	* Measured in TG mid SAX or PSAX views
  * **Ejection Fraction (EF):** Percentage change in LV volume
	* Normal: >52% (male), >54% (female)
	* Mild impairment: 41-55% (male), 41-53% (female)
	* Moderate impairment: 30-40% (both)
	* Severe impairment: <30% (both)
	* Measured using Simpson's method or area-length method
  * **Mitral Annular Plane Systolic Excursion (MAPSE):**
	* Normal: 12-15 mm
	* EF <50%: <8 mm
	* EF <30%: <7 mm
	* Measured using M-mode in ME4C or A4C views
	* men > 11mm, women >13mm, EF >50%
  * **Tissue Doppler s' velocity:**
	* Normal: >8 cm/s
	* Abnormal: <5 cm/s
	* Measured at lateral mitral annulus
  * **dP/dt:** Rate of LV pressure rise
	* Normal: >1,200 mmHg/s
	* Abnormal: <800 mmHg/s
	* Severe: <500 mmHg/s
	* Requires measurable MR jet with CWD
  * **Myocardial Performance Index (MPI):**
	* Normal: <0.4
	* Abnormal: >0.5
	* Calculated as (IVCT + IVRT)/ET
* **E Point Septal Separation**
	* Anterior leaflet of MV approaching the septum in early diastolic
	- >7mm - <30% EF

### 7.1.2. REGIONAL SYSTOLIC FUNCTION

Assessment of regional wall motion provides insight into coronary artery disease and ischemic events:

* **Classification criteria:**
  * **Normal:** >30% radial shortening and wall thickening
  * **Hypokinesis:** 10-30% radial shortening and wall thickening
  * **Akinesis:** No wall motion or thickening
  * **Dyskinesis:** Outward motion during systole without thickening
  * **Aneurysm:** Outward motion with thin-walled segment
* **Assessment challenges:**
  * Foreshortening can mimic wall motion abnormalities
  * Translocation of ventricle can be misinterpreted
  * Conduction abnormalities and pacing cause dyssynchrony
  * Significant interand intraobserver variability

⚠️ **Important Consideration:** Assessment of regional wall motion can be challenging and even expert echocardiographers demonstrate significant variability in interpretation. Novice practitioners should exercise caution with all but the most obvious abnormalities.

### 7.1.3. STRAIN

Myocardial strain provides objective and reliable measurement of systolic function:

* **Definition:** Measurement of myocardial deformation (shortening, lengthening, thickening, thinning)
* **Measurement methods:**
  * Speckle tracking: Follows movement of acoustic markers through cardiac cycle
  * Tissue Doppler: Measures velocity gradients within myocardium
* **Types of strain:**
  * **Longitudinal strain:** Shortening from base to apex (negative values)
  * **Circumferential strain:** Shortening around short axis (negative values)
  * **Radial strain:** Thickening toward center of cavity (positive values)
* **Strain rate:** Rate of change in myocardial length over time
* **Clinical applications:**
  * Early detection of subclinical dysfunction
  * Assessment of regional function
  * Identification of dyssynchrony
  * Monitoring cardiotoxicity

### 7.1.4. LEFT VENTRICULAR DIASTOLIC FUNCTION

Diastolic dysfunction is an independent predictor of perioperative and long-term morbidity and mortality:

* **Pathophysiology:**
  * Impaired active relaxation (ATP-dependent process)
  * Increased ventricular stiffness or pericardial constriction
  * Results in elevated left atrial pressure, potentially leading to PHTN and RV failure
* **Assessment parameters:**
  * **Mitral inflow velocities:**
	* E wave: Early diastolic velocity
	* A wave: Late diastolic velocity (atrial contraction)
	* E/A ratio: Changes with progression of dysfunction
  
  * **Tissue Doppler imaging:**
	* e': Early diastolic mitral annular velocity
	* Mean of lateral and septal e' used for E/e' ratio
  
  * **Left atrial volume index (LAVI):**
	* > 34 mL/m² indicates diastolic dysfunction
	* Best measured in TTE A4C and A2C views
  
  * **Tricuspid regurgitant jet velocity (TR Vmax):**
	* > 280 cm/s indicates pulmonary hypertension
	* Suggests diastolic dysfunction
* **Diastolic dysfunction grading:**
  * **Grade 1 (Impaired relaxation):**
	* E/A ratio <0.8
	* E/e' ratio <10
	* Normal LAVI and TR Vmax
  
  * **Grade 2 (Pseudonormal):**
	* E/A ratio 0.8-2.0
	* E/e' ratio 10-14
	* LAVI >34 mL/m² or TR Vmax >280 cm/s
  
  * **Grade 3 (Restrictive, potentially reversible):**
	* E/A ratio >2.0
	* E/e' ratio >14
	* LAVI >34 mL/m² and TR Vmax >280 cm/s
  
  * **Grade 4 (Restrictive, fixed):**
	* Grade 3 pattern that doesn't change with preload reduction

## 7.2. LEFT VENTRICULAR PATHOLOGY

### 7.2.1. DILATION

LV dilation often reflects chronic volume overload or cardiomyopathy:

* **Definition:** LV end-diastolic diameter >5.5 cm
* **Common causes:**
  * Chronic mitral or aortic valve regurgitation
  * Dilated cardiomyopathy
  * Chronic ischemic heart disease
  * Volume overload states
* **Assessment approaches:**
  * Measurement in ME4C/A4C or TG mid SAX/PLAX views
  * Compare with body surface area for indexed values

### 7.2.2. VENTRICULAR ANEURYSMS AND PSEUDOANEURYSMS

These represent distinct pathological entities with different management implications:

* **True aneurysms:**
  * Thinned myocardium with wide neck/base
  * Often due to ischemia/infarction
  * Wall contains all layers (endocardium, myocardium, epicardium)
  * Typically more stable, lower risk of rupture
  * Potential source of thrombi and emboli
* **Pseudoaneurysms:**
  * Outpouching through myocardial rupture with narrow neck
  * Contained by surrounding pericardium
  * High risk of uncontrolled rupture and tamponade
  * Surgical intervention often required
  * May occur after MI or cardiac surgery

![Ventricular aneurysms and pseudoaneurysms](ECHO_Untitled_1.png_f6a036.png)

### 7.2.3. HYPERTROPHY

LV hypertrophy represents myocardial remodeling in response to pressure or other stimuli:

* **Definition:**
  * Diastolic wall thickness >1.0 cm in women, >1.1 cm in men
  * Severe: >1.5 cm in women, >1.7 cm in men
* **Patterns:**
  * **Concentric:** Uniform thickening of all walls
  * **Asymmetric:** Disproportionate thickening (commonly septal)
* **Hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy (HOCM):**
  * Asymmetric septal hypertrophy causing LVOT obstruction
  * Often associated with systolic anterior motion (SAM) of mitral valve
  * Turbulent flow in LVOT visible on color Doppler
  * Important cause of perioperative low cardiac output
  * Risk factors: Hypovolemia, hypercontractility, tachycardia


### 7.2.4. RIGHT VENTRICULAR ASSESSMENT

Assessment of the RV is critical in perioperative and critical care settings, particularly for pulmonary embolism and RV infarction.

* **Size:**
  * Normal: RV diameter < 2/3 of LV diameter (RV area < 0.6 LV area).
  * Mild dilation: RV diameter = LV diameter.
  * Severe dilation: RV diameter > LV diameter.
* **Shape:**
  * Normal: Triangular in A4C view.
  * **D-Sign:** Flattening of the interventricular septum due to pressure or volume overload.
	* Systolic flattening: Pressure overload (e.g., PE, PHTN).
	* Diastolic flattening: Volume overload.
* **Function:**
  * **TAPSE (Tricuspid Annular Plane Systolic Excursion):**
	* Measures longitudinal contraction of the RV base.
	* Normal: > 17 mm (or > 1.6 cm).
	* Measured in A4C / ME4C with M-mode through the lateral tricuspid annulus.

### 7.2.5. VOLUME RESPONSIVENESS

Ultrasound provides dynamic parameters to predict fluid responsiveness.

* **Inferior Vena Cava (IVC) Assessment:**
  * View: Subcostal IVC.
  * **Spontaneously breathing patients:**
	* Small, collapsing IVC (< 2.1 cm, > 50% collapse): Suggests low RA pressure (< 3 mmHg), likely fluid responsive.
	* Large, fixed IVC (> 2.1 cm, < 50% collapse): Suggests high RA pressure (> 15 mmHg), unlikely to be fluid responsive.
  * **Mechanically ventilated patients:**
	* IVC Distensibility Index > 18% suggests fluid responsiveness.
	* Requires passive ventilation, 8 mL/kg tidal volume, no spontaneous efforts.

### 7.2.6. SPECIFIC POCUS APPLICATIONS

#### 7.2.6.1. LUNG AND PLEURAL ULTRASOUND
* **Probe:** Linear (pleura) or Curvilinear (effusion/consolidation).
* **Signs:**
  * **Sliding lung:** Normal movement of parietal/visceral pleura (ants marching).
  * **A-lines:** Normal reverberation artifacts (horizontal).
  * **B-lines:** Vertical hyperechoic artifacts (comet tails) indicating interstitial syndrome (edema, fibrosis).
  * **Seashore sign (M-mode):** Normal lung sliding.
  * **Barcode/Stratosphere sign (M-mode):** Pneumothorax (no sliding).
  * **Lung Point:** Specific for Pneumothorax (transition between sliding and no sliding).
  * **Pleural Effusion:** Anechoic space above diaphragm (Spine sign continues above diaphragm).

#### 7.2.6.2. VASCULAR ACCESS AND DVT
* **Vascular Access:**
  * IJ/Femoral vein: Compressible, non-pulsatile, expands with Valsalva.
  * Artery: Pulsatile, non-compressible.
* **DVT Study:**
  * **Compression Ultrasound:** Primary criterion for DVT.
  * Sites: Common Femoral, Saphenofemoral junction, Popliteal.
  * **Positive:** Vein does not compress completely.

  * PPV: 93%
  * NPV: 32%

## 9.2. DIAGNOSTIC LIMITATIONS AND FALSE FINDINGS

### 9.2.1. DOCUMENTED FALSE POSITIVES

The ASA/SCA Guidelines document specific cases of diagnostic errors:

* **Aortic pathology misdiagnosis:**
  * TEE detected aortic dissection; surgery revealed Takayasu arteritis
  * TEE detected intramural hematoma; surgery revealed aortic dissection
  * TEE detected type A dissection; surgery revealed aortic valve commissural tear
* **Cardiac mass misinterpretation:**
  * TEE detected periannular abscess; surgery revealed normal coronary ostium
  * TEE detected valvular tumor; surgery revealed organized thrombus
* **Missed diagnoses:**
  * TEE failed to detect aortic outflow obstruction; surgery revealed occluded valve orifice
  * TEE failed to detect endocarditis; detected by intracardiac echocardiography

### 9.2.2. CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS

* **Diagnostic accuracy varies significantly** depending on:
  * Pathology type and location
  * Imaging windows and technique
  * Operator experience and training
  * Confirmation method used in studies
* **Complementary imaging** may be required for:
  * Anterior cardiac structures (favor TTE)
  * Complex congenital heart disease
  * Assessment behind acoustic shadows

## 9.3. EVIDENCE CLASSIFICATION SYSTEM

The ASA/SCA Guidelines use a rigorous evidence classification system:

### 9.3.1. SCIENTIFIC EVIDENCE CATEGORIES

* **Category A (Supportive Literature):**
  * Randomized controlled trials with statistically significant differences (P < 0.01)
  * Level 1: Multiple RCTs with meta-analysis support
  * Level 2: Multiple RCTs without meta-analysis
  * Level 3: Single RCT
* **Category B (Suggestive Literature):**
  * Observational studies permitting beneficial/harmful relationship inference
  * Level 1: Comparative observational studies with statistical significance
  * Level 2: Non-comparative observational studies
  * Level 3: Case reports
* **Category C (Equivocal Literature):**
  * Literature cannot determine beneficial or harmful relationships
* **Category D (Insufficient Evidence):**
  * No identified studies or inadequate methodology

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The ASA/SCA evidence synthesis demonstrates that TEE diagnostic accuracy is highly dependent on the specific pathology being assessed, with excellent performance for some conditions (prosthetic valve complications) and limitations for others (myocardial infarction detection).

⚠️ **Important Consideration:** Documented false positives and false negatives in the ASA/SCA database emphasize the importance of correlating TEE findings with clinical presentation and other diagnostic modalities.

**Bottom Line:** Systematic evidence review demonstrates variable but generally high diagnostic accuracy for TEE across multiple pathological conditions, with important limitations that practitioners must understand to optimize clinical decision-making and patient outcomes.

## 9.5. RIGHT VENTRICLE

### 9.5.1. ANATOMY AND ASSESSMENT

The right ventricle has unique characteristics that influence assessment:

* **Morphology:**
  * Crescentic shape in cross-section
  * Comprises free wall, interventricular septum, and apex
  * Tricuspid valve as inflow, pulmonic valve as outflow
  * Thinner wall than LV (normally <5 mm)
  * More compliant but more sensitive to afterload changes
* **Distinguishing features on echocardiography:**
  * Coarse trabeculations
  * Moderator band
  * Tricuspid valve with papillary muscle attachment to septum
  * More apical insertion of septal leaflet compared to mitral valve
* **Blood supply:**
  * Primarily right coronary artery
  * Posterior descending artery (left dominant circulation) supplies inferior wall

![Anatomy of the right ventricle](ECHO_Untitled_1.png_f6a036.png)

### 9.5.2. DIAMETERS AND WALL THICKNESS

Quantitative assessment of RV size and wall thickness provides important diagnostic information:

* **Wall thickness:** >5 mm suggests hypertrophy
* **Dimensions:**
  * Basal diameter >42 mm suggests dilation
  * Mid-diameter >35 mm suggests dilation
  * Length >86 mm suggests dilation
* **Visual estimation of dilation:**
  * Mild: RV more than two-thirds toward LV apex
  * Moderate: RV and LV of equivalent length
  * Severe: RV extends beyond LV apex

![Right ventricular dimensions](ECHO_Untitled_1.png_f6a036.png)

### 9.5.3. RV SYSTOLIC FUNCTION

RV function assessment is challenging due to its irregular shape:

* **Tricuspid Annular Plane Systolic Excursion (TAPSE):**
  * Normal: >17 mm
  * Measured in 4-chamber view using M-mode
  * Aligns with direction of annular movement toward apex
  * Regional rather than global assessment
* **Fractional Area Change (FAC):**
  * Normal: >35%
  * Calculated as percentage change from end-diastolic to end-systolic area
  * Measured in ME4C or A4C view
* **Tissue Doppler measurements:**
  * Lateral annular systolic velocity (s') normal: >10 cm/s
  * Right ventricular index of myocardial performance (RIMP) normal: <0.55
  * Best measured in TTE A4C view

![Tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion](ECHO_Untitled_1.png_f6a036.png)

![Right ventricular fractional area change](ECHO_Untitled_1.png_f6a036.png)

![Right ventricular S'](ECHO_Untitled_1.png_f6a036.png)

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** RV function is a critical determinant of outcomes in patients with cardiorespiratory disease. Early detection and management of RV dysfunction should be prioritized, as the thin-walled RV is particularly vulnerable to increases in pulmonary vascular resistance.

**Bottom Line:** Comprehensive assessment of ventricular function involves both qualitative and quantitative approaches. Understanding normal cardiac anatomy and function provides the foundation for recognizing pathology. Diastolic dysfunction is an important predictor of outcomes and should be systematically evaluated in perioperative patients.

# 10. VALVULAR HEART DISEASE

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Cardiovascular Physiology|Part A: Valve Physiology]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#Valvular Pathology|Part B: Valvular Disease]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Perioperative Management|Part C: Clinical Management]]

Echocardiography is a principal method for evaluating cardiac valvular pathology. Assessment includes valve anatomy, impact of lesions on surrounding structures, and quantification of severity.

## 10.1. AORTIC VALVE

### 10.1.1. NORMAL ANATOMY

The aortic valve is a complex three-dimensional structure:

* **Basic structure:**
  * Extends between ventriculoaortic junction and sinotubular junction
  * Normally has three leaflets (cusps)
  * Each leaflet has free margin, two commissures, and a hinge
* **Related structures:**
  * Sinuses of Valsalva
  * Sinotubular junction
  * Coronary ostia
  * Anterior mitral valve leaflet
  * Left ventricular outflow tract

![Aortic valve model](ECHO_Untitled_1.png_f6a036.png)

### 10.1.2. AORTIC STENOSIS

Aortic stenosis (AS) is a common valvular pathology with significant hemodynamic implications:

* **Common causes:**
  * Calcific degeneration (most common in developed countries)
  * Bicuspid aortic valve
  * Rheumatic disease (globally prevalent)
* **Echocardiographic features:**
  * **Calcific disease:** Thick, stiff leaflets with echodense calcification causing acoustic shadowing
  * **Rheumatic disease:** Commissural fusion with preserved leaflet body
  * **Bicuspid valve:** Two leaflets with single raphe, fish-mouth appearance in systole
* **Associated findings:**
  * Left ventricular hypertrophy
  * Diastolic dysfunction
  * Post-stenotic aortic root dilation
  * Aortic regurgitation
* **Severity assessment:**
  * **Peak velocity:** Mild <3 m/s, Moderate 3-4 m/s, Severe >4 m/s
  * **Mean gradient:** Mild <20 mmHg, Moderate 20-40 mmHg, Severe >40 mmHg
  * **Valve area:** Mild >1.5 cm², Moderate 1.0-1.5 cm², Severe <1.0 cm²
  * **Dimensionless index:** Severe <0.25
* **Measurement methods:**
  * **Planimetry:** Direct tracing of orifice area in short-axis view (challenging with calcification)
  * **Continuity equation:** AV Area = (LVOT VTI × LVOT area) ÷ AV VTI
  * **Dimensionless index:** LVOT VTI ÷ AV VTI

![Significant aortic stenosis](ECHO_Untitled_1.png_f6a036.png)

![Common aortic valve pathologies](ECHO_Untitled_1.png_f6a036.png)

⚠️ **Important Consideration:** Patients with severe AS are particularly vulnerable during induction of anesthesia. Maintaining adequate preload, avoiding tachycardia, and ensuring adequate systemic vascular resistance are critical to prevent hemodynamic collapse.

### 10.1.3. AORTIC REGURGITATION

Aortic regurgitation (AR) may result from primary valve disease or aortic root pathology:

* **Etiology:**
  * **Primary valve disease:**
	* Congenital abnormalities
	* Infective endocarditis
	* Rheumatic heart disease
	* Associated with AS
  
  * **Aortic root disease:**
	* Degenerative disease
	* Connective tissue disorders (e.g., Marfan syndrome)
	* Aortic dissection
	* Aneurysm
* **Pathophysiologic consequences:**
  * Eccentric LV hypertrophy
  * LV dilation
  * Eventually reduced EF with chronic severe AR
* **Severity assessment:**
  * **Vena contracta:** Mild <3 mm, Moderate 3-6 mm, Severe >6 mm
  * **Pressure half-time:** Mild >500 ms, Moderate 200-500 ms, Severe <200 ms
  * **Jet width to LVOT diameter ratio:** Mild <25%, Moderate 25-64%, Severe >65%
  * **Descending aorta flow reversal:** Mild (early), Moderate (intermediate), Severe (holodiastolic)
  * **CWD jet density:** Mild/Moderate (faint), Severe (dense)

![Aortic regurgitation](ECHO_Untitled_1.png_f6a036.png)

## 10.2. MITRAL VALVE

### 10.2.1. NORMAL ANATOMY

The mitral valve has a complex structure essential for proper function:

* **Leaflets:**
  * Anterior leaflet (larger, semicircular)
  * Posterior leaflet (smaller, rectangularly shaped)
  * Posterior leaflet divided into three scallops (P1, P2, P3)
  * Corresponding anterior segments (A1, A2, A3)
* **Supporting structures:**
  * Saddle-shaped mitral annular ring
  * Chordae tendineae
  * Anterolateral and posteromedial papillary muscles
* **Key relationships:**
  * Anterior leaflet forms part of LVOT structure
  * Close relationship to aortic valve
  * Forms part of fibrous skeleton of heart

![Mitral valve anatomy](ECHO_Untitled_1.png_f6a036.png)

### 10.2.2. MITRAL STENOSIS

Mitral stenosis (MS) restricts left atrial emptying and has characteristic findings:

* **Common causes:**
  * Rheumatic heart disease (most common globally)
  * Calcific disease of mitral annulus
  * Congenital
  * Severe MAC
  * Infiltrative diseases
* **Echocardiographic features:**
  * **Rheumatic disease:**
	* Commissural fusion
	* Doming of leaflets ("hockey stick" deformity)
	* Restricted leaflet motion
  
  * **Calcific disease:**
	* Calcification of annulus with extension to leaflets
	* Reduced leaflet mobility
	* Acoustic shadowing
* **Associated findings:**
  * Left atrial enlargement
  * Bowing of interatrial septum toward right atrium
  * Spontaneous echo contrast ("smoke")
  * Small, underfilled left ventricle
  * Pulmonary hypertension
  * Right ventricular dilation and dysfunction
* **Severity assessment:**
  * **Mean gradient:** Mild <5 mmHg, Moderate 5-10 mmHg, Severe >10 mmHg
  * **Pressure half-time:** Mild ≤100 ms, Moderate 100-220 ms, Severe >220 ms
  * **Mitral valve area:** Mild >1.5 cm², Moderate 1.0-1.5 cm², Severe <1.0 cm²
  * **Pulmonary artery systolic pressure:** Mild <30 mmHg, Moderate 30-50 mmHg, Severe >50 mmHg
* **Measurement methods:**
  * **Planimetry:** Direct tracing of orifice area (ideally in 3D)
  * **Pressure half-time:** MVA = 220/PHT
  * **Mean gradient:** CWD across valve

### 10.2.3. MITRAL REGURGITATION

Mitral regurgitation (MR) can be primary (structural) or secondary (functional):

* **Classification (Carpentier):**
  * **Type 1:** Normal leaflet motion (annular dilation, leaflet perforation)
  * **Type 2:** Excessive leaflet motion (prolapse, flail)
  * **Type 3:** Restricted leaflet motion (rheumatic disease, ischemic)
* **Etiology:**
  * **Primary MR:**
	* Degenerative (myxomatous) disease
	* Mitral valve prolapse
	* Leaflet perforation
	* Ruptured chordae tendineae
	* Papillary muscle rupture
  
  * **Secondary MR:**
	* Left ventricular dilation
	* Ischemic cardiomyopathy
	* Abnormal wall motion
* **Pathophysiologic consequences:**
  * Left atrial enlargement
  * LV volume overload
  * Initial hyperdynamic LV function
  * Eventually decreased systolic function
* **Severity assessment:**
  * **Vena contracta:** Mild <3 mm, Moderate 3-7 mm, Severe >7 mm
  * **Jet area (% LA):** Mild <20%, Moderate 20-40%, Severe >40%
  * **Pulmonary venous S:D ratio:** Mild/Moderate ≥1, Severe <1 with S reversal
  * **CWD jet density:** Mild/Moderate (faint), Severe (dense)

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** When assessing mitral valve pathology, the MELAX view is particularly valuable as it allows examination of individual leaflet segments by turning the probe from left to right to visualize A1/P1 through A3/P3 regions.

## 10.3. TRICUSPID REGURGITATION

Tricuspid regurgitation (TR) is common, with varying clinical significance:

* **Prevalence:** Trivial or mild TR in ~90% of normal individuals
* **Etiology:**
  * **Primary:**
	* Ebstein's anomaly
	* Endocarditis
	* Carcinoid syndrome
	* Congenital
	* Trauma
  
  * **Secondary (more common):**
	* Right ventricular dilation
	* Pulmonary hypertension
	* Left heart disease
	* Atrial fibrillation
* **Severity assessment:**
  * **Vena contracta:** Not severe ≤7 mm, Severe >7 mm
  * **Peak velocity:** Not severe ≤2.4 m/s, Severe >2.4 m/s
  * **IVC diameter:** Not severe ≤2 cm, Severe >2 cm
  * **Hepatic vein diameter:** Not severe ≤1 cm, Severe >1 cm
  * **Hepatic vein S:D ratio:** Not severe S>D, Severe S reversal
  * **CWD jet density:** Not severe (faint), Severe (dense)

## 10.4. OTHER PATHOLOGY

### 10.4.1. DISEASES OF THE AORTA

Aortic pathology assessment is an important application of echocardiography:

* **Imaging approaches:**
  * TTE: PLAX and SS views for ascending aorta
  * TEE: ME asc aorta SAX/LAX for ascending aorta, desc aorta SAX/LAX for descending aorta
  * Epiaortic ultrasound: Intraoperative assessment of ascending aorta
* **Aortic aneurysm:**
  * Definition: Diameter >4 cm
  * High rupture risk: >5.5 cm
  * Measurement: Outer wall to outer wall perpendicular to long axis
* **Aortic dissection:**
  * Separation of intimal layer from media
  * Visualization of intimal flap
  * True vs. false lumen differentiation:
	* True lumen: Smaller, expands in systole
	* False lumen: Larger, may contain thrombus/spontaneous contrast
  * Classification (Stanford):
	* Type A: Involves ascending aorta (surgical emergency)
	* Type B: Limited to descending aorta (often medical management)
* **Aortic atheroma:**
  * Appears echodense with calcium deposits
  * Roughened surface
  * Risk factor for embolization during procedures requiring aortic manipulation

⚠️ **Important Consideration:** Stanford Type A aortic dissection is a surgical emergency requiring immediate intervention. TEE findings may include aortic regurgitation, pericardial effusion, and regional wall motion abnormalities if coronary involvement occurs.

### 10.4.2. PERICARDIAL DISEASE

Pericardial pathology can have significant hemodynamic consequences:
* **Normal pericardium:**
  * Outer fibrous and inner serosal components
  * Contains <50 mL of serous fluid
  * Appears as thin echolucent space
  * May be visible in transverse and oblique sinuses
* **Pericardial effusion:**
  * Appears as echolucent space surrounding heart
  * Characterization:
	* Transudative: More echolucent
	* Exudative: More echodense
	* Hemorrhagic: Swirling spontaneous contrast or echodense
	* May be circumferential or loculated
  
  * Assessment in ME4C or SC4C views
* **Cardiac tamponade:**
  * Key echocardiographic features:
	* Right atrial collapse during ventricular systole (early sign)
	* Right ventricular collapse during diastole (increasing severity)
	* Interventricular septal flattening or leftward displacement
	* Exaggerated respiratory variation in valve flows
	* Inferior vena cava plethora
	* Swinging heart (large effusion)
* **Constrictive pericarditis:**
  * Echogenic, thickened pericardium
  * Preserved or exaggerated septal e' velocity
  * Reduced lateral e' velocity
  * Exaggerated respiratory variation in valve flows
  * Ventricular interdependence

![Pericardial effusion](ECHO_Untitled_1.png_f6a036.png)

⚠️ **Safety Warning:** In patients with pericardial tamponade, maintaining chronotropy and preload during induction of anesthesia is critical. Avoid vasodilators and excessive positive pressure ventilation, which can precipitate cardiovascular collapse.

### 10.4.3. CARDIAC MASSES

Echocardiography is essential for evaluating cardiac masses:

* **Assessment approach:**
  * Examine from multiple views and angles
  * Establish point of origin, extent, and size
  * Assess contour, echogenicity, consistency, and mobility
* **Common types:**
  * **Myxoma:**
	* Most common primary cardiac tumor
	* Typically pedunculated or sessile mass in atria
	* Usually benign
  
  * **Thrombi:**
	* Homogeneous appearance
	* Aggregate in low-flow areas (LV apex, LA appendage)
	* Often preceded by spontaneous echo contrast
  
  * **Vegetations:**
	* Irregular, hypermobile masses on valves or foreign bodies
	* Associated with infective endocarditis
	* May have destructive impact on valve leaflets

![Cardiac masses](ECHO_Untitled_1.png_f6a036.png)

### 10.4.4. CONGENITAL HEART DISEASE

Improved survival rates have increased the prevalence of adults with treated congenital heart disease:

* **Common lesions in adults:**
  * Patent foramen ovale (PFO)
  * Atrial septal defect (ASD)
  * Ventricular septal defect (VSD)
  * Bicuspid aortic valve
  * Coarctation of the aorta
  * Repaired tetralogy of Fallot
* **Shunt assessment:**
  * Best viewed in ME bicaval view for ASD/PFO
  * Reduce Nyquist limit to 15-30 cm/s for low-flow jets
  * Note direction of flow (left-to-right or right-to-left)
  * Color Doppler or agitated saline contrast studies

![Intracardiac shunt](ECHO_Untitled_1.png_f6a036.png)

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Visual cues can help predict severe valvular disease: flow convergence proximal to the valve, turbulence after crossing the valve, and minimal leaflet motion throughout the cardiac cycle in stenotic lesions.

**Bottom Line:** Echocardiographic assessment of valvular and other cardiac pathology requires systematic evaluation of structure, function, and hemodynamic consequences. Accurate grading of severity guides management decisions and impacts perioperative planning.

# 11. POINT-OF-CARE ULTRASOUND

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Ultrasound Principles|Part A: Ultrasound Physics]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#POCUS Applications|Part B: Clinical Applications]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Procedural Skills|Part C: POCUS Skills]]

## 11.1. INTRODUCTION TO POCUS

Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) has evolved to become an essential tool for anesthesiologists:

* **Definition and scope:**
  * Goal-oriented, focused ultrasonography performed at the bedside
  * Answers specific clinical questions in real-time
  * Spans entire perioperative care spectrum
  * Can be repeated to monitor response to interventions
* **Comparison with consultative ultrasonography:**
  * Less extensive training requirements
  * Limited primarily to 2D ultrasound
  * Answers specific clinical questions rather than comprehensive evaluation
  * More accessible, faster, and less expensive
* **Clinical integration principles:**
  * Visual information must be interpreted with other clinical data
  * Effectiveness limited by provider skill and knowledge
  * Targeted applications rather than exhaustive evaluation
  * Serial examinations can track response to therapy

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** POCUS represents a "disruptive innovation" that addresses immediate clinical needs in a faster, more accessible manner than traditional consultative ultrasonography, though with more focused scope.

## 11.2. FOCUSED CARDIAC ULTRASOUND

Focused cardiac ultrasound allows rapid assessment of hemodynamic status:

* **Common indications:**
  * Perioperative hypotension or shock
  * Volume status assessment
  * Ventricular function evaluation
  * Pericardial effusion detection
  * Significant valvular abnormalities
  * LVOT obstruction assessment
* **Basic assessment parameters:**
  * LV systolic function: Wall thickening >30%, chamber emptying >50%
  * RV function: Free wall motion, interventricular septal position
  * Volume status: IVC diameter and variability, LV end-diastolic dimension
  * Pericardial effusion: Hypoechoic collection around heart
  * Gross valvular abnormalities: Restricted mobility, coaptation defects
* **Advanced assessment:**
  * Stroke volume measurement using LVOT VTI
  * Response to fluids, vasopressors, or inotropes
  * Diastolic function assessment
  * Regional wall motion abnormalities
  * Tamponade features
* **Clinical impacts:**
  * Changes perioperative management in significant percentage of cases
  * Identifies hyperdynamic, normal, or depressed LV function guiding therapy
  * Facilitates diagnosis of distributive, hypovolemic, cardiogenic, or obstructive shock
  * Reduces time to diagnosis in hemodynamically unstable patients

⚠️ **Important Consideration:** Assessment of fluid status and volume responsiveness with ultrasound is challenging. Dynamic parameters are more predictive but lose sensitivity in all but the most controlled circumstances.

## 11.3. LUNG ULTRASOUND

Lung ultrasound provides rapid diagnosis of various pathologies, often outperforming chest radiographs:

* **Examination technique:**
  * Assess at least three areas: anterior (below clavicle), anterolateral (4th-5th intercostal space), and posterolateral (7th-9th intercostal space)
  * Can use linear, phased array, or curvilinear probes
  * Depth settings: 4-6 cm for pleural surface, 14-18 cm for posterolateral views
  * Cephalad-caudad probe orientation with ribs on either side and pleural line 1-2 cm deep
* **Normal findings:**
  * "Bat sign": Ribs with hyperechoic pleural line between them
  * "Seashore sign" on M-mode: Pattern of lines (chest wall) over granular appearance (lung)
  * Lung sliding: To-and-fro movement at pleural line
  * A-lines: Horizontal reverberation artifacts
* **Pathological findings:**
  * **Pneumothorax:**
	* Absence of lung sliding
	* "Barcode sign" on M-mode
	* Absence of B-lines
	* Absence of lung pulse
	* "Lung point" is pathognomonic (transition between sliding and non-sliding lung)
  
  * **Pleural effusion:**
	* Hypoechoic area in costophrenic angles
	* Spine sign (visualization of spine above diaphragm)
	* Simple (anechoic) vs. complex (internal echoes) effusions
  
  * **Interstitial syndrome:**
	* Multiple B-lines (vertical artifacts extending to bottom of screen)
	* Different patterns suggest different etiologies:
	  * Bilateral, diffuse: Cardiogenic pulmonary edema, ARDS
	  * Focal/unilateral: Pneumonia
	  * Irregular, fixed pattern: Pulmonary fibrosis
  
  * **Consolidation:**
	* "Sonographic hepatization" of lung due to loss of aeration
	* May see air bronchograms (bright spots or lines within consolidated lung)
	* "Shred sign" at border between consolidated and aerated lung
* **Clinical protocols:**
  * BLUE protocol (Bedside Lung Ultrasound in Emergency): For acute respiratory failure
  * FALLS protocol (Fluid Administration Limited by Lung Sonography): For acute circulatory failure

![Common lung ultrasound findings](ECHO_Untitled_1.png_f6a036.png)

## 11.4. ABDOMINAL ULTRASOUND

POCUS allows assessment of abdominal structures with specific perioperative applications:

* **Gastric content assessment:**
  * Performed in supine and right lateral decubitus positions
  * Curvilinear probe in parasagittal plane under left lobe of liver
  * Cross-sectional area of antrum used to estimate volume
  * Volume (mL) = 27.0 + (14.6 × RLD CSA) − (1.28 × Age)
  * "Full stomach": Solid contents or fluid volume >1.5 mL/kg
  * Up to 5% of patients for elective surgery have full stomachs despite fasting
  * High sensitivity and negative predictive value
* **FAST examination:**
  * Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma
  * Evaluates for free fluid in pericardium, abdomen, and pelvis
  * Useful in emergency settings and in PACU
  * Aids diagnosis of post-surgical bleeding

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Lung ultrasound is particularly valuable for rapid assessment of pneumothorax in the post-procedural setting (central line placement, regional anesthetic blocks) as it is more sensitive than chest radiography and can be performed immediately at the bedside.

## 11.5. OTHER USES OF ULTRASOUND

### 11.5.1. ULTRASOUND-GUIDED VASCULAR CANNULATION

Ultrasound guidance has become standard for many vascular access procedures:

* **Applications:**
  * Internal jugular venous cannulation (standard of care)
  * Subclavian and femoral venous cannulation
  * Arterial cannulation
  * Peripheral venous access in difficult cases
  * Visualization of wires and cannulae during specialized procedures
* **Benefits:**
  * Pre-procedure assessment for vessel patency and anatomy
  * Visualization of needle tip and vascular structures during procedure
  * Avoidance of inadvertent arterial puncture or pneumothorax
  * Reduction in total number of attempts
  * Essential in patients with mechanical cardiac support and absent arterial pulsatility
* **Technical approach:**
  * Maintain sterility of probe, gel, operative area, and catheter
  * Dynamic needle guidance: Stay ahead of needle tip rather than following it
  * Maintain "bulls-eye" of needle tip in vessel lumen
  * Confirm venous access with manometry after initial entry

⚠️ **Safety Warning:** Despite ultrasound guidance for central venous cannulation, always confirm venous (vs. arterial) access with pressure transduction or blood gas analysis before dilation or catheter placement.

### 11.5.2. EPICARDIAL AND EPIAORTIC ULTRASOUND

Intraoperative ultrasound in the surgical field provides additional assessment options:

* **Epicardial echocardiography:**
  * Alternative when TEE contraindicated
  * Uses high-frequency, phased-array probe with sterile sheath
  * Mediastinal cavity filled with warm sterile saline to enhance transmission
  * Similar in orientation to TTE
  * Useful for valvular and ventricular function assessment
* **Epiaortic ultrasonography (EAU):**
  * Recommended to reduce embolic neurologic events
  * Decreased risk of postoperative stroke when used
  * High-frequency probe with saline "standoff" to prevent near-field clutter
  * Examines portions of ascending aorta and arch not clearly visualized by TEE
  * Significant site-to-site variability in usage despite guidelines

## 11.6. OUT-OF-OR ECHOCARDIOGRAPHY

TEE is increasingly used for guidance during structural heart interventions:

* **Common procedures:**
  * Transcatheter valve implantation or repair
  * Paravalvular leak closure
  * Atrial septal defect closure
  * Left atrial appendage closure
  * Pacemaker and defibrillator lead extraction
* **Role of echocardiography:**
  * Pre-procedure evaluation
  * Guidance for wire access
  * Device deployment assistance
  * Post-deployment evaluation of results
  * Immediate complication detection
* **Value of 3D TEE:**
  * Significantly improves accuracy for structural interventions
  * Enhances communication between echocardiographer and interventionalist
  * Provides realistic, intuitive views of complex anatomy

**Bottom Line:** Point-of-care ultrasound has expanded beyond cardiac assessment to include lung, abdominal, and vascular applications, significantly enhancing the anesthesiologist's diagnostic and procedural capabilities at the bedside. Integration of multiple POCUS modalities provides comprehensive, real-time assessment that directs therapeutic interventions.

# 12. TRAINING AND CERTIFICATION



Certification processes validate competency through knowledge testing and practical demonstration:

* **United States certification:**
  * National Board of Echocardiography (NBE)
  * Testamur status: Passing knowledge examination
  * Diplomate status: Completed required examination numbers with supervision
  * Recertification every 10 years
  * Specific pathways for basic PTEeXAM, advanced PTEeXAM, and critical care echocardiography
* **International certification:**
  * United Kingdom: British Society of Echocardiography accreditation
  * Europe: European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging certification
  * Australia/New Zealand: Integrated with College of Anaesthetists training
  * Japan: Japanese Board of Perioperative Transesophageal Echocardiography
  * Variable examination requirements across countries
* **Emerging certification trends:**
  * Development of tiered competency frameworks
  * Increasing use of simulation for training and assessment
  * Integration of POCUS into core residency competencies
  * Greater standardization of certification requirements

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Echocardiography simulators provide valuable training in image acquisition and interpretation in a safe environment with tracking of learning metrics, complementing hands-on clinical experience.

**Bottom Line:** Comprehensive training and certification in perioperative echocardiography and POCUS are evolving worldwide, with structured pathways to develop and validate competency. Requirements vary internationally but share the goal of ensuring safe, effective ultrasound application in clinical practice.
