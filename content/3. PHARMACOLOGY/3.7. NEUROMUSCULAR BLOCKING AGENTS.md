## 1.2. NEUROMUSCULAR JUNCTION STRUCTURE

**The neuromuscular junction (NMJ) is a specialized chemical synapse where motor neuron signals are transmitted to skeletal muscle fibers.**

* **Components of the NMJ** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Presynaptic motor nerve terminal
  * Synaptic cleft (50-70 nm wide)
  * Postsynaptic muscle membrane (motor end-plate)
  * Specialized folded junctional area with concentrated nicotinic acetylcholine receptors
  * The postsynaptic membrane has many folds; shoulders contain acetylcholine (ACh) receptors while clefts contain acetylcholinesterase (AChE) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Innervation patterns** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Most skeletal muscles (en plaque): Each muscle fiber innervated by a single axonal terminal from a fast Aα neurone at the fiber mid-point
  * Special muscles (en grappe): Intra-ocular, intrinsic laryngeal, and some facial muscles have multiple terminals from slower Aγ neurones scattered over the muscle surface, requiring wave of impulses throughout terminals
* **Receptor Distribution** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Mature nicotinic acetylcholine receptors located almost exclusively (90%) at the neuromuscular synapse
  * The neuromuscular junction comprises only about 0.1% of the overall muscle surface area
  * Nearly tenfold margin of safety exists (approximately 5 million receptors at each NMJ but only about 500,000 required for normal muscle contraction)
  * This margin of safety is reduced in conditions like Eaton–Lambert myasthenic syndrome (decreased release of ACh) and myasthenia gravis (decreased number of receptors) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Receptor Types** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * **Mature junctional receptors**: Pentameric with α₂βδε subunits
  * **Immature/extrajunctional receptors**: Pentameric with α₂βδγ subunits (important in denervation injuries and developmental states)
  * **Presynaptic α3β2 receptors**: Present on motor nerve terminals, regulate acetylcholine mobilization

## 1.3. PRESYNAPTIC EVENTS

**The presynaptic nerve terminal is responsible for acetylcholine synthesis, storage, and release.**

* **Signal initiation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Motor cortex generates neuronal signals transmitted via upper motor neurons
  * Lower motor neurons in ventral horn of spinal cord project via ventral roots to muscle fiber end-plates
  * Nerves are myelinated until the terminal aspect, which is unmyelinated
  * Each terminal fiber of a lower motor neuron supplies a single muscle fiber
* **Acetylcholine synthesis** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Acetylcholine is formed by choline acetyltransferase from acetyl coenzyme A and choline [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Choline derived from the diet and recycled from breakdown of ACh [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Synthesized in the axoplasm and transferred into small synaptic vesicles where stored prior to release [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Stored in vesicles in either the active zone (ready for release) or reserve pool
* **Release mechanism** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Action potential reaches nerve terminal, activating voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels
  * Calcium influx mobilizes acetylcholine vesicles to the active zone via intracellular transport proteins
  * Vesicles fuse with nerve membrane and release acetylcholine into synaptic cleft
  * About 200 vesicles (each containing about 10,000 ACh molecules) are released in response to each action potential [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Quanta (5,000-10,000 acetylcholine molecules) released spontaneously produce no significant response
  * Coordinated release after nerve conduction releases millions of acetylcholine molecules
* **Regulation mechanisms** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Potassium channels limit calcium influx and regulate acetylcholine release
  * Released acetylcholine may bind to presynaptic α3β2 acetylcholine receptors
  * This presynaptic feedback promotes acetylcholine vesicle movement to active zone for subsequent signals
* **Factors affecting acetylcholine release** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Hypocalcemia impedes calcium signaling causing weakness
  * Hypermagnesemia inhibits Ca²⁺ influx leading to weakness
  * Eaton–Lambert syndrome: Antibodies target calcium channels
  * Botulinum toxin: Irreversibly inhibits intracellular proteins facilitating vesicle movement

## 1.4. POSTSYNAPTIC EVENTS

**Postsynaptic events involve receptor activation and muscle fiber depolarization.**

* **Nicotinic acetylcholine receptor structure** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Pentameric protein arrangement forming ion channel
  * **Mature receptors**: α₂βδε (larger central pore, shorter opening time)
  * **Immature receptors**: α₂βδγ (smaller channel, opening time 10 times longer)
  * Integral membrane proteins with molecular weight of 250,000 Da [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Critical binding sites for quaternary nitrogen group (-N+(CH3)3) of ACh [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Receptor activation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Acetylcholine diffuses across synaptic cleft to bind nicotinic receptors
  * Opening requires simultaneous acetylcholine binding to both alpha subunits
  * Can be blocked by antagonist at just one alpha subunit
  * Binding leads to conformational change opening central pore
  * Allows Na⁺ influx, K⁺ efflux, and depolarization
  * Ion channel is non-specific, allowing Na+, K+ and Ca2+ across the membrane [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **End-plate potentials** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * **Miniature end-plate potential (MEPP)**: Small quanta released spontaneously produce limited response
  * **End-plate potential (EPP)**: Large acetylcholine release triggers sufficient depolarization for muscle excitation-contraction
  * Potentials summate until threshold potential is reached [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Action potential propagation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels propagate depolarization beyond NMJ
  * Depolarization spreads to T-tubule system
  * Calcium released from sarcoplasmic reticulum
  * Intracellular calcium allows contractile proteins actin and myosin to interact
  * Results in muscle contraction
* **Termination of signal** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Acetylcholine is rapidly hydrolyzed by acetylcholinesterase into acetate and choline
  * Choline is recycled back to nerve terminal
  * Receptors' ion channels close, allowing end-plate repolarization
  * Calcium resequestered in sarcoplasmic reticulum
  * Muscle cell relaxes
* **Muscle coordination factors** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Nerve:muscle fiber ratio varies depending on coordination required
  * Fine coordination (eye/facial muscles): ratio approaches 1:2
  * Powerful movements (back/thigh): ratio can approach 1:2,000

## 1.5. RECEPTOR UPAND DOWNREGULATION

**Pathological conditions can alter receptor expression and distribution at the neuromuscular junction.**

* **Receptor upregulation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Occurs when motor neuron disruption persists for several days
  * Decreased frequency of motor neuron activity precipitates upregulation
  * Immature nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (α₂βδγ) proliferate throughout associated muscles
  * These extrajunctional receptors contain fetal γ subunit in place of adult ε subunit [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Clinical scenarios include:
	* Denervation injuries (spinal cord injury, Guillain–Barré) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
	* Severe burns [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
	* Crush injuries [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
	* Prolonged immobilization [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
	* Prolonged use of neuromuscular blockers in ICU [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
	* Cerebrovascular accident [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
	* Sepsis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * ⚠️ Risk of life-threatening hyperkalemic response if succinylcholine administered 24+ hours after onset of injury or disease process [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Receptor downregulation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Occurs with continual agonism
  * May result from chronic administration of acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (pyridostigmine, neostigmine)
  * Common in treatment of myasthenia gravis
  * Manifestations include:
	* Extreme resistance to succinylcholine (due to high baseline agonism)
	* Sensitivity to nondepolarizing neuromuscular blocking drugs (due to fewer available receptors)
* **Acute cholinesterase inhibition** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Organophosphate poisoning inhibits acetylcholinesterase
  * Leads to prolonged agonism from persistent high acetylcholine levels

**Bottom Line:** Understanding receptor regulation is crucial for safe NMBA administration. Upregulation of immature receptors in denervation injuries and burns creates risk for hyperkalemia with succinylcholine, while downregulation in myasthenia gravis increases sensitivity to nondepolarizing agents but resistance to depolarizing agents.

## 2.2. CLASSIFICATION AND CHARACTERIZATION

**Neuromuscular blocking agents are categorized by their mechanism of action and chemical structure.**

* **Two main categories of NMBAs with distinct properties** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * **Depolarizing**: Succinylcholine
  * **Nondepolarizing**: Rocuronium, vecuronium, cisatracurium, and others
* **Non-Depolarizing NMBA Subclassification** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * **Chemical structure**:
	* **Aminosteroids**: Rocuronium, vecuronium, pancuronium
	* **Benzylisoquinoliniums**: Atracurium, cisatracurium, mivacurium
  * **Duration of action**:
	* **Long-acting**: Pancuronium
	* **Intermediate-acting**: Rocuronium, vecuronium, atracurium, cisatracurium
	* **Short-acting**: Mivacurium
* **General characteristics** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Quaternary ammonium compounds with positively charged nitrogen atoms
  * Bind to alpha subunits of nicotinic acetylcholine receptors
  * Highly ionized, hydrophilic compounds with limited lipid solubility
  * Do not cross blood-brain barrier, placenta, gastrointestinal epithelium, or renal tubules
* **Key points about NMBAs** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * ⚠️ Do not alter consciousness or cause amnesia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Have been implicated in contributing to accidental awareness during anesthesia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Postoperative residual paralysis occurs in approximately 40% of patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Neuromuscular blocking agents play a prominent role in intraoperative anaphylaxis (incidence <0.1%) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Nondepolarizing agents account for >50% of intraoperative anaphylaxis cases [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

## 2.3. COMPARATIVE PROPERTIES

**NMBAs vary in their potency, onset time, duration of action, and other properties.**

* **Potency measures** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * ED₉₅: Median dose required in 50% of patients for 95% depression of maximal muscle contraction
  * Most common NMBAs have ED₉₅ of either 0.3 mg/kg (succinylcholine, rocuronium) or 0.05 mg/kg (cisatracurium, vecuronium)
* **Onset time** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Defined as time from administration until maximal neuromuscular blockade
  * Inversely related to dose
  * Affected by:
	* Rate of delivery to site of action (blood flow, injection speed, saline flush)
	* Receptor affinity
	* Mechanism of action (depolarizing vs. competitive nondepolarizing)
	* Plasma clearance (metabolism, redistribution)
* **Duration measures** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * **DUR 25%**: Time from administration until recovery of single twitch to a height of 25% of baseline
  * **Total duration (DUR 0.90)**: Time from administration until recovery of train-of-four ratio to 0.90
  * **Recovery index**: Time of spontaneous recovery from 25% to 75% of control twitch height
* **Comparative onset and duration** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * **Vecuronium**:
	* Intubating dose: 0.1 mg/kg
	* Onset: Medium
	* Duration: Medium
	* ED₉₅: 0.05 mg/kg
  * **Rocuronium**:
	* Intubating dose: 0.6 mg/kg  
	* Onset: Rapid
	* Duration: Medium
	* ED₉₅: 0.3 mg/kg
  * **Pancuronium**:
	* Intubating dose: 0.1 mg/kg
	* Onset: Medium
	* Duration: Long
  * **Atracurium**:
	* Intubating dose: 0.5 mg/kg
	* Onset: Medium
	* Duration: Medium
	* ED₉₅: 0.25 mg/kg
  * **Cisatracurium**:
	* Intubating dose: 0.15-0.2 mg/kg
	* Onset: Medium
	* Duration: Medium
	* ED₉₅: 0.05 mg/kg
  * **Mivacurium**:
	* Intubating dose: 0.2 mg/kg
	* Onset: Medium
	* Duration: Short
	* ED₉₅: 0.08 mg/kg

## 2.4. INTERPATIENT VARIATION

**Highly significant variation exists between patients in response to all neuromuscular blocking drugs.**

* **Variability in response** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Demonstrated in classic study by Katz (1967) showing variable responses to curare in 100 patients [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
  * Six patients showed no demonstrable depression of twitch height
  * Seven patients had complete abolition of twitch
  * Remaining patients had variable depression of twitch height
  * Impossible to predict response based on age, sex, body build, or ASA classification
* **Variability in duration** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Duration of action equally variable and unpredictable
  * Studies show wide range of recovery times at PACU arrival after equivalent doses
  * 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: Because of this wide variability, neuromuscular monitoring is essential as it is impossible to use time to predict adequate recovery
* **Implications for practice** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Each patient must be individually monitored for NMBA effect
  * Dosing should be titrated based on monitoring rather than standard protocols
  * Recovery must be confirmed objectively rather than estimated by time elapsed

![Interpatient Variation](NMBA_NMBA_Untitled_6.png_ff3808.png_f4a195.png)

**Bottom Line:** Neuromuscular blocking agents are classified as either depolarizing or nondepolarizing, with the latter further subdivided by chemical structure and duration. Significant interpatient variability exists in both response and duration, making individualized monitoring essential for safe use.

## 3.2. SUCCINYLCHOLINE (SUXAMETHONIUM)

**Succinylcholine is the only clinically available depolarizing muscle relaxant with the fastest onset and shortest duration of action.**

* **Structure and chemistry** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Two ACh molecules joined by methyl group
  * Also called suxamethonium
  * First discovered by Hunt and Taveau in 1906, introduced to anesthesia in the early 1950s [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Can be thought of as two molecules of ACh joined back-to-back through their acetyl groups [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Presentation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Formulated as a colourless solution containing 50 mg/ml
  * Should be stored at 4°C
* **Mechanism of action** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **nAChR agonist** causing prolonged muscle depolarization
  * Closely resembles ACh and binds to ACh receptors, generating muscle action potential
  * Not metabolized by acetylcholinesterase, causing prolonged depolarization of the end plate
  * Continuous end-plate depolarization causes muscle relaxation because:
	* Opening of perijunctional sodium channels is time-limited
	* After initial excitation and opening, sodium channels inactivate
	* Cannot reopen until end-plate repolarizes
	* End-plate cannot repolarize while succinylcholine continues binding
  * The persistent depolarisation produces local current circuits that render the voltage-sensitive Na+ channels within 1–2 millimetres inactive [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * This area of electrical inexcitability prevents transmission of further action potentials [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Phase I vs. Phase II block** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * **Phase I block** (typical for single dose):
	* Often preceded by muscle fasciculation from pre-junctional stimulation
	* Channels remain inactive until drug diffuses away to plasma
	* No fade to train-of-four stimulation
	* No fade to tetanic stimulation
	* No post-tetanic potentiation
	* Anticholinesterase drugs potentiate blockade
  * **Phase II block**:
	* Occurs after large dose (≥10 × ED₉₅), repeated doses, or prolonged infusion (>60 min)
	* Resembles nondepolarizing blockade (shows fade)
	* Poorly understood changes in ACh receptors
	* Mechanism thought to be different (probably a presynaptic effect) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

## 3.3. PHARMACOKINETICS

**Succinylcholine has rapid onset and short duration due to rapid plasma hydrolysis.**

* **Dosing** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * **Intubating dose**: 1-1.5 mg/kg (increase to 1.5-2 mg/kg if given with defasciculating dose of nondepolarizing NMBA)
  * **ED₉₅**: Approximately 0.3 mg/kg
* **Onset and Duration** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * **Onset**: 30-60 seconds after IV administration
  * **Duration**: ~10 minutes at intubating dose
	* Diaphragm may start contracting in as little as 5 minutes
	* DUR 25% is 10-12 minutes at 1-1.5 mg/kg
	* Prolonged beyond 15 minutes with larger doses
* **Administration routes** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Usually intravenous
  * Can be administered intraosseous, intralingual, and intramuscular when IV access unavailable
  * Intramuscular administration causes delayed onset
* **Metabolism** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Rapidly metabolized by pseudocholinesterase (plasma cholinesterase)
  * Extremely efficient process - only about 10% of the original IV dose reaches the NMJ
  * Abnormal (nonfunctional) pseudocholinesterase can dramatically extend duration:
	* **Heterozygous**: Incidence ~1/480; paralysis extended 50-100% (20-30 minutes)
	* **Homozygous**: Incidence ~1/3200; paralysis extended 4-8 hours
  * Hydrolyzed to choline and succinylmonocholine (weakly active) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Succinylmonocholine further metabolized by plasma cholinesterase to succinic acid and choline [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Because metabolism is rapid, less than 10% is excreted in the urine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Dibucaine number** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Percentage of normal pseudocholinesterase inhibited by dibucaine
  * Dibucaine does not inhibit abnormal pseudocholinesterase
  * **Normal**: 80
  * **Heterozygous**: 50
  * **Homozygous**: 20

## 3.4. CLINICAL USES

**Succinylcholine's rapid onset and short duration make it valuable for specific clinical scenarios.**

* **Primary indications** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Rapid sequence induction (RSI)**: "Reference standard" for rapid attainment of optimal tracheal intubating conditions
  * Most desirable features in surveys: rapid onset (88%), short duration (64%), effective relaxation (61%)
  * Used to achieve rapid muscle relaxation required during rapid sequence induction [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 
  * Has also been used by infusion to facilitate short surgical procedures [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 
* **Special populations** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Obese patients**: Calculate dose based on actual body weight rather than ideal body weight
  * **Children**: Require higher dose than adults (1.5-2.0 mg/kg, up to 3 mg/kg in infants)
  * **Emergency airway management**: Useful for treating laryngospasm
	* ⚠️ Appropriate neuromuscular monitoring should be used to ensure full recovery before tracheal extubation
* **Declining usage** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Use has decreased due to side effect concerns
  * Alternative for RSI is high-dose rocuronium (0.9-1.2 mg/kg)
  * 2015 Cochrane review found no statistically significant difference in intubation conditions between succinylcholine and rocuronium (1.2 mg/kg) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Drug interactions** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Neostigmine inhibits pseudocholinesterase, prolonging succinylcholine effect
  * ⚠️ Not recommended to administer succinylcholine immediately after neostigmine

## 3.5. CONTRAINDICATIONS 

**Several absolute and relative contraindications limit succinylcholine use.**

* **Absolute contraindications** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * History of malignant hyperthermia (patient or relatives)
  * Conditions with upregulated extrajunctional AChR (risk of severe hyperkalemia):
	* Burn injury >24h
	* Muscular dystrophy
	* Myotonias
	* Prolonged immobility
	* Upper motor neuron diseases:
	  * Spinal cord injury
	  * Stroke
	  * Tumor
	  * Multiple sclerosis
	  * Guillain-Barré syndrome
* **FDA warning** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * "Black box" warning against use in pediatric population
  * Exceptions: Emergency tracheal intubation or instances where immediate securing of airway is necessary
  * Issued due to cases of acute rhabdomyolysis with hyperkalemia, ventricular dysrhythmias, cardiac arrest, and death in healthy children later found to have undiagnosed skeletal muscle myopathy (most frequently Duchenne muscular dystrophy)
* **Relative contraindications** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Open globe (anterior chamber) - increased IOP
  * Increased ICP
  * ⚠️ **Note**: Normokalemic ESRF is not a contraindication as induction dose typically increases K⁺ by only ~0.5 mEq/L

## 3.6. ADVERSE EFFECTS 

**Succinylcholine has numerous potential adverse effects that can limit its clinical utility.**

* **Musculoskeletal effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Fasciculations**:
	* Can be decreased with defasciculating dose of rocuronium (0.03 mg/kg; 3 min prior)
	* Larger dose of succinylcholine required after defasciculation due to receptor competition
  * **Myalgia**:
	* Less frequent in children, 50-60y, good musculature
	* More frequent in women and ambulatory patients
	* Efficacy of nondepolarizing pretreatment controversial
	* Theorized due to initial unsynchronized contraction of muscle groups
	* Myoglobinemia and increased serum creatine kinase detectable
	* Best prophylaxis: pretreatment with NSAID (number needed to treat: 2.5)
	* Lidocaine pretreatment (1-1.5 mg/kg) also decreases incidence
  * **Trismus**:
	* Usually transient
	* If marked, increased and prolonged - suspect malignant hyperthermia
* **Autonomic effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Stimulation of all ACh receptors due to resemblance to ACh
  * Stimulation of nicotinic receptors in parasympathetic and sympathetic ganglia
  * Stimulation of muscarinic receptors in sinoatrial node
  * Low doses: negative chronotropic and inotropic effects
  * Higher doses: increased heart rate, contractility, and catecholamine levels
  * **Bradycardia**:
	* Especially common in children - given with atropine 0.02 mg/kg
	* May occur in adults when second bolus administered 3-8 min after first
	* 0.4 mg atropine often given prior to 2nd dose
	* Often more severe after a second dose but may be prevented by atropine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
	* Often more pronounced in children [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * **Tachycardia** at higher doses
* **Metabolic effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Hyperkalemia**:
	* Typical increase of ~0.5 mEq/L in normal patients
	* Severe hyperkalemia possible in certain pathologic states (receptor upregulation)
	* Pretreatment with nondepolarizing NMBA does not prevent hyperkalemic response
	* ⚠️ Treatment: hyperventilation, IV calcium and bicarbonate, inhaled beta-2 agonists, glucose with insulin
	* Burn patients at risk from about 24 hours after injury and for up to 18 months [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
	* Extra-junctional ACh receptors proliferate over muscle surface, releasing K+ into circulation when activated [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
	* Patients with paraplegia, progressive muscle disease or trauma-induced immobility at risk via similar mechanism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
	* Period of particular risk in paraplegia is first 6 months, continuing in progressive muscle disease [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Ocular effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Increased intraocular pressure**:
	* Transient increase up to 15 mmHg (5 minutes duration)
	* No evidence of worse outcome in "open" eye injury
	* ⚠️ Inadequate anesthesia allowing cough/Valsalva during laryngoscopy causes much larger IOP increases
	* Raised by about 10 mmHg for a matter of minutes (normal range 10-15 mmHg) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
	* Mechanism not clearly defined but involves contraction of tonic myofibrils and transient dilation of choroidal blood vessels [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
	* Concurrent thiopental will offset this rise so IOP remains static or may even fall [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Intracranial effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Increased intracranial pressure (ICP)**:
	* Mechanism unclear but confirmed in neurosurgical patients with reduced intracranial compliance
	* Defasciculating dose of nondepolarizing NMBA prevents ICP increase
	* Effects of intubation on ICP far outweigh increases from succinylcholine
	* Not contraindicated for RSI in patients with intracranial mass lesions
* **Gastrointestinal effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Increased intragastric pressure and LES pressure from abdominal wall muscle fasciculations
  * No evidence of increased risk of gastric reflux or pulmonary aspiration
  * Intragastric pressure rises by about 10 cmH2O, but lower esophageal sphincter tone increases simultaneously so no increased risk of reflux [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Other serious reactions** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Anaphylaxis**: One of the perioperative drugs with highest incidence (~1:10,000)
  * **Malignant hyperthermia**: Known trigger, especially in combination with volatile anesthetics

## 3.7. MALIGNANT HYPERTHERMIA 

**Malignant hyperthermia is a rare genetic disorder triggered by succinylcholine and volatile anesthetics.**

* **Epidemiology** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Rare condition with incidence of 1 in 50,000-70,000 in the UK
  * Affects males (62%) more than females (38%)
  * May occur after many previous uneventful anesthetics
  * Autosomal dominant inheritance
* **Mechanism** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Ryanodine receptor on sarcoplasmic reticulum membrane (encoded on chromosome 19) is involved
  * Three isoforms: RYR1 (skeletal muscle), RYR2 (heart muscle), RYR3 (brain)
  * RYR1 functions as main Ca²⁺ channel allowing stored Ca²⁺ from sarcoplasmic reticulum into cytoplasm
  * Abnormal RYR1 receptors allow excessive Ca²⁺ to pass, resulting in generalized muscle rigidity
  * ATP consumption high as it's used to return Ca²⁺ to sarcoplasmic reticulum
  * Increased CO₂, heat, and lactate production
  * Cells eventually break down, resulting in myoglobinemia and hyperkalemia
* **Clinical presentation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Muscle rigidity
  * Hyperthermia
  * Tachycardia
  * Increased end-tidal CO₂
  * Metabolic and respiratory acidosis
  * Hyperkalemia
* **Treatment** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Intravenous dantrolene (increments of 1 mg/kg up to 10 mg/kg)
  * Aggressive cooling (using ice-cold saline to lavage bladder and peritoneum if open)
  * Correction of abnormal biochemical and hematological parameters
  * Continue treatment in ICU until symptoms completely resolve to prevent recurrence
  * Before dantrolene introduction in 1979, mortality rate was 70%; now less than 5%
* **Diagnosis** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Based on response of biopsied muscle to 2% halothane and caffeine (2 mmol/L)
  * Patients labeled:
	* 'Susceptible' (MHS) - positive to both halothane and caffeine
	* 'Equivocal' (MHE) - positive to either halothane or caffeine
	* 'Non-susceptible' (MHN) - negative to halothane and caffeine
* **Dantrolene pharmacology** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * **Presentation**: 20 mg vials with 3 g mannitol and sodium hydroxide; reconstituted with 60 ml water (pH 9.5)
  * **Mechanism**: Uncouples excitation-contraction by binding to ryanodine receptor, preventing Ca²⁺ release from sarcoplasmic reticulum
  * **Side effects**: Highly irritant when extravasated; diuresis follows IV administration
  * **Other uses**: Neuroleptic malignant syndrome, chronic spasticity, ecstasy intoxication
  * **Chronic use**: Associated with hepatitis and pleural effusion
* **Safe anesthetic drugs** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Opioids, thiopental, propofol, etomidate, ketamine
  * Benzodiazepines, atropine
  * Local anesthetics
  * Nitrous oxide

## 3.8. PROLONGED BLOCK (SUXAMETHONIUM APNOEA) 

**Genetic variants of plasma cholinesterase can dramatically prolong succinylcholine action.**

* **Mechanism** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Plasma cholinesterase activity may be reduced due to genetic variability or acquired conditions
  * Single amino acid substitutions responsible for genetically altered enzymatic activity
  * Four alleles identified: usual (normal), atypical (dibucaine-resistant), silent (absent), fluoride-resistant
  * Located on chromosome 3 and make up 10 genotypes
* **Clinical impact** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * 96% of population homozygous for normal Eu gene - metabolize succinylcholine rapidly
  * Up to 4% may be heterozygotes - mildly prolonged block of up to 10 minutes
  * Very small fraction may have genotype that confers block of a few hours
  * Management options:
	* Fresh frozen plasma administration (provides plasma cholinesterase)
	* Sedation and ventilation until spontaneous recovery
* **Dibucaine number** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Percentage of normal plasma cholinesterase inhibited by dibucaine at 10⁻⁵ mol/L concentration
  * Dibucaine inhibits normal cholinesterase but ineffective against variant forms
  * **Eu:Eu** (normal): Dibucaine number 80, normal duration
  * **Eu:Ea** (heterozygous usual/atypical): Incidence 1:25, dibucaine number 60, slightly prolonged
  * **Ea:Ea** (homozygous atypical): Incidence 1:2,800, dibucaine number 20, greatly prolonged
  * Other combinations have varying dibucaine numbers and durations
* **Acquired factors reducing plasma cholinesterase activity** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Pregnancy
  * Liver disease
  * Renal failure
  * Cardiac failure
  * Thyrotoxicosis
  * Cancer
  * Drugs: metoclopramide, ketamine, oral contraceptives, lithium, lidocaine, ester local anesthetics, cytotoxic agents, edrophonium, neostigmine, trimetaphan

**Bottom Line:** Succinylcholine remains the fastest-onset NMBA with shortest duration, making it valuable for rapid sequence induction and emergency airway management. However, its numerous potentially serious side effects (hyperkalemia, malignant hyperthermia, prolonged paralysis in pseudocholinesterase deficiency) and absolute contraindications mean alternatives like high-dose rocuronium are increasingly preferred when not time-critical.

## 4.2. CLASSIFICATION

**Nondepolarizing NMBAs are categorized by chemical structure and duration of action.**

* **Historical context** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Curare first described for anesthesia in a 1942 case series
  * Role: Facilitate tracheal intubation and provide muscle relaxation for surgery
  * Ongoing search for ideal agent with quick onset, quick offset, and no side effects
* **Chemical classification** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * **Benzylisoquinolinium compounds** (ending "-urium"):
	* Cisatracurium, atracurium, mivacurium, d-tubocurarine
	* More likely to cause histamine release (d-tubocurarine >> atracurium and mivacurium)
	* Histamine release can be attenuated with slower administration
  * **Aminosteroid compounds** (ending "-onium"):
	* Pancuronium, vecuronium, rocuronium, pipecuronium
	* Vagolytic effects (pancuronium > rocuronium > vecuronium)
* **Duration-based classification** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * **Long-acting**: Pancuronium
  * **Intermediate-acting**: Rocuronium, vecuronium, atracurium, cisatracurium (most commonly used)
  * **Short-acting**: Mivacurium
* **General properties** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Owing to relatively polar nature, unable to cross lipid membranes
  * Results in small volume of distribution
  * Some hydrolyzed in plasma (atracurium, mivacurium)
  * Others undergo hepatic metabolism (pancuronium, vecuronium)
  * Unmetabolized fraction excreted in urine or bile

## 4.3. MECHANISM OF ACTION

**Nondepolarizing NMBAs competitively inhibit nicotinic acetylcholine receptors.**

* **Receptor antagonism** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Competitive inhibition of nAChR at the neuromuscular junction
  * Binds to one or both alpha subunits without opening the channels
  * Prevents ACh from binding, resulting in no end-plate potential
  * Block both preand postsynaptic nicotinic receptors
  * Drugs with two quaternary ammonium groups (bisquaternary) more potent than those with one (monoquaternary) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Presynaptic effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Block presynaptic nAChR (α3β2) that help mobilize ACh-containing vesicles
  * Results in the "fade" seen on train-of-four
  * Fade is due to decreased ACh available for release during repeated stimulation
* **Autonomic effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * May interact with nicotinic and muscarinic cholinergic receptors in sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems at large doses
  * "Autonomic margin of safety" exists
  * Rocuronium: ED₅₀ >3-5 mg/kg to block vagal effects, >10 mg/kg to block sympathetic effects
* **Reversal mechanisms** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Unbinding from receptor, redistribution, metabolism, excretion
  * Specific reversal agents available (cholinesterase inhibitors, sugammadex)
  * Anticholinesterases should not be given until spontaneous recovery has started (at least 2 twitches)
	* ⚠️ Can paradoxically slow recovery if given too early
* **Neuromuscular block characteristics** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * No fade to train-of-four or tetanic stimulation in depolarizing (Phase I) block
  * Shows fade to train-of-four and tetanic stimulation in nondepolarizing block
  * Post-tetanic potentiation present in nondepolarizing block, absent in depolarizing block
  * Anticholinesterases antagonize nondepolarizing block but augment depolarizing block

## 4.4. AMINOSTEROID COMPOUNDS

**Aminosteroid neuromuscular blockers share similar pharmacological properties but differ in onset, duration, and side effects.**

### 4.4.1. ROCURONIUM

* **Pharmacology** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Intermediate-duration NMBA with low potency (ED₉₅ 0.3 mg/kg)
  * Potency inversely related to speed of onset
  * Minimal active metabolites
  * Cleared unchanged in bile; renal excretion accounts for 30% of dose
  * Developed from vecuronium and structurally different at only four positions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Main advantage is rapid onset (hence name "RO-curonium") due to low potency [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * A low potency relaxant must be given at higher dose, creating greater concentration gradient from plasma to NMJ [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Presentation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Prepared as colorless solution containing 50 mg in 5 ml
* **Dosing** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Intubating dose**: 0.6 mg/kg (2× ED₉₅)
  * **RSI dose**: 1-1.2 mg/kg (onset 60-90s, but much longer duration)
  * **Maintenance**: Redosing with 10-25% of intubating dose
  * **Infusion rate**: 5-12 μg/kg/min
* **Clinical considerations** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Rapid onset at high dose makes it suitable RSI alternative to succinylcholine
  * Half-life variable with wide ranges reported
  * Abdominal insufflation reduces hepatic blood flow, potentially increasing duration by 25% in laparoscopic cases
  * ED₉₅ 15-25% lower for women compared to men
  * Pediatric patients: Slightly higher dose requirement, reduced onset and duration
  * Elderly: Significantly longer duration
  * Anaphylaxis risk comparable to succinylcholine (highest among NMBAs)
	* 💡 Higher incidence in hairdressers (possibly due to repeated work exposure to quaternary ammonium compounds in hair products)
  * Minimal cardiovascular effects, although at high doses when used for rapid intubation, may cause increase in heart rate [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Pharmacokinetics** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Mainly excreted unchanged in bile and to lesser extent in urine
  * Some de-acetylated metabolites may be produced
  * Duration of action may be prolonged in hepatic and renal failure
  * Protein binding: 10%
  * Volume of distribution: 0.20 L/kg
  * Metabolism: <5%
  * Elimination: 60% bile, 40% urine

### 4.4.2. VECURONIUM

* **Pharmacology** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Quaternary aminosteroid with one active metabolite (3-desacetylvecuronium)
  * Metabolite exhibits 50-70% activity of parent compound
  * Lipid soluble with significant biliary excretion
  * A "clean" drug that doesn't affect cardiovascular system or precipitate histamine release [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Chemical structure differs from pancuronium by a single methyl group, making it the monoquaternary analogue [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Presentation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Potentially unstable in solution, presented as 10 mg freeze-dried powder containing mannitol and sodium hydroxide
  * Dissolved in 5 ml water before administration
* **Dosing** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Intubating dose**: 0.1 mg/kg (2× ED₉₅ of 0.05 mg/kg)
  * **Maintenance**: 10% of intubating dose
  * **Infusion rate**: 1-2 μg/kg/min
  * At 0.1 mg/kg, satisfactory intubating conditions reached in about 90-120 seconds [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 
* **Clinical considerations** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * No vagolytic effects or histamine release
  * Active metabolite can accumulate in renal failure with repeat dosing
  * Precipitates out of solution if administered with thiopental
  * Case reports of prolonged duration in diabetes patients
  * Significantly longer duration in elderly patients
  * No cardiac effects but unlike pancuronium or tubocurarine, may leave unchecked bradycardia associated with fentanyl and propofol [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Critical illness myopathy seen most frequently in critically ill patients in association with corticosteroids and/or muscle relaxants [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Pharmacokinetics** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Like pancuronium, metabolized in liver by de-acetylation to 3and 17-hydroxy and 3,17-dihydroxy-vecuronium
  * The 3-hydroxy metabolite carries significant muscle-relaxant properties but unlike 3-hydroxypancuronium has very short half-life
  * Of little clinical significance with normal renal function
  * With only single charged quaternary ammonium group, more lipid-soluble than pancuronium
  * Despite similar proportion metabolized in liver, far greater proportion excreted in bile
  * May accumulate during administration by infusion
  * Protein binding: 10%
  * Volume of distribution: 0.23 L/kg
  * Metabolism: 20%
  * Elimination: 70% bile, 30% urine

### 4.4.3. PANCURONIUM

* **Pharmacology** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * One of oldest nondepolarizing NMBAs with long duration
  * Active metabolites (3-OH-pancuronium has half potency)
  * Duration prolonged in renal and hepatic impairment
  * Bisquaternary aminosteroidal compound [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Presentation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Presented as colorless solution containing 4 mg in 2 ml
  * Should be stored at 4°C
* **Dosing** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Intubating dose**: 0.1 mg/kg
  * **Infusion**: Not recommended
  * At 0.1 mg/kg intubating conditions reached within 90-150 seconds, duration about 45 minutes [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 
* **Clinical considerations** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Produces tachycardia through vagolytic and direct sympathomimetic actions
  * Anticholinesterase reversal less successful than with newer drugs
  * Sugammadex can reverse shallow pancuronium block
  * Historically used in longer cases but now rarely used due to increased risk of residual neuromuscular blockade
  * Causes tachycardia by blocking cardiac muscarinic receptors; may also have indirect sympathomimetic actions by preventing noradrenaline uptake at post-ganglionic nerve endings [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Pharmacokinetics** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Between 10-40% plasma protein-bound
  * Low volume of distribution like other drugs in class
  * About 35% metabolized in liver by de-acetylation to 3and 17-hydroxy and 3,17-dihydroxy-pancuronium
  * 3-hydroxy metabolite half as potent as pancuronium
  * Unchanged drug eliminated mainly in urine while metabolites excreted in bile
  * Protein binding: 20-60%
  * Volume of distribution: 0.27 L/kg
  * Metabolism: 30%
  * Elimination: 20% bile, 80% urine

## 4.5. BENZYLISOQUINOLINIUM COMPOUNDS

**Benzylisoquinolinium compounds share certain characteristics but have important differences in metabolism and side effects.**

### 4.5.1. ATRACURIUM

* **Pharmacology** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Mixture of 10 isomers with varying potency
  * Metabolized by:
	* Hofmann elimination (pH and temperature-dependent, non-enzymatic)
	* Ester hydrolysis
  * Metabolic breakdown produces laudanosine, which can cause neuroexcitation and seizures
  * Benzylisoquinolinium compound formulated as mixture of 10 stereoisomers from four chiral centers [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Presentation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Presented as colorless solution containing 10 mg/ml in 2.5, 5, and 25 ml vials
  * Should be stored at 4°C
* **Dosing** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Intubating dose**: 0.5 mg/kg (2× ED₉₅ of 0.25 mg/kg)
  * **Maintenance**: 10% of intubating dose
  * **Infusion rate**: 10-20 μg/kg/min
  * At 0.5 mg/kg intubating conditions reached within 90-120 seconds [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 
* **Clinical considerations** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Organ-independent metabolism makes it useful in renal/hepatic dysfunction
  * Histamine release causing hypotension and tachycardia, especially with rapid administration
  * Increasing dose to speed onset increases chance of histamine release
  * Relatively long onset of action (3-5 minutes) at intubating doses
  * Following rapid administration may precipitate histamine release, which may be localized to injection site or generalized resulting in bronchospasm and hypotension [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Slow intravenous injection minimizes these effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Associated with critical illness myopathy similar to vecuronium [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Metabolism** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * **Ester hydrolysis** (60% of metabolism):
	* Non-specific esterases unrelated to plasma cholinesterase
	* Breakdown products: quaternary alcohol, quaternary acid, laudanosine
	* Acidic conditions accelerate this pathway, but pH changes in clinical range probably don't alter rate
  * **Hofmann elimination**:
	* Stable at pH 4 and 4°C
	* Spontaneous breakdown to laudanosine and quaternary monoacrylate at normal body temperature and pH
	* Acidosis and hypothermia slow the process
	* Both breakdown products potentially have serious side effects (seizures) but at concentrations exceeding clinical levels
  * These metabolic pathways result in drug elimination independent of hepatic or renal function
  * Protein binding: 15%
  * Volume of distribution: 0.15 L/kg
  * Metabolism: 90%
  * Elimination: 0% bile, 10% urine

### 4.5.2. CISATRACURIUM

* **Pharmacology** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Single isomer (cis-cis) of atracurium (represents 15% by weight but >50% in potency)
  * Four times more potent than atracurium (ED₉₅ 0.05 mg/kg)
  * Metabolized primarily by Hofmann elimination
  * Does not release histamine
  * One of 10 stereoisomers present in atracurium [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Presentation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Presented as colorless solution containing 2 or 5 mg/ml
  * Should be stored at 4°C
* **Dosing** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Intubating dose**: 0.15-0.2 mg/kg (3-4× ED₉₅)
  * **Maintenance**: 10% of intubating dose
  * **Infusion rate**: 1-3 μg/kg/min
  * 3-4 times more potent than atracurium, therefore slower onset time [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 
  * Onset can be improved by increasing dose as potential for histamine release extremely low [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 
* **Clinical considerations** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Metabolism independent of liver function
  * Less laudanosine production than atracurium
  * Slower onset than atracurium due to higher potency
  * Lowest reported rate of IgE-mediated allergic reactions among NMBAs
  * Used safely in children from 2 years and in elderly patients with minimal kinetic alteration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * No change in kinetic profile in patients with end-stage renal or hepatic impairment [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Metabolism** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Similar profile to atracurium
  * Does not undergo direct hydrolysis by plasma esterases
  * Predominant pathway is Hofmann elimination to laudanosine and monoquaternary acrylate
  * Monoquaternary acrylate hydrolyzed by non-specific plasma esterases to monoquaternary alcohol and acrylic acid
  * All metabolites void of neuromuscular-blocking properties
  * Protein binding: 15%
  * Volume of distribution: 0.15 L/kg
  * Metabolism: 95%
  * Elimination: 0% bile, 5% urine

### 4.5.3. MIVACURIUM

* **Pharmacology** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Mixture of three stereoisomers with longer central chain
  * Metabolized by pseudocholinesterase at ~80% rate of succinylcholine
  * Short duration (15-20 minutes to T1 recovery to 25%)
  * Chiral mixture of three stereospecific isomers in proportions: 36% cis-trans, 58% trans-trans, 6% cis-cis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Cis-cis isomer has ~10% potency of other isomers and not metabolized enzymatically [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Half-life 10 times that of other isomers [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Presentation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Presented as acidic (pH 3.5-5.0) aqueous solution containing 2 mg/ml in 5 and 10 ml ampoules
  * Shelf-life 18 months when stored below 25°C
* **Dosing** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Intubating dose**: 0.2 mg/kg (2.5× ED₉₅ of 0.08 mg/kg)
  * **Infusion rate**: 5-8 μg/kg/min
* **Clinical considerations** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Low potency requires larger doses, leading to histamine release
  * Splitting initial dose did not prevent side effects
  * Duration prolonged in patients with depleted pseudocholinesterase activity
  * Neostigmine inhibits pseudocholinesterase but can still effectively reverse mivacurium
  * Edrophonium preferred for reversal (doesn't inhibit pseudocholinesterase)
  * Main advantage is short duration of action [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Routine reversal with neostigmine may not be required due to rapid enzymatic metabolism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Neostigmine inhibits plasma cholinesterase and may prevent metabolism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * High doses may release histamine, resulting in blood pressure fall and bronchospasm [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Pharmacokinetics** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Plasma cholinesterase responsible for metabolism of cis-trans and trans-trans isomers
  * Patients with genetically low plasma cholinesterase levels subject to prolonged neuromuscular blockade
  * Duration significantly prolonged in patients with end-stage liver disease, mainly due to reduced plasma cholinesterase activity
  * Protein binding: 10%
  * Volume of distribution: 0.21-0.32 L/kg (isomer specific)
  * Metabolism: 90%
  * Elimination: 0% bile, 5% urine

## 4.6. ADVERSE EFFECTS 

**Nondepolarizing NMBAs produce various adverse effects based on their chemical structure and receptor interactions.**

* **Autonomic effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Interaction with nicotinic and muscarinic cholinergic receptors
  * Effects are dose-related and additive over time
  * Newer drugs have fewer autonomic effects
  * Manifestations include:
	* Hypotension (usually due to histamine release)
	* Tachycardia (pancuronium causes modest heart rate increases)
	* Bradycardia
	* Asystole (rare)
* **Histamine release** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * More common with benzylisoquinoliniums (especially d-tubocurarine)
  * Atracurium and mivacurium can trigger release, particularly at higher doses
  * Can be ameliorated by:
	* Slow injection rates
	* H₁ and H₂ antihistamine pretreatment
* **Respiratory effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Vary depending on affinity for M₁-M₃ airway receptors
  * Can result in presynaptic M₂ block with severe bronchospasm
	* Led to withdrawal of rapacuronium from market
* **Anaphylaxis** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Nondepolarizing agents account for majority of intraoperative anaphylaxis cases
  * Highest risk: Rocuronium and succinylcholine
  * Lowest risk: Cisatracurium
* **Myopathy with prolonged use** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Risk with vecuronium when combined with corticosteroids in critically ill ventilated patients

## 4.7. DRUG INTERACTIONS

**Many drugs and conditions can alter the effectiveness and duration of nondepolarizing NMBAs.**

* **Interactions between NMBAs** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Complex interactions - clinical use of combinations should be avoided
  * Additive interactions between chemically related drugs (atracurium + mivacurium)
  * Synergistic response with chemically unrelated drugs (rocuronium + cisatracurium)
  * ⚠️ Interactions depend on sequence of administration
* **Anesthetic agents** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Volatile anesthetics**:
	* Extend duration of nondepolarizing NMBAs by effects on postjunctional receptors
	* Potentiating effect: desflurane > sevoflurane > isoflurane > nitrous oxide
	* Effect exaggerated with higher MAC or longer administration
  * **Propofol**:
	* Minimal effect on neuromuscular transmission
	* May shorten time to maximal rocuronium effect during infusion
* **Other agents** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Local anesthetics**:
	* Interact with both depolarizing and nondepolarizing NMBAs
	* Preand postsynaptic effects
	* Epidural levobupivacaine prolongs vecuronium recovery
	* IV lidocaine infusion has limited clinical effect on rocuronium
  * **Antibiotics**:
	* Aminoglycosides can influence NMBAs by affecting acetylcholine release
	* Older antibiotics (streptomycin, neomycin) depress neuromuscular function
  * **Anticonvulsants**:
	* Acute administration of phenytoin/carbamazepine potentiates blockade
	* Chronic administration decreases duration of aminosteroids
	* Minimal effect on benzylisoquinoliniums with chronic use
  * **Alzheimer's disease drugs**:
	* Donepezil hydrochloride can act peripherally to increase acetylcholine
	* Reports of inadequate block with nondepolarizing NMBAs
	* Prolonged weakness with depolarizing NMBAs
  * **Other medications**:
	* Beta-agonists delay rocuronium onset
	* Calcium channel antagonists have minimal clinical effect
	* Ephedrine hastens rocuronium onset (likely via increased cardiac output)
	* Corticosteroids increase risk of myopathy with vecuronium in ICU patients
* **Pharmacological/physiological interactions** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * **Volatile anesthetics**: Prolong blockade by depressing somatic reflexes in CNS
  * **Aminoglycosides**: Decrease ACh release possibly by competing with Ca²⁺
  * **Local anesthetics**: Low doses may enhance blockade through Na⁺ channel blockade
  * **Lithium**: Prolongs blockade via Na⁺ channel blockade
  * **Diuretics**: Variable effect on cAMP; may affect via serum K⁺
  * **Ca²⁺ channel antagonists**: Reduce Ca²⁺ influx leading to reduced ACh release
  * **Hypothermia**: Reduces metabolism of muscle relaxant
  * **Acidosis**: Variable effect; prolonged for most but reduced for gallamine
  * **Electrolyte changes**:
	* Hypokalaemia increases (more negative) resting membrane potential, potentiating nondepolarizing relaxants
	* Hypermagnesaemia decreases ACh release by competing with Ca²⁺

## 4.8. ALTERED RESPONSES IN SPECIAL CONDITIONS 

**Various physiological and pathological conditions can significantly alter NMBA effectiveness.**

* **Organ dysfunction** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Hepatic dysfunction**:
	* Affects metabolism/elimination of aminosteroids
	* Benzylisoquinoliniums preferred (Hofmann degradation relatively independent of organ function)
	* Vecuronium and rocuronium blockade prolonged as they depend heavily on biliary excretion
  * **Renal failure**:
	* Pancuronium and vecuronium blockade prolonged
	* Cisatracurium preferred due to organ-independent elimination
* **Body composition changes** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Increased total body water** (hepatic failure, renal failure, burns):
	* Increases dose requirement for nondepolarizing NMBAs
  * **Aging**:
	* Decreased total body water and serum albumin
	* Reduces volume of distribution, lowering dose requirement
	* Decreased cardiac output, GFR, and liver blood flow decrease elimination
  * **Obesity**:
	* Dosage 20% more than lean body weight
* **Temperature effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Hypothermia**:
	* Decreases receptor sensitivity and acetylcholine mobilization
	* Reduces muscle contraction
	* Reduces renal and hepatic metabolism
	* Slows Hofmann degradation
	* Prolongs blockade
* **Electrolyte disturbances** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Hypokalemia**:
	* Potentiates neuromuscular block
	* Reduces effectiveness of neostigmine antagonism
  * **Hypermagnesemia**:
	* Inhibits preand postsynaptic Ca²⁺ channels
	* Potentiates neuromuscular blocking drug effect
  * **Hypocalcemia**:
	* Potentiates blockade
  * **Acidosis**:
	* Interferes with anticholinesterase drugs
	* Reduces their ability to reverse blockade
  * **Hypercarbia**:
	* Produces acidosis, reducing antagonism
* **Special populations** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **ICU patients**:
	* Prolonged use should be avoided
	* Short-term infusions may reduce barotrauma and in-hospital mortality in prone ARDS patients
	* Avoid in diabetic patients or those receiving steroids
  * **Neuromuscular disease**:
	* Generally increases sensitivity to depolarizing agents
	* Variable sensitivity to nondepolarizing NMBAs
	* ⚠️ Succinylcholine should be avoided due to MH and rhabdomyolysis risk
  * **Neonates**:
	* Increased sensitivity to nondepolarizing NMBAs due to immature NMJ
	* Larger volume of distribution (greater extracellular space)
	* Overall effect doesn't significantly decrease dosage requirements
  * **Elderly**:
	* Decreased clearance and prolonged duration

**Bottom Line:** Nondepolarizing NMBAs are classified as aminosteroids or benzylisoquinoliniums with varying durations of action. They competitively block nicotinic acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular junction. Drug choice should consider patient-specific factors including organ function, age, body composition, and potential drug interactions. Monitoring is essential due to wide interpatient variability and possible adverse effects.

## 5.2. PRINCIPLES OF OPERATION

**Neuromuscular monitoring involves direct nerve stimulation and evaluation of the resultant muscle response.**

* **Importance of monitoring** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Neuromuscular blocking drugs (NMBDs) are key components of balanced anesthesia
  * NMBDs are dose-dependent risk factors for postoperative morbidity and mortality
  * Monitoring helps prevent residual paralysis and its complications
  * International Anesthesia Research Society (2018) consensus: "Whenever a neuromuscular blocker is administered, neuromuscular function must be monitored by observing the evoked muscular response to peripheral nerve stimulation" [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **ASA 2023 Guidelines**: Practice guidelines provide evidence-based recommendations on management of neuromuscular monitoring and antagonism to enhance patient safety by reducing residual neuromuscular blockade
  * ⚠️ **Critical Safety Issue**: Residual neuromuscular blockade (TOF ratio <0.9) remains a frequent occurrence with incidence as high as 64% of patients despite current practice
  * **Clinical complications of residual block**: Upper airway obstruction, reintubation, atelectasis, pneumonia, prolonged PACU stay, decreased patient satisfaction
* **Two-step monitoring process** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  1. Direct nerve stimulation with electrical impulse
  2. Evaluation of evoked muscle response ("twitch")

* **Stand-alone nerve stimulators** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Deliver current at 0-80 mA amplitude
  * Usually applied as train-of-four (TOF)
  * Clinician subjectively assesses muscle response
  * Evaluation includes number of movements and strength differences ("fade")
* **Neuromuscular monitors** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Stimulate motor neuron
  * Measure downstream response
  * Generate TOF ratio
  * Provide objective measurement of blockade depth
* **Current characteristics** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * 60-80 mA over peripheral motor nerve
  * 200 μs duration
  * Square-wave pattern
  * Equal current intensity
  * Must generate supramaximal stimulus (60-80 mA) to ensure all composite nerve fibers are depolarized [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Duration of stimulus is 0.1 milliseconds [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Electrode types** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * ECG silver chloride pads
  * Subcutaneous needles
  * Negative electrode should be directly over nerve while positive electrode placed where it can't affect muscle in question [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

## 5.3. MONITOR TYPES AND TECHNOLOGY

**Various technologies are available for neuromuscular monitoring, each with advantages and limitations.**

* **Mechanomyography (MMG)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Directly measures force generated by distal extremity
  * Laboratory use only (not commercially available for clinical settings)
  * Considered the reference standard for comparison
* **Acceleromyography (AMG)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Stimulates motor nerve
  * Measures acceleration of innervated extremity
  * Most common: ulnar nerve stimulation → adductor pollicis measurement
  * ⚠️ May report baseline TOF ratio >1 (up to 1.6)
	  * In practical terms, the TOF ratio using AMG should be as close to 1.0 as possible, realising that even at this level of recovery, most (>75%) postsynaptic receptors are still blocked. Despite these caveats, AMG monitors are widely available, are simple to apply and use and an extensive evidence base shows their superiority over clinical and qualitative methods of recovery using peripheral nerve stimulation.
  * ⚠️ May underestimate residual blockade
* **Electromyography (EMG)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Stimulates motor nerve
  * Measures resultant compound action potentials
  * **Advantage**: Doesn't require extremity movement (useful when arms are tucked)
  * **Limitation**: Accuracy may be affected by electromagnetic interference (e.g., electrocautery), hypothermia
  * Target recovery is acceptable at TOF ratios >0.9. Electromyography monitoring is the ‘ideal quantitative monitor’ in the evidence-based consensus opinion of an expert group.
* **Kinemyography (KMG)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Uses isoelectric polymer strip in the space between thumb and index finger
  * Deforms with thumb movement
  * Measures range of motion rather than force; subject to large bias.
* **TOF Cuff** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Electrodes embedded within blood pressure cuff (compressomyography)
  * Measures pressure changes in cuff caused by muscle contractions
  * Volumetric measurement rather than force measurement
  * Onset and recovery do not correlate with responses at the ulnar nerve using AMG and EMG and appear to reflect more closely the resistant central muscles, including the larynx and diaphragm.
* **Technology comparison to reference standard** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * EMG measurements most closely match MMG
  * AMG values typically higher than MMG (TOF ratios may be exaggerated)
  * Different measurement biases exist between technologies:
	* **Bias in measured TOF ratio**: EMG may read lower; AMG typically reads higher
	* **Bias in time to reach adequate TOF ratio**: AMG reaches target values faster than MMG
	* 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: When using AMG, target a TOF ratio of 0.95-1.0 (rather than 0.9) to ensure full recovery

## 5.4. CLINICAL VS. QUANTITATIVE ASSESSMENT 

**Clinical assessment methods are inadequate for detecting residual paralysis, making quantitative monitoring essential.**

* **ASA 2023 Strong Recommendations**:
  * **Against clinical assessment alone**: "When neuromuscular blocking drugs are administered, we recommend against clinical assessment alone to avoid residual neuromuscular blockade, due to the insensitivity of the assessment" (Strength: Strong, Evidence: Moderate)
  * **For quantitative over qualitative monitoring**: "We recommend quantitative monitoring over qualitative assessment to avoid residual neuromuscular blockade" (Strength: Strong, Evidence: Moderate)
* **Historical approaches** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Time from last NMBA administration
	* Highly variable offset for all NMBAs makes this unreliable
  * Physical assessments of muscle function
	* Maximum inspiratory force
	* Vital capacity
	* Tidal volume
	* Eye opening
	* Tongue protrusion
	* Handgrip
	* Leg lift
	* Head lift
	* ⚠️ All clinical assessments are insensitive for detecting residual paralysis
* **Evidence of clinical assessment inadequacy**:
  * **Head lift study**: Only 11% sensitivity to detect residual neuromuscular blockade (TOF ratio <0.9)
  * **19% sensitivity** for TOF ratio <0.7
  * **Healthy volunteers**: Could sustain head lift with TOF ratio as low as 0.45
* **Limitations of subjective assessment** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Five-second head lift accomplished by 11/12 volunteers despite significant residual paralysis (TOF ratio ≤0.5)
  * Even in absence of any tactile or visual recognition of fade with TOF, ratio can be as low as 0.3-0.4
  * "Zone of blind paralysis": TOF ratio 0.4-0.9 cannot be subjectively detected
  * Fade with double burst stimulation still misses TOF ratios as low as 0.6
  * **Peripheral nerve stimulator limitations**: Subjective assessment cannot reliably appreciate fade until TOF ratio <0.4
* **Benefits of quantitative monitoring** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Only way to objectively measure TOF ratio
  * Dramatically reduces incidence of residual paralysis
  * Studies show numerous adverse outcomes associated with low TOF ratios
  * Widely endorsed in international guidelines
  * Essential for implementing appropriate reversal strategies
  * **Evidence**: Patients monitored quantitatively had significantly less residual neuromuscular blockade compared with qualitative or clinical assessment
* **Adoption barriers** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Provider perception that monitoring is unnecessary
  * Unreliable monitors and technical challenges
  * Workflow interruptions
  * Lack of training

## 5.5. PERIPHERAL NERVE STIMULATION 

**Proper technique is essential for accurate neuromuscular monitoring.**

* **ASA 2023 Strong Recommendations for Monitoring Site**:
  * **Adductor pollicis recommended**: "We recommend using the adductor pollicis muscle for neuromuscular monitoring" (Strength: Strong, Evidence: Moderate)
  * **Against eye muscles**: "We recommend against using eye muscles for neuromuscular monitoring" (Strength: Strong, Evidence: Moderate)
  * **Evidence**: Time to reach TOF ratio ≥0.9 at adductor pollicis was longer compared with eye muscles, with less residual neuromuscular blockade when patients monitored at adductor pollicis vs. corrugator supercilii
* **Common monitoring method** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Stimulate ulnar nerve at wrist
  * Observe adductor pollicis muscle response
  * Most studied site with best correlation to clinically relevant recovery; This most closely approximates the sensitive muscle groups needing longertimes to recovery.
* **Proper electrode placement** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Positioned on wrist over ulnar nerve
  * Negative electrode (usually black) placed distally
  * Positive electrode (usually red) placed proximally ("red toward the head")
  * Thumb adduction should only occur from conducted nerve impulses, not direct muscle stimulation
* **Positioning requirements** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Hand positioned with thumb free to move
  * Preload may be applied with some AMG systems
* **Baseline measurement** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Obtain TOF ratio before NMBD administration
  * Use as reference for post-administration comparison
* **Alternative monitoring sites** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Facial nerve → orbicularis oculi/corrugator supercilii
	* ⚠️ **Warning**: Often used when extremity access is limited but may cause direct muscle stimulation
	* ⚠️ Leads to underestimation of residual blockade (as there is relative resistance of some eye muscles)
	* Can result in excessive NMBD administration or inadequate reversal agent dosing
	* Should not be used for monitoring ideally. If monitored, the ulnar nerve response should always be measured at the end of surgery, before waking the patient and tracheal extubation.
	* There is a five-fold greater risk of residual neuromuscular block with facial nerve compared with ulnar nerve monitoring.
  * First dorsal interosseous (dorsum of hand)
	* Closely matches adductor pollicis sensitivity
  - Posterior tibial nerve may be used but time course for recovery of flexor hallucis brevis muscle may differ by several minutes from recovery of adductor pollicis muscle.
* **Safety considerations for complete recovery**:
  * Complete recovery of all muscles optimizes patient safety
  * Measurements should be obtained at sites with longer recovery times
  * Monitoring resistant muscles (eye muscles) creates potential for NMBA overdose and false reassurance of adequate reversal

## 5.6. TRAIN-OF-FOUR MONITORING

**Train-of-four monitoring is the most widely used method for assessing neuromuscular blockade.** The depth of neuromuscular blockade can be defined based on the Train-of-four (TOF) ratio, TOF count, and post-tetanic count (PTC) when using quantitative monitoring.

* **Train-of-four (TOF) stimulation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * 4 successive 200μs stimuli delivered over 2s (2Hz)
  * Should not be repeated more frequently than every 15 seconds
  * Four 0.1 millisecond stimuli delivered at 2 Hz [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Parameters measured** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * **TOF count**: Number of twitches observed (0-4)
  * **TOF ratio**: Height of 4th twitch compared to 1st (T4/T1)
* **Nondepolarizing block progression** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Sequential disappearance of twitches correlates with extent of blockade:
	* 4th twitch disappearance = 75% block
	* 3rd twitch disappearance = 80% block
	* 2nd twitch disappearance = 90% block
	* 1st twitch disappearance = 100% block
  * Clinical relaxation typically requires 75-95% blockade
  * 70-75% receptor occupancy begins to affect T4, showing T4<T1 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * No twitch reduction until 75% of NMJ receptors occupied by muscle relaxant [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * This margin of safety exists because only small number of receptors required to generate action potential [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  ![[Pasted image 20250526152242.png]]
* **Mechanism of fade** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Prejunctional effect of nondepolarizing relaxants
  * Reduces ACh available for release during stimulation
  * Blockade of ACh mobilization
* **TOF characteristics by blockade type** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Partial depolarizing (Phase I) block: Reduced but equal twitch height, no fade
  * Partial non-depolarizing block: Reducing twitch height, fade, post-tetanic facilitation
* **Other assessment methods** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * **Single twitch**: Individual stimuli at 0.1-10 Hz
	* Useful during onset of blockade
	* If frequency exceeds 0.1 Hz, muscle fatigue may occur
	* Simplest form of neurostimulation, requires baseline twitch height [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * **Tetanic stimulation**:
	* Sustained contraction for 5s indicates adequate (but not necessarily complete) reversal
	* More sensitive than TOF but painful for awake patients
	* Individual stimuli applied at frequency >30 Hz become fused into sustained contraction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
	* Most stimulators deliver 0.1ms stimuli at 50 Hz for maximum sensitivity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
	* Partial NDMR causes tetanic stimulation to fade with time due to presynaptic ACh receptor blockade [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
	* Partial DMR block reduces but doesn't show fade with tetanic stimulation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * **Double-burst stimulation (DBS)**:
	* Less painful than tetany
	* More sensitive than TOF for clinical (visual) evaluation of fade
	* Patterns include DBS3,3 and DBS3,2
	* Delivery of two bursts separated by 0.75 seconds [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
	* Each burst consists of three 0.2ms stimuli separated by 20ms (at 50 Hz) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
	* Developed for easy manual detection of small residual blockade [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
	* When assessed mechanically, no more sensitive than TOF [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * **Posttetanic count (PTC)**:
	* Used when TOF count is zero
	* Utilizes acetylcholine flood after tetanic stimulation
	* Number of twitches inversely related to blockade depth
	* 5s of 50Hz tetany, followed by 3s pause, then 15-20 single stimuli at 1Hz
	* ⚠️ Should not be repeated for at least 2-3 minutes to avoid false readings
	* Forms basis of post-tetanic count where stimuli at 1 Hz started 3 seconds after tetanic stimulation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
	* Number of twitches inversely related to depth of block, best used when receptor blockade >95% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
	* Effects of tetanic stimulation may last up to 6 minutes, giving false impression of inadequate block [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
	* Partial DMR block does not exhibit post-tetanic potentiation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]

## 5.7. DIFFERENTIAL MUSCLE SENSITIVITY

**Different muscle groups show varying sensitivity to neuromuscular blocking agents, affecting monitoring and clinical decision-making.** Resistant muscles generally have rich blood supply, ensuring a rapid onset (wash-in) but limited peak effect and rapid recovery (wash-out). More sensitive muscles are slower to recover. 

![[Pasted image 20250526152929.png]]

* **Sensitivity spectrum** (from most resistant to most sensitive) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  1. Vocal cords
  2. Diaphragm
  3. Corrugator supercilii
  4. Abdominal muscles
  5. Adductor pollicis
  6. Pharyngeal muscles

* **Clinical implications** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * To assess deep blockade (ablate diaphragmatic movement): Monitor corrugator supercilii
  * To assess readiness for extubation (return of pharyngeal muscles): Monitor adductor pollicis
  * ⚠️ Recovery of adductor pollicis does not parallel recovery of airway muscles
  * Diaphragm, rectus abdominis, laryngeal adductors, and orbicularis oculi recover sooner than adductor pollicis.
  * Appreciating the differences in muscle sensitivity explains why patients may still respond to laryngoscopy and intubation, why these responses may be present even when a measured adductor pollicis TOF ratio is reduced to zero and why ‘time-based’ decisions on readiness for intubation (or extubation) are flawed. Monitoring throughout anaesthesia allows variability between patients to be appreciated, based on individual neuromuscular responses.
* **Monitoring site selection guidance** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Safest sites** for clinical decisions: Adductor pollicis or first dorsal interosseous
  * **Most resistant sites** (avoid): Orbicularis oculi and corrugator supercilii
	* Difficult to avoid direct muscle stimulation
	* FDA-approved sugammadex dosing doesn't apply when measuring at corrugator supercilii
	* Residual paralysis incidence 5× higher when monitoring eye muscles vs. adductor pollicis
  * ⚠️ If eye muscle monitoring used intraoperatively, must switch to adductor pollicis before pharmacologic reversal
* **Important consideration** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Residual weakness in airway and respiratory muscles with apparently adequate recovery at monitoring site
  * Planning for effective reversal requires valid pre-reversal assessment at the adductor pollicis site

## 5.8. RECEPTOR OCCUPANCY RELATIONSHIPS

**Understanding receptor occupancy provides important insights for NMBA dosing and monitoring.**

* **Receptor occupancy estimate** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Only 25-30% of receptors need to be available (70-75% occupied) for normal TOF ratio
  * Small fraction of receptors need to be blocked when redosing
* **Relationship to neuromuscular block depth** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * **Pre-block**: 0% receptor occupancy → TOF ratio 1.0
  * **Complete block**: >95% occupancy → TOF count 0, posttetanic count 0
  * **Deep block**: 90-95% occupancy → TOF count 0, posttetanic count ≥1
  * **Moderate block**: 70-90% occupancy → TOF count 1-3
  * **Shallow block**: 60-70% occupancy → TOF count 4, TOF ratio <0.4
  * **Minimal block**: 60-70% occupancy → TOF count 4, TOF ratio 0.4-0.9
  * **Recovered**: <70% occupancy → TOF count 4, TOF ratio ≥0.9
* **Clinical relevance** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Redosing after partial recovery requires significantly less NMBA than initial dose
  * TOF ratio of ≥0.9 (or even when TOF = 1.0) still represents substantial receptor occupancy; thus, FVC is still only partially recovered, and the acute ventilatory response to hypoxia is depressed from normal.
  * Full functional recovery requires TOF ratio above threshold level

## 5.9. TOF RATIO CONFIRMATION BEFORE EXTUBATION 

**ASA 2023 guidelines provide strong recommendations for confirming adequate recovery before extubation.**

* **ASA 2023 Strong Recommendation**:
  * **TOF ≥0.9 confirmation required**: "When using quantitative monitoring, we recommend confirming a train-of-four ratio greater than or equal to 0.9 before extubation" (Strength: Strong, Evidence: Moderate)
* **Evidence supporting TOF ≥0.9 confirmation**:
  * **With sugammadx**: TOF ratio confirmed vs. not confirmed before extubation
	* Confirmed ≥0.9: 0.5% residual blockade (95% CI, 0.0-6.0%)
	* Not confirmed: 2.2% residual blockade (95% CI, 0.5-9.0%)
  * **With neostigmine**: TOF ratio confirmed vs. not confirmed before extubation
	* Confirmed ≥0.9: 5.3% residual blockade (95% CI, 2.5-10.7%)
	* Not confirmed: 44.9% residual blockade (95% CI, 29.9-60.8%)
* **Clinical significance of TOF ratio thresholds**:
  * **TOF ratio 0.7**: Historical threshold, but inadequate for full recovery
  * **TOF ratio 0.9**: Current evidence-based threshold for safe extubation
	* Associated with near-normal vital capacity and inspiratory force
	* Prevents upper airway obstruction and impaired airway protective reflexes
	* Reduces risk of aspiration and unpleasant symptoms of muscle weakness
* **Safety margins in neuromuscular recovery**:
  * Even with TOF ratio 0.9, approximately 70-75% of receptors remain occupied
  * Only 25-30% of receptors need to be available for normal TOF ratio
  * This represents substantial receptor blockade despite "adequate" recovery
* **Implementation requirements**:
  * Quantitative monitoring essential for accurate TOF ratio measurement
  * Document TOF ratio ≥0.9 before extubation in medical record
  * Monitor at adductor pollicis for extubation decisions
  * Allow adequate time for recovery/reversal to reach target TOF ratio

## 5.10. CLINICAL IMPLEMENTATION 

**Implementing quantitative monitoring into clinical practice requires systematic approaches to overcome barriers.**

* **Recovery indicators** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]:
  * **Full recovery indicators**:
	* TOF ratio ≥0.9
	* 5 seconds sustained tetany at 50-100Hz without fade
	* Sustained (≥5s) head lift
	* Ability to generate inspiratory pressure ≥-25 cm H₂O
	* Forceful hand grip
* **Yet, non-quantitative means CANNOT determine readiness for tracheal extubation.**
	* Statements such as ‘four strong twitches are present’ or ‘there is no fade’ are demonstrably false and do not guarantee adequate neuromuscular recovery. Fade assessed subjectively (visual or tactile means) cannot be detected reliably when the TOF ratio is >0.4, with a resultant wide gap (TOF ratio 0.4-0.9) when using subjective monitoring. This degree of block can only be appreciated when using quantitative measurement of the TOF ratio. 
	* When using double-burst stimulation (DBS), fade of the second of the two mini-tetanic responses cannot be detected reliably when the DBS (or TOF) ratio is >0.6. Although the gap using DBS is narrower (TOF ratio 0.6-0.9) than that of TOF (TOF ratio 0.4-0.9) when using subjective means, neither pattern can safely and reliably determine the adequacy of recovery, and their use should be abandoned.
* **Implementation strategies** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]:
  * Department-wide equipment trials to select ideal technology
  * Place monitors in all anesthetizing locations
  * Education about clinical importance of residual paralysis
  * Clear protocols for documentation
  * Follow-up with clinicians regarding performance
  * Department performance metrics
  * Policy linking quantitative monitoring use to credentialing
* **Practice recommendations** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]:
  * Treat quantitative monitoring as essential as blood pressure measurement
  * Use measurements to guide both administration and reversal of NMBAs
  * Document TOF ratio ≥0.9 before extubation
  * Develop protocols for using equipment when arms are tucked
  * Ensure adequate equipment support and maintenance

**Bottom Line:** Objective, quantitative neuromuscular monitoring is essential for patient safety, as subjective methods cannot reliably detect residual paralysis. Monitoring should be performed at the adductor pollicis muscle when assessing readiness for extubation, and a TOF ratio ≥0.9 must be documented before extubation. Different muscles have varying sensitivity to NMBAs, with central muscles recovering earlier than peripheral ones.

## 6.2. DEPTH OF BLOCK DEFINITIONS

**Standardized definitions of neuromuscular block depth guide reversal strategies.**

* **ASA 2023 Definitions of Neuromuscular Block Depth**:
  * **Complete block**: TOF count 0, posttetanic count 0, receptor occupancy >95%
  * **Deep block**: TOF count 0, posttetanic count ≥1, receptor occupancy 90-95%
  * **Moderate block**: TOF count 1-3, receptor occupancy 70-90%
  * **Shallow block**: TOF count 4, TOF ratio <0.4, receptor occupancy 60-70%
  * **Minimal block**: TOF count 4, TOF ratio 0.4-0.9, receptor occupancy 60-70%
  * **Recovered**: TOF count 4, TOF ratio ≥0.9, receptor occupancy <70%
* **Six levels of neuromuscular block** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Complete block**:
	* TOF count 0, posttetanic count 0
	* Receptor occupancy >95%
	* No thumb twitch can be elicited, even after tetanic stimulation
  * **Deep block**:
	* TOF count 0, posttetanic count ≥1
	* Receptor occupancy 90-95%
	* Thumb movement only after tetanic stimulation
  * **Moderate block**:
	* TOF count 1-3
	* Receptor occupancy 70-90%
	* Encompasses TOF counts 1, 2, and 3
  * **Shallow block**:
	* TOF count 4, TOF ratio <0.4
	* Receptor occupancy 60-70%
	* Requires quantitative monitoring to identify
  * **Minimal block**:
	* TOF count 4, TOF ratio 0.4-0.9
	* Receptor occupancy 60-70%
	* Requires quantitative monitoring to identify
  * **Recovered**:
	* TOF count 4, TOF ratio ≥0.9
	* Receptor occupancy <70%
	* Safe for extubation
* **Clinical significance** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * These definitions guide appropriate reversal agent selection and dosing
  * Three levels of TOF count 4 can only be accurately identified using quantitative monitoring
  * Most important is "recovered" level (TOF ratio ≥0.9) for extubation safety
  * **Critical concept**: When measuring with acceleromyography, baseline TOF ratios often exceed 1.0 (range 1.1-1.15), requiring normalization for accurate assessment

## 6.3. ANTICHOLINESTERASE DRUGS

**Anticholinesterase drugs increase acetylcholine availability to compete with nondepolarizing NMBAs.**

* **Mechanism of action** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Block breakdown of acetylcholine by acetylcholinesterase
  * Increase available ACh at synaptic cleft
  * Improve chances of competing with nondepolarizing NMBA
  * Create parallel processes:
	* Inhibition of acetylcholinesterase → increased ACh concentration
	* Continued metabolism/elimination of NMBA
  * Antagonize AChE, slowing down ACh hydrolysis so more ACh is available at NMJ [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Available agents** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Neostigmine**:
	* Most commonly used
	* Quaternary compound, doesn't cross blood-brain barrier
	* Forms carbamylated enzyme complex with acetylcholinesterase
	* Also inhibits pseudocholinesterase (can prolong succinylcholine)
  * **Pyridostigmine**:
	* 20% as potent as neostigmine
	* Slower onset (10-15 minutes)
	* Slightly longer duration (>2 hours)
  * **Edrophonium**:
	* <10% as potent as neostigmine
	* Rapid onset (1-2 minutes)
	* Shortest duration
	* Competitive inhibitor at anionic site
	* Less pronounced muscarinic effects
  * **Physostigmine**:
	* Tertiary amine that crosses blood-brain barrier
	* Used for atropine/scopolamine overdose treatment
	* Limited usefulness as reversal agent due to CNS effects
* **Classification by mechanism** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * **Easily reversible inhibition**: Edrophonium
  * **Formation of carbamylated enzyme complex**: Neostigmine, pyridostigmine, physostigmine
  * **Irreversible inactivation**: Organophosphorous compounds
* **Factors limiting effectiveness** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Ceiling effect: Once acetylcholinesterase is completely inhibited, no further benefit
  * Depth of block: Major determinant of effectiveness
  * ⚠️ Cannot guarantee complete reversal to TOF ratio 0.9 within 10 minutes for moderate or deeper blocks

![Anticholinesterase Classification](NMBA_NMBA_image_1.png_6139c9.png_3a8c26.png)

## 6.4. NEOSTIGMINE 

**Neostigmine is the most commonly used anticholinesterase for NMBA reversal.**

* **ASA 2023 Conditional Recommendation for Neostigmine**:
  * **Reasonable alternative at minimal block**: "We suggest neostigmine as a reasonable alternative to sugammadx at minimal depth of neuromuscular blockade" (Strength: Conditional, Evidence: Low)
  * **Key limitation**: Only highly effective when administered from minimal blockade (TOF ratio 0.4-0.9)
  * **Evidence**: Both ASA and ESAIC guidelines recommend neostigmine only after achieving recovery levels as high as possible, ideally from minimal block (TOF ratio >0.4) but at minimum, once TOF ratio has recovered to 0.2
* **Pharmacokinetics** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Onset**: Effects apparent in 5 minutes
  * **Peak effect**: 10 minutes
  * **Duration**: More than 1 hour
  * **Maximum antagonist effect**: Reached within approximately 10 minutes; no benefit administering more than 10 minutes before emergence
* **Presentation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Available as tablets and solution for intravenous injection (and in combination with glycopyrrolate)
* **FDA-approved dosing guidelines** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **FDA-approved dose**: 30 μg/kg at minimal neuromuscular blockade, consistent with approved dosage recommendations
  * **Shallow block** (TOF count 4 and TOF ratio <0.4): 40-50 mcg/kg - suggest continued quantitative monitoring to demonstrate adequate recovery; variable recovery period of 20-30 minutes
  * **Minimal block** (TOF count 4, TOF ratio ≥0.4): 15-30 mcg/kg - maximal antagonism reached within 10 minutes
  * **Moderate block** (TOF 1-3): Consider waiting until TOF count is 4
  * **Near-complete recovery** (TOF ratio >0.6): 10-20 mcg/kg
  * ⚠️ Doses higher than 50 mcg/kg not recommended (ceiling effect)
  * Limit dose to 15-30 mcg/kg if TOF shows 4 twitches without subjective fade
  * Use ideal body weight for dosing (hydrophilic drug)
* **Depth-dependent effectiveness** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Critical concept**: Degree of spontaneous recovery at time of antagonism is major determinant of successful and timely antagonism
  * **TOF count 4 requirement**: Administering neostigmine at TOF count of 4 much more likely to yield satisfactory antagonism than at lower TOF count
  * **TOF ratio ≥0.4**: Studies show cohort antagonized at TOF ratio 0.4 had timely successful antagonism (TOF ratio ≥0.9 within 10 min)
  * **Quantitative determination**: TOF ratio ≥0.4 more reliable than subjective determination of "no fade" and associated with improved predictability
* **Limitations** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Only highly effective when block is minimal
	  * Both the ASA and the ESAIC guidelines recommend reversal with neostigmine only after achieving recovery levels as high as possible, ideally from minimal block levels (TOF ratio >0.4) but at a minimum, once TOF ratio has recovered to 0.2.7,8 Quantitative monitoring is essential to provide certainty of adequate recovery before awakening and extubation.
  * Early administration for deeper blocks creates longer "zone of blind paralysis"
  * Less effective with volatile anesthetics compared to TIVA
  * Should be administered 10-15 minutes before extubation (peak effect time)
  * **Cannot guarantee complete reversal**: Unable to reliably antagonize moderate or deeper blocks to TOF ratio ≥0.9 within 10 minutes
* **Managing inadequate neostigmine reversal**:
  * If recovery time exceeds 10 minutes, unlikely due to delayed neostigmine activity
  * More likely explanation: insufficient spontaneous recovery before administration
  * **Options when TOF ratio <0.9 after 10 minutes**:
	1. Allow continued spontaneous recovery
	2. Administer sugammadx (if rocuronium/vecuronium used)
	3. Additional neostigmine if low initial dose (not exceeding 50 μg/kg total)
* **Anticholinergic co-administration** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Required to block muscarinic side effects
  * **Glycopyrrolate**: 0.2 mg per 1 mg neostigmine (1:5 ratio)
	* Preferred due to similar onset/offset to neostigmine
	* Does not cross blood-brain barrier
  * **Atropine**: 0.4 mg per 1 mg neostigmine
	* Crosses blood-brain barrier
	* Can cause central anticholinergic syndrome
* **Mechanism of action** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Both ACh and carbamate esters are hydrolyzed when reacting with AChE
  * ACh acetylates AChE while carbamate esters produce carbamylated enzyme
  * Carbamylated enzyme has much slower rate of hydrolysis
  * Stops AChE from hydrolyzing ACh
  * Also inhibits plasma cholinesterase, potentially prolonging succinylcholine actions
* **Adverse effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Cardiovascular**: Bradycardia if administered alone, limited role in treating SVT
  * **Respiratory**: May precipitate bronchospasm in asthmatics
  * **Gastrointestinal**: Increases salivation and intestinal motility, may cause abdominal cramps
* **Neostigmine-induced weakness** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Anticholinesterase drugs possess intrinsic neuromuscular blocking properties
  * Risk highest in patients with no NMBA or complete recovery
  * Healthy volunteers given 35 mcg/kg showed 20% reduction in hand grip strength
  * Repeated dose (15 min later) caused 41% reduction
  * ⚠️ **Paradoxical weakness**: Higher doses may cause weakness when dose exceeding 30 μg/kg administered after spontaneous recovery to TOF ratio ≥0.9
  * ⚠️ Should not be administered to patients with adequate spontaneous recovery (TOF ratio ≥0.9)
* **Pharmacokinetics** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Poorly absorbed from gut with low oral bioavailability
  * Minimally protein-bound
  * Low volume of distribution
  * Partially metabolized in liver
  * Approximately 55% excreted unchanged in urine

## 6.5. OTHER ANTICHOLINESTERASE CONSIDERATIONS

**Each anticholinesterase has unique properties affecting clinical use.**

* **Pyridostigmine** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Dosing**: 0.25 mg/kg (20 mg total in adults)
  * **Anticholinergic pairing**:
	* 0.05 mg glycopyrrolate per 1 mg pyridostigmine (preferred due to slower onset - less tachycardia)
	* 0.1 mg atropine per 1 mg pyridostigmine
  * Primary use: Oral treatment of myasthenia gravis
  * Slower onset of action than neostigmine with longer duration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Relies more on renal elimination than neostigmine (75% excreted unchanged) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Does not cross blood-brain barrier [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Edrophonium** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Dosing**: 0.5-1 mg/kg
  * Should not reduce dose as longer-acting relaxants may outlast effects
  * May not effectively reverse intense blockade
  * **Anticholinergic pairing**:
	* Atropine: 0.014 mg/1 mg edrophonium (rapid onset suited)
	* Glycopyrrolate: 0.007 mg/1 mg edrophonium (given several minutes prior)
  * Preferred for mivacurium reversal (doesn't inhibit pseudocholinesterase)
  * Not available in US since 2018
  * Only drug in the "easily reversible inhibition" group - phenolic quaternary amine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * At IV dose of 2-10 mg, rapidly distinguishes between myasthenic crisis (muscle power improved) and cholinergic crisis (clinical picture worsened) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Mechanism: Quaternary amine attracted to anionic site of AChE while hydroxyl group forms hydrogen bond at esteratic site, stabilizing complex [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * ACh competes with edrophonium for AChE because no true covalent bond formed [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Also causes increased ACh release [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Low lipid solubility, not absorbed orally, doesn't cross blood-brain barrier or placenta [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Up to 65% excreted unchanged in urine, rest undergoes glucuronidation in liver with bile excretion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Only slight muscarinic side effects but may still cause bradycardia and salivation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Physostigmine** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Dosing**: 0.01-0.03 mg/kg
  * Crosses blood-brain barrier; inhibits central acetylcholinesterase
  * **Clinical uses**:
	* Atropine/scopolamine overdose treatment
	* Central anticholinergic syndrome
	* Prevention of postoperative shivering (0.04 mg/kg)
	* Can transiently antagonize morphine-induced respiratory depression
  * Use atropine (not glycopyrrolate) for muscarinic side effects
  * Has tertiary amine structure with different properties [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Well absorbed from gut and crosses blood-brain barrier [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Previously used to treat anticholinergic poisoning [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Organophosphorus compounds** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Highly toxic, mainly used as insecticides or nerve gases
  * Ecothiopate iodide used to treat glaucoma
  * Highly lipid-soluble, rapidly absorbed across skin
  * Mechanism: Phosphorylate esteratic site of AChE forming very stable complex
  * Complex resistant to hydrolysis or reactivation, recovery depends on enzyme synthesis
  * Also inhibit plasma cholinesterase
  * Toxic manifestations include nicotinic and muscarinic effects, autonomic instability
  * Treatment: Pralidoxime, obidoxine (AChE reactivators), atropine, anticonvulsants, ventilation
* **Cost considerations** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Neostigmine price varied significantly over time
  * Was briefly more expensive than sugammadex (2015)
  * Currently 10-50% the cost of sugammadex (200 mg vial)

## 6.6. SELECTIVE RELAXANT BINDING DRUGS: SUGAMMADEX 

**Sugammadex provides rapid and reliable reversal of aminosteroid neuromuscular blockers.**

* **Chemistry** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Gamma (γ) cyclodextrin, a selective relaxant binding agent (SRBA)
  * Cyclodextrins are ring structures of 6 (α), 7 (β), or 8 (γ) sugars
  * Inside of ring is hydrophobic, outside is hydrophilic
  * Hydrophobic drugs form complexes with cyclodextrins, increasing solubility
* **Pharmacology** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Modified γ-cyclodextrin with hydrophobic central cavity
  * Highly water-soluble
  * Encapsulates steroidal NMBAs (rocuronium > vecuronium >> pancuronium)
  * No affinity for benzylisoquinolinium NMBAs
  * Binding is tight with no clinically relevant dissociation
  * Vecuronium binding only one-third as tight as rocuronium
* **Presentation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * 100 mg/ml solution in 2 or 5 ml vials
  * 3-year shelf life
* **Mechanism of action** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Rapid binding of NMBA in plasma
  * Creates concentration gradient between NMJ and plasma
  * Net movement of NMBA from NMJ to plasma
  * Process continues until neuromuscular function returns
  * Also binds active metabolites (e.g., 3-desacetylvecuronium)
  * Selectively encapsulates aminosteroidal NDMRs, effectively removing them from plasma and NMJ [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Specific mechanism without predictable side effects of anticholinesterases [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Dosing** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Routine reversal** (TOF count ≥2): 2 mg/kg
	* Reverses to TOF ratio ≥0.9 in 2-4 minutes
  * **Deep block reversal** (TOF count 1 to PTC 1-2): 4 mg/kg
  * **Immediate reversal** (after RSI dose; PTC 0): 16 mg/kg
  * Elderly patients may take longer (up to 10 minutes)
  * ⚠️ **Clinical Pearl**: Quantitative monitoring remains essential as FDA notes reversal is not always successful
* **Pharmacokinetics** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Distributes in extracellular fluid
  * Limited metabolism
  * Eliminated primarily via renal excretion (elimination half-life ~100 minutes)
  * In severe renal impairment, both sugammadex and complex can be removed using high-flux hemodialysis
  * Volume of distribution 11-14 liters [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Linear kinetics at therapeutic doses [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Not metabolized, excreted solely via kidneys [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Effective half-life 2.5 hours [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Adverse effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Biologically inactive with no known receptor interactions
  * No hemodynamic side effects
  * **Oral contraceptives**: Equivalent to missing daily dose; precautions needed for remainder of menstrual cycle
  * **Drug interactions**: Limited to toremifene, flucloxacillin, fusidic acid
  * **Allergic reactions**: Most cases within 4 minutes of administration; respond to standard treatment
  * **Coagulation**: 5.5% increase in aPTT, 3% increase in PT (return to baseline within 60 minutes)
	* No reported increase in postoperative bleeding
  * Hypersensitivity reactions remain concern; single case of sugammadex-induced anaphylaxis identified in NAP6 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Severe bradycardia very rarely seen following use [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* **Drug interactions** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Main interactions relate to displacement or capture
  * Displacement of rocuronium/vecuronium possible with flucloxacillin, diclofenac, fusidic acid, toremifene
  * Can lead to recurarization
  * Capture of other medicines may reduce their efficacy
  * Most significant is potential reduction in oral contraceptive efficacy
  * Patients should use additional contraception for seven days following administration
* **Special populations** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Renal failure**: Equivalent speed reversal at TOF count ≥2; prolonged reversal from deep block
  * **Mild hypothermia** (34.5-35°C): Minimally prolongs reversal time
  * **Obesity**: Debate about ideal vs. lean vs. actual body weight dosing (currently recommended: actual body weight)
  * **Pediatrics**: Monitoring essential as weight-based dosing without monitoring has resulted in residual paralysis
* **Re-establishment of block** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Product insert recommends 24 hours before administering aminosteroid NMBA
  * Case reports show possible earlier re-establishment with higher doses
  * Alternatives: succinylcholine or benzylisoquinolinium drugs
* **Other uses** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Has been used to treat suspected rocuronium-induced anaphylaxis
  * Does not currently form part of any approved anaphylaxis guideline

## 6.7. FUTURE REVERSAL AGENTS

**Novel agents are being developed to address limitations of current reversal drugs.**

* **Calabadion** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:

  * Calabadion 1: Affinity for rocuronium significantly lower than sugammadex

  * Complex excreted unchanged in urine
  * Succinylcholine can be used for re-establishing blockade if needed
* **L-cysteine** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Can reverse gantacurium and other fumarates
  * Different mechanism than cyclodextrins

## 6.8. BENZYLISOQUINOLINIUM ANTAGONISM 

**ASA 2023 provides specific guidance for reversal of atracurium and cisatracurium.**

* **ASA 2023 Conditional Recommendation**:
  * **Neostigmine for cisatracurium/atracurium**: "To avoid residual neuromuscular blockade when atracurium or cisatracurium are administered and qualitative assessment is used, we suggest antagonism with neostigmine at minimal neuromuscular blockade depth. In the absence of quantitative monitoring, at least 10 min should elapse from antagonism to extubation. When quantitative monitoring is utilized, extubation can be done as soon as a train-of-four ratio greater than or equal to 0.9 is confirmed before extubation" (Strength: Conditional, Evidence: Very low)
* **Key clinical considerations**:
  * **Sugammadx ineffective**: Benzylisoquinolinium NMBAs can only be antagonized with acetylcholinesterase inhibitor (neostigmine)
  * **Longer recovery times**: Neostigmine antagonism of cisatracurium/atracurium associated with longer time to recovery than recognized
  * **Time requirements**: Times to TOF ratio ≥0.9 after neostigmine ranged from 1-143 minutes in studies
* **Depth-dependent reversal success**:
  * **Optimal conditions**: Full antagonism within 10 minutes most likely when:
	1. Neostigmine given once muscle relaxant no longer required
	2. Some spontaneous recovery present
	3. Emergence from anesthesia expected in ~10 minutes
  * **TOF count 4 with no fade**: Success likely with median time <10 minutes
  * **Fewer than 4 twitches**: Success unlikely within reasonable timeframe
* **Monitoring requirements**:
  * **With quantitative monitoring**: Extubation when TOF ratio ≥0.9 confirmed
  * **Without quantitative monitoring**: Minimum 10-minute wait from antagonism to extubation
  * **Verification essential**: Wide range in recovery time necessitates measuring TOF ratio with quantitative monitor
* **Dosing considerations**:
  * Standard neostigmine dosing applies (15-50 mcg/kg based on depth)
  * Anticholinergic co-administration required
  * Consider depth of blockade when timing neostigmine administration

## 6.9. FACTORS AFFECTING REVERSAL 

**Multiple factors influence the effectiveness and timing of neuromuscular blockade reversal.**

* **Depth of blockade** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Most important determinant of reversal success
  * Anticholinesterases cannot reliably antagonize moderate or deeper blocks to TOF ratio ≥0.9
  * Only minimal block (TOF count 4, TOF ratio ≥0.4) can be reliably reversed with neostigmine
* **Patient factors** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Age**: Reversal faster in children, slower in elderly
  * **Weight**: Limited data for obese patients; ideal body weight recommended for neostigmine
  * **Organ dysfunction**: Renal/hepatic impairment may prolong NMBA effect
* **Anesthetic technique** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Volatile anesthetics potentiate NMBAs and prolong reversal time
  * TIVA associated with faster reversal
* **Drug interactions** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Drugs that potentiate NMBAs also prolong reversal time:
	* Volatile anesthetics
	* Magnesium
	* Hypothermia
* **Timing considerations** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Neostigmine takes ~10 minutes for peak effect
  * Administration too early relative to extubation may result in recurarization
  * Administering <10 minutes before extubation may result in incomplete reversal

## 6.10. REVERSAL STRATEGIES 

**Evidence-based approaches to NMBA reversal optimize patient safety.**

* **Recommended reversal strategies by block depth** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Complete block** (TOF count 0, PTC 0):
	* Wait for spontaneous recovery to at least moderate block
	* Sugammadex 16 mg/kg only in emergency situations
  * **Deep block** (TOF count 0, PTC ≥1):
	* Sugammadex 4 mg/kg
	* Anticholinesterases ineffective - wait for spontaneous recovery to at least moderate block
  * **Moderate block** (TOF count 1-3):
	* Sugammadex 2 mg/kg
	* Anticholinesterases less reliable - consider waiting for shallow block
  * **Shallow block** (TOF count 4, TOF ratio <0.4):
	* Sugammadex 2 mg/kg
	* Neostigmine 40-50 mcg/kg with quantitative monitoring
  * **Minimal block** (TOF count 4, TOF ratio 0.4-0.9):
	* Sugammadex 2 mg/kg
	* Neostigmine 15-30 mcg/kg
  * **Recovered** (TOF ratio ≥0.9):
	* No reversal needed
	* ⚠️ Avoid neostigmine (may cause weakness)
* **Prevention of residual paralysis** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Always use quantitative monitoring
  * Confirm TOF ratio ≥0.9 before extubation
  * Monitor at adductor pollicis for extubation decisions
  * Match reversal agent and dose to depth of block
  * Allow adequate time for reversal agent to work
* **Managing failed reversal** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * If neostigmine unsuccessful after 10 minutes, most likely explanation is block too deep
  * Options:
	* Continue monitoring until spontaneous recovery
	* Administer sugammadex for rescue (caution if large neostigmine dose given)

![[Pasted image 20250526160044.png]]

**Bottom Line:** Reversal strategy should be based on quantitatively measured depth of block. Anticholinesterases (neostigmine) are effective only for minimal or shallow block and have a ceiling effect. Sugammadex provides rapid and reliable reversal of aminosteroid NMBAs at any depth of block when dosed appropriately. A TOF ratio ≥0.9 must be confirmed before extubation for patient safety.

## 6.11. ASA 2023 IMPLEMENTATION RECOMMENDATIONS 

**ASA 2023 guidelines provide specific strategies for implementing quantitative monitoring in clinical practice.**

* **Implementation strategies for quantitative monitoring**:
  * **Equipment placement**: Place quantitative monitors in all anesthetizing locations
  * **Educational efforts**: 
	* Departmental education on benefits of monitoring
	* Equipment instructional videos
	* Individual-level training and feedback
  * **Workflow integration**:
	* Electronic medical record alerts for real-time reminders to record TOF ratios
	* Clear protocols for documentation
	* Performance feedback on departmental and individual levels
* **Quality improvement approaches**:
  * **Successful implementation examples**:
	* One project reduced residual paralysis from 62% to 3.5% over 9 years by increasing quantitative monitoring from 2% to 60% of patients
	* Another achieved 97% documentation of TOF ratio ≥0.9 through systematic approach
	* Two-year period without PACU reintubations associated with residual paralysis after implementation
  * **Key success factors**:
	* Involving end users in equipment purchasing decisions
	* Communication regarding acquisition and ongoing costs
	* Systematic feedback and performance metrics
	* Cultural shift toward treating monitoring as essential patient safety measure
* **Implementation barriers to address**:
  * **Provider education**: Address perception that monitoring is unnecessary
  * **Technical support**: Ensure reliable equipment with adequate maintenance
  * **Workflow integration**: Minimize interruptions through proper planning
  * **Training programs**: Provide adequate education on proper technique
* **Performance metrics and feedback**:
  * Track percentage of patients with documented TOF ratio ≥0.9 before extubation
  * Monitor incidence of residual neuromuscular blockade
  * Assess PACU outcomes (reintubation, airway interventions)
  * Provide individual and departmental performance reports
* **Policy recommendations**:
  * Treat quantitative monitoring as essential as blood pressure measurement
  * Link quantitative monitoring use to credentialing requirements
  * Develop protocols for equipment use when arms are tucked or inaccessible
  * Establish clear documentation requirements
* **Research gaps identified by ASA 2023**:
  * **TOF ratio cutoffs**: Need for studies on acceleromyography vs. electromyography in context of patient outcomes
  * **Sugammadx dosing**: Lower-than-recommended doses associated with reparalysis; need for rocuronium vs. vecuronium dose optimization
  * **Routine avoidance studies**: Economic and safety analysis of avoiding reversal in patients with spontaneous TOF ratio ≥0.9
  * **Pulmonary complications**: Further investigation of relationship between residual blockade and postoperative complications in high-risk patients

## 6.12. SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS FOR SUCCINYLCHOLINE 

**ASA 2023 provides guidance on managing succinylcholine-induced blockade.**

* **Depolarizing blockade monitoring**:
  * Succinylcholine is the only depolarizing NMBA in clinical use
  * Rapidly metabolized by pseudocholinesterase with no antagonist available
  * Blockade must resolve spontaneously
* **Normal vs. abnormal recovery patterns**:
  * **Normal pseudocholinesterase activity**: Gradual but equal return of twitch height as blockade resolves
  * **Genetic variants**: Fade may appear mimicking phase 2 depolarizing block
  * **Monitoring recommendation**: Use single twitch baseline and percentage recovery to gauge return of strength
* **ASA guidance for delayed recovery**:
  * **Clinical signs of delayed recovery**: Use neuromuscular monitoring to guide extubation
  * **Abnormal pseudocholinesterase**: Single twitch monitoring most effective for both normal and abnormal variants
  * **Alert mechanism**: Monitoring will detect when rate of block regression is outside normal range
* **Practical considerations**:
  * Most clinicians administer single dose without subsequent nondepolarizing NMBAs
  * Monitoring becomes essential when recovery is unexpectedly prolonged
  * Consider genetic testing for pseudocholinesterase variants in cases of prolonged block

## 7.2. MECHANISM OF ACTION

**Anticholinergic drugs competitively block muscarinic receptors, preventing activation by acetylcholine.**

* **Primary agents** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * **Atropine**
  * **Scopolamine**
  * **Glycopyrrolate**
* **Receptor pharmacology** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Competitively block binding by ACh, preventing muscarinic receptor activation
  * Little or no muscarinic receptor subtype specificity
  * Exert similar anticholinergic effects in most target organs except:
	* Heart (cardiac effects vary)
	* Central nervous system (tertiary vs. quaternary amines)
  * Muscarinic receptor subgroups include:
	* Central nervous system (M₁, M₄, M₅)
	* Autonomic ganglia and gastric parietal cells (M₁)
	* Cardiac (M₂)
	* Smooth muscle (M₃)

## 7.3. GENERAL EFFECTS

**Anticholinergic drugs affect multiple organ systems through blockade of muscarinic receptors.**

* **Cardiovascular effects (M₂)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Blockade of muscarinic receptors in sinoatrial node produces tachycardia
  * Atropine at smaller IV doses can produce transient bradycardia (paradoxical mechanism unclear)
  * Little effect on ventricular function and peripheral vasculature (paucity of direct cholinergic innervation)
* **Respiratory effects (M₃)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Inhibit secretion production from nose to bronchi
  * Relax bronchial smooth musculature, reducing airway resistance
  * Increase anatomic dead space
* **Cerebral effects (M₁, M₄, M₅)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Wide-ranging effects including both stimulation and depression
  * Vary based on agent's ability to cross blood-brain barrier
* **Gastrointestinal effects (M₁)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Inhibit salivary and gastric secretions
  * Decrease intestinal motility and peristalsis
  * Reduce lower esophageal sphincter pressure
* **Ophthalmic effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Mydriasis (pupillary dilation)
  * Cycloplegia (inability to accommodate for near vision)
  * Effects on cranial nerves II (afferent) and III (efferent) controlling pupillary reactivity and accommodation
* **Genitourinary effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Decrease ureter and bladder tone
  * Can cause urinary retention
* **Other effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Inhibit sweat glands → rise in body temperature (atropine fever)
	* ACh is neurotransmitter in these postganglionic neurons
  * Bind to presynaptic muscarinic receptors on norepinephrine-secreting postganglionic neurons
	* May enhance sympathetic nervous system activity
	* ACh-induced stimulation normally inhibits norepinephrine release

## 7.4. MUSCARINIC ANTAGONIST TOXICITY 

**Anticholinergic toxicity presents with characteristic symptoms and requires specific treatment.**

* **Classic symptoms** "dry as a bone; red as a beet; blind as a bat; hot as a hare; mad as a hatter" [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Dry mucous membranes
  * Flushed skin
  * Visual disturbances
  * Hyperthermia
  * Altered mental status
* **Central anticholinergic syndrome** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Excitation, restlessness
  * Sedation, confusion
  * Hallucinations
  * Stupor (scopolamine)
  * Delirium (atropine)
  * Psychosis
  * Seizures
  * Coma
* **Treatment** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Physostigmine 1-2 mg
	* ⚠️ Not to exceed 3 mg to avoid peripheral cholinergic agonist activity
	* Crosses BBB (unlike other anticholinesterases)
	* May need repeated treatment as duration shorter than anticholinergic
  * Neostigmine, pyridostigmine, and edrophonium ineffective (cannot cross BBB)

## 7.5. ATROPINE

**Atropine is a tertiary amine anticholinergic with potent cardiac and bronchial effects.**

* **Chemistry and structure** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Tertiary amine that easily penetrates BBB
  * Commercial mixture is racemic
* **Dosing** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * IV/IM 0.01-0.02 mg/kg; up to 0.4-0.6 mg in adults
  * Larger doses up to 2 mg may be required for complete vagal nerve blockade in severe bradycardia
* **Clinical considerations** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Cardiovascular**:
	* Potent effect on heart
	* Most effective for treating bradyarrhythmia
	* ⚠️ Patients with CAD, AS, HOCM, pheochromocytoma, or thyrotoxicosis may not tolerate tachycardia
	* Paradoxically, very low doses (<0.1 mg) may decrease heart rate through blockade of presynaptic M₁ receptors
  * **Respiratory**:
	* Potent effect on bronchial smooth muscle
	* Antisialagogue effect (reduces secretions)
  * **Gastrointestinal**:
	* 5-HT₃ antagonism - reduces lower esophageal sphincter opening pressure
	* Theoretical risk of passive regurgitation
  * **Central effects**:
	* Higher doses (1-2 mg) may produce restlessness, disorientation, hallucinations, delirium
  * **Special applications**:
	* Used in organophosphate poisoning for muscarinic symptom management
	* ⚠️ Cannot reverse muscle weakness from nicotinic activation; pralidoxime still required
* **Contraindications/Cautions** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Narrow-angle glaucoma
	* Pupillary dilation thickens peripheral iris, narrows iridocorneal angle
	* Impairs aqueous humor drainage and increases intraocular pressure
  * Prostatic hypertrophy
  * Bladder-neck obstruction
  * Febrile patients (inhibits sweating)
* **Derivatives** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Ophthalmic applications**: Widely used for pupillary dilation to facilitate posterior chamber and retina examination
  * **Respiratory**: Ipratropium and tiotropium used in COPD
	* Quaternary ammonium structures, poorly absorbed systemically
	* Few anticholinergic side effects except xerostomia
	* Less bronchodilation than β₂-adrenergic agonists
	* Effectively inhibit airway reactivity to methacholine, histamine, prostaglandin F₂α
	* Ineffective against leukotriene-induced bronchoconstriction

## 7.6. SCOPOLAMINE

**Scopolamine is a tertiary amine anticholinergic with prominent CNS effects.**

* **Pharmacology** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Tertiary amine that easily penetrates BBB
  * Produces prominent CNS effects
* **Clinical applications** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **CNS depression**: Previously used for sedation, amnesia, euphoria as premed before midazolam's introduction
  * **Amnesia**: Used in patients with high risk for intraoperative awareness (hemodynamically unstable trauma)
  * **Anti-nausea**: Transdermal formulation for prophylaxis against motion sickness (kinetosis) and PONV
  * **Cardiovascular**: Produces little change in HR when administered IM for premedication
* **Adverse effects** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Mydriasis and cycloplegia
  * More potent antisialagogue than atropine
  * Greater central nervous system effects (may be desirable for premedication)
  * Pronounced mydriatic effect - avoid in closed angle glaucoma

## 7.7. GLYCOPYRROLATE

**Glycopyrrolate is a quaternary amine anticholinergic that doesn't cross the blood-brain barrier.**

* **Pharmacology** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Quaternary amine structure
  * Cannot cross blood-brain barrier
  * Almost devoid of central nervous system and ophthalmic effects
* **Dosing** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Usually 1/2 that of atropine
  * Premedication dose: 0.005-0.01 mg/kg (up to 0.2-0.3 mg in adults)
* **Clinical considerations** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Potent inhibition of salivary and respiratory tract secretions
  * Primary rationale for use as premedication is antisialagogue effect
  * Longer duration of action than atropine (2-4h vs. 30min after IV administration)
  * Preferred anticholinergic for combination with neostigmine due to matching pharmacokinetics

![Anticholinergic Comparison](NMBA_NMBA_image_3.png_c27446.png_bec6c7.png)

**Bottom Line:** Anticholinergic drugs block muscarinic receptors throughout the body, with atropine having potent cardiac effects, scopolamine producing prominent CNS effects, and glycopyrrolate providing peripheral anticholinergic activity without CNS effects. They are commonly used to counteract muscarinic side effects of anticholinesterases during neuromuscular blockade reversal, with glycopyrrolate typically paired with neostigmine due to similar pharmacokinetics.

## 8.2. CHOLINERGIC PHARMACOLOGY

**Cholinergic drugs enhance or mimic the action of acetylcholine at various receptors.**

* **Basic physiology** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Acetylcholine (ACh) acts at both muscarinic and nicotinic receptors throughout the body
  * ACh synthesis: Enzyme cholineacetyltransferase catalyzes reaction between acetylcoenzyme A and choline
  * ACh degradation: Rapidly hydrolyzed by acetylcholinesterase into acetate and choline
  * ⚠️ ACh itself has virtually no therapeutic applications due to rapid hydrolysis, extensive adverse effects, and diffuse action
* **Distribution of cholinergic innervation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Entire parasympathetic nervous system (ganglia and effector cells)
  * Parts of sympathetic nervous system (ganglia, adrenal medulla, sweat glands)
  * Some CNS neurons
  * Somatic nerves innervating skeletal muscle
* **Mechanism of action** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * ACh causes membrane conformation changes at postsynaptic receptors
  * Increases permeability to Na⁺, K⁺, and Cl⁻ ions
  * Results in membrane depolarization
  * Critical binding sites:
	* "Ester" site: Binds ester part of molecule
	* "Ion" site: Binds quaternary amine

## 8.3. CHOLINERGIC RECEPTORS

**Cholinergic receptors are divided into two major classes with different responses to drugs.**

* **Receptor classification** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Based on alkaloid reaction:
	* **Nicotinic receptors**: Stimulated by nicotine, located at autonomic ganglia and skeletal muscle
	* **Muscarinic receptors**: Activated by muscarine, found on end-organ effector cells
* **Receptor locations** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * **Nicotinic**:
	* Autonomic ganglia (sympathetic/parasympathetic)
	* Neuromuscular junction (skeletal muscle)
  * **Muscarinic**:
	* Glands (lacrimal, salivary, gastric)
	* Smooth muscle (bronchial, GI, bladder, vascular)
	* Heart (SA/AV nodes)
* **Receptor agonists** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * **Nicotinic**: Acetylcholine, nicotine
  * **Muscarinic**: Acetylcholine, muscarine
* **Receptor antagonists** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * **Nicotinic**: Nondepolarizing muscle relaxants
  * **Muscarinic**: Antimuscarinic agents (atropine, scopolamine, glycopyrrolate)
* **Key concept** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]:
  * Both receptor types respond to acetylcholine despite different responses to specific agonists/antagonists

## 8.4. CHOLINERGIC AGONISTS

**Cholinergic agonists are classified based on chemical structure and pharmacological properties.**

* **Chemical classification** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Choline esters**: Derivatives of choline
  * **Alkaloids**: Plant-derived compounds
* **Pharmacological properties** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * Methacholine: Choline + beta-methylation, resistant to cholinesterase
  * Bethanechol: Carbamate derivative of methacholine, resistant to hydrolysis
  * Carbamylcholine: Used for miosis in wide-angle glaucoma
  * Pilocarpine: Alkaloid, used for glaucoma and xerostomia treatment
* **Therapeutic applications** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * **Methacholine**:
	* Provocative agent in reactive airway disease testing
	* Treatment of SVT when other measures fail
  * **Bethanechol**:
	* Nonobstructive urinary retention (postoperative)
	* Neurogenic bladder
	* Paralytic ileus
	* Gastric atony following vagotomy
  * **Pilocarpine**:
	* Glaucoma (topical)
	* Xerostomia after head/neck irradiation (oral)
	* Sjögren's syndrome (increases salivary and lacrimal secretion)
* **Contraindications** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]:
  * ⚠️ Muscarinic agonists particularly dangerous in:
	* Myasthenia gravis patients on anticholinesterases
	* Bulbar palsy
	* Cardiac disease
	* Asthma
	* Peptic ulcer
	* Progressive muscular atrophy
	* Mechanical intestinal obstruction
	* Urinary retention

**Bottom Line:** Cholinergic pharmacology involves agents that act at both muscarinic and nicotinic receptors. Direct-acting cholinergic drugs have limited clinical applications due to their broad effects and side effect profiles. Anticholinesterases are more widely used to indirectly increase acetylcholine levels at the neuromuscular junction for reversal of nondepolarizing neuromuscular blockade, though their non-specific effects necessitate co-administration of anticholinergic drugs to block muscarinic side effects.
