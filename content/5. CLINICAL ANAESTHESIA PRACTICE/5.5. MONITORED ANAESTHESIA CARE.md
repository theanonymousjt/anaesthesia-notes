## 1. TERMINOLOGY AND DEFINITIONS

> **Syllabus**: [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2: Airway, Oxygenation & Ventilation|Part B: C2]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3: Sedation & Anaesthesia Techniques|Part B: C3]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Patient Care|Part C: PC]]

### 1.1. DEFINITION OF MONITORED ANESTHESIA CARE

**Monitored anesthesia care is a specific anesthesia service performed by a qualified anesthesiologist for a diagnostic or therapeutic procedure.**

* Monitored anesthesia care does not describe the continuum of depth of sedation
* Monitored anesthesia care is provided by a qualified anesthesiologist, thus can safely encompass the complete spectrum of sedation from light sedation/analgesia to conversion to general anesthesia if required

### 1.2. ASA OFFICIAL DEFINITION AND CLARIFICATION

**The ASA Position Statement on Monitored Anesthesia Care provides formal clarification of MAC in relation to sedation levels** [ASA 2024]:

* **MAC does NOT describe the continuum of depth of sedation**, rather it describes "a specific anesthesia service performed by a qualified anesthesia provider, for a diagnostic or therapeutic procedure" [ASA 2024]
* **Indications for monitored anesthesia care include** "the need for deeper levels of analgesia and sedation than can be provided by moderate sedation (including potential conversion to a general or regional anesthetic)" [ASA 2024]
* **Distinguished from moderate sedation by** the scope of anesthesia service and provider qualifications rather than specific sedation depth
* **Key distinction**: MAC encompasses the full spectrum of anesthesia care services, while sedation levels describe momentary states of consciousness that can change during procedures [ASA 2024]

### 1.3. ASA CONTINUUM OF DEPTH OF SEDATION

**The ASA defines sedation as a continuum with four distinct levels, recognizing that patients may move between levels during procedures** [ASA 2024]:

**🎯 Critical Concept**: The table and definitions are intended to guide the assessment of a patient's level of sedation at any moment which can change during the procedure [ASA 2024].

#### 1.3.1. FOUR LEVELS OF SEDATION/ANESTHESIA

| **Level** | **Responsiveness** | **Airway** | **Spontaneous Ventilation** | **Cardiovascular Function** |
|-----------|-------------------|-------------|---------------------------|---------------------------|
| **Minimal Sedation (Anxiolysis)** | Normal response to verbal stimulation | Unaffected | Unaffected | Unaffected |
| **Moderate Sedation/Analgesia ("Conscious Sedation")** | Purposeful* response to verbal or tactile stimulation | No intervention required | Adequate | Usually maintained |
| **Deep Sedation/Analgesia** | Purposeful* response following repeated or painful stimulation | Intervention may be required | May be inadequate | Usually maintained |
| **General Anesthesia** | Unarousable even with painful stimulus | Intervention often required | Frequently inadequate | May be impaired |

*Note: Reflex withdrawal from a painful stimulus is NOT considered a purposeful response [ASA 2024].

#### 1.3.2. DETAILED DEFINITIONS OF SEDATION LEVELS

**Minimal Sedation (Anxiolysis)** [ASA 2024]:
* **Definition**: A drug-induced state during which patients respond normally to verbal commands
* **Cognitive effects**: Although cognitive function and physical coordination may be impaired, airway reflexes, and ventilatory and cardiovascular functions are unaffected
* **Typical administration**: Usually accomplished by a single oral dose of a sedative or an analgesic administered before the procedure

**Moderate Sedation/Analgesia ("Conscious Sedation")** [ASA 2024]:
* **Definition**: A drug-induced depression of consciousness during which patients respond purposefully to verbal commands, either alone or accompanied by light tactile stimulation
* **Airway management**: No interventions are required to maintain a patent airway, and spontaneous ventilation is adequate
* **Cardiovascular status**: Cardiovascular function is usually maintained
* **Typical administration**: Usually accomplished by titration of IV sedatives and/or analgesics during the procedure
* ⚠️ **Important note**: The effect of administering other drugs, including analgesics, may increase the depth of sedation

**Deep Sedation/Analgesia** [ASA 2024]:
* **Definition**: A drug-induced depression of consciousness during which patients cannot be easily aroused but respond purposefully following repeated or painful stimulation
* **Ventilatory function**: The ability to independently maintain ventilatory function may be impaired
* **Airway management**: Patients may require assistance in maintaining a patent airway, and spontaneous ventilation may be inadequate
* **Cardiovascular status**: Cardiovascular function is usually maintained
* **Typical administration**: Usually accomplished by titration of IV sedatives and/or analgesics and/or anesthetics during the procedure

**General Anesthesia** [ASA 2024]:
* **Definition**: A drug-induced loss of consciousness during which patients are not arousable, even by painful stimulation
* **Ventilatory function**: The ability to independently maintain ventilatory function is often impaired
* **Airway management**: Patients often require assistance in maintaining a patent airway, and positive pressure ventilation may be required because of depressed spontaneous ventilation or drug-induced depression of neuromuscular function
* **Cardiovascular status**: Cardiovascular function may be impaired

#### 1.3.3. RESCUE REQUIREMENTS AND COMPETENCY EXPECTATIONS

**Because sedation is a continuum, it is not always possible to predict how an individual patient will respond** [ASA 2024]:

* **Practitioners intending to produce a given level of sedation should be able to rescue patients whose level of sedation becomes deeper than initially intended** [ASA 2024]
* **Individuals administering Moderate Sedation/Analgesia ("Conscious Sedation") should be able to rescue patients who enter a state of Deep Sedation/Analgesia** [ASA 2024]
* **Those administering Deep Sedation/Analgesia should be able to rescue patients who enter a state of General Anesthesia** [ASA 2024]

**Definition of "Rescue"** [ASA 2024]:
* **Rescue of a patient from a deeper level of sedation than intended is an intervention by a practitioner proficient in airway management and advanced life support**
* **The qualified practitioner corrects adverse physiologic consequences of the deeper-than-intended level of sedation** (such as hypoventilation, hypoxia and hypotension) and returns the patient to the originally intended level of sedation
* ⚠️ **Critical principle**: It is not appropriate to continue the procedure at an unintended level of sedation [ASA 2024]

**💡 Clinical Pearl**: The unpredictable nature of sedation depth progression mandates that all practitioners providing sedation be trained to manage at least one level deeper than their intended target sedation level.

### 1.4. MONITORED ANESTHESIA CARE VS MODERATE SEDATION

**MAC and moderate sedation differ in provider qualifications, intended depth, and management capabilities.**

* During moderate sedation (conscious sedation), care can be provided by:
  * Qualified anesthesia practitioners (physician anesthesiologists, anesthesiology fellows, anesthesiology residents, certified anesthesiologist assistants [CAAs], and certified registered nurse anesthetists [CRNAs])
  * Nonanesthesiologist sedation practitioners (physicians, dentists, or podiatrists with appropriate training and licensure)
* A proper preoperative history and physical, including airway examination, is required for both MAC and moderate sedation
* Sedation/analgesia provided by nonanesthesiologist providers should not intend to attain a level of sedation in which the patient is unresponsive
* Monitored anesthesia care implies the potential for a deeper level of sedation than that provided by sedation/analgesia and is always administered or medically directed by a qualified anesthesiologist

### 1.5. ASA STATEMENTS ON MONITORED ANESTHESIA CARE

**The American Society of Anesthesiologists provides specific guidelines for MAC provision.**

* Monitored anesthesia care includes all aspects of anesthesia care:
  * Preprocedure assessment and optimization
  * Intraprocedure care
  * Postprocedure management
* The anesthesiologist provides or medically directs specific services, including:
  * Preprocedural assessment and management of patient comorbidity and periprocedural risk
  * Diagnosis and treatment of clinical problems that occur during the procedure
  * Support of vital functions (hemodynamic stability, airway management, management of procedure-induced pathologic changes)
  * Administration of sedatives, analgesics, hypnotics, anesthetic agents, or other medications as necessary for patient safety
  * Psychological support and physical comfort
  * Provision of other medical services as needed to complete the procedure safely
* MAC may include varying levels of sedation, awareness, analgesia, and anxiolysis as necessary
* The qualified anesthesiologist provider must be prepared to convert to general anesthesia when necessary
* If the patient loses consciousness and the ability to respond purposefully, the anesthesia care is a general anesthetic, irrespective of whether airway instrumentation is required
* MAC is a physician service provided to an individual patient, whereas moderate sedation is a service directed by the proceduralist who is focused on successfully completing the scheduled procedure
* MAC should be subject to the same level of payment as general or regional anesthesia

**ASA statements on procedural aspects:**
* MAC should be requested by the attending physician and made known to the patient, in accordance with accepted procedures of the institution
* The service must include:
  * Performance of a preanesthetic examination and evaluation
  * Prescription of anesthetic care
  * Personal participation in, or medical direction of, the entire plan of care
  * Continuous physical presence of the anesthesiologist or, in the case of medical direction, of the resident or CRNA being medically directed
  * Proximate presence, or in the case of medical direction, availability of the anesthesiologist for diagnosis and treatment of emergencies
* All institutional regulations pertaining to anesthesia services shall be observed
* Standard services include:
  * Usual noninvasive cardiocirculatory and respiratory monitoring
  * Oxygen administration, when indicated
  * Administration of sedatives, tranquilizers, antiemetics, narcotics, other analgesics, β-blockers, vasopressors, bronchodilators, antihypertensives, or other pharmacologic therapy as required

**Bottom Line:** Monitored anesthesia care is a specific anesthesia service provided by qualified anesthesiologists that encompasses a spectrum of sedation depths and requires all aspects of standard anesthetic care, including preoperative assessment, intraoperative management, and postoperative care, with the capability to convert to general anesthesia if needed.

## 2. PREOPERATIVE ASSESSMENT

> **Syllabus**: [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2: Airway, Oxygenation & Ventilation|Part B: C2]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3: Sedation & Anaesthesia Techniques|Part B: C3]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Patient Care|Part C: PC]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Medical Knowledge|Part C: MK]]

**In addition to standard preoperative evaluation, MAC requires specific considerations related to patient factors, procedural requirements, and airway management.**

### 2.1. MEDICAL STATUS EVALUATION

**Review previous medical records and interview the patient or family to identify:**
* **Abnormalities of the major organ systems** (e.g., cardiac, renal, pulmonary, neurologic, sleep apnea, metabolic, endocrine) [ASA 2018]
* **Adverse experience with sedation/analgesia**, as well as regional and general anesthesia [ASA 2018]
* **History of a difficult airway** [ASA 2018]
* **Current medications, potential drug interactions, drug allergies, and nutraceuticals** [ASA 2018]
* **History of tobacco, alcohol or substance use or abuse** [ASA 2018]
* **Frequent or repeated exposure to sedation/analgesic agents** [ASA 2018]

**Conditions that increase risk of adverse events during MAC include:**
  * Severe cardiovascular disease (especially pulmonary hypertension)
  * Anxiety
  * Chronic pain (especially those with significant narcotic use)
  * Obstructive sleep apnea
  * Morbid obesity
  * Chronic renal failure
  * Chronic hepatic disease
  * Elderly patients (>70 years)
  * ASA III to IV physical status

**Additional high-risk conditions identified by ASA guidelines** [ASA 2018]:
* **Extremes of age** - particularly infants and very elderly patients
* **Respiratory conditions** including respiratory distress syndrome
* **History of gastric bypass surgery** - may affect medication absorption and aspiration risk
* **Pediatric patients who are precooperative** or who have behavior or attention disorders
* **Cardiovascular disorders** requiring special consideration
* **History of long-term benzodiazepine use** - may require higher doses or alternative approaches
* **Mitochondrial disease** - case reports indicate increased adverse outcomes
* **Grand mal epilepsy** - requires special sedation considerations
* **Psychotropic drug use** - potential interactions with sedative medications

### 2.2. AIRWAY ASSESSMENT

**Comprehensive airway evaluation is essential for MAC as sedation may unexpectedly deepen.**

* Prepare for potential airway management at any point during the procedure should sedation become deeper than intended
* Assess for risk factors for difficult mask ventilation and review difficult airway algorithm
* ⚠️ Consider that airway access may be limited during certain procedures

**ASA-recommended airway assessment procedures** [ASA 2018]:
* **Physical examination of the airway** including:
  * Mouth opening and tongue size
  * Neck mobility and thyromental distance
  * Mallampati classification
  * Presence of loose teeth or dental appliances
  * Previous difficult intubation history
* **Special considerations for moderate sedation**:
  * Patient must maintain spontaneous ventilation throughout procedure
  * No interventions required to maintain patent airway when properly sedated
  * Assessment for potential airway obstruction during sedation
  * Evaluation of baseline respiratory function

### 2.3. MONITORED ANESTHESIA CARE CANDIDACY

**Patient factors that may contraindicate MAC:**
* Increased aspiration risk
* Anticipated difficult airway
* Inability to tolerate respiratory depression (severe pulmonary hypertension, intracranial pathology)
* Inability to remain motionless, lie flat and cooperate
* Presence of coexisting sensorineural or cognitive defects
* 💡 "Don't offer MAC to a patient you don't want to talk to."

**Case factors affecting MAC suitability:**
* Anticipated depth of sedation required
* Need for paralysis
* Length of case (e.g., tourniquet time)
  * ⚠️ Longer cases increase risk as every moment is another moment with an unsecured airway
  * As sedation is a continuum, the patient may reach deeper-than-intended levels, leading to disinhibition, respiratory, or hemodynamic compromise
* Level of fire risk
* Ease of airway accessibility (head and neck procedures, prone positioning, head turned away from provider)
  * These cases may be more safely managed under minimal/no sedation or general anesthesia

### 2.4. CONVERSION OF MONITORED ANESTHESIA CARE TO GENERAL ANESTHESIA

**Common reasons for conversion from MAC to general anesthesia:**
* Failed neuraxial or regional anesthesia
* Patient inability to tolerate the procedure
* Hypoxia or airway obstruction
* Other factors include:
  * Coughing
  * Altered mental status
  * Hemodynamic instability
  * Aspiration
  * Hypercarbia
  * Loss of intravenous access
  * Change of anticipated procedure type
  * Prolonged surgical time
  * Surgeon request

### 2.5. PATIENT EXPECTATIONS

* Effective communication with patients regarding MAC is essential
* Patients should understand that they:
  * May be awake and have memories of their procedures
  * May experience moments of discomfort or pain
  * May need to follow instructions periodically
  * May need conversion to general anesthesia if necessary

### 2.6. SPECIALIST CONSULTATION AND FASTING GUIDELINES

**Specialist consultation recommendations** [ASA 2018]:
* **Consult with a medical specialist** (e.g., physician anesthesiologist, cardiologist, endocrinologist, pulmonologist, nephrologist, pediatrician, obstetrician, or otolaryngologist) when appropriate before administration of moderate procedural sedation to patients with significant underlying conditions
* **If a specialist is needed**, select a specialist based on the nature of the underlying condition and the urgency of the situation
* **For severely compromised or medically unstable patients** (e.g., ASA status IV, anticipated difficult airway, severe obstructive pulmonary disease, coronary artery disease, or congestive heart failure) or if sedation to the point of unresponsiveness will be necessary, consult with a physician anesthesiologist

**Preprocedure fasting guidelines** [ASA 2018]:
* **Inform patients or legal guardians before the day of the procedure** that they should not drink fluids or eat solid foods for a sufficient period of time to allow for gastric emptying before the procedure
* **On the day of the procedure**, assess the time and nature of last oral intake
* **Evaluate the risk of pulmonary aspiration** of gastric contents when determining:
  * The target level of sedation
  * Whether the procedure should be delayed
* **In urgent or emergent situations** where complete gastric emptying is not possible, do not delay moderate procedural sedation based on fasting time alone
* **💡 Clinical Pearl**: Most institutions follow a policy requiring a preprocedural fasting period of 6 hours for solid food and 2 hours for clear liquids, though aspiration during elective procedures is rare [ASA 2018]

**Informed consent and patient preparation** [ASA 2018]:
* **Before the procedure**, inform patients or legal guardians of the benefits, risks, and limitations of moderate sedation/analgesia and possible alternatives, and elicit their preferences
* **Provide comprehensive information** about:
  * Expected level of consciousness during procedure
  * Potential need for deeper sedation or conversion to general anesthesia
  * Recovery expectations and discharge criteria
  * Post-procedure care instructions

**Bottom Line:** Comprehensive preoperative assessment for MAC requires evaluation of medical comorbidities, airway assessment, determination of patient and procedure suitability for MAC, consideration of conversion factors, and clear communication of expectations with the patient. This assessment is critical for patient safety and procedure success.

## 3. INTRAOPERATIVE MONITORING

> **Syllabus**: [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2: Airway, Oxygenation & Ventilation|Part B: C2]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3: Sedation & Anaesthesia Techniques|Part B: C3]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C4: Monitoring|Part B: C4]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Patient Care|Part C: PC]]

### 3.1. STANDARD MONITORING REQUIREMENTS

**MAC requires the same monitoring standards as other anesthesia techniques.**

* Follow ASA standards for basic anesthesia monitoring:
  * Pulse oximetry
  * End-tidal CO2 monitoring
  * ECG
  * Blood pressure measurement every 5 minutes
  * Temperature monitoring (for risk of hypothermia and rarely hyperthermia in conditions like thyroid storm or malignant hyperthermia)
* Provider must be present throughout the entirety of monitored anesthesia care
* Continually evaluate:
  * (A, D) Patient's response to verbal stimulation (consciousness)
  * (B) Observation of the rate, depth, and pattern of respiration
  * (C) Palpation of the arterial pulse and assessment of peripheral perfusion by extremity temperature and capillary refill
* Continuously observe for:
  * Diaphoresis
  * Pallor
  * Shivering
  * Cyanosis
  * Acute changes in neurologic status

### 3.2. ASA MONITORING STANDARDS FOR MODERATE SEDATION

**Level of consciousness monitoring** [ASA 2018]:
* **Periodically monitor** (e.g., at 5-min intervals) a patient's response to verbal commands during moderate sedation, except in patients who are unable to respond appropriately (e.g., patients where age or development may impair bidirectional communication) or during procedures where movement could be detrimental
* **During procedures where verbal response is not possible** (e.g., oral surgery, restorative dentistry, upper endoscopy), check the patient's ability to give a "thumbs up" or other indication of consciousness in response to verbal or tactile (light tap) stimulation
* **This suggests** that the patient will be able to control their airway and take deep breaths if necessary [ASA 2018]

#### 3.2.1. ASSESSMENT ACCORDING TO ASA SEDATION CONTINUUM

**Consciousness assessment should align with ASA continuum definitions** [ASA 2024]:

**For Minimal Sedation (Anxiolysis)**:
* **Expect normal response to verbal stimulation**
* **Patient should respond immediately and appropriately** to questions and commands
* **Cognitive function may be impaired** but response pattern remains normal

**For Moderate Sedation/Analgesia ("Conscious Sedation")**:
* **Expect purposeful response to verbal or tactile stimulation**
* **Verbal commands should elicit appropriate responses**, though possibly delayed
* **Light tactile stimulation** (gentle touch, light shaking) should produce purposeful movement
* ⚠️ **Reflex withdrawal is NOT considered purposeful** - look for coordinated, appropriate responses

**For Deep Sedation/Analgesia**:
* **Requires repeated or painful stimulation for purposeful response**
* **Patient cannot be easily aroused** with simple verbal commands
* **May require increasingly vigorous stimulation** to elicit appropriate responses
* ⚠️ **This level approaches general anesthesia** and requires advanced airway management skills

**Assessment Red Flags** [ASA 2024]:
* **Progression to deeper sedation than intended** requires immediate intervention
* **Loss of purposeful response** indicates need for rescue interventions
* **Inadequate spontaneous ventilation** at any level requires airway support
* **Cardiovascular instability** may indicate excessive sedation depth

**Ventilation and oxygenation monitoring** [ASA 2018]:
* **Continually monitor ventilatory function** by observation of qualitative clinical signs
* **Continually monitor ventilatory function with capnography** unless precluded or invalidated by the nature of the patient, procedure, or equipment
  * For uncooperative patients, institute capnography after moderate sedation has been achieved
  * **💡 Evidence-based recommendation**: Meta-analysis shows capnography reduces frequency of hypoxemic events (oxygen saturation <90%) compared to monitoring without capnography [ASA 2018]
* **Continuously monitor all patients by pulse oximetry** with appropriate alarms

**Hemodynamic monitoring** [ASA 2018]:
* **Determine blood pressure** before sedation/analgesia is initiated unless precluded by lack of patient cooperation
* **Once moderate sedation/analgesia is established**, continually monitor blood pressure (e.g., at 5-min intervals) and heart rate during the procedure unless such monitoring interferes with the procedure (e.g., MRI where stimulation from the blood pressure cuff could arouse an appropriately sedated patient)
* **Use electrocardiographic monitoring** during moderate sedation in patients with clinically significant cardiovascular disease or those undergoing procedures where dysrhythmias are anticipated

**Contemporaneous recording** [ASA 2018]:
* **Record patients' level of consciousness**, ventilatory and oxygenation status, and hemodynamic variables at a frequency that depends on the type and amount of medication administered, the length of the procedure, and the general condition of the patient
* **At a minimum, record**: (1) before administration of sedative/analgesic agents, (2) after administration of sedative/analgesic agents, (3) at regular intervals during the procedure, (4) during initial recovery, and (5) just before discharge
* **Set device alarms** to alert the care team to critical changes in patient status

**Dedicated monitoring personnel** [ASA 2018]:
* **Assure that a designated individual** other than the practitioner performing the procedure is present to monitor the patient throughout the procedure
* **The individual responsible for monitoring** should be trained in the recognition of apnea and airway obstruction and be authorized to seek additional help
* **The designated individual may assist** with minor, interruptible tasks once the patient's level of sedation/analgesia and vital signs have stabilized, provided that adequate monitoring for the patient's level of sedation is maintained

### 3.3. DEPTH OF SEDATION ASSESSMENT

**Various scales exist to assess sedation depth during MAC.**

* ASA defines four levels of sedation:
  * Minimal sedation (anxiolysis)
  * Moderate sedation/analgesia (conscious sedation)
  * Deep sedation/analgesia
  * General anesthesia

#### 3.3.1. ASA CONTINUUM OF SEDATION - DETAILED ASSESSMENT CRITERIA

**The ASA continuum provides specific assessment criteria for each sedation level, updated October 2024** [ASA 2024]:

**Assessment Framework**:
* **The sedation continuum table and definitions are intended to guide the assessment of a patient's level of sedation at any moment**
* **Patient's sedation level can change during the procedure**
* **Assessment should be continuous and systematic**

**Responsiveness Assessment** [ASA 2024]:
* **Minimal Sedation**: Normal response to verbal stimulation
* **Moderate Sedation**: Purposeful response to verbal or tactile stimulation
* **Deep Sedation**: Purposeful response following repeated or painful stimulation
* **General Anesthesia**: Unarousable even with painful stimulus
* ⚠️ **Critical distinction**: Reflex withdrawal from a painful stimulus is NOT considered a purposeful response

**Airway Function Assessment** [ASA 2024]:
* **Minimal Sedation**: Unaffected
* **Moderate Sedation**: No intervention required
* **Deep Sedation**: Intervention may be required
* **General Anesthesia**: Intervention often required

**Spontaneous Ventilation Assessment** [ASA 2024]:
* **Minimal Sedation**: Unaffected
* **Moderate Sedation**: Adequate
* **Deep Sedation**: May be inadequate
* **General Anesthesia**: Frequently inadequate

**Cardiovascular Function Assessment** [ASA 2024]:
* **Minimal Sedation**: Unaffected
* **Moderate Sedation**: Usually maintained
* **Deep Sedation**: Usually maintained
* **General Anesthesia**: May be impaired

#### 3.3.2. PRACTICAL ASSESSMENT TECHNIQUES FOR SEDATION LEVELS

**Verbal Stimulation Techniques**:
* **Call patient by name** and ask simple questions ("How are you feeling?")
* **Simple commands** ("Take a deep breath," "Squeeze my hand")
* **Assess quality of response**: Immediate vs. delayed, coherent vs. confused

**Tactile Stimulation Techniques**:
* **Light touch** on shoulder or arm
* **Gentle shaking**
* **Sternal rub** for assessment of deeper sedation levels
* ⚠️ **Avoid painful stimulation** unless assessing for deep sedation or general anesthesia

**Monitoring Purposeful vs. Reflexive Responses**:
* **Purposeful response indicators**:
  * Following verbal commands appropriately
  * Coordinated movement in response to stimulation
  * Appropriate verbal responses
* **Reflexive response indicators** (NOT purposeful):
  * Withdrawal from painful stimulus without coordinated movement
  * Grimacing or moaning without appropriate response
  * Uncoordinated movement patterns

### 3.4. SUPPLEMENTAL OXYGEN ADMINISTRATION

**Oxygen administration can mask hypoventilation but is generally recommended during MAC.**

* Sedation can lead to alveolar hypoventilation
* Effect of supplemental O2 is illustrated by the alveolar gas equation:

![Alveolar gas equation](MAC_image_3.png_dffcd7.png)

* Example scenario demonstrating effect of supplemental oxygen:
  * Healthy adult male given opioid, PaCO2 increases to 80mmHg
  * Resulting PAO2 is 50mmHg
  * Assuming normal A-a gradient (10mmHg) - PaO2 will be 40mmHg (SaO2 ~75%)
  * If FiO2 increased to 28% - PaO2 becomes ~90mmHg

![image.png](MAC_image_4.png_739c6f.png)

* Key takeaways regarding oxygen administration:
  * In isolated hypoventilation, modest increases in inspired oxygen are remarkably effective at restoring oxygen saturation to acceptable levels
  * ⚠️ A patient receiving minimal supplemental O2 with acceptable O2 saturation may have significant undetected alveolar hypoventilation
  * While the necessity of routine O2 supplementation is somewhat controversial, it is generally prudent
  * Capnography should be utilized whether supplemental oxygen is used or not to improve prevention of hypoxic events
* Nasal prongs/cannula delivery of oxygen:
  * Traditional heuristic rule: FiO2 delivered increases by 4% from baseline of 20% for every liter-per-minute increase of 100% O2 flow
  * True effective FiO2 may be slightly less (at 2.5% for every L/min increase at 97% O2 and 1% for every L/min increase at 47% O2)

### 3.5. HEAD AND NECK FIRES WITH USE OF OXYGEN

**Oxygen use during head and neck procedures presents significant fire risk.**

* Head and neck cautery fires are among the most common equipment-related problems during MAC
* ⚠️ Fire risk factors include combination of:
  * Oxygen
  * Electrocautery
  * Alcoholic prep solutions
  * Flammable drapes
* Venturi masks can deliver sub-100% FiO2 mixtures by entraining room air
  * Should be avoided if drapes are present over the patient's face due to entrainment of oxygen-rich gas accumulated under the drapes
  * Should be avoided if cautery is used near the face

### 3.6. SUPPLEMENTAL OXYGEN ADMINISTRATION - ASA RECOMMENDATIONS

**Evidence-based oxygen therapy recommendations** [ASA 2018]:
* **Use supplemental oxygen during moderate procedural sedation/analgesia** unless specifically contraindicated for a particular patient or procedure
* **Meta-analysis evidence**: Use of supplemental oxygen versus no supplemental oxygen is associated with a reduced frequency of hypoxemia during procedures with moderate sedation [ASA 2018]
* **Clinical considerations**:
  * Various methods of oxygen administration can be used (nasal cannula, face mask, specialized devices)
  * Literature insufficient to determine which method is most effective
  * Balance fire risk considerations with hypoxemia prevention

### 3.7. EMERGENCY SUPPORT REQUIREMENTS

**Comprehensive emergency preparedness is essential for safe moderate sedation** [ASA 2018]:

**Pharmacologic support**:
* **Assure that pharmacologic antagonists** for benzodiazepines and opioids are immediately available in the procedure suite or procedure room
* **Assure that an individual is present** who understands the pharmacology of the sedative/analgesics administered and potential interactions with other medications and nutraceuticals

**Airway management capabilities**:
* **Assure that appropriately sized equipment** for establishing a patent airway is available
* **Assure that at least one individual** capable of establishing a patent airway and providing positive pressure ventilation is present in the procedure room
* **Assure that suction, advanced airway equipment**, a positive pressure ventilation device, and supplemental oxygen are immediately available in the procedure room and in good working order
* **Assure that a member of the procedural team** is trained in the recognition and treatment of airway complications (e.g., apnea, laryngospasm, airway obstruction), opening the airway, suctioning secretions, and performing bag-valve-mask ventilation

**Cardiovascular support capabilities**:
* **Assure that a member of the procedural team** has the skills to establish intravascular access
* **Assure that a member of the procedural team** has the skills to provide chest compressions
* **Assure that a functional defibrillator** or automatic external defibrillator is immediately available in the procedure area

**Advanced support access**:
* **Assure that an individual or service** (e.g., code blue team, paramedic-staffed ambulance service) with advanced life support skills (e.g., tracheal intubation, defibrillation, resuscitation medications) is immediately available
* **Assure that members of the procedural team** are able to recognize the need for additional support and know how to access emergency services from the procedure room (e.g., telephone, call button)

**Bottom Line:** Intraoperative monitoring during MAC requires standard ASA monitoring, continuous assessment of consciousness and vital functions, appropriate depth of sedation evaluation, judicious use of supplemental oxygen, and awareness of fire risks, particularly during head and neck procedures.

## 4. RESCUE CAPABILITIES AND REQUIREMENTS

> **Syllabus**: [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2: Airway, Oxygenation & Ventilation|Part B: C2]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3: Sedation & Anaesthesia Techniques|Part B: C3]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Patient Care|Part C: PC]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Systems-Based Practice|Part C: SBP]]

### 4.1. ASA RESCUE REQUIREMENTS

**Because sedation is a continuum, practitioners must be prepared to rescue patients from deeper-than-intended sedation levels** [ASA 2024]:

**Fundamental Rescue Principle**:
* **Practitioners intending to produce a given level of sedation should be able to rescue patients whose level of sedation becomes deeper than initially intended** [ASA 2024]
* **It is not always possible to predict how an individual patient will respond** to sedative medications [ASA 2024]

#### 4.1.1. SPECIFIC RESCUE COMPETENCY REQUIREMENTS

**For Moderate Sedation Providers** [ASA 2024]:
* **Must be able to rescue patients who enter a state of Deep Sedation/Analgesia**
* **Required skills include**:
  * Recognition of deep sedation
  * Airway management techniques
  * Positive pressure ventilation
  * Advanced life support capabilities

**For Deep Sedation Providers** [ASA 2024]:
* **Must be able to rescue patients who enter a state of General Anesthesia**
* **Required skills include**:
  * Advanced airway management (including intubation)
  * Mechanical ventilation capabilities
  * Full anesthesia management skills
  * Crisis resource management

#### 4.1.2. DEFINITION AND COMPONENTS OF RESCUE

**Rescue Definition** [ASA 2024]:
* **Rescue is an intervention by a practitioner proficient in airway management and advanced life support**
* **The qualified practitioner corrects adverse physiologic consequences** of the deeper-than-intended level of sedation including:
  * Hypoventilation
  * Hypoxia  
  * Hypotension
* **Returns the patient to the originally intended level of sedation**

**Critical Rescue Principle** [ASA 2024]:
* ⚠️ **It is NOT appropriate to continue the procedure at an unintended level of sedation**
* **Procedure should be paused** until appropriate sedation level is restored
* **Patient safety takes precedence** over procedural completion

#### 4.1.3. RESCUE SKILL REQUIREMENTS

**Airway Management Capabilities**:
* **Basic airway maneuvers**: Head tilt, chin lift, jaw thrust
* **Positive pressure ventilation**: Bag-mask ventilation techniques
* **Advanced airway devices**: Supraglottic airways, endotracheal intubation
* **Surgical airway access**: When indicated and within scope of practice

**Cardiovascular Support**:
* **IV access establishment and maintenance**
* **Vasopressor administration**
* **Fluid resuscitation**
* **Cardiac monitoring and arrhythmia management**

**Pharmacologic Rescue**:
* **Reversal agent administration**: Naloxone, flumazenil
* **Appropriate dosing and timing** of reversal agents
* **Recognition of reversal agent limitations** and duration of action

**Team-Based Rescue**:
* **Clear communication** during rescue scenarios
* **Role assignment** and task delegation
* **Equipment accessibility** and preparation
* **Emergency system activation** when appropriate

**💡 Clinical Pearl**: The best rescue is prevention through careful patient selection, appropriate dosing, and vigilant monitoring. However, when rescue becomes necessary, rapid recognition and competent intervention are essential for patient safety.

## 5. PHARMACOLOGIC PRINCIPLES

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|Part A: Pharmacology]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2: Airway, Oxygenation & Ventilation|Part B: C2]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Medical Knowledge|Part C: MK]]

### 5.1. PHARMACOKINETIC CONSIDERATIONS

**The objective of MAC drug delivery is to achieve and maintain therapeutic drug concentrations at the site of action.**

* When administering drugs for MAC, practitioners must:
  * Deliver therapeutic concentration of drug to site of action
  * Consider variability in pharmacokinetics (PK) and pharmacodynamics (PD)
* Tips for drug administration during MAC:
  * To avoid excessive sedation, titrate in small increments or use adjustable infusions
  * Use easily titratable drugs (e.g., propofol) for optimal control
  * Adjust administration according to magnitude of noxious stimuli
  * Continuous infusions are generally superior to intermittent bolus administration (less fluctuation in drug concentration)

![image.png](MAC_image_6.png_81aac0.png)

### 5.2. DISTRIBUTION, ELIMINATION AND DURATION OF ACTION

**Drug distribution occurs in phases and significantly influences clinical effect duration.**

* Distribution phase:
  * Drug is transported to rapidly equilibrating Vessel Rich Group (VRG) of tissues
  * Simultaneous but slower distribution to less well-perfused tissues (muscle and skin)
  * Results in brisk decrease in plasma levels
  * Over time, distribution occurs to poorly perfused tissues (bone and fat)
  * Lipophilic drugs may accumulate in poorly perfused tissues (fat)
  * This can lead to delayed recovery when drug is eventually released back into central compartment after administration is discontinued
* Elimination half-life (T1/2):
  * Was previously thought to be the main predictor of anesthetic drug's duration of action
  * Only relevant in single-compartment models
  * Most drugs in anesthesia are lipophilic and thus are more suited to multicompartmental modeling

### 5.3. CONTEXT-SENSITIVE HALF-TIME

**Context-sensitive half-time provides a more accurate prediction of drug offset than simple elimination half-life.**

* Context-sensitive half-time:
  * Developed to improve understanding of anesthetic drug disposition
  * Describes the time required for the plasma drug concentration to decline by 50% after terminating an infusion of a particular duration
  * Reflects combined effects of distribution and metabolism on drug disposition
  * Effect of distribution on plasma drug concentration varies:
	* In magnitude and direction over time
	* Depends on the drug concentration gradients between various compartments
* Clinical examples of context-sensitive half-time:
  * During early phase of lipophilic drug infusion - distributive factors tend to decrease plasma concentrations as drug is transported to unsaturated peripheral tissues
  * Once infusion is discontinued, drug will return from peripheral tissues and re-enter central circulation
* Key principles:
  * Longer infusion duration generally leads to increased context-sensitive half-time
  * This effect is particularly marked in fentanyl and thiopental
  * Fentanyl: When eliminated by hepatic clearance, there is immediate replacement by drug returning from peripheral compartment
	* Although fentanyl has a shorter elimination half-life than sufentanil (462 vs. 577 minutes), its context-sensitive half-time is much greater than sufentanil after an infusion longer than 2 hours
	* Storage and later release of fentanyl from peripheral binding sites delay the decline in plasma concentration
	* Context-sensitive half-times have no constant relationship to elimination half-times
  * Thiopental vs Propofol:
	* Similar context-sensitive half-times following brief infusion
	* Thiopental's context-sensitive half-time increases rapidly with longer infusions
	* This confirms clinical impression that thiopental is not ideal for continuous infusion during ambulatory procedures
	* Propofol that returns to plasma from peripheral compartments is rapidly cleared by metabolism
	* Difference due to: (1) high metabolic clearance of propofol compared to thiopental, and (2) relatively slow rate at which propofol returns to plasma from peripheral compartments
* Case of alfentanil vs sufentanil:
  * Alfentanil has shorter elimination half-life than sufentanil (111 vs 577 min) but has longer context-sensitive half-time
  * Sufentanil has significantly greater volume of distribution compared to alfentanil
  * Upon termination of sufentanil, decay in plasma concentration is accelerated by both elimination and continued redistribution to peripheral compartments
  * This demonstrates that shorter elimination half-life may not necessarily be superior

![image.png](MAC_image_7.png_0ba412.png)

* Relating context-sensitive half-time to recovery:
  * Context-sensitive half-time describes how long it takes for plasma concentration to decrease by 50%
  * Time to recovery depends on additional factors:
	* Difference between plasma concentration at infusion end and concentration below which awakening occurs
	* Awakening is a function of effect-site (brain) concentration decay, not plasma decay
  * If drug effect can be measured (e.g., EEG analysis for opioids), the half-time of equilibration between blood concentration and effect can be determined
* **t1/2ke0** - time for plasma-effect site equilibration:
  * Shorter t1/2ke0 means more rapid equilibration and shorter delay in onset
  * Thiopental, propofol, and alfentanil have short t1/2ke0 values compared to midazolam, sufentanil, and fentanyl
  * t1/2ke0 for fentanyl is 6.4 minutes vs 1.1 minutes for alfentanil
  * For brief stimuli where opioid is needed, alfentanil may be preferable to fentanyl
  * Drugs with higher t1/2ke0 should be allowed to equilibrate and reach peak effect before further administration to avoid overdosing
  * Example: Using midazolam's shortest quoted equilibration half-time (0.9 minutes), it takes 2.7 minutes (three half-times) to reach 87.5% equilibration
  * Low cardiac output will delay drug arrival at site of action and effectively prolong t1/2ke0
  * ⚠️ Use careful, well-spaced small boluses of drug in patients with low cardiac output

![image.png](MAC_image_8.png_4e9f6c.png)

### 5.4. DRUG INTERACTIONS

**Understanding drug interactions is essential for optimizing MAC technique while minimizing side effects.**

* No single drug can provide all components of MAC (analgesia, anxiolysis, and hypnosis) with acceptable safety margin or ease of titratability
* Drug combinations leverage synergism:
  * Reduce individual required doses
  * Potentially reduce side effects (if dosed correctly)
  * Synergistic effects can extend both desired and unwanted effects
  * Synergistic effect depends on the desired clinical endpoint
* Clinical examples of synergism:
  * Fentanyl (primarily analgesic) reduces propofol requirements for skin incision response more than it reduces propofol requirements for induction
  * Midazolam (significant hypnotic properties) displays significant synergism with propofol for inducing hypnosis and preventing movement in response to painful stimulus
* Concept of Cpss50 (plasma concentration at steady state to abolish purposeful movement at skin incision in 50% of patients):
  * Analogous to MAC for volatile anesthetics
  * Measure of potency
  * Synergism suggests that rapid recovery would be facilitated by using opioids in combination with other agents rather than as sole drugs
  * Opioid + benzodiazepine combination shows marked synergism for hypnosis
	* Approximately 25% of the median effective dose for each individual drug is required in combination to induce hypnosis in 50% of patients
  * When analgesic concentrations of fentanyl (0.6 ng/mL) are combined with propofol, the Cpss50 of propofol is reduced by 50% compared to propofol alone
  * No significant further reduction beyond fentanyl concentration of 3 ng/mL
* Key practice point:
  * Maximum benefit of opioid supplementation for potentiating other sedatives occurs when opioid is used in the analgesic dose range
  * ⚠️ Significant increases in depth of sedation can occur with only modest increments in opioid or hypnotic/sedative dosage

**Bottom Line:** Understanding pharmacologic principles is fundamental to safe MAC practice. Context-sensitive half-time, drug equilibration rates, and synergistic interactions are more important than simple elimination half-lives in predicting drug effects and recovery. Careful titration with awareness of these principles allows optimization of desired effects while minimizing adverse effects.

## 6. SPECIFIC DRUGS FOR MAC

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|Part A: Pharmacology]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2: Airway, Oxygenation & Ventilation|Part B: C2]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Medical Knowledge|Part C: MK]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Patient Care|Part C: PC]]

**MAC requires combinations of drugs to provide hypnosis, sedation, and analgesia tailored to procedure requirements and patient factors.**

![image.png](MAC_image_9.png_be08c7.png)

### 6.1. PROPOFOL

**Propofol is a short-acting IV hypnotic ideal for MAC due to rapid onset, easy titratability, and favorable recovery profile.**

* Favorable properties for use as hypnotic-sedative in MAC:
  * Favorable side effect profile with low PONV
  * Ease of titratability
  * Advantageous pharmacokinetic profile:
	* Short context-sensitive half-time even with prolonged administration
	* Short effect-site equilibration time leading to quick recovery
* Limitations:
  * Minimal analgesic properties
  * Should be used with local/regional anesthesia to exploit hypnotic-sedative properties
* Advantages compared to benzodiazepines for hypnotic component:
  * Midazolam has short elimination half-life but longer context-sensitive half-time (twice that of propofol)
  * Propofol avoids prolonged postoperative sedation and psychomotor impairment seen with benzodiazepines

**ASA Guidelines on Propofol Use** [ASA 2018]:
* **Classification**: Propofol is considered a sedative intended for general anesthesia
* **Special requirements**: When moderate procedural sedation with propofol is intended, provide care consistent with that required for general anesthesia
* **Practitioner qualifications**: Assure that practitioners administering propofol are able to reliably identify and rescue patients from unintended deep sedation or general anesthesia
* **Vascular access**: Maintain vascular access throughout the procedure and until the patient is no longer at risk for cardiorespiratory depression
* **Administration**: Administer in small, incremental doses or by infusion, titrating to desired endpoints
* **Evidence from literature**: Meta-analysis shows faster recovery times for propofol versus midazolam, with equivocal findings for patient recall and frequency of hypoxemia [ASA 2018]

### 6.2. BENZODIAZEPINES

**Benzodiazepines provide hypnotic, anxiolytic, and amnestic effects valuable in MAC but must be carefully dosed.**

* Clinical properties:
  * Provide hypnotic, anxiolytic, and amnestic effects
  * Not preferred as sole anesthetic due to high doses required and adverse effects
  * Prolonged recovery of psychomotor and cognitive function at higher doses
  * Most effective when used in combination with other agents
* Midazolam - preferred benzodiazepine for MAC:
  * Relatively short elimination half-life
  * Decreased likelihood of concomitant drug interactions
  * Best used at lower doses prior to propofol infusion
  * Provides specific amnestic and anxiolytic component of "balanced" sedation technique
  * Less suitable as the major hypnotic component
* Dosing considerations:
  * Dose reduction required for elderly patients
  * Careful titration essential to avoid oversedation

**ASA Guidelines on Benzodiazepines** [ASA 2018]:
* **Classification**: Benzodiazepines are sedatives NOT intended for general anesthesia
* **Combination use**: Combinations of sedative and analgesic agents may be administered as appropriate for the procedure and patient condition
* **Individual administration**: Administer each component individually to achieve the desired effect (e.g., additional analgesic medication to relieve pain; additional sedative medication to decrease awareness or anxiety)
* **Vascular access**: In patients receiving intravenous medications for sedation/analgesia, maintain vascular access throughout the procedure and until the patient is no longer at risk for cardiorespiratory depression
* **Titration**: Administer intravenous sedative/analgesic drugs in small, incremental doses, or by infusion, titrating to the desired endpoints
* **Timing**: Allow sufficient time to elapse between doses so the peak effect of each dose can be assessed before subsequent drug administration
* **Evidence**: Meta-analysis shows equivocal findings for pain, hypoxemia, and patient recall when midazolam is combined with opioids versus midazolam alone [ASA 2018]

![image.png](MAC_image_10.png_a0b148.png)

### 6.3. REMIMAZOLAM

**Remimazolam is a newer benzodiazepine with improved pharmacokinetic profile compared to midazolam.**

* Regulatory and pharmacologic profile:
  * Approved in 2020 by FDA
  * Short-acting benzodiazepine, GABA-A agonist
  * Metabolized by nonspecific tissue esterases
  * Albumin-bound
  * Excreted in urine
  * Dose adjustment required in severe hepatic impairment (HPS >10)
* Pharmacokinetic advantages over midazolam:
  * Shorter context-sensitive half-life (12 min vs 122 min)
  * Smaller volume of distribution (35L vs 82L)
  * Higher mean clearance (70L/h vs 23L/h)
  * Shorter mean terminal half-life (0.75h vs 4.3h)
  * Faster time to recovery (5.5 min vs 40 min)
* Clinical use:
  * Can be reversed with flumazenil
  * FDA recommendation: IV 5mg over 1 min, followed by 2.5mg IV over 15 second period with 2 min between administration of supplemental doses
  * Further clinical studies pending

### 6.4. OPIOIDS

**Opioids provide the essential analgesic component of MAC but require careful monitoring for respiratory depression.**

* Clinical role in MAC:
  * Provide analgesia component
  * Lack amnestic properties
  * Indicated when regional/local anesthetic techniques are inappropriate or ineffective
  * Administered immediately prior to painful or invasive portions of procedure
  * Blunt sympathetic response to pain (desirable in significant cardiac disease)
* Drug selection considerations:
  * Choice depends on onset, availability, duration, and potential side effects
  * Common choices: alfentanil, fentanyl, remifentanil
* Pharmacokinetic considerations:
  * Time-to-peak effect after bolus administration:
	* Fentanyl: 3.6 minutes
	* Alfentanil: 1.4 minutes
	* Sufentanil: 5.6 minutes
* Remifentanil - ultrashort-acting opioid ideal for MAC:
  * Potent with rapid clearance by nonspecific esterases
  * Rapid termination of effect
  * No requirement for dose adjustment in hepatic or renal disease
  * Consistent context-sensitive half-life (3-5 min)
  * Short t1/2ke0 of 1-1.5 minutes (compared to alfentanil: 0.6-1.2 min; fentanyl: 4-5 min; morphine: 20 min)
* Practical clinical guidelines for remifentanil use:
  * Therapeutic endpoint should be effective analgesia and patient comfort rather than sedation
  * Use of midazolam decreases remifentanil dose requirements by up to 50%
  * ⚠️ Avoid bolus administration due to increased risk of respiratory depression and chest wall rigidity
  * If bolus is necessary, administer slowly
  * If respiratory depression occurs, it should resolve within 3 minutes of discontinuation
  * Adding midazolam provides anxiolytic-sedative and amnestic components, increasing patient satisfaction
  * ⚠️ Caution with midazolam dosing as it increases risk of respiratory depression, apnea, and excessive sedation
  * Use as adjustable infusion, preceded by a small bolus of midazolam
  * Example regimen:
	* "Loading": 0.1 μg/kg/min approximately 5 minutes prior to first painful stimulus
	* Wean to 0.05 μg/kg/min as maintenance
	* Increments of 0.025 μg/kg/min as required for titration to pain/respiratory depression
  * ⚠️ Abrupt discontinuation will result in abrupt offset of analgesic effect

**ASA Guidelines on Opioid Use** [ASA 2018]:
* **Classification**: Opioids are analgesics used in combination with sedatives
* **Administration principles**: Same principles as benzodiazepines apply for titration and vascular access
* **Evidence from meta-analysis**: When midazolam is combined with opioids versus opioids alone, RCTs report equivocal findings for patient recall, pain during procedure, and frequency of hypoxemia [ASA 2018]
* **Reversal agent availability**: Naloxone must be immediately available whenever opioids are administered
* **Monitoring considerations**: Particular attention to respiratory depression and airway patency required

### 6.5. KETAMINE

**Ketamine provides dissociative anesthesia with analgesic properties and minimal cardiorespiratory depression.**

* Clinical properties:
  * Hypnotic-sedative with analgesic properties
  * Minimal respiratory and cardiovascular depression
  * Increases oral secretions with risk of laryngospasm
  * Frequently administered with an antisialagogue (atropine or glycopyrrolate)
  * Can induce hallucinations
* Contraindications:
  * Raised intraocular pressure
  * Raised intracranial pressure
* Dosing:
  * Administer in small IV increments titrating to effect (0.25 to 0.5 mg/kg)
  * Onset: 1-2 minutes
  * Duration of action: 20-60 minutes

**ASA Guidelines on Ketamine Use** [ASA 2018]:
* **Classification**: Ketamine is considered a sedative intended for general anesthesia
* **Special requirements**: When moderate procedural sedation with ketamine is intended, provide care consistent with that required for general anesthesia
* **Practitioner qualifications**: Assure that practitioners administering ketamine are able to reliably identify and rescue patients from unintended deep sedation or general anesthesia
* **Evidence from literature**:
  * RCTs comparing ketamine with midazolam report equivocal findings for sedation scores, recovery time, and oxygen saturation levels
  * RCTs comparing ketamine combined with midazolam versus midazolam alone show equivocal findings for sedation scores, sedation time, recovery, and recovery agitation
  * Ketamine may require less airway assistance and has lower frequencies of myoclonus compared to etomidate [ASA 2018]

### 6.6. "KETOFOL"

**Ketofol combines propofol and ketamine to balance their individual side effect profiles.**

* Rationale for combination:
  * Propofol + ketamine in the same syringe
  * Theoretical benefit is lower incidence of side effects compared to when used alone
  * Literature shows mixed results
* Potential advantages:
  * Ketamine's analgesic effect reduces propofol dosage required
  * Balanced negative side effects of each drug
  * Preserved hemodynamic stability
  * Decreased nausea and vomiting
  * Improved procedural conditions
  * Decreased airway complications
* Clinical considerations:
  * No consensus on exact mixture ratio
  * Ratios range from 1:1 (1 mg ketamine to 1 mg propofol) to 1:10
  * Regardless of ratio, dosing typically follows propofol pattern:
	* 0.25 mg/kg bolus
	* Followed by continuous infusion of 25-50 μg/kg/min or incremental boluses of 0.25-0.5 mg/kg every 3-5 minutes
  * Anesthesiologist must understand pharmacology of both medications and appropriateness based on patient comorbidities and surgical needs

### 6.7. DEXMEDETOMIDINE

**Dexmedetomidine is an α2-agonist providing sedation and analgesia with minimal respiratory depression.**

* Clinical properties:
  * Provides sedation (induces a natural sleep-like state)
  * Provides analgesia
  * No amnestic properties
* Dosing:
  * Initial bolus of 0.5-1 μg/kg over 10-20 minutes
  * Followed by continuous infusion of 0.2-0.7 μg/kg/h
* Pharmacodynamic effects:
  * Potentiates opioid-induced analgesia
  * Potentiates benzodiazepine-induced hypnosis
  * Relatively minor effect on respiratory function
  * ⚠️ Does not protect against respiratory depression (can decrease upper-airway patency)
  * Decreases cerebral metabolic rate and cerebral blood flow
* Cardiovascular effects:
  * Associated with reduction of sympathetic outflow and increased cardiac vagal activity
  * May cause hypotension and bradycardia, particularly during rapid IV infusion or bolus
  * Has peripheral vasoconstrictive effects that can occasionally precipitate hypertension
* Practical advantages:
  * No pain on injection
  * Takes longer compared to propofol to reach target sedation level

**ASA Guidelines on Dexmedetomidine Use** [ASA 2018]:
* **Classification**: Dexmedetomidine is a sedative NOT intended for general anesthesia
* **Alternative use**: Dexmedetomidine may be administered as an alternative to benzodiazepine sedatives on a case-by-case basis
* **Evidence from literature**:
  * One RCT comparing dexmedetomidine with midazolam reports equivocal outcomes for recovery time, oxygen saturation levels, apnea, and bradycardia
  * Another RCT reports longer recovery time for dexmedetomidine compared with midazolam
  * One RCT reports lower frequency of hypoxemia when dexmedetomidine is combined with an opioid analgesic compared with midazolam combined with an opioid analgesic
  * One RCT reports deeper sedation and lower frequency of hypoxemia when dexmedetomidine combined with midazolam and meperidine is compared with midazolam combined with meperidine [ASA 2018]
* **Administration**: Same titration and monitoring principles apply as with other sedatives

![image.png](MAC_image_11.png_cd8d2d.png)

**Bottom Line:** Drug selection for MAC requires balancing pharmacologic properties to achieve optimal sedation, analgesia, and amnesia while maintaining hemodynamic stability and respiratory function. Propofol, benzodiazepines, opioids (especially remifentanil), ketamine, and dexmedetomidine can be used alone or in combination based on procedure requirements and patient factors.

## 7. REVERSAL AGENTS: NALOXONE AND FLUMAZENIL

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#Pharmacology|Part A: Pharmacology]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2: Airway, Oxygenation & Ventilation|Part B: C2]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Medical Knowledge|Part C: MK]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Patient Care|Part C: PC]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Systems-Based Practice|Part C: SBP]]

### 7.1. NALOXONE FOR OPIOID REVERSAL

**Naloxone effectively reverses opioid-induced sedation and respiratory depression.**

**Evidence for efficacy** [ASA 2018]:
* **Placebo-controlled RCT**: Naloxone effectively reverses the effects of meperidine as measured by increasing alertness scores and respiratory rate
* **Observational studies**: Report reversal of respiratory depression, apnea, and oxygen desaturation after naloxone administration
* **Case reports**: Document successful reversal in various clinical scenarios

**Clinical use guidelines** [ASA 2018]:
* **Assure immediate availability**: Specific antagonists must be immediately available in the procedure room whenever opioid analgesics are administered for moderate procedural sedation/analgesia, regardless of route of administration
* **Administer naloxone** to reverse opioid-induced sedation and respiratory depression
* **Dosing**: Standard adult dose 0.04-0.4 mg IV, may repeat as needed
* **Onset**: 1-2 minutes when given intravenously
* **Duration**: 30-45 minutes (shorter than most opioids, requiring vigilant monitoring)

### 7.2. FLUMAZENIL FOR BENZODIAZEPINE REVERSAL

**Flumazenil effectively antagonizes benzodiazepine effects with excellent evidence base.**

**Evidence for efficacy** [ASA 2018]:
* **Meta-analysis of double-blind placebo-controlled RCTs**: Flumazenil effectively antagonizes the effects of sedation within 15 minutes for patients who have been administered benzodiazepines
* **Placebo-controlled RCTs**: Flumazenil administration is associated with shorter recovery times for benzodiazepine sedation
* **Meta-analysis evidence**: Flumazenil effectively antagonizes the effects of benzodiazepines when combined with opioids

**Clinical use guidelines** [ASA 2018]:
* **Assure immediate availability**: Must be immediately available whenever benzodiazepines are administered
* **Administer flumazenil** to reverse benzodiazepine-induced sedation and respiratory depression
* **Dosing**: Initial dose 0.2 mg IV, then 0.1 mg every 60 seconds as needed (maximum 1 mg)
* **Onset**: 1-2 minutes when given intravenously
* **Duration**: 45-90 minutes

### 7.3. GENERAL PRINCIPLES FOR REVERSAL AGENT USE

**Systematic approach to respiratory depression and reversal** [ASA 2018]:

**First-line interventions** (before pharmacologic reversal):
1. **Encourage or physically stimulate patients** to breathe deeply
2. **Administer supplemental oxygen**
3. **Provide positive pressure ventilation** if spontaneous ventilation is inadequate

**Indications for reversal agents**:
* **Use reversal agents** in cases where airway control, spontaneous ventilation, or positive pressure ventilation is inadequate
* **Not for routine use**: Do not use sedation regimens that are intended to include routine reversal of sedative or analgesic agents

**Post-reversal monitoring** [ASA 2018]:
* **After pharmacologic reversal**, observe and monitor patients for a sufficient time to ensure that sedation and cardiorespiratory depression does not recur once the effect of the antagonist dissipates
* ⚠️ **Critical consideration**: Reversal agents often have shorter durations than the drugs they reverse, requiring extended monitoring
* **Re-sedation risk**: Patients may become re-sedated as antagonist effects wear off

**Special considerations**:
* **Benzodiazepine tolerance**: Patients with chronic benzodiazepine use may require higher doses of flumazenil
* **Partial reversal**: Sometimes partial reversal is preferred to avoid complete awakening and patient discomfort
* **Combination reversal**: When both opioids and benzodiazepines have been used, both reversal agents may be needed

**💡 Clinical Pearl**: The need for reversal agents often indicates inadequate initial dosing or monitoring. Focus on prevention through careful titration and vigilant monitoring rather than relying on reversal agents as routine practice.

## 8. COMPLICATIONS

> **Syllabus**: [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2: Airway, Oxygenation & Ventilation|Part B: C2]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3: Sedation & Anaesthesia Techniques|Part B: C3]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Patient Care|Part C: PC]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Systems-Based Practice|Part C: SBP]]

**The overall rate of complications during MAC is similar to that for general anesthesia, requiring vigilant monitoring and preparedness.**

### 8.1. RESPIRATORY COMPLICATIONS

**Respiratory complications are the most common serious adverse events during MAC.**

* Respiratory depression:
  * Caused by sedative-hypnotics and opioids
  * Loss of upper airway dilator muscle tone due to sedative-hypnotics
  * Leads to increased airway resistance during inspiration due to airway collapse
  * Results in increased work of breathing and possible loss of airway patency
  * Increased use of intercostal and accessory muscles potentiates airway collapse (by generating more negative pressure)
  * Patients with pre-existing respiratory compromise and limited reserve will become hypercarbic, acidotic, and hypoxemic
* Aspiration:
  * Results from depressed laryngeal and pharyngeal reflexes
  * ⚠️ Propofol is particularly potent in suppressing upper airway protective reflexes
* Laryngospasm:
  * Triggered by stimulation of laryngeal mucosa above the vocal cords (internal branch of superior laryngeal nerve → vagus nerve)
  * Causes contraction of:
	* Lateral cricoarytenoid muscles (adduction and medial rotation of arytenoid cartilage)
	* Thyroarytenoid muscles (shortened vocal cords causing glottic closure) via recurrent laryngeal nerve
	* Cricothyroid muscles (tensing of vocal cords) via external branch of superior laryngeal nerve
  * Repetitive suprathreshold stimulation of the superior laryngeal nerve can result in:
	* Heavy after-discharge activity in adductor motor output
	* True laryngeal spasm with vocal cord closure persisting beyond stimulus cessation
	* Potential progression to hypoxemia, negative-pressure pulmonary edema, and cardiac arrest

#### 8.1.1. RESPIRATORY COMPLICATIONS RELATED TO SEDATION DEPTH

**Complications Associated with Progression Along Sedation Continuum** [ASA 2024]:

**Minimal to Moderate Sedation Transition**:
* **Decreased respiratory drive** as sedation deepens
* **Loss of protective airway reflexes** earlier than expected
* **Increased sensitivity to supplemental oxygen masking hypoventilation**

**Moderate to Deep Sedation Transition**:
* **Inadequate spontaneous ventilation** becomes prominent risk
* **Airway intervention may be required** without warning
* **Loss of purposeful response** to verbal commands leads to inability to cooperate with airway positioning

**Deep Sedation to General Anesthesia Transition**:
* **Frequently inadequate spontaneous ventilation**
* **Intervention often required** for airway maintenance
* **Complete loss of protective reflexes**
* **Potential cardiovascular impairment** compounding respiratory issues

#### 8.1.2. SEDATION-SPECIFIC RESPIRATORY MANAGEMENT

**Recognition of Sedation-Related Respiratory Depression**:
* **Early signs**: Decreased respiratory rate, shallow breathing pattern
* **Progressive signs**: Snoring, upper airway obstruction, oxygen desaturation
* **Late signs**: Apnea, cyanosis, loss of consciousness beyond intended level

**Intervention Strategies by Sedation Level**:
* **Moderate sedation**: Verbal stimulation, repositioning, supplemental oxygen
* **Deep sedation**: Physical stimulation, airway maneuvers, positive pressure ventilation
* **Unintended general anesthesia**: Full airway management, consider intubation

**💡 Clinical Pearl**: Respiratory complications often herald progression to deeper sedation levels than intended, requiring immediate assessment and potential rescue interventions according to ASA guidelines.

### 8.2. CARDIOVASCULAR COMPLICATIONS

* Risk of hypotension and hypertension depending on:
  * Anesthetic agents used
  * Procedural factors
  * Patient comorbidities
* Common causes:
  * Propofol-induced vasodilation and myocardial depression
  * Dexmedetomidine-related bradycardia
  * Inadequate analgesia leading to sympathetic stimulation and hypertension

### 8.3. LOCAL ANESTHETIC SYSTEMIC TOXICITY

**LAST risk is increased during MAC due to the concurrent use of sedatives.**

* LAST risk is potentiated in cases of:
  * Low cardiac output (reducing hepatic blood flow and clearance)
  * Hypercarbia and acidosis from hypoventilation:
	* Can increase delivery of local anesthetic to the brain
	* Can cause neurotoxicity
  * Sedation impairing interpretation of early signs of neurotoxicity
  * Benzodiazepines and their anticonvulsant properties potentially masking true seizures while not preventing toxicity

### 8.4. UNINTENDED SEDATION DEPTH TRANSITIONS

#### 8.4.1. SEDATION CONTINUUM-RELATED COMPLICATIONS

**The unpredictable nature of sedation depth creates specific risk patterns** [ASA 2024]:

**Factors Contributing to Unintended Depth Transitions**:
* **Individual patient variability** in drug response and pharmacokinetics
* **Drug interactions** between sedatives, analgesics, and patient medications
* **Cumulative effects** of repeated dosing or continuous infusions
* **Physiologic changes** during procedures affecting drug distribution and metabolism
* **Procedural stimulation** varying throughout the case

#### 8.4.2. SPECIFIC TRANSITION-RELATED COMPLICATIONS

**Minimal to Moderate Sedation Transitions**:
* **Loss of cognitive reserve** for airway protection
* **Decreased response to hypercarbic drive**
* **Impaired ability to follow respiratory coaching**
* **Early loss of protective reflexes** in vulnerable patients

**Moderate to Deep Sedation Transitions**:
* **Sudden loss of purposeful response** to verbal commands
* **Unpredictable airway obstruction** requiring intervention
* **Inadequate spontaneous ventilation** without warning signs
* **Loss of ability to cooperate** with positioning and safety measures

**Deep Sedation to General Anesthesia Transitions**:
* **Complete loss of airway protection**
* **Cardiovascular instability** beyond expected moderate sedation effects
* **Need for full anesthesia management** when not planned or prepared
* **Risk of awareness** if procedure continues without appropriate anesthetic depth

#### 8.4.3. RECOGNITION AND MANAGEMENT OF DEPTH TRANSITIONS

**Early Recognition Strategies**:
* **Systematic assessment** using ASA continuum criteria every 5 minutes
* **Trending of responses** rather than isolated assessments
* **Correlation of clinical signs** with expected sedation level
* **High index of suspicion** for deeper sedation in high-risk patients

**Management of Unintended Depth Transitions** [ASA 2024]:
* **Immediate cessation** of further sedative administration
* **Assessment and support** of airway, breathing, and circulation
* **Implementation of rescue protocols** appropriate to the deeper sedation level
* ⚠️ **Do NOT continue procedure** at unintended sedation depth
* **Consider reversal agents** when appropriate and available
* **Escalate care** if unable to return to intended sedation level

**Prevention Strategies**:
* **Conservative initial dosing** with careful titration
* **Adequate time intervals** between doses for peak effect assessment
* **Awareness of synergistic effects** of drug combinations
* **Continuous monitoring** rather than intermittent assessment
* **Clear rescue plans** established before sedation initiation

**💡 Clinical Pearl**: The ASA principle that "it is not appropriate to continue the procedure at an unintended level of sedation" emphasizes that patient safety must take precedence over procedural completion when sedation depth exceeds intended levels.

**Bottom Line:** Complications during MAC primarily involve respiratory depression, airway obstruction, aspiration risk, cardiovascular instability, and potential local anesthetic systemic toxicity. Understanding and preparing for unintended sedation depth transitions according to ASA guidelines is essential for safe MAC practice.

## 9. RECOVERY CARE AND DISCHARGE CRITERIA

> **Syllabus**: [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2: Airway, Oxygenation & Ventilation|Part B: C2]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C3: Sedation & Anaesthesia Techniques|Part B: C3]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Patient Care|Part C: PC]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Systems-Based Practice|Part C: SBP]]

**Patients receiving moderate procedural sedation may continue to be at risk for complications after procedure completion.**

### 9.1. CONTINUED OBSERVATION AND MONITORING

**ASA recommendations for post-procedure care** [ASA 2018]:

**Observation requirements**:
* **After sedation/analgesia**, observe and monitor patients in an appropriately staffed and equipped area until they are near their baseline level of consciousness and are no longer at increased risk for cardiorespiratory depression
* **Monitor oxygenation continuously** until patients are no longer at risk for hypoxemia
* **Monitor ventilation and circulation** at regular intervals (e.g., every 5 to 15 minutes) until patients are suitable for discharge

**Factors contributing to continued risk**:
* **Decreased stimulation** from the proceduralist may unmask residual sedation
* **Delayed drug absorption** after nonintravenous administration
* **Slow drug elimination** may contribute to residual sedation and cardiorespiratory depression during recovery period
* **Outpatient considerations**: Medical supervision may not be available once the patient leaves the medical facility

### 9.2. PREDETERMINED DISCHARGE CRITERIA

**Evidence-based discharge planning** [ASA 2018]:
* **Design discharge criteria** to minimize the risk of central nervous system or cardiorespiratory depression after discharge from observation by trained personnel
* Although well-accepted clinical practice, literature is insufficient to evaluate the impact of using predetermined discharge criteria on patient outcomes

**Recommended discharge criteria components** [ASA 2018]:
* **Level of consciousness**: Patient should be near baseline level of consciousness
* **Cardiovascular stability**: Stable vital signs for appropriate observation period
* **Respiratory function**: Adequate spontaneous ventilation and oxygenation
* **Ambulation**: Ability to ambulate safely (when appropriate for baseline functional status)
* **Pain control**: Adequate pain management with oral medications if needed
* **Nausea/vomiting**: Absence of significant nausea or protracted vomiting
* **Responsible adult**: Availability of responsible adult for escort and supervision (outpatient procedures)

#### 9.2.1. SEDATION CONTINUUM CONSIDERATIONS FOR DISCHARGE

**Recovery assessment should consider the deepest level of sedation achieved during the procedure** [ASA 2024]:

**Patients Who Received Deeper Sedation Than Intended**:
* **Extended observation period** may be required
* **More rigorous assessment** of neurologic function
* **Careful evaluation** for residual sedation effects
* **Documentation** of return to intended consciousness level

**Level-Specific Recovery Considerations**:
* **Minimal sedation**: Standard discharge criteria typically adequate
* **Moderate sedation**: Ensure return to purposeful responsiveness
* **Deep sedation (intended or unintended)**: Confirm adequate airway reflexes and spontaneous ventilation
* **Conversion to general anesthesia**: Full anesthesia recovery protocols apply

**Assessment of Recovery Using ASA Continuum Criteria**:
* **Responsiveness**: Normal response to verbal stimulation (minimal sedation baseline)
* **Airway function**: Unaffected protective reflexes
* **Spontaneous ventilation**: Unaffected respiratory pattern and effort
* **Cardiovascular function**: Return to baseline hemodynamic parameters

**Red Flags for Delayed Discharge**:
* **Continued deeper sedation** than baseline level
* **Persistent respiratory depression** or airway compromise
* **Cardiovascular instability** beyond normal recovery pattern
* **Inability to achieve** consistent purposeful responses
* **Evidence of residual drug effects** affecting safety

**💡 Clinical Pearl**: Recovery criteria should be tailored to the deepest level of sedation achieved during the procedure, not just the intended level, to ensure patient safety according to ASA continuum principles.

**Post-discharge instructions should include** [ASA 2018]:
* **Activity restrictions**: No driving, operating machinery, or making important decisions for 24 hours
* **Diet progression**: Instructions for resuming normal diet as tolerated
* **Medication guidelines**: Clear instructions about prescribed and over-the-counter medications
* **Emergency contacts**: Who to call and when to seek immediate medical attention
* **Follow-up care**: When and how to contact primary care provider or proceduralist

**Quality improvement considerations** [ASA 2018]:
* **Systematic monitoring** of discharge criteria compliance
* **Tracking of post-discharge complications** or unplanned returns
* **Regular review and updating** of discharge protocols based on outcomes data

## 12. FUTURE DIRECTIONS

> **Syllabus**: [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2: Airway, Oxygenation & Ventilation|Part B: C2]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Practice-Based Learning and Improvement|Part C: PBLI]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#Systems-Based Practice|Part C: SBP]]

**Several emerging technologies and techniques aim to improve MAC safety, precision, and patient experience.**

### 12.1. PATIENT-CONTROLLED SEDATION AND ANALGESIA

* Emerging approaches for patient control of MAC:
  * Propofol: 0.7-mg/kg boluses with a 3-minute lockout period
  * Combined regimen: 0.5-mg midazolam and 25-μg fentanyl with a 5-minute lockout interval
  * Alfentanil loading dose + midazolam premedication followed by alfentanil boluses with lockout
* Potential advantages:
  * Improved patient satisfaction
  * Individualized sedation depth
  * Potential for reduced total drug administration

### 12.2. TARGET CONTROLLED INFUSIONS (TCI)

* Computer-controlled infusion systems that maintain target plasma or effect-site drug concentrations
* Based on pharmacokinetic models for specific drugs
* Allows more precise control of drug levels compared to manual infusion

### 12.3. CLOSED LOOP ANESTHESIA DELIVERY SYSTEMS

* Automated systems that adjust drug delivery based on physiologic feedback
* Input variables (BP, HR, BIS) control infusion output
* Potential for more stable sedation with fewer fluctuations and reduced provider workload

* **Standardization efforts**: Harmonized protocols across different clinical settings

* **Pharmacogenomics**: Individualized dosing based on genetic factors
* **Artificial intelligence**: Decision support systems for sedation management
* **Telemedicine applications**: Remote consultation and monitoring capabilities
* **Patient-reported outcomes**: Enhanced focus on patient experience and satisfaction

**Bottom Line:** Future MAC techniques focus on increased precision of drug delivery through patient-controlled systems, target-controlled infusions, and closed-loop delivery systems. These approaches aim to optimize sedation quality while minimizing adverse effects through individualized and automated drug administration, supported by robust quality improvement processes and evidence-based practice integration.
