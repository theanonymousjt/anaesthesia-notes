## 1.2. CLINICAL ASSESSMENT AND DIAGNOSTIC CONSIDERATIONS
<!-- EPA-4:K4:airway_imaging_interpretation -->

**Comprehensive evaluation of upper airway patency and potential obstruction in ENT surgical patients.**

* Air flows seamlessly through upper respiratory passages in healthy individuals with minimal work of breathing
* ⚠️ **Critical Recognition:** Clinically evident upper airway obstruction is a late sign - significant obstruction and anatomic distortion may be present with minimal evidence of disease
* Airway obstruction due to malformation, tumor, infection, or trauma may significantly alter clinical presentation making gas exchange laborious and energy-consuming
* Increased work of breathing can lead to patient exhaustion, inadequate gas exchange, and ventilatory failure

### 1.2.1. PHYSICAL EXAMINATION COMPONENTS

* **Observation:** Presence of audible respirations, mouth breathing, nasal quality of speech, chest retractions
* **Airway Assessment:** Tonsillar size evaluation, ease of mask ventilation prediction, intubation difficulty assessment
* **Respiratory Sounds:** 
  * Inspiratory stridor: Upper airway obstruction
  * Expiratory stridor: Lower airway obstruction  
  * Biphasic stridor: Midtracheal lesions
* **Associated Signs:** Wheezing, rales, prolonged expiration indicating partial obstruction

## 1.3. RADIOLOGICAL EVALUATION
<!-- EPA-4:K4:airway_imaging -->

### 1.3.1. IMAGING MODALITIES

* **Plain Films:** Tracheal and laryngeal air columns
* **Computed Tomography (CT):** Detailed anatomic assessment of airway structures
* **Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):** Soft tissue evaluation, airway dimensions
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Radiologic evaluation helpful when tumor, mass lesions, or infection present to appreciate anatomic distortion and determine appropriate airway management technique

### 1.3.2. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS

* Significant anatomic distortion appreciation
* Assist anesthesiologist in determining most appropriate airway securing technique
* Pre-operative planning for complex airway management

**Bottom Line:** Thorough airway evaluation combining clinical assessment and appropriate imaging is essential for safe ENT anesthesia, as upper airway obstruction may be present with minimal clinical signs.

## 2.2. TONSILLECTOMY AND ADENOIDECTOMY

**One of the most commonly performed pediatric surgical procedures with specific anesthetic considerations and complications.**

![Figure 48-1 Events leading to cor pulmonale.](https://via.placeholder.com/600x400?text=Figure+48-1+Events+leading+to+cor+pulmonale)

### 2.2.1. SURGICAL INDICATIONS

* **Primary Indications:**
  * Chronic or recurrent acute tonsillitis
  * Peritonsillar abscess
  * Tonsillar hyperplasia
  * Obstructive sleep apnea syndrome (OSAS)
* **Additional Considerations:**
  * Cardiac valvular disease patients at risk for endocarditis from recurrent streptococcal bacteremia
  * Adenoidal hyperplasia leading to nasopharyngeal obstruction

### 2.2.2. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF COMPLICATIONS

* Untreated adenoidal hyperplasia consequences: Failure to thrive, speech disorders, obligate mouth breathing, sleep disturbances, orofacial abnormalities with upper airway narrowing, dental abnormalities
* Tonsillar hyperplasia effects: Chronic airway obstruction, sleep apnea, CO₂ retention, cor pulmonale, failure to thrive, swallowing disorders, speech abnormalities

## 2.3. PREOPERATIVE EVALUATION

**Thorough assessment focusing on sleep-disordered breathing and airway compromise.**

### 2.3.1. HISTORY AND PHYSICAL EXAMINATION

* **Essential History:** Sleep-disordered breathing (SDB) assessment
* **Physical Observation:** 
  * Audible respirations, mouth breathing, nasal speech quality, chest retractions
  * Mouth breathing may result from chronic nasopharyngeal obstruction
  * Elongated face, retrognathic mandible, high-arched palate may be present

![Figure 48-2 Classification of tonsil size, including the percentage of oropharyngeal area occupied by hypertrophied tonsils.](https://via.placeholder.com/600x400?text=Figure+48-2+Tonsil+Size+Classification)

### 2.3.2. LABORATORY AND DIAGNOSTIC STUDIES

* **Consider:** Hematocrit and coagulation parameters measurement
* **Medication History:** Current antibiotics, antihistamines, other medicines
* ⚠️ **Anticoagulation Risk:** Many non-prescription cold medications and antihistamines contain aspirin affecting platelet function
* **Imaging:** Chest radiographs and ECGs not required unless specific abnormalities elicited (recent pneumonia, bronchitis, URI, or history consistent with cor pulmonale)
* **Echocardiogram:** May be indicated in children with cardiac abnormalities history

### 2.3.3. CARDIOVASCULAR COMPLICATIONS

* **Long-standing hypoxemia and hypercarbia:** Can lead to cor pulmonale
* **ECG changes:** Right ventricular hypertrophy evidence
* **Radiographic findings:** Cardiomegaly consistent findings
* Each apneic episode causes increased pulmonary artery pressure with significant systemic and pulmonary artery hypertension
* Leading to ventricular dysfunction and cardiac dysrhythmias
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Cardiac enlargement is frequently reversible with surgical removal of tonsils and adenoids

## 2.4. SLEEP-DISORDERED BREATHING AND OBSTRUCTIVE SLEEP APNEA

**Spectrum of disorders requiring careful perioperative management due to increased anesthetic risks.**

### 2.4.1. CLASSIFICATION AND EPIDEMIOLOGY  

* **SDB Spectrum:** Ranges from primary snoring to OSAS
* **Prevalence:** SDB affects 10% of population, but only 1-4% progress to OSAS
* **OSAS Definition:** Characterized by periodic, partial, or complete upper airway obstruction during sleep
* **Age Distribution:** Approximately 10% of OSAS present in preschool and school-aged children, thought to decline after 9 years of age

### 2.4.2. OSAS TYPES AND RISK FACTORS

* **Type 1 OSAS:** Characterized by lymphoid hyperplasia without obesity
* **Type 2 OSAS:** Obese patients with minimal lymphoid hyperplasia
* **Risk Factors:**
  * Body mass index ≥95th percentile for age
  * Craniofacial abnormalities (small maxilla and mandible, large tongue, thick neck)
  * Anatomic nasal obstruction and Class 4 touching tonsils
  * Associated syndromes with additional comorbidities

### 2.4.3. ASSESSMENT METHODS

* **Polysomnography (PSG):** Formal sleep studies (hospital or home-based)
* **Nocturnal oximetry:** Alternative assessment method
* **Clinical questionnaires:** 
  * STOP-BANG questionnaire for adults (Snoring, Tiredness, Observation of apnea, Blood pressure)
  * STBUR questionnaire for pediatrics (Snoring, Trouble Breathing, Un-Refreshed after sleep)

### 2.4.4. ⚠️ KEY CLINICAL CONSIDERATIONS

* **AAP vs AAO-HNS Guidelines:** Different recommendations for PSG use
* **2017 Survey Finding:** Only 3.5% of pediatric otolaryngologists referred >90% of children with OSAS for polysomnography before adenotonsillectomy
* **Clinical Features:** Repetitive arousal from sleep, episodic oxygen desaturation, hypercarbia, cardiac dysfunction

### 2.4.5. LONG-TERM EFFECTS AND COMORBIDITIES

* **Cognitive:** Increased hyperactivity, altered prefrontal cortex neurochemical substrate
* **Metabolic:** Risk factor for metabolic syndrome in obese children (insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, hypertension)
* **Cardiovascular:** Altered blood pressure regulation, sympathetic activity changes, endothelial dysfunction, systemic inflammation
* **Specific Complications:** Systemic hypertension, left ventricular geometry changes, pulmonary artery hypertension

### 2.4.6. TREATMENT APPROACHES

* **Primary Treatment:** Surgical removal of tonsils and adenoids (80% success rate in resolving OSAS)
* **Additional Options:** 
  * Expansion pharyngoplasty
  * Supraglottoplasty  
  * Mandibular distraction osteogenesis (MDO)
  * Uvulopharyngopalatoplasty
* **MDO Specific:** 92% improvement in patients with craniofacial abnormalities (Treacher Collins, Pierre Robin Sequence)
* **Alternative Treatment:** Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) if surgical intervention fails

### 2.4.7. TONSILLOTOMY VS TONSILLECTOMY

* **Tonsillotomy Benefits:** Lower postsurgical hemorrhage rates, decreased pain
* **Concerns:** Tonsillar regrowth necessitating additional surgeries, inferior OSAS treatment due to subtotal tissue removal
* **Cochrane Review Findings:** Faster return to normal activity, slight reduction in postoperative complications, insufficient long-term data

### 2.4.8. DRUG-INDUCED SLEEP ENDOSCOPY (DISE)

* **Purpose:** Characterize anatomic basis of obstruction in severe/refractory OSAS
* **Technique:** Patients sedated to reproduce obstructive symptoms during sleep
* **Anesthetic Goals:** Maintain spontaneous respiration, ensure timely emergence
* **Agents Used:** Propofol and dexmedetomidine infusion ± ketamine bolus
* **Study Impact:** Changed surgical plan in 78% of cases compared to clinical examination alone

## 2.5. ANESTHETIC MANAGEMENT

**Specific techniques and considerations for safe perioperative care in pediatric tonsillectomy patients.**

### 2.5.1. ANESTHETIC GOALS

1. Render child unconscious
2. Provide surgeon with optimal operating conditions  
3. Establish intravenous access for volume expansion and medications
4. Provide rapid emergence with awake, protective airway reflexes

### 2.5.2. PREMEDICATION CONSIDERATIONS

* **General Rule:** Use sparingly in children with obstructive sleep apnea, intermittent obstruction, or very large tonsils
* ⚠️ **Avoid:** Sedative premedication in children with airway compromise
* **Antisialagogue:** May be helpful to minimize secretions in operative field

### 2.5.3. INDUCTION TECHNIQUES

* **Standard Approach:** Volatile anesthetic agent, oxygen, and nitrous oxide by mask
* **Family-Centered Care:** Parental presence during mask induction helpful in anxious unpremedicated child
* **Intubation:** Best accomplished under deep inhalation anesthesia or aided by short-acting nondepolarizing muscle relaxant
* **Alternative:** Many clinicians eliminate neuromuscular blockade, enhance anesthesia depth with propofol

### 2.5.4. PAIN MANAGEMENT STRATEGIES

* **Multimodal Approach:** Acetaminophen as part of regimen to reduce opioid consumption
* **Dosing Study:** Patients receiving fentanyl 1-2 mcg/kg plus acetaminophen (15 mg/kg IV or 40 mg/kg rectally) had median time to postoperative rescue analgesia of 7 and 10 hours respectively
* **Adjuvants:** Dexmedetomidine 0.5-1 mcg/kg infusion may attenuate emergence delirium in toddlers
* **NSAID Timing:** Avoid intraoperatively to decrease postoperative hemorrhage risk; safe and effective in post-acute recovery period

### 2.5.5. ⚠️ OPIOID SAFETY IN OSAS PATIENTS

* **Critical Guideline:** Standard opioid dose should be reduced by 50% due to increased sensitivity
* **Codeine Contraindication:** FDA black box warning for pediatric tonsillectomy patients due to rapid metabolism risk
* **AAO-HNS Guidelines:** Strictly contraindicate codeine use in this population

### 2.5.6. AIRWAY MANAGEMENT OPTIONS

#### 2.5.6.1. TRADITIONAL ENDOTRACHEAL INTUBATION

* **Blood Management:** Supraglottic area packed with petroleum gauze or use cuffed endotracheal tube
* **Cuff Considerations:** Careful attention to inflation pressure essential to avoid postextubation croup
* **Monitoring:** Precordial stethoscope, ECG, automated blood pressure, pulse oximetry, end-tidal capnography

#### 2.5.6.2. LARYNGEAL MASK AIRWAY (LMA) TECHNIQUE

* **Evolution:** First described 1990, widespread use with flexible model availability
* **Advantages:** Protects vocal cords from blood/secretions, decreased postoperative stridor and laryngospasm, increased immediate postoperative oxygen saturation
* **Technical Considerations:** 
  * Wide, rigid tube of standard LMA doesn't fit under mouth gag
  * Flexible model has soft, reinforced shaft fitting easily under mouth gag
  * Same depth of anesthesia required as for laryngoscopy and intubation

#### 2.5.6.3. LMA INSERTION CHALLENGES AND SOLUTIONS

* **Tonsillar Enlargement Issues:** Can make insertion difficult
* **Maneuvers to Overcome:**
  * Increased head extension
  * Lateral insertion of mask
  * Anterior displacement of tongue
  * Pressure on LMA tip using index finger
  * Laryngoscope use if other methods fail

### 2.5.7. EMERGENCE CONSIDERATIONS

* **Goal:** Rapid emergence with alert, protective reflexes
* **Timing:** Child should be awake and able to clear blood/secretions efficiently before extubation
* **Safety:** No difference in airway complications between awake or deep extubation
* **LMA Removal:** May be removed before emergence if spontaneous breathing regular, or after consciousness return

**Bottom Line:** Pediatric tonsillectomy requires careful balance of airway management techniques, reduced opioid dosing in OSAS patients, and recognition of increased bleeding risk, with emphasis on rapid emergence and airway protection.

## 2.6. COMPLICATIONS AND POST-OPERATIVE CARE

**Recognition and management of potentially life-threatening complications following adenotonsillectomy.**

### 2.6.1. MORTALITY AND MORBIDITY RISK FACTORS

* **High-Risk Patients:** Post-tonsillectomy mortality increased within first 48 hours in patients who are obese, have neurologic impairment, or cardiopulmonary compromise
* **PONV Incidence:** 30-65% after tonsillectomy

### 2.6.2. POST-OPERATIVE NAUSEA AND VOMITING (PONV)

* **Mechanisms:** 
  * Irritant blood in stomach
  * Gag reflex stimulation from inflammation/edema
  * CNS stimulation from gastric distention
* **Prevention:** Decompressing stomach with orogastric tube may help prevent response
* **Treatment:** 
  * Ondansetron 0.10-0.15 mg/kg ± dexamethasone 0.5 mg/kg very effective
  * Reduces nausea, vomiting, pain, and time to oral intake
* **Dexamethasone Considerations:** Some concern for increased bleeding risk, but AAO-HNS currently recommends single intraoperative dose

### 2.6.3. DEHYDRATION MANAGEMENT

* **Incidence:** 1% of cases due to poor oral intake from nausea, vomiting, or pain
* **Prevention:** Vigorous intravenous hydration during surgery offsets lower postoperative fluid intake

### 2.6.4. ⚠️ POST-TONSILLECTOMY HEMORRHAGE

* **Most Severe Complication:** Frequency 0.1-8.1% (up to 11.1% with coblation tonsillectomy)
* **Timing Pattern:** 
  * 75% occurs within 6 hours of surgery
  * Remaining 25% within first 24 hours
  * May occur until sixth postoperative day ("six hours or six days" guideline)
* **Bleeding Sites:** 67% tonsillar fossa, 26% nasopharynx, 7% both

#### 2.6.4.1. MANAGEMENT OF POST-TONSILLECTOMY BLEEDING

* **Initial Control:** Pharyngeal packs and cautery
* **Surgical Intervention:** Return to OR for exploration and hemostasis if conservative measures fail
* ⚠️ **Critical Consideration:** Unappreciated large volumes of swallowed blood - patients must be considered full stomach
* **Anesthetic Approach:**
  * Rapid-sequence induction with cricoid pressure and styletted endotracheal tube (controversial but may benefit some circumstances)
  * Check blood pressure in erect and supine positions to exclude orthostatic changes
  * Establish intravenous access and hydration before anesthesia induction

#### 2.6.4.2. EQUIPMENT PREPARATION FOR BLEEDING CASES

* **Prepare in Duplicate:** Variety of laryngoscope blades, endotracheal tubes, functioning suction apparatus
* **Rationale:** Blood in airway may impair vocal cord visualization and cause endotracheal tube plugging
* **Rescue Techniques:** SGA use described when ongoing bleeding complicates intubation

### 2.6.5. PAIN MANAGEMENT

* **Adenoidectomy:** Usually minimal pain
* **Tonsillectomy:** May be severe, contributes to poor fluid intake and discomfort
* **Technique-Related Pain:** Increased requirements with laser or electrocautery vs. sharp dissection and ligation
* **Interventions:**
  * Intraoperative corticosteroids may decrease edema and discomfort
  * Peritonsillar space infiltration with local anesthetic and epinephrine reduces intraoperative blood loss but doesn't significantly decrease postoperative pain

### 2.6.6. PERITONSILLAR ABSCESS (QUINSY TONSIL)

* **Clinical Presentation:** Fever, pain, trismus
* **Anatomic Consideration:** Large mass in lateral pharynx interfering with swallowing and breathing
* **Treatment:** Surgical drainage ± tonsillectomy, intravenous antibiotic therapy

![Figure 48-3 Patient with a peritonsillar abscess on the left side. Note the displacement of the uvula.](https://via.placeholder.com/600x400?text=Figure+48-3+Peritonsillar+Abscess)

![Figure 48-4 Neck radiograph of a patient with a peritonsillar abscess (arrow).](https://via.placeholder.com/600x400?text=Figure+48-4+Neck+Radiograph+Peritonsillar+Abscess)

![Figure 48-5 Computed tomography scan of a patient with a peritonsillar abscess (arrow).](https://via.placeholder.com/600x400?text=Figure+48-5+CT+Scan+Peritonsillar+Abscess)

#### 2.6.6.1. ANESTHETIC CONSIDERATIONS FOR PERITONSILLAR ABSCESS

* **Airway Assessment:** Usually doesn't interfere with mask ventilation after GA induction
* **Vocal Cord Visualization:** Should not be impaired (supraglottic process above laryngeal inlet)
* **Intubation Technique:** 
  * Careful laryngoscopy avoiding manipulation
  * Gentle intubation (tonsillar area tense and friable)
  * ⚠️ **Risk:** Inadvertent rupture causing purulent material spillage into trachea
* **Positioning:** Head-down position helpful during laryngoscopy to decrease aspiration risk

### 2.6.7. NEGATIVE-PRESSURE PULMONARY EDEMA

* **Mechanism:** Sudden relief of airway obstruction after adenotonsillectomy
* **Pathophysiology:**
  * Normal intrapleural pressure: -2.5 to -10 cm H₂O during inspiration
  * Obstructed child may generate -30 cm H₂O, disrupting pulmonary microvasculature capillary walls
  * Negative transpulmonary gradient increases venous return, enhancing pulmonary blood volume
  * "Leaky capillaries" facilitate fluid transudation into alveolar space

#### 2.6.7.1. PREVENTION AND TREATMENT

* **Prevention:** Apply moderate continuous positive pressure during induction allowing circulatory adaptation
* **Clinical Signs:** 
  * Intubated patient: Frothy pink fluid in endotracheal tube
  * Extubated patient: Decreased oxygen saturation, wheezing, dyspnea, increased respiratory rate
* **Differential Diagnosis:** Aspiration, ARDS, congestive heart failure, volume overload, anaphylaxis
* **Diagnosis:** Chest radiograph showing diffuse, usually bilateral interstitial pulmonary infiltrates
* **Treatment:** Usually supportive - patent airway, oxygen administration, diuretic therapy if hypervolemic
* **Severe Cases:** Endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation with PEEP may be necessary
* **Resolution:** Usually rapid, most cases resolve within 24 hours without treatment

### 2.6.8. DISCHARGE CRITERIA AND ADMISSION GUIDELINES

#### 2.6.8.1. SAME-DAY DISCHARGE

* **Adenoidectomy:** May be safely discharged same day after anesthesia recovery
* **Tonsillectomy:** Many centers discharging same day without adverse outcomes
* **Observation Requirements:** 
  * 4-6 hours observation for early hemorrhage
  * Free from significant nausea, vomiting, pain
  * Fluid intake by mouth not required, but adequate IV hydration essential
* **Admission Indications:** Excessive somnolence, severe vomiting

#### 2.6.8.2. HOSPITAL ADMISSION GUIDELINES (TABLE 48-1)

**AAO-HNS Inpatient Criteria:**
* Age ≤3 years
* Severe OSAS (apnea-hypopnea index ≥10 events/hour, oxygen saturation nadir <80%)
* Abnormal coagulation values ± bleeding disorder
* Systemic disorders increasing cardiopulmonary, metabolic, or medical risk
* Craniofacial/airway abnormalities (Treacher Collins, Crouzon, Goldenhar, Pierre Robin, CHARGE, achondroplasia, Down syndrome, choanal atresia, laryngotracheal stenosis)
* Extended travel time, weather conditions, home social conditions inconsistent with close observation

#### 2.6.8.3. INTENSIVE CARE CONSIDERATIONS

* **AAP Recommendation:** Children with oxygen saturation <80% in post-tonsillectomy recovery should be admitted for respiratory depression monitoring
* **ICU Admission:** Reserved for very severe OSAS, unmanageable comorbidities, significant airway obstruction requiring intervention beyond repositioning and oxygen supplementation

**Bottom Line:** Post-tonsillectomy complications range from common PONV to life-threatening hemorrhage and pulmonary edema, requiring vigilant monitoring and appropriate escalation of care based on patient risk factors and clinical presentation.

## 2.7. LARYNGOLOGY PROCEDURES

**Specialized voice disorder management requiring specific anesthetic techniques to optimize surgical visualization.**

### 2.7.1. CLINICAL CONTEXT

* **Subspecialty Development:** Significant advances in voice disorders knowledge, phoniatrics emergence in adult practice, now prevalent in pediatric and adult populations
* **Common Disorders:** 
  * Vocal nodules from laryngeal hyperfunction causing hoarseness
  * Papillomatosis of vocal cords
  * Vocal fold paralysis

### 2.7.2. TREATMENT APPROACHES

* **Papillomatosis:** Laser ablation using laser reflective endotracheal tube (adults), spontaneous ventilation with natural airway (children)
* **Medialization:** Vocal cord medialization or injection laryngoplasty accomplished during spontaneous ventilation with natural airway
* 💡 **Optimal Technique:** Total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) with topicalization of vocal cords during spontaneous ventilation offers surgeon best view of surgical field

**Bottom Line:** Laryngology procedures require specialized anesthetic techniques prioritizing spontaneous ventilation and vocal cord visualization while maintaining patient safety and surgical conditions.

## 2.9. MYRINGOTOMY AND TUBE INSERTION

**Short procedure for chronic serous otitis treatment with specific anesthetic considerations.**

### 2.9.1. SURGICAL PURPOSE

* **Indication:** Chronic serous otitis in children leading to hearing loss
* **Technique:** 
  * Myringotomy creates tympanic membrane opening for fluid drainage
  * Ventilation tube placement prevents occlusion during healing
* **Tube Function:** Small plastic tube serves as ostium vent, allows continued drainage until natural extrusion (6 months to 1 year) or surgical removal

### 2.9.2. ANESTHETIC MANAGEMENT

* **Technique:** Potent inhalation agent, oxygen, and nitrous oxide via face mask
* **Premedication:** Not recommended (sedative drugs outlast surgical duration)
* **URI Considerations:** 
  * Patients frequently have concomitant respiratory infections
  * May proceed with facemask anesthesia avoiding endotracheal intubation
  * No significant perioperative morbidity difference between asymptomatic and URI patients
  * Supplemental postoperative oxygen recommended for URI symptoms

### 2.9.3. ANALGESIC OPTIONS

* **Routes:** Intranasal or intramuscular medications
* 💡 **Superior Analgesia:** Dual therapy with fentanyl and ketorolac vs. single-agent administration without clinically significant increases in PACU time or PONV

## 2.10. MIDDLE EAR AND MASTOID SURGERY

**Complex procedures requiring specialized positioning, monitoring, and anesthetic considerations.**

### 2.10.1. COMMON PROCEDURES

* **Tympanoplasty and Mastoidectomy:** Most common middle ear and accessory structure procedures
* **Positioning Requirements:** 
  * Head positioned lower than operative table
  * Extreme lateral rotation may be required
  * ⚠️ **Pediatric Risk:** Cervical spine ligament laxity and immature odontoid process make children prone to C1-C2 subluxation

### 2.10.2. FACIAL NERVE MONITORING

* **Surgical Requirement:** Often involves surgical identification and preservation of facial nerve
* **Monitoring Methods:** 
  * Electrical stimulation verification
  * Brainstem auditory-evoked potential
  * Electrocochleogram monitoring
* **Anesthetic Implications:** Complete muscle relaxation must be avoided
* **Muscle Relaxant Use:** If opioid-relaxant technique chosen, maintain at least 30% muscle response per twitch monitor

![Figure 48-6 Illustration of the facial nerve and monitoring electrodes.](https://via.placeholder.com/600x400?text=Figure+48-6+Facial+Nerve+Monitoring)

### 2.10.3. HEMOSTASIS CONSIDERATIONS

* **Surgical Field:** Bleeding must be minimized during small structure surgery
* **Blood Pressure Management:** 
  * Prevention of excessive increases, maintain normotension
  * Relative hypotension can be effective but requires risk-benefit consideration
  * ⚠️ **Hypotension Risks:** Even short periods associated with acute kidney injury, MI, stroke, postoperative delirium
* **Contraindications:** Intracranial hypertension, hypovolemia, vascular/cerebrovascular/end-organ disease history

### 2.10.4. EPINEPHRINE USE

* **Application:** Concentrated epinephrine (often 1:1,000) injected into tympanic vessel area for vasoconstriction
* **Optimization:** Concentrations as low as 1:400,000 may give equivalent local blood flow reduction with less potential adverse effects from inadvertent intravascular injection
* **Monitoring:** Close attention to volume to avoid dysrhythmias and blood pressure swings

### 2.10.5. ⚠️ NITROUS OXIDE CONSIDERATIONS

* **Middle Ear Physiology:** Air-filled, nondistensible cavities where increased gas volume increases pressure
* **N₂O Effects:** 
  * Diffuses along concentration gradient into middle ear more rapidly than nitrogen moves out
  * Passive venting occurs at 20-30 cm H₂O pressure
  * Pressure buildup within 5 minutes exceeds eustachian tube venting ability
* **Graft Procedures:** N₂O should be discontinued or limited to maximum 50% during tympanic membrane graft application to avoid pressure-related displacement

### 2.10.6. POST-DISCONTINUATION EFFECTS

* **Negative Pressure Creation:** Quick N₂O reabsorption creates middle ear void
* **Complications:**
  * Serous otitis
  * Ossicle disarticulation (especially stapes)
  * Hearing impairment lasting up to 6 weeks
* **PONV Relationship:** High N₂O incidence related to negative middle ear pressure stimulating vestibular system via round window traction
* **Age Considerations:** Children <3 years most affected
* **Management:** If N₂O unavoidable, vigorous antiemetic use warranted

**Bottom Line:** Middle ear surgery requires careful consideration of nitrous oxide effects on air-filled spaces, facial nerve monitoring requirements, and hemostatic management while maintaining patient safety throughout complex positioning requirements.

## 3.2. EPIGLOTTITIS

**Potentially fatal infectious disease requiring emergent airway management.**

### 3.2.1. EPIDEMIOLOGY AND CAUSATIVE ORGANISMS

* **Historic Impact:** One of most feared infectious diseases in children and adults
* **Primary Organism:** Haemophilus influenzae type B
* **Current Incidence:** Conservative estimate 10-40 cases per million people in United States
* **Vaccination Impact:** Since 1985 widespread Hib vaccination dramatically reduced overall incidence among children
* **Age Distribution:** 
  * Usually 2-7 years of age
  * Very young (<1 year) unusual, only ~4% of cases
  * Adult peak: 20-40 years
* **Prevention:** Hib polysaccharide vaccination recommended before 2 years of age

### 3.2.2. CLINICAL PRESENTATION

* **Characteristic Signs:** Sudden onset of fever, dysphagia, drooling, thick muffled voice, preference for sitting position with head extended and leaning forward
* **Respiratory Signs:** Retractions, labored breathing, cyanosis (when obstruction present)
* **Early Presentation:** Patient may be pale and toxic without respiratory distress
* **Pathophysiology:** More appropriate designation "supraglottitis" - tissues from vallecula to arytenoids involved in infectious process

### 3.2.3. ⚠️ CRITICAL MANAGEMENT PRINCIPLES

* **Absolute Contraindication:** Direct visualization of epiglottis in unanesthetized patient due to laryngospasm risk
* **Pressure Differential:** Negative pressure inside vs. atmospheric pressure outside extrathoracic airway results in slight narrowing during inspiration
* **Dynamic Collapse:** Exaggerated pressure differential in obstruction may become life-threatening in struggling, agitated patient
* **Avoid Before Airway Secured:** Blood draws, IV catheter insertion, excessive manipulation, sedation

### 3.2.4. DIAGNOSTIC APPROACH

* **Oxygen Administration:** By mask if clinical situation allows
* **Radiologic Examination:** Lateral neck soft tissue films may show:
  * Aryepiglottic fold thickening
  * Epiglottic swelling ("thumbprint" sign)
* **Transport Considerations:** Only if skilled personnel and adequate equipment accompany patient
* **Severe Compromise:** Proceed directly to OR from emergency department with anesthesiologist and surgeon

### 3.2.5. ANESTHETIC MANAGEMENT

* **Airway Establishment:** All epiglottitis cases require artificial airway via tracheal intubation
* **Alternative (Less Favored):** Tracheostomy in centers lacking experienced personnel
* **Patient Positioning:** Keep in sitting position while monitors placed
* **Essential Monitoring:** Pulse oximetry, capnography availability, precordial stethoscope may be helpful
* **Family Presence:** One parent may accompany and remain during induction if helpful

#### 3.2.5.1. OR PREPARATION AND EQUIPMENT

* **Required Equipment/Personnel:** Laryngoscopy, rigid bronchoscopy, tracheostomy capability
* **Video Laryngoscopy:** Consider standard blade video laryngoscopy for optimal first attempt success and shared airway view due to potentially distorted anatomy

#### 3.2.5.2. INDUCTION PROTOCOL

1. **Inhalation Induction:** Oxygen and increasing sevoflurane concentrations
2. **IV Access:** Secure after loss of consciousness
3. **Positioning:** Lower to supine position after IV established
4. **Intubation:** Laryngoscopy followed by oral tracheal intubation usually without muscle relaxants
5. **Tube Selection:** At least one size (0.5 mm) smaller than typically chosen, stylet often helpful

#### 3.2.5.3. POST-INTUBATION MANAGEMENT

* **Surgical Examination:** Surgeon examines larynx noting epiglottis, aryepiglottic folds, surrounding tissue appearance
* **Cultures:** Tissue and blood cultures obtained
* **Antibiotic Therapy:** Initiated immediately
* **ICU Transfer:** For continued observation with appropriate sedation

#### 3.2.5.4. EXTUBATION CRITERIA

* **Timing:** Usually attempted 48-72 hours later in OR
* **Assessment:** 
  * Significant leak around tracheal tube present
  * Visual inspection via flexible fiberoptic bronchoscopy confirms swelling reduction

**Bottom Line:** Epiglottitis requires immediate recognition, calm patient management, and expert airway control in the operating room with full resuscitation capabilities and experienced personnel available.

## 3.3. LARYNGOTRACHEOBRONCHITIS (CROUP)

**Viral airway infection with subglottic narrowing requiring supportive management and occasional intubation.**

### 3.3.1. CLINICAL CHARACTERISTICS

* **Age Range:** 6 months to 6 years, primarily <3 years
* **Etiology:** Usually viral
* **Onset:** More insidious than epiglottitis
* **Presentation:** Low-grade fever, inspiratory stridor, "barking" cough

### 3.3.2. DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES

* **Radiologic Findings:** Subglottic narrowing of airway column secondary to circumferential soft-tissue edema producing "steeple" sign characteristic of LTB
* **Hospitalization:** Approximately 6% of LTB patients require admission

### 3.3.3. TREATMENT APPROACH

#### 3.3.3.1. MILD-TO-MODERATE CASES

* **Environmental:** Cool, humidified mist and oxygen therapy (usually administered in tent)

#### 3.3.3.2. SEVERE CASES

* **Clinical Signs:** Tachypnea, tachycardia, cyanosis
* **Pharmacological:** Racemic epinephrine administered by nebulizer beneficial
* **Corticosteroids:** Current opinion supports short course may be helpful (previously controversial)

#### 3.3.3.3. RARE SEVERE CASES

* **Indication:** Thick airway secretions present
* **Intervention:** Child requires intubation allowing pulmonary toilet and suctioning
* **Management:** ICU management and extubation carried out similarly to epiglottitis

**Bottom Line:** Croup typically responds to supportive care with humidified oxygen and racemic epinephrine, but severe cases may require intubation for airway clearance and ventilatory support.

## 3.4. FOREIGN-BODY ASPIRATION

**Significant cause of pediatric morbidity and mortality requiring emergent surgical removal via rigid or flexible bronchoscopy.**

### 3.4.1. EPIDEMIOLOGY AND INTRODUCTION

* **Clinical Significance:** Tracheobronchial foreign body aspiration (FBA) is a common cause of morbidity and mortality in young children
* **Procedure Requirements:** Children with known or suspected FBA may require rigid or flexible bronchoscopy, or rarely thoracotomy, for removal of foreign bodies
* **Procedure of Choice:** Rigid bronchoscopy is the procedure of choice for foreign body removal

### 3.4.2. CLINICAL PRESENTATION AND HISTORY

* **Key History:** Coughing, choking, or cyanosis while eating suggests foreign-body aspiration possibility
* **Common Objects:** Peanuts, popcorn, jelly beans, hot dogs most commonly associated with pulmonary aspiration
* **Emergency Presentation:** Any patient with refractory wheezing should be suspected
* **Physical Findings:** Decreased breath sounds, tachypnea, stridor, wheezing, fever indicating obstructive process with airway inflammation
* **Respiratory Sounds:** May include cyanotic spells, sudden onset of cough or wheezing (often focal and monophonic), and/or unilaterally diminished breath sounds

### 3.4.3. PREANESTHESIA EVALUATION AND TIMING

**Comprehensive assessment focusing on urgency determination and aspiration risk.**

#### 3.4.3.1. EVALUATION COMPONENTS

* **Standard Evaluation:** The usual preanesthesia evaluation should be performed whenever possible, including a history and anesthesia-directed physical examination
* **Urgent Cases:** Children in respiratory distress, or with suspected laryngeal or tracheal foreign body aspiration (FBA) should be taken urgently to the operating room for bronchoscopy, as rapid intervention is associated with better outcomes
* **Timing Discussion:** For other children, the timing of the bronchoscopy should be discussed with the surgeon
* **Stable Patients:** For children who are stable and at low risk of progression of airway obstruction, the procedure may be scheduled during optimal operating room staffing conditions and after routine preoperative fasting (ie, six hours for solid foods, one hour for clear liquids) to minimize the risk of aspiration on induction of anesthesia

#### 3.4.3.2. ⚠️ FULL STOMACH CONSIDERATIONS

* **Critical Principle:** Full stomach concerns should not delay procedures for children thought to be at risk of progression of airway obstruction
* **Evidence:** Aspiration of stomach contents has not been reported in large series of children with FBA, whereas there are multiple reports of progression from partial to complete airway obstruction, from movement or swelling of the foreign body, or swelling of the tracheobronchial mucosa
* **Management:** For patients with full stomachs, a large bore gastric tube can be placed and suctioned after induction of anesthesia to reduce the volume of stomach contents

#### 3.4.3.3. ASSOCIATED COMPLICATIONS

* **Pulmonary Effects:** Inhaled foreign bodies may cause pneumonia, atelectasis, hyperinflation distal to the obstruction, and/or bronchospasm, which may affect anesthetic management
* **Pre-operative Assessment:** Most children will come to the operating room after evaluation that has determined the location of the foreign body, timing of aspiration, and type of foreign body
* **Surgical Planning:** Issues including the plan for rigid or flexible bronchoscopy, the mode of ventilation during maintenance of anesthesia, and the need for neuromuscular blockade, should be discussed with the surgeon to plan the anesthetic
* **Communication:** For any surgery, and particularly for procedures in which the airway is used by both the surgical and anesthesia teams, excellent, closed-loop communication and in-depth pre-planning for both anesthesia and surgery are warranted to maximize patient safety

### 3.4.4. OPERATING ROOM TEAM PREPARATION

* **Bronchoscopist Presence:** In addition to the usual preparation for anesthesia, the bronchoscopist should be present in the operating room, with the equipment necessary for emergency airway rescue, prior to induction of anesthesia
* **Team Involvement:** All operating room nursing and technician staff should be part of the pre-procedure planning process

### 3.4.5. RADIOLOGIC FINDINGS

* **Identifiable Objects:** Some foreign bodies visible on radiologic examination
* **Radiolucent Objects:** 90% are radiolucent
* **Secondary Signs:** Air trapping, infiltrate, atelectasis noted
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl:** For stable patients with a high clinical suspicion of aspiration, it is reasonable to proceed directly to bronchoscopy, even if the plain radiographs are normal or inconclusive. Alternatively, CT can be performed first to help clarify the diagnosis

### 3.4.6. ANATOMIC CONSIDERATIONS

* **Most Common Site:** Main stem bronchus (right more frequent than left)
* **Object Types:** 
  * Food particles comprise majority
  * Beads, pins, small toys not unusual
* **Complications by Type:**
  * **Vegetable items:** Expand with moisture, can fragment into multiple pieces
  * **Oil-containing objects (peanuts):** Cause chemical inflammation
  * **Sharp objects:** Cause bleeding in addition to obstruction
### 3.4.7. ⚠️ EMERGENCY MANAGEMENT PRINCIPLES

* **All aspirated foreign bodies in airway should be removed in OR and considered emergencies**
* **No sedation before removal**
* **Full-stomach Precautions:** If patient recently eaten, rapid sequence induction with gentle cricoid pressure
* **Fasting Patients:** May use inhalation induction with sevoflurane in oxygen by mask

### 3.4.8. ANESTHESIA FOR RIGID BRONCHOSCOPY

**Comprehensive anesthetic approach for the procedure of choice in foreign body removal.**

#### 3.4.8.1. GENERAL ANESTHESIA REQUIREMENT

* **Mandatory:** General anesthesia is required for children who undergo rigid bronchoscopy
* **Anesthetic Goals:**
  * Render child unconscious
  * Provide surgeon with optimal operating conditions
  * Establish intravenous access for volume expansion and medications
  * Provide rapid emergence with awake, protective airway reflexes

#### 3.4.8.2. PREMEDICATION STRATEGY

* **Standard Approach:** Premedication for children with foreign body aspiration (FBA) should be administered as it would be for other children who undergo anesthesia
* **Routine Practice:** Intravenous midazolam routinely administered to facilitate separation from parents or caregivers and transfer into the operating room suite for the procedure

#### 3.4.8.3. MONITORING AND INTRAVENOUS ACCESS

* **Monitoring:** The basic physiologic monitoring modalities used for adults during anesthesia are used for children as well, with appropriately sized equipment
* **IV Timing:** Unless an intravenous catheter is already in place, the decision to place one before or after induction of general anesthesia depends on the patient's age and the clinical scenario

#### 3.4.8.4. INDUCTION OF ANESTHESIA

**Critical Principle: Maintain Spontaneous Ventilation**

* **Primary Goal:** Whether an inhaled or intravenous induction is performed, the goal should be to maintain spontaneous ventilation, particularly for children with proximal (ie, laryngeal or tracheal) FBA
* **Rationale:** The use of positive pressure ventilation may theoretically move a foreign body in the tracheobronchial tree and convert a partial obstruction to a complete obstruction
* **Distal FB Exception:** Avoidance of positive pressure ventilation is less important if the foreign body is located distally in the bronchus

**Standard Induction Protocol:**
1. **Inhalation Induction:** 100 percent oxygen and sevoflurane with maintained spontaneous ventilation during induction
2. **Topicalization:** After induction, the bronchoscopist sprays the vocal cords and trachea with 1 or 2% lidocaine, often only 2 to 3 mL total volume, limited by weight-based maximum doses (ie, 4 to 5 mg/kg without epinephrine, 5 to 7 mg/kg with epinephrine)
3. **Antisialagogue Consideration:** The provider may consider using an antisialagogue if there are excess secretions, although evidence supporting this practice is lacking

#### 3.4.8.5. MAINTENANCE OF ANESTHESIA

**Deep General Anesthesia Required**

* **Depth Requirement:** Patients require deep general anesthesia to tolerate rigid bronchoscopy
* **Decision Points:** The decisions to employ an intravenous versus an inhaled technique, to administer neuromuscular blocking agents (NMBAs), and to use intermittent positive pressure ventilation, spontaneous ventilation, or jet ventilation should be discussed with the bronchoscopist
* **Practice Variation:** The optimal choice of anesthetic agents and mode of ventilation are debated among pediatric anesthesiologists, and practice varies

**Choice of Anesthetic Agents:**

* **Combined Technique (Recommended):** Combination of inhaled sevoflurane, intravenous propofol (either as a continuous infusion or administered as boluses as needed), and intravenous dexmedetomidine (either as a continuous infusion or administered as an upfront bolus) for induction and maintenance of anesthesia
* **Remifentanil Addition:** Others add a remifentanil infusion to help the patient tolerate the rigid bronchoscope; remifentanil may be particularly useful in this setting because of its rapid onset and offset
* **NMBA Use:** Avoid NMBAs unless required urgently

**Considerations for Agent Selection:**

* **Inhalation Anesthesia Challenges:**
  * Maintaining an adequate depth of anesthesia may be difficult with inhaled anesthetics (ie, sevoflurane, isoflurane) because of intermittent interruption of ventilation and leak around the bronchoscope
  * Inhalation anesthesia during rigid bronchoscopy also pollutes the operating room with anesthetic gases
* **TIVA Considerations:**
  * Total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) allows for a continuous level of anesthesia regardless of ventilation, but can also more easily abolish spontaneous ventilation
  * TIVA is usually administered with NMBAs and controlled ventilation
  * In one study, TIVA administered with spontaneous ventilation (without NMBAs) was associated with increased movement, breath holding, and laryngospasm, compared with inhalation anesthesia
* **Combined Approach Benefits:** The combination of inhalation anesthesia with intravenous agents helps achieve a more stable, adequate depth of anesthesia than inhalation agents alone, while allowing spontaneous ventilation

**Oxygenation Strategies:**

* **Supplemental Oxygen:** Can be administered via nasal canula or through an oral RAE tube placed in the corner of the mouth, during spontaneous ventilation or during apneic oxygenation
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Simple nasal canula is less noisy and distracting for clinicians than humidified high flow nasal canula and provides plenty of oxygen reserve during airway manipulation and bronchoscopy

#### 3.4.8.6. MODE OF VENTILATION DURING RIGID BRONCHOSCOPY

**Preoperative Planning Essential**

* **Discussion Required:** The plan for ventilation during the procedure should be discussed preoperatively with the bronchoscopist, including the possible need for changes during the procedure as dictated by the clinical conditions
* **Study Evidence:** In one small study of children who underwent rigid bronchoscopy for FBA, 36 children were randomly assigned to either spontaneous ventilation or controlled ventilation with paralysis during halothane anesthesia. All 17 children in the spontaneous ventilation group required conversion to assisted ventilation because of oxygen desaturation or inadequate depth of anesthesia
* **Clinical Practice:** In practice, the choice between spontaneous and controlled ventilation is often one of personal preference, and does not usually affect the outcome of the procedure

**Spontaneous Ventilation Advantages:**

* **Foreign Body Safety:** Spontaneous ventilation during the maintenance of anesthesia minimizes the chance that an unstable foreign body will be moved more distally
* **Physiologic Benefits:** Negative pressure (spontaneous) ventilation can theoretically allow for better oxygenation and ventilation for some obstructive lesions
* **Caveat:** The ability to ventilate with positive pressure around an airway obstruction is not guaranteed, even with a ventilating bronchoscope

**Positive Pressure Ventilation Options:**

* **Ventilating Bronchoscope:** In most cases of foreign body retrieval, a ventilating rigid bronchoscope is used, which provides the option to attempt positive pressure ventilation through the ventilating port when needed due to the hypoventilation that occurs at the required depth of anesthesia or as a result of administration of neuromuscular blocking agents (NMBAs)
* **Non-ventilating Bronchoscope:** If a non-ventilating bronchoscope is used, intermittent mask ventilation is an option

#### 3.4.8.7. EMERGENCE FROM ANESTHESIA

* **Secretion Management:** Once the foreign body and the bronchoscope are removed, airway secretions should be suctioned while the child is still deeply anesthetized to minimize the chance of laryngospasm
* **Technique Selection:** The technique for emergence is driven largely by the state of pulmonary gas exchange and the amount of airway edema
* **Uncomplicated Cases:** For uncomplicated procedures without airway edema, allow the child to emerge without an airway device in place, with oxygen administered by face mask
* **Complicated Cases:** Patients with airway edema, persistent oxygen desaturation, or residual neuromuscular blockade may require endotracheal intubation and delayed emergence or recovery in the intensive care unit

### 3.4.9. ADDITIONAL INTRAOPERATIVE CONSIDERATIONS

#### 3.4.9.1. INDUCTION MODIFICATIONS

* **Prolonged Induction:** May occur secondary to airway obstruction
* **Avoid N₂O:** Prevent air trapping distal to obstruction
* **Stomach Evacuation:** Orogastric tube after induction, then airway given to surgeon

#### 3.4.9.2. INTRAOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT CHALLENGES

* **Ventilation Challenges:** 
  * Hypoxia and hypercarbia may occur from inadequate ventilation
  * Causes: Massive leak around bronchoscope, inadequate gas exchange through narrow-lumen bronchoscope with internal telescope
* **Solutions:** 
  * Frequent telescope removal
  * Bronchoscope withdrawal to mid-trachea allowing adequate ventilation
* **Bronchospasm Management:** Increasing anesthesia depth, nebulized albuterol, intravenous bronchodilators
* ⚠️ **Pneumothorax:** Rare but suspect if acute deterioration occurs during procedure

#### 3.4.9.3. POST-REMOVAL PROTOCOL

* **Complete Examination:** Entire tracheobronchial tree examined for additional objects or fragments
* **Airway Clearance:** Often requires vigorous irrigation and suctioning distal to obstruction preventing postobstructive pneumonia
* **Anti-inflammatory:** Steroids administered if airway mucosa inflammation observed
* **Close Observation:** Required for early intervention if respiratory compromise from airway edema or infection

### 3.4.10. POSTOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT

**Comprehensive post-procedure care focusing on early discharge versus hospital admission criteria.**

#### 3.4.10.1. DISCHARGE OUTCOMES

* **Uncomplicated Cases:** Most patients who undergo uncomplicated removal soon after aspiration of a foreign body are discharged from the hospital within four hours of bronchoscopy
* **Complicated Cases:** Patients who require lengthy bronchoscopy or develop complications may require admission to the hospital and/or intensive care unit for further treatment

#### 3.4.10.2. INDICATIONS FOR HOSPITAL ADMISSION

**Patients requiring admission include those with:**
* Airway trauma
* Pneumonia
* Atelectasis
* Pneumothorax or pneumomediastinum
* Requirement for postoperative oxygen supplementation

**Bottom Line:** Foreign-body aspiration requires immediate recognition and emergent bronchoscopic removal with careful anesthetic management balancing ventilation strategy, airway protection, and prevention of further foreign body displacement. Most uncomplicated cases can be discharged within hours, but prolonged procedures or complications necessitate hospital or ICU admission.

### 4.2.2. AIRWAY MANAGEMENT METHODS FOR SHARED AIRWAY SURGERY

## 4.3. STRIDOR EVALUATION AND MANAGEMENT

**Systematic approach to noisy breathing from obstructed airflow.**

### 4.3.1. STRIDOR CLASSIFICATIONS

* **Inspiratory Stridor:** Upper airway obstruction
* **Expiratory Stridor:** Lower airway obstruction  
* **Biphasic Stridor:** Midtracheal lesions

### 4.3.2. DIAGNOSTIC APPROACH

#### 4.3.2.1. HISTORY TAKING

* **Age of Onset:** Suggests cause
  * **At/Shortly After Birth:** Laryngotracheomalacia usually present
  * **Later Development:** Cysts or mass lesions develop later in life
* **Positional Factors:** Information about positions making stridor better/worse should be obtained
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Placing patient in position allowing gravity to aid obstruction reduction can benefit anesthetic induction

#### 4.3.2.2. PHYSICAL EXAMINATION COMPONENTS

* **General Condition Assessment:** Patient status and degree of airway compromise
* **Specific Signs:** Listed in Table 48-3 should be noted

**Table 48-3: Clinical Components of Stridor Evaluation**
* Respiratory rate, heart rate, wheezing, cyanosis
* Chest retractions, nasal flaring, level of consciousness

#### 4.3.2.3. LABORATORY AND IMAGING STUDIES

* **Laboratory:** Hemoglobin assessment
* **Imaging:** 
  * Chest radiograph
  * Barium swallow (aid in identifying lesions compressing trachea)
  * MRI and CT scans (indicated in isolated instances, not routinely ordered)

### 4.3.3. COMMON CAUSES BY ANATOMIC LOCATION

| Supraglottic Airway | Larynx | Subglottic Airway |
|---------------------|---------|-------------------|

| Vocal cord paralysis | Infection (tonsillitis, peritonsillar abscess) | Vascular ring |
| Subglottic stenosis | Foreign body | Foreign body |

### 4.3.4. SPECIFIC CONDITIONS

#### 4.3.4.1. LARYNGOMALACIA

* **Most Common Cause:** Stridor in infants
* **Pathophysiology:** 
  * Long epiglottis prolapses posteriorly
  * Prominent arytenoid cartilages with redundant aryepiglottic folds obstructing glottis during inspiration
* **Natural History:** Many times improves as patients grow older
* **Surgical Indication:** Patients with severe obstructive symptoms may need intervention

#### 4.3.4.2. VOCAL CORD PARALYSIS

* **Unilateral (UVCP):** 
  * Most patients stridulous but rarely overt airway obstruction
  * Younger children: weak cry, frequent aspirations from inability to fully oppose vocal cords and protect airway
  * Older patients: may only have hoarseness and poor vocal projection
* **Bilateral:** Would have signs of overt airway obstruction (unlike unilateral)

### 4.3.5. DIAGNOSTIC PROCEDURES

* **Office Examination:** Preliminary examination with small, flexible fiberoptic bronchoscope through nares into oropharynx observing vocal cord movement
* **OR Examination:** May be accomplished before anesthetic induction in awake or lightly anesthetized patient during spontaneous respiration
* ⚠️ **Critical Requirement:** Patients must breathe spontaneously for vocal cord movement assessment
* **Procedure Sequence:**
  1. Deepen anesthesia and topicalize vocal cords with 1-2% lidocaine
  2. Insert rigid bronchoscope through vocal cords
  3. Inspect subglottic area
  4. Evaluate lower trachea and bronchi with rigid or flexible fiberoptic bronchoscope

### 4.3.6. TREATMENT INTERVENTIONS

#### 4.3.6.1. LARYNGOMALACIA TREATMENT

* **Supraglottoplasty:** Redundant tissue removed in targeted manner to improve airflow mechanics

#### 4.3.6.2. UVCP TREATMENT

* **Goal:** Medialize paralyzed cord for contact with contralateral functioning vocal cord allowing airway protection
* **Options:**
  * **Periodic:** Injection laryngoplasty
  * **Permanent:** Medialization thyroplasty, reinnervation procedures
* **Reinnervation Function:** Don't return vocal cord mobility but restore tone for more medial cord position

## 4.4. BRONCHOSCOPY

**Diagnostic and therapeutic procedure requiring specialized anesthetic techniques.**

### 4.4.1. PREMEDICATION CONSIDERATIONS

* **Small Infants:** May be brought to OR unpremedicated
* **Older Children/Adults:** 
  * ⚠️ **Avoid Heavy Premedication:** May cause respiratory depression and worsening airway obstruction
  * Only light sedation suggested
* **Aspiration Prophylaxis:** Full regimen may be indicated for prolonged airway manipulation protection

### 4.4.2. ANESTHETIC GOALS

* **Patient State:** Analgesia, unconscious patient, "quiet" surgical field
* **Avoid:** Coughing, bucking, straining during rigid bronchoscope instrumentation (may cause difficulty and airway damage)
* **Emergence:** Quick return to consciousness with intact airway reflexes

### 4.4.3. MONITORING AND POSITIONING

* **Standard Monitoring:** Pulse oximeter, blood pressure cuff, ECG, precordial stethoscope applied before induction
* **Induction Method:** 
  * **Children:** Inhalation induction by mask with oxygen and volatile agent in increasing concentrations
  * **Adults:** Intravenous drugs
* **Positioning:** Place patient in position producing least adverse effect on airway symptoms (often sitting position)
* **Secretion Management:** IV antisialagogue may decrease secretions compromising bronchoscope view

### 4.4.5. VENTILATION TECHNIQUES

#### 4.4.5.1. RIGID BRONCHOSCOPE VENTILATION

* **Method:** Inserted through vocal cords, ventilation through side port attached to anesthesia circuit
* **Challenges:** High resistance when viewing telescope in place due to partial lumen occlusion
* **Requirements:** 
  * High fresh gas flow rates
  * Large tidal volumes
  * High inspired volatile anesthetic concentrations to compensate for leaks and resistance
* **Manual Ventilation:** Higher-than-normal rates most effective, adequate time for passive chest recoil

#### 4.4.5.2. JET VENTILATION TECHNIQUES
**Comprehensive guide to jet ventilation for shared airway surgery.**

##### TYPES OF JET VENTILATION

| Type | Frequency | Description | Applications |
|------|-----------|-------------|--------------|
| **Manual (MJV)** | ~20/min | Hand-held device, operator-controlled | Simple procedures |
| **LFJV** | 10-30/min | Low-frequency, normofrequent | Standard respiratory cycles |
| **HFJV** | 120-600/min | High-frequency, tidal volumes 1-3 mL/kg | Most common for airway surgery |
| **SHFJV** | Dual jet | Combined HFJV + LFJV | Severe stenosis, optimal CO₂ clearance |

##### EQUIPMENT REQUIREMENTS

* **Core Components:**
	* High-pressure non-collapsible oxygen tubing
	* Pressure regulator (<50 psi)
	* Electromagnetic solenoid valves
	* Blunt-ended stainless steel needle (13-gauge adults, 16-gauge paediatrics)
* **Commercial Systems:** Monsoon III (Acutronic), TwinStream (Carl Reiner)

##### DELIVERY ROUTES

| Route | Placement | Advantages | Considerations |
|-------|-----------|------------|----------------|
| **Supraglottic** | Via suspension laryngoscope | Laser-safe, optimal visualisation | Requires deep anaesthesia |
| **Transglottic** | Catheter through glottis | Good CO₂ clearance | Risk of catheter displacement |
| **Transtracheal** | Via cricothyroid membrane | Secure airway | Higher complication rate |

##### VENTILATION PARAMETERS

| Parameter | Adults | Paediatrics | Notes |
|-----------|--------|-------------|-------|
| **Driving Pressure** | 20-50 psi | 5-30 psi | Start low, titrate up |
| **Frequency (HFJV)** | 120-600/min | 120-400/min | >150/min may impair CO₂ clearance |
| **Inspiration Time** | 30-40% | 30-40% | I:E ratio ~1:3 essential |
| **FiO₂** | Variable | Variable | <0.4 for laser surgery |

* 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: 100% O₂ as jet gas delivers effective FiO₂ of 0.8-0.9 due to Venturi air entrainment
* ⚠️ **Critical**: Adequate exhalation time (3-4 seconds) prevents air trapping and barotrauma

##### INDICATIONS

* Laryngeal surgery (papilloma, webs, lesions)
* Microlaryngeal surgery
* Tracheal stenosis surgery
* Laser surgery of airway (laser-safe with supraglottic approach)
* Rigid bronchoscopy requiring tubeless field
* Direct laryngoscopy requiring optimal surgical exposure

##### CONTRAINDICATIONS

**Absolute:**
* Complete upper airway obstruction
* Significant laryngeal or cricoid cartilage damage
* Active pneumonia or asthma (increased compliance/resistance)
* Inability to ensure expiration below vocal cords

**Relative:**
* Morbid obesity with poor baseline oxygenation
* Severe small airway disease
* Aspiration risk
* Uncontrolled coagulopathy

##### ⚠️ COMPLICATIONS

| Complication | Mechanism | Prevention |
|--------------|-----------|------------|
| **Barotrauma** (pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum) | Air trapping, inadequate exhalation | Adequate I:E ratio, monitor pressures |
| **Subcutaneous emphysema** | Mucosal disruption | Avoid excessive pressure |
| **Gastric distension** | Malpositioned catheter | Confirm airway position before jet |
| **Hypoxemia** | Inadequate oxygenation | Monitor SpO₂, adjust parameters |
| **Hypercapnia** | Inadequate minute ventilation | Monitor ETCO₂/PtcCO₂ |
| **Necrotising tracheobronchitis** | Mucosal drying (>2 hours use) | Humidification with sterile water |
| **Cardiovascular collapse** | Severe barotrauma, auto-PEEP | Alarm settings, gradual titration |

##### SAFETY PROTOCOLS AND MONITORING

**Alarm Settings (Critical):**
* **Pause Pressure (PP)**: <10 cmH₂O (especially in stenosis - prevents auto-PEEP)
* **Peak Inspiratory Pressure (PIP)**: Start at 20 mbar

**Monitoring Requirements:**
* Pulse oximetry (continuous)
* End-tidal capnography (5 LFJV breaths for sampling)
* Transcutaneous CO₂ (PtcCO₂) for continuous monitoring
* Chest rise and fall observation (PRIMARY visual monitor)
* BIS monitoring (awareness risk with TIVA)
* Arterial blood gas when needed

**Prevention Measures:**
* Confirm airway position with fiberoptic before jet initiation
* Ensure adequate paralysis (avoid laryngospasm)
* Start at lowest parameters, increase gradually
* Ensure expiratory pathway patency
* Permanent anaesthesiologist presence required

##### ANAESTHETIC TECHNIQUE FOR JET VENTILATION

* **Preferred Approach**: TIVA with Target Control Infusion
	* Propofol infusion (maintenance)
	* Remifentanil infusion (optimal conditions, rapid offset)
	* Rocuronium for deep muscle relaxation (PTC 1-2)
* **Induction**: Standard IV induction; topical lidocaine to larynx
* **Muscle Relaxation**: Deep paralysis ensures optimal surgical conditions
* **Reversal**: Sugammadex 4 mg/kg for rapid, predictable recovery
* ⚠️ **Inhalational anaesthesia**: Difficult to maintain adequate depth due to open airway; OR pollution

##### LASER SURGERY CONSIDERATIONS

* FiO₂ <0.4 to prevent combustion
* Supraglottic jet ventilation is laser-safe (no flammable materials in field)
* Wet gauze on exposed face/neck skin
* All personnel wear laser-specific eye protection

🔑 **HY**: Transtracheal jet ventilation has higher complication rate than transglottic/supraglottic routes, primarily from barotrauma-related airway obstruction

**Bottom Line:** Jet ventilation provides optimal surgical conditions for shared airway surgery by eliminating the endotracheal tube from the surgical field. HFJV at 120-600/min with appropriate I:E ratio (1:3) and careful pressure management minimises barotrauma risk. TIVA with deep paralysis is the preferred anaesthetic technique, with continuous monitoring and permanent anaesthesiologist presence essential for safety.

### 4.4.6. ANESTHETIC MAINTENANCE OPTIONS

* **Volatile Anesthetic:** Augmented by propofol infusion (100-300 mcg/kg/min)
* **TIVA:** When controlled volatile agent administration not possible during open airway surgery
* **TIVA Combinations:** Propofol infusions supplemented by ketamine, dexmedetomidine, opiates (particularly remifentanil)

### 4.4.7. POST-PROCEDURE MANAGEMENT

* **Airway Options:** 
  * Place tracheal tube for anesthesia recovery control
  * Manage airway without tracheal tube if not needed
* **Additional Advantage:** ETT allows small, flexible fiberoptic bronchoscope passage for distal airway examination (also possible via supraglottic airway)

**Bottom Line:** Airway surgery and bronchoscopy require careful coordination between teams, understanding of complex ventilation options, and preparation for multiple airway management scenarios while prioritizing patient safety throughout shared airway procedures.

## 4.5. LASER SURGERY OF THE AIRWAY

**Advanced surgical technique requiring specialized safety protocols and anesthetic considerations.**

### 4.5.1. LASER TECHNOLOGY AND APPLICATIONS

* **LASER:** Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation
* **Advantages:** Precision targeting, minimal bleeding and edema, surrounding structure preservation, rapid healing
* **Components:** Tube with reflective mirrors and amplifying medium generating electron activity for light production

### 4.5.2. CO₂ LASER CHARACTERISTICS

* **Most Widely Used:** Medical practice laser
* **Applications:** 
  * Laryngeal or vocal cord papillomas
  * Laryngeal webs
  * Resection of redundant subglottic tissue
  * Coagulation of hemangiomas
* **Surgeon Benefits:** Invisible beam affords unobstructed lesion view during resection
* **Mechanism:** Energy absorbed by tissue water rapidly increases temperature, denaturing protein and vaporizing target tissue
* **Hemostasis:** Thermal energy cauterizes capillaries as tissues vaporized, minimizing bleeding and postoperative edema

### 4.5.3. ⚠️ SAFETY CONSIDERATIONS

#### 4.5.3.1. EYE PROTECTION

* **Universal Risk:** Eyes especially vulnerable to injury
* **Personnel Protection:** All OR personnel must wear laser-specific eye goggles with side protectors
* **CO₂ Laser Penetration:** Limited (0.01 mm) - may damage only cornea
* **Other Lasers (Nd:YAG):** Deeper penetration may cause retinal injury and scarring
* **Patient Protection:** 
  * Tape eyes shut
  * Apply wet gauze pads  
  * Place metal shield
  * Damp gauze absorbs stray beams preventing eye penetration

#### 4.5.3.2. FIRE AND BURN PREVENTION

* **Laser Radiation:** Increases absorbent material temperature
* **Flammable Objects:** Surgical drapes must be kept away from laser beam path
* **Skin Protection:** Wet towels applied to exposed face and neck skin when laser used in airway
* **Deflected Beam Protection:** Prevents cutaneous burns

#### 4.5.3.3. RESPIRATORY PROTECTION

* **Laser Smoke Plumes:** May cause lung damage; interstitial pneumonia reported with long-term exposure
* **Infectious Risk:** Cancer cells and virus particles (including HIV) may be vaporized and inhaled via smoke plume
* **Protection:** Specially designed surgical masks for filtering laser smoke recommended

### 4.5.4. ANESTHETIC CONSIDERATIONS

#### 4.5.4.1. GAS MANAGEMENT

* **Combustion Support:** Both N₂O and oxygen support combustion
* **Primary Gas:** Should consist of blended air and oxygen OR helium and oxygen
* **Oxygenation Monitoring:** Pulse oximeter ensures adequate oxygenation at lowest possible inspired oxygen concentration

#### 4.5.4.2. ENDOTRACHEAL TUBE SELECTION

* **Standard PVC Tubes:** 
  * ⚠️ **All flammable:** Can ignite and vaporize producing hydrochloric acid when contacted by laser beam
* **Cuff Inflation:** Use sterile saline instead of air
* **Cuff Rupture Detection:** Methylene blue added to saline for ready detection of laser spark rupture
* **Laser-Specific Tubes:** 
  * Manufactured specifically for laser surgery
  * Some have double cuff protecting airway if cuff rupture occurs
  * Unique matte finish prevents reflected laser beam scattering
  * Some combine both features

#### 4.5.4.3. METAL LASER TUBES

* **Material:** Nonreflective flexible metal specifically manufactured for laser surgery
* **Size Consideration:** Outer diameter considerably greater than PVC counterpart, especially small pediatric sizes

### 4.5.5. VENTILATION TECHNIQUES

#### 4.5.5.1. APNEIC TECHNIQUE

* **Preferred by Some Surgeons:** Especially when working on small infant/child airways
* **Advantage:** Open surgical field without ETT potentially obscuring view
* **Method:** 
  * Child anesthetized and immobilized using muscle relaxant or deep volatile anesthetic
  * Trachea not intubated
  * Surgeon uses laser for brief periods
  * Between applications: mask ventilation
* **Safety:** Ventilate lungs with oxygen during apnea
* **Risks:** Greater potential for debris entering trachea, airway trauma from repeated intubations

#### 4.5.5.2. JET VENTILATOR TECHNIQUE

* **Modification:** Of apneic technique providing oxygenation/ventilation during laser surgery
* **Setup:** Operating laryngoscope fitted with catheter delivering oxygen through variable pressure reducing valve
* **Gas Mixture:** Additional room air entrained, lungs ventilated with combination
* **Advantages:** 
  * Quiet surgical field (large diaphragmatic excursions eliminated)
  * Uninterrupted ventilation
* **Limitations:** Difficult/impossible in morbidly obese patients and those with severe small airway disease

#### 4.5.5.3. APNEIC OXYGENATION

* **Method:** High-flow humidified oxygen source
* **Capability:** Maintain oxygenation for significant periods without surgery interruption
* **Applications:** Described in adults and children

#### 4.5.5.4. SPONTANEOUS VENTILATION TECHNIQUE

* **Setup:** Surgical laryngoscope with oxygen insufflation port inserted into larynx
* **Anesthetic Approach:**
  * Induction: Volatile agent by mask
  * Maintenance: TIVA without muscle relaxant
  * Adjuvants: Propofol ± short-acting narcotic, dexmedetomidine, or ketamine
  * Topicalization: Vocal cords sprayed with 4% lidocaine to decrease reactivity

**Advantages:**
* Extended periods of uninterrupted laser application
* Better surgical visualization

**Disadvantages:**
* Absence of complete airway control
* Limited laryngospasm protection
* Limited debris protection
* Vocal cord motion during surgery
* Difficult scavenging

**Bottom Line:** Laser airway surgery requires specialized safety protocols, appropriate gas management, careful endotracheal tube selection, and coordination between surgical and anesthesia teams to prevent fires while maintaining adequate ventilation and oxygenation.

## 4.6. NASAL SURGERY AND FUNCTIONAL ENDOSCOPIC SINUS SURGERY

**Procedures requiring optimal surgical field visualization and management of vasoconstrictor effects.**

### 4.6.1. ANESTHETIC EVOLUTION

* **Historical:** Nasal procedures completed under topical or local anesthesia with sedation allowing patient signaling
* **Current Practice:** General anesthesia preferred as endoscopic nasal surgery became more extensive; local with sedation reserved for straightforward cases

### 4.6.2. SURGICAL FIELD OPTIMIZATION

* **Bleeding Control:** Must be kept to minimum for optimal visualization
* **Current Practice:** 
  * Vasoconstrictors usage
  * Head elevation
  * Avoid excessive blood pressure increases

### 4.6.3. PREOPERATIVE CARDIOVASCULAR ASSESSMENT

* **Essential Evaluation:** Focused cardiovascular history documenting:
  * Coronary artery disease
  * Peripheral artery disease  
  * Cardiac arrhythmias
* **Significance:** Conditions might be exacerbated by sympathomimetic agents used for local vasoconstriction
* **Impact on Management:** Positive findings may alter:
  * Degree of head elevation
  * Use of topical constrictors
  * Anesthetic technique
  * Lower blood pressure limit safely tolerated

### 4.6.4. ⚠️ VASOCONSTRICTOR MANAGEMENT

#### 4.6.4.1. AGENTS AND APPLICATIONS

* **Intranasal Vasoconstriction:** Local anesthetics combined with cocaine, epinephrine, phenylephrine
* **Systemic Absorption Effects:** Hypotension, hypertension, bradycardia, tachycardia, arrhythmias

#### 4.6.4.2. SPECIAL POPULATIONS

* **Beta-Blocker Patients:** Alpha-agonist–induced hypertension may lead to pulmonary edema and cardiac failure
* **Calcium Channel Blocker Patients:** Similar risks as beta-blocker therapy
* **Management:** Prompt treatment needed preventing serious complications

### 4.6.5. PATIENT POSITIONING AND ANESTHETIC TECHNIQUE

* **Head Elevation:** 15 degrees to facilitate venous drainage
* **Hemodynamic Effects:** May provide modest hypotension, potential for lower extremity venous pooling
* **Anesthetic Maintenance:** Inhalation-based or TIVA
* **TIVA Advantages:** 
  * May reduce coughing on emergence
  * Decreased PONV

**Bottom Line:** Nasal surgery requires careful preoperative cardiovascular assessment, judicious use of vasoconstrictors with monitoring for systemic effects, and appropriate patient positioning to optimize surgical field while maintaining hemodynamic stability.

## 4.7. SKULL BASE SURGERY

**Complex multidisciplinary procedures requiring specialized anesthetic considerations and blood conservation strategies.**

### 4.7.1. SURGICAL CONTEXT

* **Multidisciplinary:** Involves otorhinolaryngology, neurosurgery, and anesthesiology
* **Patient Population:** 
  * **Adults:** Most have malignant tumors
  * **Smaller Group:** Benign tumors, congenital malformations, other abnormalities

### 4.7.2. PREOPERATIVE IMAGING AND PROCEDURES

* **Noninvasive Testing:** CT and MRI scans to determine bony and soft-tissue abnormality extent
* **Invasive Angiography:** May be needed for tumor location and blood supply determination
* **Surgical Planning:** Studies program intraoperative localizing systems guiding surgical dissection
* **Pre-surgical Embolization:** May be performed 1-2 days before surgery to shrink lesion and decrease operative blood loss

### 4.7.3. ⚠️ SPECIAL PREOPERATIVE CONSIDERATIONS

#### 4.7.3.1. PREVIOUS TREATMENT EFFECTS

* **Prior Surgery:** May affect previously accessible airway, requiring advanced airway management techniques
* **Chemotherapy Implications:**
  * **Cardiotoxic Agents:** Dose-dependent effects may require cardiac evaluation including echocardiogram
  * **Impact:** Decreased cardiac function may affect anesthetic agent choice and require invasive blood pressure monitoring
  * **Neurotoxic Agents:** May reduce muscle relaxant dose or prolong duration requiring neuromuscular monitoring

#### 4.7.3.2. RADIATION THERAPY EFFECTS

* **Systemic Problems:** Usually absent unless pituitary gland damaged
* **Panhypopituitarism:** Can cause hypothyroidism, hypoadrenocorticism, diabetes insipidus
* **Local Effects:** 
  * Fibrosis and ankylosis of temporomandibular joint rendering direct laryngoscopy difficult
  * Increased blood loss and poor wound healing at operative site
  * Secondary radiation fibrosis makes surgical dissection more difficult and time-consuming
  * May necessitate free or vascularized grafts for surgical site closure

### 4.7.4. AIRWAY MANAGEMENT

* **General Rule:** Usually don't have difficult airway or require special intubation techniques
* **Route Selection:**
  * **Oral Intubation:** Preferred for lesions requiring midline surgical approach
  * **Oral or Nasal:** May be used if lesion doesn't cross midline
* **Securing Methods:**
  * **Nasal ETT:** Heavy suture through nasal septum and around tube
  * **Oral ETT:** Wire to teeth, suture to gingival periosteum, or circummandibular wire

### 4.7.5. KEY ANESTHETIC CONSIDERATIONS

#### 4.7.5.1. MUSCLE RELAXATION MANAGEMENT

* **Facial Nerve Risk:** Like other procedures with facial nerve injury/transection risk during dissection
* **Nerve Monitoring:** Necessary to avoid paralysis for periodic facial nerve stimulation verifying integrity
* **Relaxant Use:** May be given if nerve stimulation no longer required

#### 4.7.5.2. BLOOD CONSERVATION STRATEGIES

* **Assessment:** Minimizing blood loss and replacement planning must be considered
* **Large-Volume Loss Approaches:**
  * **Preoperative:** Iron and erythropoietin for identified anemia
  * **Intraoperative:** Acute normovolemic hemodilution to minimize blood loss
  * **Pharmacological:** Antifibrinolytic therapy (successful in craniofacial procedures)
  * **Blood Products:** Directed donor or autologous blood donation to minimize/decrease allogeneic exposure
  * **Blood Salvage:** Cell saver usually not appropriate (non-sterile approaches, tumor resection contraindication)

**Bottom Line:** Skull base surgery requires comprehensive preoperative assessment of prior treatments, careful airway management planning, facial nerve monitoring considerations, and aggressive blood conservation strategies due to potential for significant blood loss in complex, prolonged procedures.

## 5.2. UPPER AIRWAY INFECTIONS

**Adult infectious processes presenting similar airway compression and distortion as pediatric conditions.**

### 5.2.1. ADULT EPIGLOTTITIS

* **Similar Presentation:** Fever, chills, drooling, difficulty speaking and swallowing as pediatric epiglottitis
* **Key Difference:** Usually don't appear with critical airways from swelling (unlike pediatric cases)
* **Causative Organisms:** Mainly gram-negative bacteria

### 5.2.2. LUDWIG'S ANGINA AND SUBMANDIBULAR CELLULITIS

* **Clinical Presentation:** Same symptoms as epiglottitis (fever, chills, drooling, speech/swallowing difficulty)
* **Etiology:** Often result of dental abscesses
* **Anatomic Extension:** Into submandibular, submental, and sublingual areas
* **Critical Complication:** Sublingual space involvement pushes tongue upward and backward leading to potential asphyxiation from airway obstruction
* **Emergency Intervention:** May require emergent surgical drainage to relieve airway obstruction

### 5.2.3. ⚠️ AIRWAY MANAGEMENT CHALLENGES

#### 5.2.3.1. PREFERRED APPROACH

* **Gold Standard:** Awake tracheostomy with local anesthesia considered safest for these patients
* **Critical Safety:** If awake tracheostomy performed, avoid positive-pressure ventilation until proper tracheal tube placement confirmed
* **Risk:** Insufflation into false or blind passage can lead to significant patient morbidity

#### 5.2.3.2. ALTERNATIVE TECHNIQUES

* **Options:** 
  * Fiberoptic nasal intubation
  * Direct laryngoscopy after inhalational anesthesia
* **Post-Procedure:** Patients require ICU care whether tracheostomy or endotracheal tube present once abscess drained
* **Extubation Timing:** Should not extubate trachea until swelling resolution occurs (increased swelling may develop initially)

**Bottom Line:** Adult upper airway infections can present with similar severity to pediatric conditions, requiring expert airway management with preference for awake tracheostomy in severe cases and careful ICU monitoring until resolution.

## 5.3. MAXILLOFACIAL TRAUMA

**Complex injury pattern requiring comprehensive assessment and specialized airway management.**

### 5.3.1. ANESTHETIC CHALLENGES

* **Injury Recognition:** Nature and extent of injury and consequent anatomic alteration
* **Airway Planning:** Create plan for safe airway securing  
* **Implementation:** Execute plan without further damage
* **Maintenance:** Maintain airway during anesthetic administration
* **Extubation:** Determine when and how to extubate trachea
* **Workspace:** Create comfortable environment for surgeon and anesthesiologist in limited space

## 5.4. FACIAL FRACTURE CLASSIFICATION

**Systematic approach to understanding facial skeleton injury patterns.**

### 5.4.1. FACIAL SKELETON DIVISION

* **Lower Third:** Mandible (symphysis, body, angle, ramus, condyle, coronoid process)
* **Middle Third:** Zygomatic arch, zygomaticomaxillary complex, maxillae, nasal bones, orbits  
* **Superior Third:** Frontal bone

### 5.4.2. MANDIBULAR FRACTURES

#### 5.4.2.1. ANATOMIC CONSIDERATIONS

* **Unique Shape:** Horseshoe configuration causes forces to gather at vulnerability points often distant from impact
* **Common Fracture Sites:** 
  * Posteriorly where cortex thinner (angle, ramus, condyle)
  * Body at first or second molar level
* **TMJ Involvement:** Condylar fracture has potential for TMJ involvement and jaw mobility limitations

#### 5.4.2.2. TRAUMA PATTERN DISTRIBUTION

* **High-Velocity, High-Impact:** Automobile accidents
  * Greater tendency for posterior fractures (angle, ramus, condyle)
* **Low-Velocity Impact:** Fist, blunt weapon, fall
  * Greater tendency for symphysis, parasymphysis, body fractures
* **Skull Base Extension:** Mandibular fractures typically don't extend into skull base

### 5.4.3. LEFORT FRACTURE CLASSIFICATION

**Historical Context:** Determined by Rene LeFort of Lille, France in 1901

#### 5.4.3.1. LEFORT I FRACTURE

* **Pattern:** Horizontal maxillary fracture
* **Anatomy:** Passes above nasal floor involving lower third of septum
* **Structures:** Mobilizes palate, maxillary alveolar process, lower third pterygoid plates, palatine bone parts
* **Displacement:** May be posterior, lateral, or rotated about vertical axis

#### 5.4.3.2. LEFORT II FRACTURE  

* **Pattern:** Pyramidal fracture
* **Starting Point:** Junction of thick upper nasal bone part with thinner portion forming upper anterior nasal aperture margin
* **Path:** Crosses medial orbital wall (lacrimal bone) beneath zygomaticomaxillary suture, lateral antral wall, posteriorly through pterygoid plates
* **Displacement:** May be posterior or rotated about axis

#### 5.4.3.3. LEFORT III FRACTURE

* **Pattern:** Fracture line parallels skull base
* **Effect:** Separates midfacial skeleton from cranium base
* **Path:** Through nasal base, ethmoid bone, orbital plates; crosses sphenoid lesser wing to pterygomaxillary fissure and sphenopalatine fossa; from inferior orbital fissure base extends laterally/upward to frontozygomatic suture and downward/backward to pterygoid plate root
* **Force Required:** Results from massive force applied to midface
* **Complications:** Zygomata displaced using rotational force, zygomatic arches usually fractured
* **Clinical Sign:** "Dish face deformity" - normal facial convexity becomes concave
* **Subtle Finding:** Maxillary and mandibular incisive edges opposed (instead of normal maxillary shingling over mandibular) indicates minimal posterior midface displacement

### 5.4.4. ⚠️ CONTRAINDICATIONS AND RISKS

#### 5.4.4.1. NASOTRACHEAL INTUBATION

* **Absolute Contraindication:** LeFort II or III fractures where cribriform plate involvement possible
* **Risks:** 
  * Foreign material from nasopharynx may cause meningitis
  * Endotracheal tube can enter cranial cavity
  * Positive-pressure bag-mask ventilation can force material or air into skull
* **Requirement:** Radiographic studies before nasotracheal intubation when skull base trauma suspected

### 5.4.5. ASSOCIATED INJURIES

#### 5.4.5.1. INJURY PATTERN STATISTICS

* **Low-Velocity, Low-Impact Injuries:**
  * 4% additional major life-threatening injuries
  * 10% other minor injuries
* **High-Velocity, High-Impact Accidents:**
  * 32% major additional injuries  
  * 31% additional minor injuries
  * ⚠️ **Critical Finding:** 1.2% cervical spine fractures

#### 5.4.5.2. CONCURRENT TRAUMA PATTERNS

* **Cervical Spine Injuries:** Multiple studies report incidence as high as 10.8%
* **Head Injuries:** Incidence up to 88.7% with facial skeletal trauma
* **Anatomic Correlation:** Area of cervical injury frequently related to maxillofacial trauma site
  * **Upper Face Injuries:** Associated with mid-to-lower cervical spine injuries
  * **Unilateral Mandibular Injuries:** Involve upper cervical spine

## 5.5. AIRWAY MANAGEMENT CONSIDERATIONS

**Comprehensive approach to securing airway in anatomically distorted trauma patients.**

### 5.5.1. PREOPERATIVE ASSESSMENT

* **Extent Evaluation:** To degree clinical situation allows, assess facial trauma extent and concomitant injuries
* **Integration:** Incorporate assessment information into airway management plan
* **Cervical Spine Protection:** Patients with known or suspected injuries should have appropriate protective precautions during airway management

### 5.5.2. ⚠️ INTUBATION CHALLENGES

#### 5.5.2.1. ANATOMIC DISTORTION

* **Factors:** Distorted or disrupted anatomy obscured by blood, emesis, displaced teeth
* **Traditional Approach Limitations:** Direct laryngoscopy not recommended as primary technique due to low success rate in difficult intubation concerns

#### 5.5.2.2. ADVANCED AIRWAY TECHNIQUES

* **Preferred Methods:** 
  * Video laryngoscopy
  * Flexible fiberoptic bronchoscopy intubation
* **Approach Options:**
  * Awake or minimally sedated (cooperative patients)
  * Sedated or anesthetized (uncooperative patients)
* **Surgical Airway:** May be required for extensive airway injury not permitting intubation or laryngotracheal disruption conditions worsened by conventional techniques

### 5.5.3. MOUTH OPENING ASSESSMENT

#### 5.5.3.1. DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS

**Must determine if restriction results from:**
1. **Pain:** Simple fractures very painful; once anesthetized, mouth opens and intubation proceeds without difficulty
2. **Trismus:** Spasm of mastication muscles from trauma or infection; usually overcome by general anesthesia and muscle relaxation
3. **Mechanical Problems:** Direct or indirect trauma interference

#### 5.5.3.2. MECHANICAL INTERFERENCE TYPES

* **Direct Trauma:** Mandibular condyle or zygomatic arch injury causing fractures interfering with normal TMJ function
* **Indirect Trauma:** Force transmission up body and ramus to condyles
  * Compression fractures in glenoid fossa
  * Greenstick fractures impeding opening
* **Long-Standing Trismus:** Some degree of joint immobility will occur
* **Infectious Trismus:** Affected muscles become edematous and swell causing mechanical opening interference

**Bottom Line:** Maxillofacial trauma requires systematic assessment of fracture patterns, recognition of associated injuries (particularly cervical spine), and selection of appropriate advanced airway techniques while avoiding nasotracheal intubation in midface fractures due to potential skull base involvement.

# 6. DENTAL ANAESTHESIA

**Exam Weighting:** Medium

## 6.1. HISTORICAL CONTEXT AND REGULATORY FRAMEWORK

### 6.1.1. UK REGULATORY CHANGES

**Since 31 December 2001, general anaesthesia for dental treatment in the UK must occur in a hospital setting:**

* **Background:** Following several deaths from dental chair GA, the Department of Health mandated that all dental GA must take place in hospitals with immediate access to critical care facilities
* **Current standards:**
  * GA only performed by trained anaesthetists in hospital setting
  * Standards of GA for dentistry same as any other surgical setting
  * Guidelines from Association of Paediatric Anaesthetists must be followed for paediatric patients
  * Special care dentistry requires additional resources and trained staff

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Dental treatment is one of the most common reasons for paediatric general anaesthesia. The regulatory framework ensures patient safety but requires coordination between dental and anaesthetic teams.

## 6.2. INDICATIONS FOR GENERAL ANAESTHESIA

### 6.2.1. CORE PRINCIPLE

**GA should only be used when benefits substantially outweigh risks.**

### 6.2.2. PRIMARY INDICATIONS

**1. Cooperation Difficulties (~45% of cases):**
* **Autism spectrum disorder** (most common diagnosis - 33%)
* Intellectual disability
* Dementia
* Severe dental phobia
* Very young children (<3 years) requiring complex treatment

**2. Medical Complexity:**
* Motor dysfunction (cerebral palsy, Parkinson's disease)
* Neurological conditions (epilepsy, brain disorders)
* Congenital syndromes (Down syndrome)
* Patients who cannot cooperate with sedation

**3. Dental Treatment Factors:**
* Extensive treatment needs
* Local anaesthesia contraindicated
* Acute orofacial infection precluding LA
* Previous failed sedation attempts

### 6.2.3. NOT RECOMMENDED FOR

* Dental examination alone
* Routine anxiety control (consider LA with or without sedation first)
* Simple procedures where sedation would suffice

**Bottom Line:** GA is a last resort after LA and sedation options have been considered. The main indications are cooperation difficulties (particularly autism and learning disability), medical complexity, and extensive treatment needs.

## 6.3. GA vs SEDATION: DECISION MAKING

### 6.3.1. SEDATION OPTIONS

| Technique | Advantages | Fasting | Notes |
|-----------|------------|---------|-------|
| **Nitrous oxide (inhalational)** | Safest dental sedation; rapid onset/offset | Not required | Widely used in paediatric dental practice |
| **IV sedation** | Titratable, reliable | Required | Requires pulse oximetry, capnography; reversal agents available |
| **Oral sedation** | Non-invasive | Required | Variable absorption and effect |

### 6.3.2. WHEN TO CHOOSE GA OVER SEDATION

* Patients unable to maintain cooperation for sedation duration
* Extensive treatment needs requiring prolonged immobility
* Significant medical comorbidities requiring full monitoring
* High aspiration risk
* Failed sedation attempts

### 6.3.3. DAY-CASE vs INPATIENT CRITERIA

**Day-Case Suitable:**
* Majority of dental extractions/restorations
* Medically stable patients
* Adequate social support

**Inpatient Required:**
* Children with cardiac disease
* Coagulation disorders
* Significant medical comorbidities
* High-risk airways
* Social circumstances precluding safe discharge

## 6.4. PREOPERATIVE ASSESSMENT

### 6.4.1. COMPREHENSIVE EVALUATION

* Full dental and medical history
* Assessment of patient preferences and capacity to consent
* Evaluation of medical comorbidities (respiratory, cardiovascular, hepatic, renal)
* Social support structure and transport arrangements
* Formal anaesthetic pre-assessment per national standards

### 6.4.2. PATIENT-SPECIFIC CONSIDERATIONS

**Down Syndrome:**
* Characteristic features: microbrachycephaly, flat nasal bridge, short neck, protruding large tongue
* ~50% have upper respiratory obstruction
* 40-50% have congenital heart anomalies causing bradycardia
* **Atlantoaxial instability** risk - cervical spine precautions

**Autism Spectrum Disorder:**
* Lower anaesthetic sensitivity - may require **higher propofol doses**
* Individual assessment of cooperation level
* Sensory considerations for environment

**Cerebral Palsy:**
* Risk of aspiration from gastroesophageal reflux
* 30% also have epilepsy
* Involuntary movements create management challenges
* **Complication rate: 44.4%** (highest among disability populations)

**Drug Interactions:**
* Propofol dose requirements increased in patients on **antiepileptic drugs**
* SSRIs and MAO inhibitors increase hypo/hypertension risks

### 6.4.3. DENTAL ASSESSMENT

* Complete oral examination documenting existing conditions
* Risk stratification for difficult intubation
* **Identification of loose, mobile, or at-risk teeth**
* Preoperative radiographs as needed

⚠️ **Safety Warning:** Dental damage is the most common anaesthesia-related complaint, accounting for ~1/3 of medicolegal cases. Preoperative dental examination and documentation are essential.

## 6.5. AIRWAY MANAGEMENT

### 6.5.1. KEY PRINCIPLES

* **Shared airway** with surgeon - requires coordination
* Risk of soiling with blood, debris, and surgical materials
* Airway must remain accessible without obstructing surgical field

### 6.5.2. ENDOTRACHEAL TUBE SELECTION

**Nasotracheal Intubation (Preferred for Dental Surgery):**
* Provides better visualization of oral cavity
* Easier to fix and stabilize through nasal pathway
* Avoids interference with surgical field
* **Cuffed ETT is the default** approach

**Tube Types:**

| Tube Type | Characteristics | Use |
|-----------|-----------------|-----|
| **Nasal RAE** | Preformed curve at nasal entrance | Avoids surgical field obstruction |
| **Armoured (reinforced)** | Flexible, kink-resistant | Risk of tissue damage |
| **Parker Flex-tip** | Posterior bevel, anterior tip | Reduces turbinate/septal trauma |

### 6.5.3. CONTRAINDICATIONS TO NASAL INTUBATION

* Basilar skull fracture with CSF leakage
* Coagulopathy
* Nasal polyps
* Frequent epistaxis history
* Prosthetic cardiac valves (bacteremia risk)

### 6.5.4. NASAL INTUBATION TECHNIQUE

1. Apply topical vasoconstrictors (epinephrine, phenylephrine)
2. Select appropriate nostril (preoperative imaging helps)
3. Use thermosoftened tube tips or specialized tubes
4. Insert along **inferior turbinate pathway** (safer than upper pathway)
5. Direct or video-laryngoscopy for glottic visualization
6. Confirm depth 2-3 cm above carina

### 6.5.5. NASAL INTUBATION COMPLICATIONS

| Complication | Notes |
|--------------|-------|
| **Epistaxis** | Most common; from Kiesselbach's plexus trauma |
| Intracranial placement | Cribriform plate fracture risk |
| Bacteremia | Risk in prosthetic valve patients |
| Sinusitis | With prolonged intubation |
| Turbinate avulsion | Rare but serious |

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Laryngeal mask airways are inappropriate for routine dental treatment due to shared airway requirements and aspiration risk.

## 6.6. THROAT PACKS

### 6.6.1. TRADITIONAL INDICATIONS

* Prevent aspiration and ingestion of blood/debris
* Reduce postoperative nausea and vomiting (disputed)
* Stabilize airway during GA
* Prevent leakage with uncuffed tubes
* Seal pharyngeal space during pediatric ventilation

### 6.6.2. CURRENT EVIDENCE

**Benefits:**
* **Foreign body prevention:** Prevents dental debris from entering hypopharynx
* **Airway stabilization:** Useful with cuffless tubes or frequent head repositioning

**Evidence AGAINST Routine Use:**
* No proven prevention of PONV
* Increased postoperative sore throat and dysphagia
* Increased foreign body sensation

### 6.6.3. CRITICAL RISK: RETAINED THROAT PACK

⚠️ **Safety Warning:** Retained throat pack is a **WHO Never Event** and can cause fatal airway obstruction.

**Current Recommendations:**
* Throat packs **no longer recommended for ROUTINE insertion**
* Use selectively on case-by-case basis
* If used, **formal recording/documentation is MANDATORY**

**Safety Protocols:**
* Visual reminder clamp on surgeon's gown
* Checklists with documented in/out timestamps
* Anaesthetist confirms removal before extubation
* Laryngoscopy before extubation to ensure airway clarity

## 6.7. INTRAOPERATIVE CONSIDERATIONS

### 6.7.1. SHARED AIRWAY MANAGEMENT

* Coordination with dental team essential
* Head positioning may need adjustment during procedure
* Risk of airway disconnection or obstruction
* **WHO Surgical Safety Checklist** completion mandatory

### 6.7.2. DENTAL TREATMENT PRINCIPLES UNDER GA

* Complete all treatment during single GA episode when possible
* Restore or extract all carious teeth
* Treatment must be easily maintained by patient/caregivers
* Use permanent restorative materials
* Endodontic treatment: single-stage completion preferred

### 6.7.3. LOCAL ANAESTHESIA UNDER GA

* Used for extractions unless contraindicated
* Provides postoperative analgesia
* **Complete hemostasis before extubation**

## 6.8. COMPLICATIONS

### 6.8.1. COMPLICATION RATES

| Setting | Complication Rate |
|---------|-------------------|
| Local anaesthesia | 0.4% |
| Conscious sedation | 0.9% |
| General anaesthesia | 1.5% |
| Disability populations | 20-30% |

**By Diagnosis:**
* Cerebral palsy: 44.4%
* Autism spectrum disorder: 30.4%
* Down syndrome: 29.2%

### 6.8.2. RESPIRATORY COMPLICATIONS

**Laryngospasm:**
* Common in shared airway procedures
* Risk increased with blood/debris in airway
* Manage with CPAP, deepening anaesthesia, or muscle relaxation
* Can lead to post-obstructive pulmonary oedema

**Aspiration:**
* Foreign body aspiration most common respiratory emergency in dental clinics
* Dental debris (tooth fragments, restorative materials, crowns)
* Blood aspiration risk ("coroner's clot" - NAP4)
* Prevention: throat pack, complete hemostasis, careful extubation

**Airway Obstruction:**
* Blood, debris, or retained throat pack
* Laryngospasm
* Swelling from surgical trauma

### 6.8.3. DENTAL DAMAGE DURING LARYNGOSCOPY

**Incidence:** 0.06% to 12% (50-75% occur during tracheal intubation)

**Risk Factors:**
* Difficult laryngoscopy requiring multiple attempts
* Poor preexisting dentition
* Periodontal disease
* Loose or mobile teeth
* Using maxillary incisors as fulcrum
* Emergency cases (often lack mouth guard)

**Most Affected:** Upper central incisors (left > right)

**Types of Injury:**
* Enamel fractures (most common)
* Avulsion (50% of injuries)
* Crown fractures
* Subluxation/luxation

**Prevention:**
* Preoperative dental examination and documentation
* Risk stratification for difficult intubation
* Modified Macintosh blade reduces contact by >80%
* Mouthguards in high-risk patients
* Avoid using teeth as fulcrum
* Document post-intubation dental status

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Dental injury accounts for ~22% of anesthesiology medicolegal claims. Thorough preoperative dental assessment and documentation provides both patient safety and medicolegal protection.

## 6.9. RECOVERY AND DISCHARGE

### 6.9.1. FIRST-STAGE RECOVERY

* Patient awake with protective airway reflexes returned
* Control of nausea, vomiting, and postoperative pain achieved
* One-to-one observation by trained staff
* Careful inspection for ongoing bleeding

### 6.9.2. DISCHARGE CRITERIA (DAY CASE)

* Stable vital signs
* Control of pain and PONV
* Ability to stand and walk unaided
* Reasonable resumption of daily activities
* Bleeding controlled

**Paediatric-Specific:**
* Child able to remain awake for at least 20 minutes in quiet environment
* If reversal agents used (flumazenil, naloxone): extended observation required
* Re-sedation risk must be considered

### 6.9.3. REQUIRED FOR DISCHARGE

* Responsible adult escort
* Transport arranged
* 24-hour supervision at home
* Verbal and written postoperative instructions
* Warning signs of complications communicated
* Contact information for out-of-hours care

### 6.9.4. POSTOPERATIVE INSTRUCTIONS

* Soft diet initially
* Avoid hot foods/drinks while numb
* Bleeding management (bite on gauze)
* Pain management
* Signs requiring medical attention

## 6.10. SPECIAL POPULATIONS

### 6.10.1. PATIENTS WITH LEARNING DISABILITIES

* **Mental capacity assessment mandatory**
* Informed consent requirements per Mental Capacity Act 2005
* "Best interests" meetings required for those lacking capacity
* May require clinical holding
* Reasonable adjustments to standard care

### 6.10.2. CONSENT REQUIREMENTS

**Mandatory Information:**
* Nature of proposed dental and anaesthetic care
* Material risks including perioperative mortality and morbidity
* Reasonable alternatives to GA
* Possibility of more extensive treatment than anticipated
* Risk of tooth loss or edentulous outcome
* Need for clinical holding if anticipated

**Bottom Line:** Dental anaesthesia requires hospital setting, careful patient selection (autism and learning disability are most common indications), nasotracheal intubation with coordination for shared airway, vigilance for dental damage (most common medicolegal complaint), and special attention to consent in vulnerable populations.
