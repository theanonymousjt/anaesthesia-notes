### 1.2. BASIC MECHANISM AND CLASSIFICATION

* **Definition:** Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are among the most commonly used drugs worldwide due to their anti-inflammatory, analgesic, and antipyretic effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Mechanism of Action:** Inhibit cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes → block conversion of arachidonic acid to PGH2 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **COX Enzyme Structure:**
  * Two active sites: (1) COX site and (2) Peroxidase site [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * NSAIDs bind to the COX site to exert their effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Classification by Chemical Group:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * **Salicylates:** Aspirin
  * **Acetic Acid Derivatives:** Diclofenac, ketorolac, indomethacin
  * **Anthralinic Acids:** Mefenamic acid
  * **Pyrazolones:** Phenylbutazone
  * **Proprionic Acids:** Ibuprofen, naproxen
  * **Para-aminophenols:** Paracetamol (acetaminophen)
  * **Oxicams:** Tenoxicam, piroxicam, meloxicam (preferential COX-2)
  * **Pyrazole:** Parecoxib, celecoxib, (rofecoxib)
  * **Methylsulfone:** Etoricoxib
  * **Phenylacetic Acid Derivative:** Lumiracoxib

### 1.3. COX ISOFORMS AND FUNCTIONS

* **COX-1 (Constitutive):**
  * Produces prostaglandins for "house-keeping" functions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Maintains gastric protection and hemostasis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Widespread throughout body including gut and platelets [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Responsible for production of prostaglandins that control renal blood flow and form the protective gastric mucosal barrier [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Mediates synthesis of thromboxane in platelets [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **COX-2 (Inducible):**
  * Mediates pain, inflammation, fever, and carcinogenesis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Produced in response to inflammation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Binding site can accommodate larger molecules (basis for COX-2 selective agents) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Mediates production of prostacyclin (PGI2) in vascular endothelium [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **COX-3:**
  * Centrally located, similar to COX-1 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * May be responsible for paracetamol's effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Variant of COX-1 that exists centrally, possibly the mechanism by which paracetamol reduces pain and pyrexia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Molecular Differences:** The main molecular difference between COX-1 and COX-2 lies in the substitution of isoleucine for valine, which allows access to a hydrophobic side pocket that acts as an alternative specific binding site for drugs [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl:** COX selectivity is relative, not absolute [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

### 1.4. PAIN PATHWAY MECHANISMS

* **Peripheral Effects:**
  * Prostaglandin E2: Key mediator of peripheral/central pain sensitization [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Prostaglandins indirectly cause pain by sensitizing nociceptors to other mediators (histamine, bradykinin) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Central Effects:** Prostaglandins enhance pain transmission by:
  * ↑ Release of substance P and glutamate from first-order pain neurons [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * ↑ Sensitivity of second-order pain neurons [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Inhibiting neurotransmitters from descending pain-modulating pathways [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Prostaglandin Formation** (see Figure 10.4) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * Membrane phospholipids → Phospholipase A2 (inhibited by steroids) → Arachidonic acid
  * Arachidonic acid can follow:
	* Lipoxygenase pathway → Leukotrienes
	* Cyclooxygenase pathway (inhibited by NSAIDs) → Cyclic endoperoxidases →
	  * Thromboxane A2 (platelets)
	  * PGI2/prostacyclin (vascular endothelium)
	  * Prostaglandins PGE2, PGF2α, PGD2 (widespread)

### 1.5. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS IN PERIOPERATIVE SETTING

* **Effectiveness:** Proven efficacy for postoperative pain [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Opioid-Sparing Effects:** ↓ Opioid requirements and opioid-related side effects (PONV, sedation) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Analgesic Ceiling:** Unlike opioids, NSAIDs exhibit a maximum analgesic effect [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* 💡 NSAIDs are key components in multimodal analgesia regimens [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Uses of NSAIDs:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Anti-inflammatory
  * Analgesia
  * Antipyretic
  * Uricosuric (promote urinary excretion of uric acid)
* **Drug Interactions** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 
  * Caution with anticoagulants such as heparin or warfarin (NSAIDs may displace warfarin from protein binding sites)
  * May increase serum lithium levels
  * Highly protein-bound NSAIDs can displace other protein-bound drugs

**Bottom Line:** NSAIDs work by inhibiting cyclooxygenase enzymes, preventing prostaglandin synthesis. COX-1 maintains homeostasis while COX-2 mediates inflammation. These drugs provide effective perioperative analgesia with opioid-sparing effects, though they have a ceiling effect unlike opioids.

#### 1.6.1. KETOROLAC

* **Classification:** Parenteral nonselective NSAID [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Drug Class:** Acetic acid derivative [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Dosing:**
  * 30 mg IV initially followed by 15-30 mg q6-8h, not to exceed 5 days [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Maximum daily dose (MDD) = 120 mg [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* ⚠️ **Dose Adjustment:** Reduce dose in elderly (>65 years) and in renal failure patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Clinical Use:** Commonly employed in multimodal perioperative pain management [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Half-life:** 6 hours [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Properties:** Potent analgesic activity but limited anti-inflammatory activity; also potent antipyretic [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* ⚠️ **Special Consideration:** Hypovolemia should be corrected before administration [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

#### 1.6.2. MELOXICAM

* **Classification:** COX-2 preferential NSAID [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Drug Class:** Enolic acid (oxicam) derivative [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **FDA Approval:** 2020 [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Dosing:**
  * 30 mg IV once daily [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Initial oral dose is 7.5 mg/day, which may be doubled [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Clinical Use:** Perioperative pain management [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Half-life:** 15-20 hours [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **COX-2 Selectivity:** Similar to celecoxib, 3-50 times as potent against COX-2 than COX-1 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Side Effect Profile:** Reduced GI side-effect profile compared with diclofenac at dose of 7.5 mg/day; renal side-effect profile similar to other NSAIDs [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 1.6.3. CELECOXIB (CELEBREX)

* **Classification:** COX-2 specific inhibitor [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Current Status:** Only COX-2 specific inhibitor available in the US [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Dosing:**
  * 400 mg PO loading dose, then 200 mg PO q12h for several days [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Maximum daily dose (MDD) = 400 mg [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Used to a maximum dose of 200 mg bd for chronic pain associated with osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Half-life:** 11 hours [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **COX-1:COX-2 Inhibitory Ratio:** 1:30 (30x more selective for COX-2 than COX-1) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* ⚠️ **Contraindications:** Avoid in patients with coronary heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, or in patients allergic to sulfonamides [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **CLASS Trial Results:** Incidence of ulcer complications with celecoxib was similar to non-specific NSAIDs, though this may have been confounded by concurrent aspirin therapy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Formulation:** Presented as 100 mg tablets [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 1.6.4. DISCONTINUED COX-2 INHIBITORS

* Rofecoxib (Vioxx) and Valdecoxib (Bextra) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Reason for Recall:** Adverse cardiovascular risks [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Rofecoxib History:** First licensed COX-2 inhibitor, prescribed to 80 million patients over 5 years (annual revenue $2.5 billion) prior to withdrawal in 2004 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

##### 1.6.5.7. COX-2 AGENTS

* **COX-2 Preferential:** Meloxicam [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **COX-2 Specific:** Celecoxib, Etoricoxib, Valdecoxib, Parecoxib [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

##### 1.6.5.8. PARECOXIB AND VALDECOXIB 

* Parecoxib is a prodrug converted to active valdecoxib [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Parecoxib plasma half-life: 20 minutes; no actions of its own [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Valdecoxib withdrawn (April 2005) due to serious dermatological side effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* COX-1:COX-2 inhibitory ratio: 1:61 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Parecoxib is a parenteral COX-2 antagonist requiring reconstitution with 0.9% saline [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Initial dose: 40 mg followed by 20-40 mg 6-12 hourly (max 80 mg/day) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Hypersensitivity reactions including exfoliative dermatitis, Stevens-Johnson syndrome reported with valdecoxib [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

##### 1.6.5.9. ETORICOXIB 

* Highly selective COX-2 inhibitor with UK license but not US approval [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Chemical class: Methylsulfone [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* COX-1:COX-2 inhibitory ratio: 1:344 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Effective for pain control compared to COX-1 inhibitors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Oral bioavailability: >95% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Volume of distribution: 1.5 L/kg; 90% plasma protein-bound [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Elimination half-life: 22 hours [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Metabolized via CYP3A4; half-life increases in hepatic failure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* GI irritation similar to placebo and significantly lower than COX-1 inhibitors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* Similar cardiovascular risk profile to diclofenac [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

**Bottom Line:** Multiple NSAID agents are available for perioperative pain management, with various routes of administration. Ketorolac is the most commonly used parenteral NSAID, while celecoxib offers COX-2 selectivity. Dosing adjustments are necessary in elderly and renal patients. COX-2 selective inhibitors carry cardiovascular risk, leading to the discontinuation of some agents.

#### 1.7.1. COMMON SIDE EFFECTS

* **Platelet Dysfunction:** Altered hemostasis, especially with nonselective NSAIDs [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Reduced production of cyclic endoperoxidases and thromboxane A2 prevents platelet aggregation and vasoconstriction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Effects of aspirin last for platelet lifespan (up to 14 days) because platelets cannot generate new COX and enzyme inhibition is irreversible [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * COX-2 inhibitors have no effect on platelet function even at high doses [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **GI Ulceration:** Dyspepsia from inhibition of protective prostaglandins [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Intestinal erosions not limited to the stomach are common during prolonged administration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Can range from mild pain to iron deficiency anemia and fatal hemorrhage [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Mucosal protection involves multiple elements (mucous layer, bicarbonate secretion, rapid cell turnover, abundant blood supply) that are prostaglandin-dependent [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Upper GI Bleeding:** From mucosal effects and inhibition of platelet aggregation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **GI Risk Hierarchy:**
  * Ketorolac and piroxicam: High risk [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
  * Diclofenac and naproxen: Medium risk [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Ibuprofen (<1.2 g/day): Lowest risk among non-selective NSAIDs [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * COX-2 inhibitors: Lowest risk overall [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

#### 1.7.2. RENAL EFFECTS

* ⚠️ **Nephrotoxicity Risk Factors:** Increased in hypovolemia, CHF, chronic renal insufficiency [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Mechanism:** Prostaglandins regulate renal blood flow, natriuresis, and GFR [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Renally produced prostaglandins (PGE2 and PGI2) are essential in maintaining adequate renal perfusion when circulating vasoconstrictors (renin, angiotensin, noradrenaline) are high [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * NSAIDs may alter this balance, reducing renal perfusion and potentially causing acute renal failure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Consequences:** Both traditional NSAIDs and COX-2 inhibitors can cause fluid retention and hypertension [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Special Risk:** COX-2 selective inhibitors may have increased renal effects [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* ⚠️ **Contraindications:** Avoid in patients with:
  * Reduced creatinine clearance [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Dependency on renal prostaglandin release for vasodilation (to avoid hemodynamically mediated AKI) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * High-risk conditions: hypovolemia, heart failure, cirrhosis, diabetic nephropathy, hypercalcemia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Aspirin Effects on Uric Acid** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
  * At low doses (<2 g/day): Urate retention due to inhibited tubular secretion
  * At higher doses (>5 g/day): Uricosuric as reabsorption of urates is inhibited more than secretion
* **NSAID Nephropathy:** Can develop after chronic aspirin use, leading to fluid overload and heart failure [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Features include papillary necrosis and interstitial fibrosis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 1.7.3. CARDIOVASCULAR CONCERNS

* 💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Authors do not recommend COX-2 inhibitors for patients with known CAD or cerebrovascular disease [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **COX Balance Effects:**
  * COX-1 decreases thrombosis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * COX-2 increases risk of heart attack, thrombosis, and stroke [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Possible MI Risk:** Increased risk with COX-2 inhibitors [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Diclofenac (150 mg/day) and ibuprofen (2.4 g/day) have similar risk profiles to COX-2 inhibitors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * For 1,000 patients receiving these drugs for 1 year, there will be three extra coronary events, one of which will be fatal [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * More recent warnings state that there is no duration of NSAID use that is without increased risk [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Stroke Risk:** No statistically significant increase in stroke risk [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Risk appears higher in younger men and those with prior history of stroke or TIA [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **UK Committee on Safety of Medicines Advice:** COX-2 use contraindicated in patients with ischemic heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, mild heart failure, and peripheral vascular disease [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 1.7.4. ORTHOPEDIC CONSIDERATIONS

* **Bone Healing:** Both COX-1 and COX-2 play significant roles in bone fusion following fracture [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Traditional NSAIDs:** Known to inhibit healing process, particularly after lumbar spinal fusion [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **COX-2 Inhibitors:** Effect on bone fusion remains controversial [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

#### 1.7.5. RESPIRATORY EFFECTS

* **Bronchospasm:** Can trigger bronchospasm in 20% of asthmatic patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Usually middle-aged people with brittle asthma or nasal polyps [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Due to increased production of leukotrienes from arachidonic acid [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Associated with chronic rhinitis and nasal polyps; children are relatively spared [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * By inhibiting cyclo-oxygenase, more arachidonic acid is converted to leukotrienes which cause bronchospasm [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Aspirin also causes abnormal platelet reaction in susceptible patients, releasing cytotoxic mediators [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 1.7.6. OTHER ADVERSE EFFECTS 

* **Hepatotoxicity:** Normally observed after prolonged or excessive NSAID use [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Up to 15% of patients may experience elevated serum transaminase levels, even after short courses [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 1.7.7. CONTRAINDICATIONS TO NSAID USE

* **Absolute Contraindications:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Allergy
  * Pre-eclampsia
  * Uncontrolled hypertension
  * Severe asthma or known NSAID-sensitive asthma
  * Severe hemorrhage
  * Renal injury
* **Relative Contraindications:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Elderly
  * Diabetes
  * Vascular disease
  * Post-operatively for cardiac, hepatobiliary, or renal surgery

**Bottom Line:** NSAIDs carry significant side effect risks including platelet dysfunction, GI ulceration, nephrotoxicity, and cardiovascular concerns. Use with caution in patients with renal impairment, cardiovascular disease, or asthma. Orthopedic healing may also be impaired with NSAID use.

### 1.8. PHARMACOKINETICS OF COX INHIBITORS

#### 1.8.1. ABSORPTION

* **Oral Administration:** Peak blood concentration in <3 hours [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Alternative Delivery:** Available as LA "cocktail" for injection around surgical sites and arthroplasty sites (e.g., ketorolac) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **General Properties:** NSAIDs are highly protein-bound in plasma and have low volumes of distribution [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Route of Administration:** Usually oral or rectal, though some agents may be given intravenously (diclofenac, ketorolac, parecoxib) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 1.8.2. DISTRIBUTION

* **Lipid Solubility:** Can permeate blood-brain barrier → produces central analgesia and antipyresis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Joint Penetration:** Most NSAIDs can penetrate joint spaces (for anti-inflammatory effect) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Exception:** Acetaminophen has limited joint space penetration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Protein Binding:** Most NSAIDs are highly protein-bound [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Ibuprofen: Over 90% protein bound [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Diclofenac: 99% protein bound [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Small volume of distribution [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Drug Interactions:** Effects of other highly protein-bound drugs (e.g., warfarin) may be potentiated as they become displaced [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 1.8.3. METABOLISM

* **Primary route:** Hepatic biotransformation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Specific Pathways:**
  * Ibuprofen: S enantiomer is active, R enantiomer largely converted to S enantiomer in the gut and liver [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Diclofenac: Hydroxylation in the liver followed by conjugation to inactive metabolites [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* ⚠️ **Acetaminophen Toxicity:** Can cause fulminant hepatic failure at high doses [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **General Pattern:** Most NSAIDs are metabolized in the liver and excreted in inactive form [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 1.8.4. EXCRETION

* **Primary Route:** Most NSAIDs undergo renal excretion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Secondary Routes:** Some have biliary excretion
  * Diclofenac: Up to 40% excreted in the bile [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Combined Routes:** Many NSAIDs are excreted in both urine and bile [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

**Bottom Line:** NSAIDs generally have good oral absorption with high protein binding and primarily hepatic metabolism. They cross the blood-brain barrier and penetrate joint spaces effectively. Most are eliminated via renal excretion, with some undergoing biliary elimination.

### 1.9. ASPIRIN

#### 1.9.1. UNIQUE CHARACTERISTICS

* **Chemical Structure:** Acetylsalicylic acid, a salicylate compound [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

![Aspirin.png](NONOPIOID_NONOPIOID_Aspirin.png_7973e0.png_d43f68.png)

* **Mechanism:** Irreversibly inhibits COX-1 by acetylating a serine residue in the enzyme [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Duration:** Nearly 1-week persistence of clinical effects (e.g., inhibition of platelet aggregation) after discontinuation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* 🔑 **HY:** Only NSAID that irreversibly inhibits platelet function [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Low-Dose Mechanism:** Selectively inhibits platelet cyclo-oxygenase while preserving vessel wall cyclo-oxygenase, reducing TXA2-induced vasoconstriction and platelet aggregation while leaving vessel wall synthesis of prostaglandins unaltered [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 1.9.2. CLINICAL USES

* **Pain Management:** Used for mild to moderate pain [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Antipyretic:** Reduces fever by inhibiting prostaglandin E1 in the brain [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Anti-inflammatory:** At higher doses (>3-4g/day) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Antiplatelet:** Low-dose (75-100 mg) used for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular protection [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Obstetric:** Used in pregnancy for eclampsia and pre-eclampsia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Pediatric:** Used in specific conditions like juvenile arthritis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* ⚠️ **Caution:** Because of the risk of Reye syndrome, avoid the use of aspirin in children <12 years old [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Presentation and Dosing:**
  * Tablets: 75mg and 300mg [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Standard adult dose: 500-1,000 mg q4-6h, MDD is 4,000 mg [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

#### 1.9.3. PHARMACOKINETICS

* **Absorption:**
  * Well absorbed from the stomach as it is a weak acid with a pKa of 3, unionized in the stomach [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Even faster absorption in the proximal duodenum due to increased surface area [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Peak plasma levels occur around 1.5-2 hours after ingestion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * 80-100% bioavailability [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Salicylate ions may become trapped in mucosal cells due to their alkaline nature [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Distribution:**
  * Aspirin itself does not significantly bind to plasma proteins [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Main metabolite, salicylate, is 50-90% protein bound, mainly to albumin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Aspirin has ability to acetylate molecules like DNA, hemoglobin, and proteins [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Metabolism:**
  * Undergoes hydrolysis in plasma to form salicylic acid [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Salicylate undergoes hepatic metabolism via CYP2C19, CYP3A4 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Half-life:
	* Aspirin: 0.25 hours (15 minutes) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* Salicylate: 2-3 hours at low doses, 15-30 hours at high doses [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
	* This dose-dependent half-life is clinically significant [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Salicylate is rapidly hydrolyzed by intestinal and hepatic esterases to salicylate, which undergoes further hepatic metabolism to salicyluric acid and glucuronide derivatives [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
  * Elimination half-life varies because glycine conjugation (converting salicylate to salicyluric acid) may become saturated in overdose resulting in zero-order kinetics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Excretion:**
  * Salicylate and salicyluric acid are renally excreted [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Excretion enhanced under alkaline conditions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

#### 1.9.4. SIDE EFFECTS AND TOXICITY

* **Respiratory Effects:**
  * Direct stimulation of respiratory center, causing hyperventilation in overdose [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Uncouples oxidative phosphorylation, increasing oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
  * Initially minute ventilation increases to keep PaCO2 static, but at high levels, respiratory center is directly stimulated causing respiratory alkalosis, later complicated by metabolic acidosis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
  * In children, the respiratory center is depressed by rising levels, and metabolic acidosis occurs earlier, leading to mixed respiratory and metabolic acidosis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
* **Cardiovascular Effects:**
  * Inhibits vessel vasodilation at higher doses [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Hematological Effects:**
  * Bleeding due to irreversible platelet inhibition [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Gastrointestinal Effects:**
  * Mucosal ulceration and potential bleeding [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Renal Effects:**
  * Reduces renal perfusion [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Inhibits renal excretion of urate at low doses, inhibits reabsorption at higher doses (uricosuric) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Metabolic Effects:**
  * Uncouples oxidative phosphorylation, increasing O2 consumption and CO2 production [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Specific Concern:**
  * ⚠️ Can trigger Reye syndrome in children - a rare hepatic and neurological syndrome with up to 40% mortality [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Characterized by encephalopathy, cerebral edema, hepatic failure, and mitochondrial failure [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Causes widespread mitochondrial damage, fatty changes in liver progressing to hepatic failure, encephalopathy with cerebral edema [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
  * Aspirin only recommended for children below 12 years when specifically indicated (e.g., juvenile arthritis/Still's disease) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .

#### 1.9.5. ASPIRIN OVERDOSE MANAGEMENT 

* **Recognition:**
  * Thorough history [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Can mimic pneumonia, meningitis, and general sepsis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Clinical Features:**
  * **Common Features** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 
	* Usually conscious (only unconscious in massive overdose)
	* Sweaty
	* Tinnitus
	* Blurred vision
	* Tachycardia
	* Pyrexia
	* Hyperventilation
	* Respiratory alkalosis (later complicated by metabolic acidosis)
  * **Rare Features** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 
	* Nausea, vomiting, epigastric pain
	* Oliguria
	* GI bleed
	* Pulmonary edema (due to increased capillary permeability)
	* Coagulopathy
	* Hypokalemia
	* Hypoor hyperglycemia
	* Encephalopathy, unconsciousness
  * **CNS:** Tinnitus, blurred vision, hallucinations, confusion, seizures and coma if severe [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * **Cardiovascular:** Tachycardia and hypotension due to dehydration and uncoupled phosphorylation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * **GI:** Nausea and vomiting due to stimulation of chemoreceptor trigger zone, GI bleeding if severe [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * **Respiratory:** Hyperventilation, pulmonary edema and ARDS if severe [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * **Renal:** Oliguria, hypokalaemia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * **Metabolic:** High anion-gap metabolic acidosis, pyrexia, glycemic derangement [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * **Lethality:** Lethal dose can be as low as 150mg per kilogram [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * **Chronicity:** Chronic toxicity (25% mortality) is worse than acute toxicity (2% mortality) because tissue levels are far higher [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Treatment:**
  * **Airway and Breathing:** If intubation needed, hyperventilate to counteract metabolic acidosis, aim for blood pH of 7.5 [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * **Circulation:** Correct hypovolemia with cool IV fluids [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
	* Use isotonic bicarbonate if pH <7.5 [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
	* Use plasmalyte/Hartmann's if pH >7.5 [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
	* ⚠️ Normal saline will worsen acidosis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * **Disability:** Measure and correct blood glucose and monitor GCS if not intubated [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
	* Even if blood glucose normal, consider IV dextrose if symptomatic [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * **Specific Management:**
	* Measure salicylate level and correlate with time of ingestion [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
	* Consider activated charcoal if >150mg/kg ingested [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
	* Urinary alkalinization with sodium bicarbonate (50ml 8.4% over 10 mins) to increase renal excretion of salicylate [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
	* Replace potassium [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
	* Aim for urine pH >7.5 [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
	* Consider hemodialysis for:
	  * Neurological symptoms or pulmonary edema [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
	  * pH <7.2 [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
	  * High salicylate level (>7.0mmol/liter) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
	* Standard treatment [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 
	  * Activated charcoal
	  * Forced alkaline diuresis
	  * Hemofiltration/hemodialysis

**Bottom Line:** Aspirin uniquely provides irreversible inhibition of platelet function, making it valuable for cardiovascular protection but requiring caution with perioperative timing. Its toxicity can be severe, affecting multiple organ systems and requiring prompt recognition and management, including alkalinization and potentially hemodialysis.

## 2. ACETAMINOPHEN (PARACETAMOL)

Part B Reference: C2 Pain Management (Page 9)

### 2.2. MECHANISM AND PROPERTIES

* **Classification:** Para-aminophenol derivative derived from acetanilide with a phenol group [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

![Paracetamol.png](NONOPIOID_NONOPIOID_Paracetamol.png_012f6d.png_5c7f61.png)

* **Effects:** Analgesic and antipyretic properties, minimal anti-inflammatory effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Primary Mechanism:** Centrally acting COX inhibitor with minimal peripheral effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Unique Action:** Does not enter active site of COX enzyme or bind to COX site [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Instead prevents COX activation by reducing heme at the peroxidase site [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Despite reducing COX-dependent prostaglandin production, paracetamol doesn't have significant anti-inflammatory effect, likely due to indirect action [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * May work by preventing COX-3 oxidation, which is easier in the brain where peroxidase is very low, but not in inflamed tissues where peroxidase is high [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Alternative Classification:** While paracetamol has essentially no effect on cyclooxygenase in vitro, it has been classified as a NSAID due to its moderate analgesic and antipyretic properties [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Antipyretic Action:** Likely due to inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis within the CNS by inhibition of COX-3, a COX-1 variant [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

### 2.3. ADDITIONAL MECHANISMS

* **Serotonergic Pathways:** Stimulates descending serotoninergic pathways [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * This means paracetamol can be partly inhibited by 5HT-3 inhibitors such as ondansetron [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Other Pathways:** May also act on:
  * Opioid receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Cannabinoid receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * TRPV1 receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * NMDA receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * TNFα and nitric oxide pathways [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **In brain:** May modulate endogenous cannabinoid/vanilloid receptor systems [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

### 2.4. PHARMACOKINETICS

* **Absorption:**
  * Well absorbed orally [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Bioavailability 80% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Absorbed well from small bowel [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Distribution:**
  * 10% protein bound [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Larger volume of distribution than NSAIDs [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Unlike other NSAIDs, does not cause gastric irritation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Metabolism:**
  * Primarily hepatic via glucuronidation and sulphation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * These pathways can be saturated [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Secondary pathway: Formation of N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine (NAPQI) - highly hepatotoxic [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * NAPQI is usually conjugated with glutathione and only becomes a problem in overdose when glutathione reserves are exhausted [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Metabolized by liver mainly to glucuronide conjugates but also to sulfate and cysteine conjugates [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .
* **Excretion:**
  * Elimination T1/2 is actually 1-2H, thus minimal interval between doses is 6H.
	  * 4H is seen in the nomogram because it is toxic dose that saturates the CYP450 system
  * Renal excretion of metabolites [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Conjugates are actively excreted in the urine, only a small fraction excreted unchanged [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] .

### 2.5. ADVANTAGES OVER NSAIDS

* No gastrointestinal ulceration [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* No impaired platelet function [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* No adverse cardiorenal effects [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* No impairment of bone fusion after orthopedic procedures [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

### 2.6. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS

* **Multimodal Analgesia:** Opioid-sparing effects [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Combination Therapy:** Superior analgesia when combined with an NSAID vs. either drug alone [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Equivalence:** 2g oral acetaminophen ≈ 200mg celecoxib in adults [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Dosing:**
  * 1g QDS standard adult dose [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * 15mg/kg if less than 50kg up to 60mg/kg/day [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Pediatric dose: 15-30mg/kg loading dose then 10-15mg/kg every 4 hours, max 90mg/kg/day [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Maximum daily dose (MDD) in the healthy adult is 4,000 mg [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Presentations:**
  * 500mg tablets [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * 500mg tablets with weak opioids such as codeine [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Suppositories 125mg and 1g [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Liquid 120mg/5ml [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Solution for injection 1g or 500mg [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * A preparation containing 100 mg methionine and 500 mg paracetamol is available at increased cost [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
* ⚠️ **Cautions:** Dose adjustment required below 50kg body weight and hepatic impairment [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

### 2.7. ACETAMINOPHEN (OFIRMEV)

* **US Release:** November 2010 [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Formulation:** 1g (1,000 mg/100 mL) infusion [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Administration:** Does not require reconstitution, can infuse via peripheral IV over 15 minutes [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Cost Consideration:** Oral dosing may be more cost-effective with equivalent analgesic effect [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Best Candidates:** Patients unable to tolerate oral intake or with recent abdominal surgery/delayed gastric absorption [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Intravenous dosing guidelines:**
  * Adult >50 kg: 1 g q4–6h not to exceed 4 g/d [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Adult <50 kg: 15 mg/kg q4–6h not to exceed 3 g/d [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Pedi >33 kg: 15 mg/kg q4–6h not to exceed 3 g/d [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Pedi 10–33 kg: 15 mg/kg q4–6h not to exceed 2 g/d [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * ⚠️ Hepatotoxicity can occur in chronic alcoholics receiving therapeutic doses [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Administer intravenous formulation over 15 min [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

### 2.8. TOXICITY AND MANAGEMENT  

* **Epidemiology:** Most common cause of acute hepatic injury in the UK, either due to intentional overdose or accidental in chronic pain patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Mechanism:** Saturation of main metabolic pathway → production of NAPQI → rapid exhaustion of liver glutathione stores [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Leads to acute centrilobular hepatic necrosis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Following toxic dose, normal hepatic conjugation pathways become saturated, more NAPQI is produced which rapidly exhausts hepatic glutathione [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
  * NAPQI forms covalent bonds with sulfhydryl groups on hepatocytes resulting in cell death [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
* **Clinical Features:**
  * Often difficult to detect initially - few early symptoms besides possible vomiting [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * After 24 hours: Right upper quadrant pain, deranged liver function and clotting [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * After 72 hours: Deterioration to fulminant hepatic failure and multiple organ failure if severe [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Other features: Hyperglycemia, hemolytic anemia, distributive shock, cholestasis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Features from supplementary source include [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 
	* Patient usually remains conscious
	* Nausea and vomiting
	* Epigastric pain
	* Sweating
	* Erythema, urticaria, mucosal lesions
	* Acute hemolytic anemia
	* Peripheral vasodilation and shock after massive overdose
	* Delayed hyperglycemia
	* Hepatic failure after 48 hours
	* Liver function and clotting worst at 3-5 days
	* Cholestasis
	* Fulminant hepatic failure at 3-7 days
* **Treatment:**
  * **General:** ABC resuscitation, full set of bloods (particularly clotting and LFTs) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * **Specific:**
	* Use paracetamol treatment nomogram to guide treatment with N-acetylcysteine (NAC) [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].
	* Give NAC if:
	  * Staggered overdose or unknown time of ingestion [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].
	  * Serum paracetamol level above nomogram at designated time since ingestion [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].
	  * Evidence of liver injury [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].
	* NAC dosing:
	  * 150mg/kg over first hour [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].
	  * 50mg/kg over 4 hours [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].
	  * 100mg/kg over 16 hours [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].
	  * Generally very safe, some risk of hypersensitivity reaction [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].
	* Activated charcoal if single large dose ingested less than an hour ago [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].
	* Last option is liver transplant - needs discussion with liver center if clotting not improving with NAC [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].
	* NAC acts as a glutathione donor [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].
	* Treatment from supplementary source [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]] 
	  * Activated charcoal within 1 hour of ingestion
	  * Early intravenous acetylcysteine
	  * Fluids and antiemetics
	  * Early referral to specialist center
	* Oral methionine and oral/intravenous acetylcysteine directed at replenishing hepatic glutathione [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]] .
	* Methionine enhances glutathione synthesis while acetylcysteine is hydrolyzed to cysteine, a glutathione precursor [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]] .
	* IV acetylcysteine preferred as vomiting is common in overdose [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]] .

**Bottom Line:** Acetaminophen (paracetamol) is a centrally-acting analgesic with minimal peripheral effects, making it valuable in multimodal analgesia with fewer systemic side effects than NSAIDs. The IV formulation provides flexibility for perioperative use. Overdose can cause severe hepatotoxicity requiring prompt recognition and N-acetylcysteine treatment.

### 3.2. CLASSIFICATION AND DEVELOPMENT

* **Classification:**
  * α2-δ subunit calcium channel ligands [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Two main agents: **GABAPENTIN** (Neurontin) and **PREGABALIN** (Lyrica) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Historical Development:**
  * Initially introduced as Anti-Epileptic Drugs (AEDs) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Later discovered to have analgesic properties [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * No evidence that one agent is predictably more efficacious than the other [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

### 3.3. MECHANISM OF ACTION

* **Primary Target:** Binding to voltage-gated calcium channels and NMDA receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Not GABA-Related:** Despite structural similarities to GABA, do not act through GABA receptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Two Antinociceptive Mechanisms:**
  1. Modulation of calcium-induced release of glutamate in dorsal horn [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  2. Activation of descending noradrenergic pathways in spinal cord and brain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

![image.png](NONOPIOID_NONOPIOID_image.png_12df08.png_5b5022.png)

### 3.4. PHARMACOKINETICS

* **GABAPENTIN:**
  * Absorbed only in duodenum through saturable transport system [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Non-linear pharmacokinetics (increasing dose → decreasing bioavailability) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Absorption impaired by antacids including Bicitra [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **PREGABALIN:**
  * Absorbed throughout small intestine via non-saturable transport [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Linear pharmacokinetic profile (dose-independent absorption) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * More potent than gabapentin [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
* **Both drugs:**
  * Renally excreted [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Minimal liver metabolism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

### 3.5. CLINICAL USES

* **Treatment of partial-onset seizures** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Neuropathic pain** (e.g., postherpetic neuralgia) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Chronic pain states** (e.g., fibromyalgia) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Perioperative pain management** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Component of multimodal postoperative pain protocols** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Note:** Adequate trial of gabapentin may require up to a month; pregabalin requires less time due to higher potency [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

### 3.6. BENEFITS IN PERIOPERATIVE SETTING

* **Decreased postoperative pain** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Reduced opioid requirements** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Decreased incidence of:**
  * Postoperative delirium [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Vomiting [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Pruritus [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Urinary retention [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Prevention of surgery-induced central sensitization** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **May prevent transition from acute to chronic pain** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

### 3.7. DOSING GUIDELINES

* **GABAPENTIN:**
  * Opioid-naïve patients: ≤300 mg orally preoperatively [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Higher doses only for opioid-dependent patients or those at risk for chronic postsurgical pain [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **PREGABALIN:**
  * Dosing should be adjusted based on patient factors and surgical procedure [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

### 3.8. SIDE EFFECTS AND CONSIDERATIONS

* **Common Side Effects:**
  * Sedation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Headache [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Dizziness [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Visual disturbances [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* ⚠️ **Risk of postoperative respiratory depression** (especially when combined with opioids) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Clinical Considerations:**
  * Should not be combined with preoperative sustained-release opioids [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Used in many ERAS protocols as single dose or several doses perioperatively [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Meta-analysis (281 trials, 24,682 patients): associated with lower pain scores at 6 hours postoperatively, but difference not clinically significant [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * 💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Role as regular adjunct for optimizing perioperative pain control remains inconclusive [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Gabapentinoids (gabapentin and pregabalin) are calcium channel ligands with proven effectiveness for neuropathic pain and some benefit in perioperative settings. They can reduce opioid requirements and associated side effects but may cause sedation and respiratory depression when combined with opioids, requiring careful dosing and monitoring.

#### 4.2.1. MECHANISM OF ACTION

  2. Opioid-tolerant patients undergoing any surgery [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  3. Patients with opioid tolerance with sickle cell crisis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  4. Patients with OSA (Obstructive Sleep Apnea) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Contraindications:**
  * Poorly controlled cardiovascular disease [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Pregnancy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Active psychosis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Severe liver disease [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

#### 4.2.5. SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS

* **Synergistic Effects:** May act synergistically when combined with opioids [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **PCA Limitations:** Not recommended as sole IV agent in PCA for routine use [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Refractory Pain:** For morphine-resistant pain: 250 μg/kg ketamine + 15 μg/kg morphine as bolus dose is effective [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Low-dose ketamine is a valuable adjunct for perioperative analgesia, particularly for opioid-tolerant patients and those undergoing painful procedures. Its NMDA antagonism can reduce opioid requirements and improve pain control without significant complications when used at appropriate subanesthetic doses.

### 4.3. DEXTROMETHORPHAN

#### 4.3.1. MECHANISM AND CLASSIFICATION

* **Administration Routes:**
  * Oral [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

  * Metabolized to dextrorphan [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Most side effects come from this metabolite [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

#### 4.3.3. EFFICACY IN SURGICAL SETTINGS

* **Preoperative Oral Administration:**
  * 150 mg orally preoperatively: Reduces PCA morphine requirements in abdominal hysterectomy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Intramuscular Administration:**
  * 120 mg intramuscular preincisional: Provides analgesia in elective upper abdominal surgery [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Postoperative Regimen:**
  * 200 mg orally every 8 hours: Modest reduction in morphine consumption after knee surgery [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

#### 4.3.4. LIMITATIONS

* **Physical Properties:** Can trigger histamine release [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Availability:** Generally only available in oral liquid form [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Comparative Efficacy:** Less commonly used than IV ketamine for moderate to severe perioperative pain [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Side Effects:** Nausea and vomiting (most common) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Dextromethorphan, while effective as an NMDA antagonist with analgesic properties, has limited use in perioperative pain management due to its availability primarily in oral formulations and its modest efficacy compared to ketamine. It may provide some benefit in reducing opioid requirements when administered preor perioperatively.

#### 5.2.1. CLASSIFICATION AND SELECTIVITY

* **Analgesia Mediated at Multiple Sites:**
  * Supraspinally (locus coeruleus) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Spinally (substantia gelatinosa) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].
  * Peripherally [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]].

#### 5.2.3. ADMINISTRATION ROUTES

* Oral [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* Transdermal [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* Intravenous [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* Perineural [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* Neuraxial [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

#### 5.2.4. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS

* **Premedication:**
  * 5 μg/kg oral before knee surgery decreases PCA morphine use and reduces postoperative nausea and vomiting [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Combined Administration:**
  * Combined oral (3-5 μg/kg) and transdermal (0.2 mg/24h) administration decreases postoperative PCA morphine requirement by 50% following prostatectomy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Intrathecal:**
  * 25 μg added to bupivacaine/morphine spinal anesthetic reduces postoperative morphine use and improves Was pain scores at 24 hours in TKA [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Perineural:**
  * 0.5-1.0 μg/kg with local anesthetic enhances efficacy and increases duration of blocks [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Multimodal Approach:**
  * Four-drug combination (with bupivacaine, buprenorphine, dexamethasone) provides >24 hours of postoperative analgesia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

#### 5.2.5. SIDE EFFECTS

* Sedation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* Hypotension [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* Bradycardia (if dose exceeds 150 μg) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Clonidine is a selective α2-adrenergic agonist with multiple routes of administration and proven efficacy as an analgesic adjunct. It can significantly reduce opioid requirements when used perioperatively but requires monitoring for hypotension and bradycardia, particularly at higher doses.

### 5.3. DEXMEDETOMIDINE

#### 5.3.1. PROPERTIES AND SELECTIVITY

7. ICU sedation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

#### 5.3.4. ICU BENEFITS

* Decreases CNS sympathetic outflow (dose-dependent) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* Provides sedation without significant respiratory depression [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* Opioid-sparing analgesia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* Not GABAergic (unlike propofol and midazolam) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* No anticholinergic effects [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* Promotes more physiologic sleep patterns [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* Attenuates neurocognitive impairment (delirium and agitation) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* Promotes early extubation and shorter ICU LOS [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

#### 5.3.5. ADDITIONAL BENEFITS

* Does not decrease gut motility [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* Prevents postoperative nausea/vomiting [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* Prevents shivering [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

#### 5.3.6. DOSING GUIDELINES

* **Intravenous:**
  * Loading dose: 1 μg/kg over 10-20 min [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

* **Intramuscular:** 2.5 μg/kg as premedication [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Spinal:** 0.1-0.2 μg/kg [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Epidural:** 1-2 μg/kg [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Peripheral nerve block:** 1 μg/kg [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Buccal:** 1-2 μg/kg [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Intranasal:** 1-2 μg/kg [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

#### 5.3.7. ADVERSE EFFECTS

* Bradycardia and hypotension (most frequent) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Can be treated with atropine, glycopyrrolate, and ephedrine [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Dexmedetomidine is a highly selective α2-adrenergic agonist with multiple clinical applications from ICU sedation to perioperative analgesia. It offers advantages including opioid-sparing effects, minimal respiratory depression, and reduced delirium, though bradycardia and hypotension require monitoring and potential treatment.

**Bottom Line:** Intravenous lidocaine can provide effective analgesia and anti-inflammatory effects in the perioperative setting, particularly for abdominal procedures. 🔑 **HY:** Dexamethasone serves triple duty as analgesic, anti-inflammatory, and antiemetic agent [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

💡 **Clinical Pearl:** When administering dexamethasone, always dilute and give slowly to prevent perineal irritation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Dexamethasone is a versatile adjunct in multimodal analgesia with analgesic, anti-inflammatory, and antiemetic properties. Careful administration technique is important to minimize perineal irritation, and it can be particularly valuable in opioid-tolerant patients or as part of perineural blocks.

## 7. DISEASE-MODIFYING ANTI-RHEUMATIC DRUGS (DMARDS)

### 7.1. TRADITIONAL DMARDS 

* **Definition:** Drugs that modify the course of rheumatic diseases, thereby indirectly reducing pain [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
* **Indications:** Treatment of rheumatoid arthritis, autoimmune diseases including vasculitis and inflammatory bowel disease [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
* **General Properties:** Have immunosuppressant properties and require careful perioperative management [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
* **Common Traditional DMARDs:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 
  * Methotrexate
  * Leflunomide
  * Hydroxychloroquine
  * Sulfasalazine
  * Cyclophosphamide
  * Cyclosporine
  * Tacrolimus

#### 7.1.1. SPECIFIC AGENTS

**Methotrexate:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 
* **Administration:** Oral or parenteral; weekly dosing (source of potential serious medication errors) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
* **Mechanism:** Structural similarity to folate; competitively inhibits dihydrofolate reductase binding, reducing metabolic pathways involving purine/pyrimidine metabolism, amino acid synthesis, and polyamine synthesis [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
* **Additional Mechanism:** Increases extracellular adenosine concentration (potent anti-inflammatory) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
* **Effects on Immune System:** Widespread effects on T cells and mononuclear cells [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
* ⚠️ **Contraindications:** Pregnancy (embryotoxic and teratogenic), renal disease [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
* **Side Effects:** Pulmonary and hepatotoxicity; bone marrow suppression (can develop abruptly) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
* **Monitoring:** Regular blood tests; patients advised to report infection symptoms, bruising, mouth ulcers, or shortness of breath [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
* **Management:** Folate administration may reduce symptoms without reducing efficacy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .

**Sulfasalazine:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 
* **Classification:** Prodrug formed of 5-aminosalicylic acid (5-ASA) linked to sulfapyridine [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
* **Metabolism:** Broken down in large bowel to constituent parts [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
* **Active Components:** Sulfapyridine active in rheumatoid arthritis; 5-ASA active in inflammatory bowel disease [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
* **Side Effects:** Insomnia, taste disturbance, tinnitus [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
* ⚠️ **Serious Side Effect:** Agranulocytosis (rare) - requires blood count monitoring [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .

**Hydroxychloroquine:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 
* **Uses:** Management of rheumatoid arthritis and systemic lupus [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
* **Side Effects:** Abdominal pain, diarrhea, emotional lability [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
* **Long-Term Use:** Requires retinal screening [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
* ⚠️ **Toxicity:** Extremely toxic in overdose; causes sodium channel blockade leading to neurological symptoms (coma) and cardiac effects (prolonged QTc, Torsades de Points, cardiac arrest) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
* **Toxicity Risk:** Related to ingested dose and degree of hypokalemia at presentation [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .

### 7.2. BIOLOGICAL DMARDS 

* **Classification:** More recent class of drugs targeting specific aspects of the immune system [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .
* **Mechanisms of Action:** Various, including:
  * Interfering with cytokine function, signal transduction, or production
  * Inhibition of T-cell activation
  * Depletion of B cells
* ⚠️ **Safety Concerns:** All have potential to cause:
  * Life-threatening myelosuppression and subsequent symptoms
  * Reactivation of latent infections including TB
  * Susceptibility to new infections
  * Impaired wound healing
* **Specific Agent - Tocilizumab:**
  * Inhibits IL-6 production, which is key for CRP production
  * Important consideration: CRP rise may be delayed in patients with post-operative infection [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .

### 7.3. PERIOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT OF DMARDS 

#### 7.3.1. PREOPERATIVE CONSIDERATIONS

* **Withholding Period:** Varies by agent and surgery risk (see Figure 10.7 in supplementary source) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] 
  * **Traditional DMARDs:**
	* Hydroxychloroquine: Minimal withholding (0-1 week)
	* Azathioprine, methotrexate: Moderate withholding (2-3 weeks)
	* Methotrexate (combination therapy): Longer withholding (4-5 weeks)
  * **Biological DMARDs:**
	* Etanercept: Moderate withholding (3 weeks)
	* Most biologicals: Longer withholding (5-7 weeks)
	* Secukinumab, high-dose tocilizumab: Extended withholding (7-8 weeks)
  * ⚠️ For high-risk surgery (abdominal & revision arthroplasty): Multiply withholding period by 2.5

#### 7.3.2. POSTOPERATIVE CONSIDERATIONS

* **Restart Timing:** Can recommence 2 weeks post-operatively if:
  * Wound healing is satisfactory
  * No signs of infection
* ⚠️ **Caution:** If in doubt, DMARDs should be withheld [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] .

**Bottom Line:** Disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARDs) require careful perioperative management due to their immunosuppressive properties. Traditional agents like methotrexate and newer biological agents have specific withholding periods before surgery, and postoperative restart depends on wound healing and absence of infection.

### 8.4. COMPARISON OF COX INHIBITORS

| **Agent Type** | **COX Selectivity** | **Key Advantages** | **Major Risks** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Traditional NSAIDs | Nonselective (COX-1 and COX-2) | Effective analgesia, anti-inflammatory | GI bleeding, platelet dysfunction, renal effects |
| COX-2 Selective (Celecoxib) | Primarily COX-2 | ↓ GI effects, no platelet effects | CV risks, renal effects |
| Acetaminophen | Central COX, minimal peripheral | No GI/platelet/bone effects | Hepatotoxicity at high doses |
| Aspirin | Irreversible COX-1 inhibition | Antiplatelet effects | Prolonged bleeding risk, GI effects |

**Bottom Line:** Multimodal analgesia combines medications with different mechanisms of action to optimize pain control while minimizing side effects of any single agent. This approach is especially important for opioid-sparing strategies and managing pain in opioid-tolerant patients. The "MAGIC" mnemonic provides a practical framework for implementing this comprehensive approach.
