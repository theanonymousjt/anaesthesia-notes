# 1. INTRODUCTION TO ACUTE PAIN MANAGEMENT

## 1.1. DEFINITION AND GLOBAL CONTEXT

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|Part A: Pain Physiology]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|Part B: Pain Medicine]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C: Medical Knowledge]]

**Exam Weighting:** High (Fundamental to all perioperative care)

* **Global surgical context**: Over 300 million surgical procedures performed annually worldwide, many in inpatient settings [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* **Importance of management**: Appropriate management of acute perioperative pain using multimodal opioid-sparing analgesia is crucial [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Guideline development timeline**:
	* 2012: American Society of Anesthesiologists developed treatment guidelines for acute postoperative pain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
	* 2016: Guidelines updated by American Society of Pain Medicine, American Society of Regional Anesthesia (ASRA), and American Society of Anesthesiologists [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
	* 2016: U.S. Health and Human Services (HHS) Pain Management Best Practices Inter-Agency Task Force published acute pain guidelines emphasizing opioid-sparing multimodal management [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
	* 2021: Multisociety collaborative effort developed seven guiding principles for acute perioperative pain management [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* **Evidence-based approach**: Responses to placebo across all clinical conditions are small but consistently positive and are more prominent, although highly variable in magnitude, in studies of pain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* **Importance of psychosocial context**: Placebo effects are the consequence of the psychosocial context (or treatment ritual) on the patient's mind, brain and body, even when no traditional placebo is given [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 

**Bottom Line:** Effective acute pain management requires an understanding of evolving guidelines and a commitment to multimodal opioid-sparing approaches that address the global burden of surgical pain.

### 1.1.1. ASA PRACTICE GUIDELINES FRAMEWORK [ASA 2012]

* **Definition of acute pain in perioperative setting** `[ASA:2012]`
	* Pain present in a surgical patient after a procedure
	* May result from trauma from the procedure or procedure-related complications
	* Pain management refers to actions before, during, and after procedure to reduce or eliminate postoperative pain before discharge
* **Purpose of Guidelines** `[ASA:2012]`
	1. Facilitate safety and effectiveness of acute pain management in perioperative setting
	2. Reduce risk of adverse outcomes
	3. Maintain patient's functional abilities, physical and psychological well-being
	4. Enhance quality of life for patients with acute pain during perioperative period
* **Adverse outcomes from undertreatment** `[ASA:2012]`
	* Thromboembolic and pulmonary complications
	* Additional time spent in intensive care unit or hospital
	* Hospital readmission for further pain management
	* Needless suffering
	* Impairment of health-related quality of life
	* Development of chronic pain
* **Adverse outcomes from pain management** `[ASA:2012]`
	* Respiratory depression, brain or other neurologic injury
	* Sedation, circulatory depression
	* Nausea, vomiting, pruritus
	* Urinary retention, impairment of bowel function
	* Sleep disruption

## 1.2. ACUTE PAIN DEFINITION AND IMPLICATIONS

> **Syllabus**: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|Part A: Pain Physiology]] | [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|Part B: Pain Medicine]] | [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|Part C: Medical Knowledge]]

**Exam Weighting:** High (Core knowledge)

* **Definition**: *An unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with, or resembling that associated with, actual or potential tissue damage* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* **Alternative definition** (International Association for the Study of Pain): *An unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with actual or potential tissue damage or described in terms of such damage* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* **Duration**: Generally resolves within 1 month [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* **Consequences of inadequate pain management**:
	* Adverse physiologic effects → significant morbidity and mortality [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
	* Delayed patient recovery and return to daily activities [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
	* Patient dissatisfaction with surgical experience [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
	* Adverse psychological effects including anxiety, fear, frustration, and psychological distress [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
	* ⚠️ Increased incidence of persistent postoperative pain conditions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* **Neuronal plasticity**: Acute pain-induced change in the central nervous system [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
	* Mechanism: Causes sensitization of the nervous system → allodynia and hyperalgesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
	* 🔑 Poorly managed acute pain can trigger pathophysiologic processes in both peripheral and central nervous systems that can lead to chronicity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* **SURGICAL PROCEDURES ASSOCIATED WITH CHRONIC PAINFUL CONDITIONS**
	* Amputation of a limb [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Lateral thoracotomy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Inguinal herniorrhaphy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Abdominal hysterectomy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Saphenous vein stripping [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Open cholecystectomy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Nephrectomy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Mastectomy [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Psychosocial factors in development of chronic pain**:
	* High pain-related fear avoidance beliefs in patients with back pain of less than 6 months duration are associated with poor outcomes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
	* Significant association between high levels of catastrophizing in acute and subacute back pain and pain and disability at later points of time [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
	* Psychological factors associated with poor postoperative pain control include anxiety (state and trait), catastrophizing, expectation of pain, depression, negative affect and neuroticism/psychological vulnerability [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
	* Significant association between anxiety, pain catastrophizing, depression, psychological vulnerability and stress and the subsequent development of chronic postsurgical pain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 

**Bottom Line:** Acute pain is more than just a symptomatic experience; if inadequately managed, it can lead to persistent pain states through neuroplastic changes in the nervous system, highlighting the importance of effective early intervention.

## 1.3. GUIDING PRINCIPLES FOR ACUTE PERIOPERATIVE PAIN MANAGEMENT

### 1.3.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Physiological basis for multimodal analgesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* **Part B:** Clinical implementation of perioperative pain management [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Part C:** System-based approaches to pain management [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#2. Patient Care and Procedural Skills|ⓘ]]

**Exam Weighting:** High (Core clinical application)



1. **Preoperative evaluation** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Assessment of medical and psychological conditions
	* Review of concomitant medications
	* History of chronic pain
	* Substance abuse disorder history
	* Previous postoperative treatment regimens and responses
	* Purpose: To guide the perioperative pain management plan
2. **Pain assessment** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Use validated pain assessment tools
	* Track responses to postoperative pain treatments
	* Adjust treatment plans according to assessment results
	* Note: Regular assessment of pain leads to improved acute pain management [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]] 
3. **Multimodal analgesia** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Utilize variety of analgesic medications and techniques
	* Combine with nonpharmacologic interventions
	* Evidence shows multimodal analgesia compared to mainly opioid-based analgesia improves pain control and reduces opioid consumption and adverse effects [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]] 
4. **Patient education** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Provide patientand family-centered individually tailored education
	* Include information on treatment options for managing postoperative pain
	* Written information given to patients is better than verbal information given at the time of the interview [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]] 
5. **Medication education** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Educate all adult patients and primary caregivers on:
		* Pain treatment plan
		* Proper storage and disposal of opioids
		* Tapering protocols
6. **Dynamic management** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Adjust pain management plan based on:
		* Adequacy of pain relief
		* Presence of adverse events
7. **Specialist consultation** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Have access to pain specialist consultation for:
		* Patients with inadequately controlled postoperative pain
		* Patients at high risk of inadequately controlled postoperative pain
8. **Economic considerations** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]] 
	* Long-term economic consequences from the progression of acute to chronic postsurgical and post-traumatic pain can be significant
	* Strategies to optimize acute and subacute pain management (including involvement of transitional pain services) may reduce the economic burden of chronic pain and inappropriate prescription opioid use
	* Patients' willingness to pay for good pain relief is high

💡 **Clinical Pearl**: The transition from acute to chronic pain is not simply a matter of time but involves neuroplastic changes that can be prevented with aggressive multimodal analgesia initiated early in the perioperative period [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Effective perioperative pain management requires comprehensive assessment, tailored planning, patient education, and continuous monitoring with the flexibility to adjust treatment as needed.

### 1.3.2. ASA INSTITUTIONAL REQUIREMENTS [ASA 2012]

**CORE INSTITUTIONAL FRAMEWORK** `[ASA:2012]`
* **Education and training programs** for healthcare providers:
	* Associated with decreased pain levels `[ASA:B2]`
	* Decreased nausea and vomiting `[ASA:B2]`
	* Improved patient satisfaction `[ASA:B2]`
	* Content should range from basic bedside pain assessment to sophisticated techniques
	* Essential for new personnel, skill maintenance, and when approaches are modified
* **Acute Pain Service framework**:
	* Associated with reductions in perioperative pain `[ASA:B2]`
	* Should involve standardized institutional policies and procedures
	* Integrated approach to minimize analgesic gaps
* **24-hour anesthesiologist availability**:
	* Required for consultation with ward nurses, surgeons, other physicians
	* Essential for evaluating patients experiencing problems with pain relief
* **Standardized pain assessment tools**:
	* Should use validated instruments for pain intensity evaluation
	* Must document effects of pain therapy and side effects
	* Regular documentation improves acute pain management outcomes

⚠️ **Critical Requirement**: Anesthesiologists responsible for perioperative analgesia should be available at all times to consult and assist in evaluating patients experiencing problems with pain relief `[ASA:2012]`

## 3.2. PAIN VS. NOCICEPTION

### 3.2.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Neurophysiology of nociception [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* **Part B:** Clinical distinction between pain and nociception [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Basic concept)



* **Nociception** is the process by which noxious signals are encoded as action potentials and transmitted from the periphery to the central nervous system (CNS) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
* **Pain** results from the brain's interpretation of these nociceptive signals, resulting in the perception of an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
* The distinction is important because:
	* Nociception can occur without conscious pain perception (e.g., under general anesthesia)
	* Pain can occur without active nociception (e.g., phantom limb pain)
	* Understanding this distinction guides appropriate interventions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
* **Neurobiological basis**: Nociception involves multiple levels of processing:
	* Transduction (conversion of noxious stimuli into electrical impulses)
	* Transmission (propagation of impulses along afferent fibers)
	* Modulation (alterations in signal strength at various levels of the nervous system)
	* Perception (the conscious experience of pain) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 

**Bottom Line:** Nociception refers to the neural process of encoding and transmitting potentially harmful stimuli, while pain is the subjective perception that results from the brain's interpretation of these signals.

## 3.3. PAIN CLASSIFICATION

### 3.3.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Classification systems for pain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* **Part B:** Clinical application of pain classification [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Important clinical concept)



* **Traditional Duration-Based Classification**:
	* **Acute pain**: Duration <12 weeks [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
	* **Chronic pain**: Pain that extends beyond the expected period of healing following tissue injury [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
* **Location/Source-Based Classification** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
	* **Superficial or cutaneous pain**:
		* Due to skin damage
		* Characterized by sharp, well-localized pain
	* **Deep pain**:
		* Dull, aching and poorly localized
		* Arises from structures such as muscles, tendons, and ligaments
	* **Visceral pain**:
		* Dull, diffuse and poorly localized
		* Arises from the viscera (e.g., spasm or overdistension of a hollow viscus)
* **Mechanism-Based Classification**:
	* **Nociceptive pain**: Caused by stimulation of nociceptors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
	* **Neuropathic pain**: Caused by damage to the neurons themselves [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
	* **Mixed pain states**: Combination of mechanisms [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
* **Temporal pattern classification** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
	* **Background pain**: Persistent baseline discomfort
	* **Intermittent pain**: Recurring episodes between pain-free periods
	* **Breakthrough pain**: Temporary flares despite analgesic treatment; pain that escalates above a persistent background pain

💡 **Clinical Pearl**: Modern approaches to pain management increasingly emphasize mechanism-based classification over duration-based classification, as this better guides targeted therapeutic interventions.

**Bottom Line:** Pain can be classified based on duration (acute vs. chronic), location/source (superficial, deep, or visceral), and mechanism (nociceptive, neuropathic, or mixed), with mechanism-based classification being particularly useful for guiding treatment decisions.

## 3.4. PAIN PATHWAYS

### 3.4.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Neuroanatomy of pain transmission `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]`
* **Part B:** Implications for pain interventions [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Exam Weighting:** High (Fundamental concept)



### 3.4.2. NOCICEPTOR TYPES AND CHARACTERISTICS

* **Definition**: A nociceptor is a free, unmyelinated nerve ending capable of generating action potentials in response to a variety of stimuli that are generated by cellular damage [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
* **Activating stimuli** include [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
	* K+ released from damaged cells
	* Histamine released from mast cells near the site of tissue damage
	* Bradykinin increased at the site of injury as a result of inflammation
	* Leukotrienes and prostaglandins synthesized in response to cellular damage
	* Serotonin released by platelets in response to vascular injury

### 3.4.3. NERVE FIBER TYPES IN PAIN TRANSMISSION

* **Type Aδ fibers** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
	* Carry impulses produced in response to mechanical and thermal stimuli
	* Have myelinated axons of large diameter
	* Relatively high conduction velocity of around 20 m/s
	* Produce the first sensation of pain, often perceived as being sharp and well-localized
* **Type C fibers** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
	* Carry impulses produced in response to thermal, mechanical, and chemical stimuli
	* Have unmyelinated axons of small diameter
	* Low action potential conduction velocity (0.5–4.0 m/s)
	* Produce a sensation of dull, poorly localized pain that often follows the early sharp pain mediated by Aδ fibers

### 3.4.4. SPINOTHALAMIC AND TRIGEMINAL PATHWAYS

**Spinothalamic Tract** (for body below the neck) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
* **First-order neuron** (C or Aδ fiber):
	* Relays action potentials from nociceptor to substantia gelatinosa (Rexed lamina II) or nucleus proprius (Rexed laminae III, IV, and V) in the dorsal horn
	* Synapses with a second-order interneuron using substance P as neurotransmitter
* **Second-order neuron**:
	* Decussates in the anterior commissure
	* Ascends the spinal cord in the spinothalamic tract
	* Synapses with a third-order neuron in the thalamus
* **Third-order neuron**:
	* Relays nociceptive action potentials to the somatosensory cortex

**Trigeminal Pathway** (for face) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
* **First-order neuron** (C or Aδ fiber):
	* Relays action potentials from the face to the trigeminal nucleus
	* Most sensory information travels through the trigeminal nerve
	* Some afferents from oropharynx and ear travel in facial, glossopharyngeal, and vagus nerves
	* All synapse in the trigeminal nucleus (Gasserian ganglion)
* **Trigeminal nucleus components**:
	* Spinal trigeminal nucleus: receives pain and temperature information
	* Main trigeminal nucleus: receives touch and proprioception from face and mouth
	* Mesencephalic trigeminal nucleus: receives proprioceptive information from jaw
* **Second-order neurons**:
	* Immediately decussate
	* Ascend through brainstem to thalamus
	* Synapse with third-order neurons
* **Third-order neurons**:
	* Relay action potentials to the somatosensory cortex

**Bottom Line:** Pain transmission involves specialized nociceptors that activate either Aδ fibers (fast, sharp, well-localized pain) or C fibers (slow, dull, poorly localized pain). These signals travel via the spinothalamic tract (for body pain) or trigeminal pathway (for facial pain) through first-, second-, and third-order neurons before reaching the somatosensory cortex for conscious perception.

## 3.5. PAIN MODULATION MECHANISMS

### 3.5.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Neurophysiology of pain modulation `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]`
* **Part B:** Clinical implications for pain control `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]`

**Exam Weighting:** High (Important for understanding analgesic mechanisms)



Pain modulation is demonstrated by the fact that the pain associated with an injury may not always correlate with the severity of that injury (e.g., soldiers with severe battle wounds experiencing little to no pain) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 

### 3.5.2. SEGMENTAL INHIBITION

* **Historical context: Gate Theory (1965)** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
	* Originally hypothesized that C fibers carried pain while Aδ fibers carried touch, pressure, and vibration
	* Proposed that both fiber types converge on the same second-order neuron
	* Greater activity of Aδ fibers was thought to reduce transmission of pain through C fibers
	* This explained phenomena like pain reduction from rubbing an injured area
* **Current understanding** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
	* Gate theory has been largely disproved with the identification of dedicated nociceptive Aδ fibers
	* Alternative mechanisms for segmental inhibition exist
	* These may explain the effectiveness of techniques like transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS)

### 3.5.3. ENDOGENOUS OPIOID SYSTEM

* **Distribution of opioid receptors** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
	* Located throughout CNS, especially in:
		* Periaqueductal gray matter
		* Ventral medulla
		* Spinal cord
* **Receptor classification** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
	* G protein-coupled receptors of three classes: μ, κ, and δ
* **Endogenous ligands** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
	* Enkephalins
	* Endorphins
	* Dynorphin
* **Mechanism of action** `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]` 
	* When endogenous opioids bind to receptors, they reduce transmission of nociceptive impulses
	* This occurs by either:
		* Opening K+ channels on the postsynaptic membrane, hyperpolarizing the second-order neuron
		* Inhibiting Ca2+ influx into the presynaptic terminal, reducing neurotransmitter (substance P) release

### 3.5.4. DESCENDING INHIBITION

* **Neural pathway** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
	* Neurons from periaqueductal gray matter project to dorsal horn via raphe nuclei in brainstem
	* These inhibit ascending second-order nociceptive neurons
	* This reduces perception of pain by inhibiting neurotransmission at first-to-second-order neuron synapse
* **Neurotransmitters** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
	* Serotonin and noradrenaline are the main neurotransmitters
	* Explains analgesic properties of:
		* Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors
		* Tricyclic antidepressants
		* α2-adrenergic agonists like clonidine

![Descending inhibition pathway](ACUTE_ACUTE_ACUTE_ACUTE_ACUTE_image_1.png_3ffee2.png_a1c910.png_27cd9b.png_466979.png_d512d6.png) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 

**Bottom Line:** Pain perception is modulated by multiple mechanisms including segmental inhibition at the spinal level, the endogenous opioid system, and descending inhibitory pathways from the brain. These systems explain how pain can be reduced by various interventions and why pain intensity doesn't always correspond to injury severity.

## 3.6. HYPERALGESIA AND ALLODYNIA

### 3.6.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Neurophysiology of sensitization [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* **Part B:** Clinical recognition and management [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Important clinical concept)



* **Hyperalgesia definition**: Increased pain from a stimulus that normally provokes pain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
* **Types of hyperalgesia** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
	* **Primary hyperalgesia**:
		* Occurs in damaged tissues (e.g., areas of sunburnt skin have greater sensitivity)
		* Mechanism: Release of substance P, bradykinin, and histamine at the site of tissue damage
		* These chemicals sensitize nociceptors, decreasing their threshold potential for action potential generation
	
	* **Secondary hyperalgesia**:
		* Occurs in tissues surrounding the areas of damage
		* Mechanism: Increased release of substances (substance P, calcitonin gene-related peptide, glutamate) by activated second-order interneurons
		* These sensitize neighboring second-order interneurons
		* Due to somatotopic organization of brain and spinal cord neurons, neighboring neurons correspond to surrounding tissue
* **Allodynia definition**: Pain due to a stimulus that does not normally provoke pain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
	* Examples: Light touch or cold breeze perceived as painful
	* Often a feature of neuropathic pain (e.g., trigeminal neuralgia)
* **Clinical significance** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
	* Hyperalgesia serves a protective function, preventing further damage to injured tissue
	* Allodynia has no useful purpose and represents pathological pain processing
	* Both phenomena indicate central sensitization of pain pathways
* **Proposed mechanism of allodynia** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
	* May involve reorganization of spinal cord circuitry
	* Interneurons serving nociceptors may be exchanged with those transmitting mechanoreceptor impulses

**Bottom Line:** Hyperalgesia (increased pain from normally painful stimuli) and allodynia (pain from normally non-painful stimuli) represent sensitization of the pain processing system. Primary hyperalgesia occurs at the injury site due to peripheral sensitization, while secondary hyperalgesia affects surrounding areas due to central sensitization.

## 3.7. NEUROPATHIC PAIN

### 3.7.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Pathophysiology of neuropathic pain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* **Part B:** Clinical assessment and management [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Important clinical concept)



* **Definition**: Pain caused by a lesion or disease of the somatosensory nervous system [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
* **Origin sites** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
	* **Peripheral nervous system (PNS)**:
		* Examples: Diabetes, herpes zoster infection, cancer invasion
	* **Central nervous system (CNS)**:
		* Examples: Multiple sclerosis, spinal cord injury
* **Clinical characteristics** compared to nociceptive pain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
	* Nociceptive pain is usually sharp or aching
	* Neuropathic pain may be:
		* Episodic (electric shock-like)
		* Continuous burning sensation
		* Associated with abnormal sensations (paresthesias, allodynia)
		* May follow non-dermatomal distribution
* **Examples of mechanisms** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
	* **Diabetic neuropathy**:
		* Ischemia causes demyelination of nerve fibers
		* Newly exposed axons fire ectopic action potentials
		* Perceived as shooting or burning pain
	
	* **Nerve transection**:
		* Injured axons attempt to regrow with nerve growth factor from Schwann cells
		* Disorganized regrowth leads to:
			* Spontaneous action potential generation
			* Altered threshold potentials
* **High-risk procedures for neuropathic pain** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Limb amputations
	* Breast surgery
	* Gallbladder surgery
	* Thoracic surgery
	* Inguinal hernia repair
* **Assessment requirements** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
	* Appropriate assessments (including screening tools) are required to determine the presence of neuropathic pain
	* Proper identification is critical for appropriate management

⚠️ **Clinical Warning**: Patients with neuropathic pain are at increased risk of progressing to chronic pain states [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Neuropathic pain results from direct damage to the nervous system, either peripherally or centrally. It presents differently from nociceptive pain with unique clinical features like burning, electric shock sensations, and allodynia. Understanding its mechanisms is crucial for appropriate management and prevention of chronicity.

## 3.8. SYMPATHETIC INVOLVEMENT IN PAIN

### 3.8.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Sympathetic contributions to pain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* **Part B:** Clinical implications in pain syndromes [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Exam Weighting:** Low (Specialized concept)



* **Sympathetic involvement in trauma** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
	* Tissue trauma damages both somatic and sympathetic nerves
	* A small number of patients develop chronic sympathetic nervous system dysfunction
	* This results in complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS)
* **Clinical manifestations of CRPS** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
	* **Vasomotor changes**: Affected limb may be hotter or colder than contralateral limb
	* **Sudomotor changes**: Reduced sweating
	* **Trophic changes**:
		* Reduced hair or nail growth
		* Osteoporosis of underlying bone
	* **Neuropathic pain**: Hyperalgesia and allodynia are common
* **Current understanding** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
	* The significance of the sympathetic nervous system in acute pain development remains debated
	* Evidence suggests sympathetic-sensory coupling may occur following nerve injury
	* This could explain sympathetically maintained pain states

**Bottom Line:** The sympathetic nervous system can contribute to pain states, particularly in conditions like CRPS, where autonomic dysfunction leads to vasomotor, sudomotor, and trophic changes alongside pain. The exact role of sympathetic involvement in acute pain development remains an area of active research.

# 4. SURGICAL STRESS RESPONSE









## 4.1. NEUROENDOCRINE RESPONSE

### 4.1.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Physiological stress response to surgery `[[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]`

**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Important physiological concept)



* **Surgical stress vs. postoperative pain** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
	* Similar but distinct concepts
	* Surgical stress causes release of cytokines:
		* Interleukin-1
		* Interleukin-6
		* Tumor necrosis factor-α
	* Precipitates adverse neuroendocrine and sympathoadrenal responses
	* Results in detrimental physiologic responses (particularly in high-risk patients)
* **Neuroendocrine response characteristics** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
	* **Increased secretion of catabolic hormones**:
		* Cortisol
		* Glucagon
		* Growth hormone
		* Catecholamines
	* **Decreased secretion of anabolic hormones**:
		* Insulin
		* Testosterone
	* **Results in**:
		* Hyperglycemia
		* Negative nitrogen balance
* **Consequences of neuroendocrine response** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
	* Poor wound healing
	* Muscle wasting
	* Fatigue
	* Impaired immunocompetency
* **Mitigation strategies** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
	* Epidural analgesia provided with local anesthetics for at least 24 hours compared to systemic opioid analgesia reduces perioperative mortality and multiple morbidities (including ileus, pneumonia, respiratory depression and arrhythmias)
	* Local anesthetic administration improves oxygenation and reduces pulmonary infections and other pulmonary complications compared with parenteral opioids
	* The combination of thoracic epidural analgesia with local anesthetics and nutritional support leads to preservation of total body protein after upper abdominal surgery

**Bottom Line:** The neuroendocrine response to surgical stress involves increased secretion of catabolic hormones and decreased anabolic hormones, leading to hyperglycemia, negative nitrogen balance, and ultimately impaired recovery through poor wound healing, muscle wasting, and diminished immune function.

# 5. PREVENTIVE ANALGESIA









## 5.1. DEFINITION AND TERMINOLOGY

### 5.1.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Neurophysiology of pain prevention [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* **Part B:** Clinical strategies for preventive analgesia `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]`
* **Part C:** Evidence-based implementation [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#2. Patient Care and Procedural Skills|ⓘ]]

**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Important clinical concept)



* **Definition and terminology** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
	* *Preventive analgesia*: Any antinociceptive regimen delivered during the perioperative period that attenuates pain-induced sensitization
	* Replaces older term "preemptive analgesia"
	* ⚠️ "Preemptive analgesia" should be considered obsolete
* **Previous concept: Preemptive analgesia** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
	* Defined as analgesic regimen administered prior to surgical incision
	* Claimed to be more effective than same regimen given after surgery
	* Evidence for clinical benefit in humans has been mixed
* **Evidence from Cochrane Review** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]] 
	* The timing of opioid administration (preincision rather than postincision) may reduce further opioid consumption over 24 h, but has no effect on pain scores
	* Pre-emptive use of paracetamol across a range of procedures reduces pain scores up to 2 h, opioid consumption for up to 24 h and postoperative nausea and vomiting
	* Pre-emptive epidural analgesia has a significant effect on postoperative pain relief
* **Goal of preventive analgesia** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
	* Block development of sustained pain
	* Theoretical mechanism: Preventing NMDA receptor activation in dorsal horn associated with:
		* Wind-up
		* Facilitation
		* Central sensitization expansion of receptive fields
		* Long-term potentiation
	* These mechanisms can lead to chronic pain states if left unchecked

**Bottom Line:** Preventive analgesia is a comprehensive approach that extends throughout the perioperative period, focusing on blocking nociceptive input and preventing central sensitization, replacing the more limited concept of preemptive analgesia which emphasized only the timing of analgesics before surgical incision.

## 5.2. CRITICAL PRINCIPLES FOR SUCCESS

### 5.2.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part B:** Implementation requirements for preventive analgesia `[[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]`
* **Part C:** Clinical application of principles [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#2. Patient Care and Procedural Skills|ⓘ]]

**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Important clinical concept)



* **Critical principles for successful preventive analgesia** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	1. **Depth of analgesia**: Must be adequate to block all nociceptive input during surgery
	2. **Extent of technique**: Must be extensive enough to include entire surgical field
	3. **Duration of analgesia**: Must include both surgical and postsurgical periods
* **Special considerations** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Patients with pre-existing chronic pain may respond poorly to these techniques
	* Reason: Pre-existing sensitization of the nervous system
* **Evidence for effectiveness** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]] 
	* Epidural, regional and systemic local anesthetic administration shows preventive analgesic effects in reducing chronic postsurgical pain
	* NMDA-receptor antagonists (ketamine) reduce the incidence of chronic postsurgical pain in selected procedures

**Bottom Line:** Successful preventive analgesia requires adequate blockade of all nociceptive input from the surgical field, maintained throughout both the surgical and postsurgical periods, though patients with pre-existing chronic pain may show reduced response to these techniques.

## 5.3. IMPLEMENTATION STRATEGIES

### 5.3.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part B:** Clinical approaches to preventive analgesia [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Part C:** Evidence-based implementation [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#2. Patient Care and Procedural Skills|ⓘ]]

**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Important clinical application)



* **Multimodal approach** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Combined pharmacological agents targeting different pain pathways
	* Regional anesthesia techniques where appropriate
	* NMDA antagonists (e.g., ketamine, magnesium) to prevent central sensitization
	* Non-opioid analgesics (NSAIDs, acetaminophen) for baseline analgesia
	* Gabapentinoids for neuropathic components
* **Timing considerations** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Initiate analgesic regimen before surgical incision
	* Maintain throughout surgery
	* Continue into postoperative period
	* Duration should be tailored to expected duration of surgical pain
* **Specific regimens with supporting evidence** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]] 
	* Following thoracotomy, epidural analgesia reduces the incidence of chronic postsurgical pain
	* Following breast cancer surgery, paravertebral block, local infiltration and IV lidocaine reduce the incidence of chronic postsurgical pain
	* For iliac crest bone graft harvest, continuous local anaesthetic infiltration reduces the incidence of chronic postsurgical pain
	* Pregabalin reduces the incidence of chronic postsurgical neuropathic pain, but does not affect non-neuropathic chronic postsurgical pain
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: When implementing preventive analgesia, remember it must cover the entire perioperative period, not just pre-incision, to be effective [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]].
* 🔑 **HY**: The distinction between preventive vs. preemptive analgesia is important for exams and clinical practice - focus on the comprehensive approach of preventive analgesia rather than simply timing analgesics before incision [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Implementing preventive analgesia requires a multimodal approach that starts before surgery, continues throughout the procedure, and extends into the postoperative period, with specific agents chosen to target different pain pathways and prevent central sensitization.

# 6. STRATEGIES FOR ACUTE PAIN MANAGEMENT









## 6.1. PAIN TYPE-SPECIFIC APPROACHES

### 6.1.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part C:** Implementation of evidence-based approaches [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#2. Patient Care and Procedural Skills|ⓘ]]

**Exam Weighting:** High (Core clinical application)



* **Nociceptive Pain** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* *Primary type* of postoperative pain
	* **Best treated with**:
		* Opioids
		* NSAIDs (Nonsteroidal Anti-inflammatory Drugs)
		* Para-aminophenol agents (e.g., acetaminophen)
		* Regional anesthesia techniques
	* **Evidence for effectiveness** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]] 
		* Nonselective NSAIDs are effective in the treatment of acute postoperative pain, renal colic, migraine, primary dysmenorrhoea, acute muscle injury, chronic low-back pain and acute ankle sprain
		* Paracetamol is an effective analgesic for acute pain with adverse effects comparable to placebo
		* Nonselective NSAIDs given in addition to paracetamol improve analgesia compared with either medicine given alone, in particular ibuprofen combined with paracetamol
* **Neuropathic Pain** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* *Definition*: Pain resulting from nerve injury due to cutting, traction, compression, or entrapment
	* **Clinical features**:
		* Continuous burning sensation
		* Paroxysmal shooting or electric pain
		* Associated with allodynia, hyperalgesia, and dysesthesias
		* May have delayed onset
		* Often follows non-dermatomal distribution
	* **Best treated with** adjuvant medications:
		* NMDA receptor antagonists
		* α2-agonists
		* α2–δ subunit calcium channel ligands
	* **Evidence for effectiveness** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]] 
		* Tramadol is an effective treatment for neuropathic pain
		* Alpha-2-delta ligands (gabapentinoids) are the only anticonvulsants with proven efficacy in the treatment of chronic neuropathic pain
		* Tricyclic antidepressants and serotonin–noradrenaline-reuptake inhibitors are effective analgesics for chronic neuropathic pain and fibromyalgia, and more effective than selective serotonin-reuptake inhibitors
	* ⚠️ **Critical Concept**: Patients with neuropathic pain are at increased risk of progressing to chronic pain states [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* **HIGH-RISK PROCEDURES FOR NEUROPATHIC PAIN**
		* Limb amputations
		* Breast surgery
		* Gallbladder surgery
		* Thoracic surgery
		* Inguinal hernia repair

**Bottom Line:** Effective acute pain management requires identifying the type of pain (nociceptive vs. neuropathic) and tailoring interventions accordingly, with opioids, NSAIDs, and regional techniques for nociceptive pain, and adjuvant medications for neuropathic pain, which carries a higher risk of chronicity.

* **Epidural morphine + bupivacaine vs. epidural bupivacaine alone**:
	* Improved pain scores and greater pain relief `[ASA:A1]`
	* Higher frequency of pruritus `[ASA:A1]`
	* Equivocal findings for nausea/vomiting `[ASA:C1]`

**SYSTEMIC MULTIMODAL TECHNIQUES** `[ASA:2012]`
* **IV morphine + ketorolac vs. IV morphine alone**:
	* Meta-analyses show improved pain scores and reduced analgesic use `[ASA:A1]`
	* Equivocal findings for nausea/vomiting `[ASA:C1]`
* **IV morphine + ketamine vs. IV morphine alone**:
	* Meta-analyses show equivocal findings for pain scores, analgesic use, and nausea `[ASA:C1]`
* **IV opioids + calcium channel blockers (gabapentin/pregabalin)**:
	* Lower pain scores and reduced opioid use `[ASA:A1]`
	* No differences in nausea/vomiting `[ASA:C1]`
* **PCA opioids + oral NSAIDs/acetaminophen**:
	* Inconsistent findings for pain scores and morphine use `[ASA:C2]`

**ASA EXPERT CONSENSUS RECOMMENDATIONS** `[ASA:2012]`
* Anesthesiologists should use multimodal pain management therapy whenever possible
* Unless contraindicated, patients should receive around-the-clock regimen of NSAIDs, COXIBs, or acetaminophen
* Regional blockade with local anesthetics should be considered
* Dosing should optimize efficacy while minimizing adverse events
* Choice of medication, dose, route, and duration should be individualized

### 6.3.5. ASA EVIDENCE FOR PERIOPERATIVE TECHNIQUES [ASA 2012]

**CENTRAL REGIONAL OPIOID ANALGESIA** `[ASA:2012]`
* **Preincisional epidural/intrathecal morphine**:
	* Improved pain relief vs. oral/IV/IM morphine `[ASA:A2]`
	* Preoperative intrathecal morphine vs. placebo shows inconsistent findings `[ASA:C2]`
* **Postoperative epidural morphine**:
	* Meta-analyses show improved pain relief vs. placebo `[ASA:A1]`
	* Increased frequency of pruritus `[ASA:A1]`
	* Improved pain scores vs. intramuscular morphine `[ASA:A1]`
	* Higher frequency of pruritus and urinary retention `[ASA:A1]`

**PATIENT-CONTROLLED ANALGESIA** `[ASA:2012]`
* **IV PCA vs. nurse-administered analgesia**:
	* Equivocal findings for analgesic efficacy `[ASA:C2]`
* **IV PCA morphine vs. intramuscular morphine**:
	* Meta-analysis shows improved pain scores `[ASA:A1]`
* **IV PCA with background infusion vs. without**:
	* More analgesic use with background infusion `[ASA:A1]`
	* Equivocal findings for pain relief, nausea, vomiting, pruritus, sedation `[ASA:C1]`

**PERIPHERAL REGIONAL TECHNIQUES** `[ASA:2012]`
* **Intercostal/interpleural blocks**:
	* Preincisional bupivacaine vs. saline: improved pain relief `[ASA:A2]`
	* Postincisional bupivacaine: improved pain relief and reduced analgesic consumption `[ASA:A2]`
* **Plexus blocks**:
	* Preincisional blocks: less analgesic use vs. saline `[ASA:A1]`
	* Preincisional blocks vs. no block: lower pain scores `[ASA:A1]`
* **Local infiltration**:
	* Preincisional bupivacaine vs. saline: improved pain scores `[ASA:A1]`
	* Preincisional ropivacaine: improved pain scores and reduced analgesic use `[ASA:A1]`

⚠️ **ASA Recommendation**: These modalities should be used in preference to intramuscular opioids ordered "as needed" `[ASA:2012]`

### 6.5.2. PEDIATRIC PATIENTS [ASA 2012]

* **Special considerations** `[ASA:2012]`:
	* Biopsychosocial nature of pain requires special attention
	* Developmental differences in pain experience and expression
	* Different response to analgesic pharmacotherapy
	* Caregiver misperceptions about analgesia risks and benefits
	* Fear of opioid-induced respiratory depression leads to underuse
* **Emotional component** `[ASA:2012]`:
	* Particularly strong in infants and children
	* Absence of parents, security objects, familiar surroundings causes suffering
	* Fear of injections makes invasive routes aversive
* **ASA Recommendations** `[ASA:2012]`:
	* Aggressive and proactive pain management necessary to overcome historic undertreatment
	* Developmentally appropriate pain assessment and therapy required
	* Analgesic therapy should depend on age, weight, comorbidity
	* Unless contraindicated, should involve multimodal approach
	* Behavioral techniques especially important for emotional component
	* Appropriate monitoring essential due to synergistic effects

### 6.5.3. GERIATRIC PATIENTS [ASA 2012]

* **Special considerations** `[ASA:2012]`:
	* More likely to have conditions requiring surgery (arthritis, cancer)
	* Pain often undertreated and more vulnerable to detrimental effects
	* Physical, social, emotional, cognitive changes affect pain management
	* Different attitudes than younger adults in expressing pain
	* Altered physiology affects drug distribution and metabolism
	* Observational studies show lower analgesic doses than younger adults `[ASA:B2]`
* **ASA Recommendations** `[ASA:2012]`:
	* Pain assessment and therapy should be integrated into perioperative care
	* Use pain assessment tools appropriate to cognitive abilities
	* Extensive and proactive evaluation to overcome communication barriers
	* Recognize different responses to pain and medications due to comorbidity
	* Vigilant dose titration to ensure adequate treatment while avoiding adverse effects

### 6.5.4. OTHER SPECIAL POPULATIONS [ASA 2012]

* **Populations at risk** `[ASA:2012]`:
	* Critically ill patients
	* Cognitively impaired (e.g., Alzheimer's disease)
	* Patients with communication difficulties (cultural/language barriers)
* **Considerations** `[ASA:2012]`:
	* Techniques reducing drug dosages may be suitable (regional/multimodal analgesia)
	* Behavioral modalities generally less suitable for cognitively impaired
	* PCA techniques depending on self-administration less suitable
* **ASA Recommendations** `[ASA:2012]`:
	* Recognize need for additional interventions to ensure optimal pain management
	* Consider therapeutic trial of analgesic for increased BP/HR or agitated behavior when other causes excluded

## 7.1. PAIN ASSESSMENT FUNDAMENTALS

### 7.1.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part B:** Clinical assessment tools and strategies [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Part C:** Integration of assessment into care pathways [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]

**Exam Weighting:** High (Essential clinical skill)



**Patient observation is critical** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Physical positioning and movement can reveal unmanaged pain
* Example: Patient lying completely still despite reporting low pain scores
* Common error: Accepting verbal scores without functional assessment

**Pain assessment limitations** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Remember: *"Pain is what a patient says it is"* (Dr. John Bonica - father of pain medicine)
* Finite measurements may miss important clinical context
* Effective assessment requires initial evaluation AND vigilant reassessment
* Goals: Determine if treatment objectives are met, identify adverse effects, make necessary adjustments

**Evidence-based considerations** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]] 
* There is good correlation between the visual analogue and verbal numerical rating scales
* Regular assessment of pain leads to improved acute pain management
* Appropriate assessments (including screening tools) are required to determine the presence of neuropathic pain
* Functional outcomes rather than pain scores alone should be used to guide acute pain management, including non-pharmacological approaches
* Self-reporting of pain should be used whenever appropriate as pain is by definition a subjective experience
* The pain measurement tool chosen should be appropriate to the individual patient and the clinical context (eg intensive care, ward, community). Developmental, cognitive, emotional, language and cultural factors should be considered
* Uncontrolled or unexpected pain requires a reassessment of the diagnosis and consideration of alternative causes for the pain (eg new surgical/ medical diagnosis, neuropathic pain)

**Bottom Line:** Comprehensive pain assessment requires more than just collecting pain scores; it demands careful observation of patient behaviors and physical limitations, recognition of the subjective nature of pain experience, and ongoing reassessment to evaluate treatment effectiveness.

### 7.1.2. ASA ASSESSMENT EVIDENCE AND RECOMMENDATIONS [ASA 2012]

* **Evidence for standardized assessment** `[ASA:2012]`:
	* Observational studies suggest pain outcomes not fully documented in patient records `[ASA:B2]`
	* Regular assessment of pain leads to improved acute pain management `[ASA:B2]`
* **ASA Assessment Requirements** `[ASA:2012]`:
	* Use standardized, validated instruments for:
		* Regular evaluation and documentation of pain intensity
		* Effects of pain therapy
		* Side effects caused by therapy
	* Assessment tools should be appropriate to:
		* Individual patient capabilities
		* Clinical context (ICU, ward, community)
		* Developmental, cognitive, emotional factors
		* Language and cultural considerations
* **Documentation standards** `[ASA:2012]`:
	* Pain intensity evaluation using validated tools
	* Treatment effects monitoring
	* Side effect documentation
	* Outcomes assessment for continuous quality improvement
* **Clinical application principles** `[ASA:2012]`:
	* Self-reporting should be used whenever appropriate as pain is subjective
	* Functional outcomes rather than pain scores alone should guide management
	* Uncontrolled or unexpected pain requires reassessment of diagnosis
	* Consider alternative causes (new surgical/medical diagnosis, neuropathic pain)

💡 **Clinical Pearl**: ASA emphasizes that anesthesiologists responsible for perioperative analgesia should be available at all times to assist in evaluating assessment problems `[ASA:2012]`

## 7.3. PAIN CLASSIFICATION BY PATTERN AND MECHANISM

### 7.3.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part B:** Clinical categorization of pain [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Part C:** Application to treatment planning [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#3. Professionalism|ⓘ]]

**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Important clinical concept)



**Pain categories by pattern** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Background pain: Persistent baseline discomfort
* Intermittent pain: Recurring episodes between pain-free periods
* Breakthrough pain: Temporary flares despite analgesic treatment; Pain that escalates above a persistent background pain

**Pain categories by mechanism** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Nociceptive pain: Normal neural processing of tissue damage
* Neuropathic pain: Abnormal neural processing/nerve injury
* Mixed pain states: Combination of mechanisms
* ⚠️ Different mechanisms require different analgesic approaches

**Clinical application** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]] 
* Understanding the pattern and mechanism helps select appropriate management strategies
* For example, nociceptive pain typically responds well to anti-inflammatories and opioids, while neuropathic pain often requires adjuvant medications such as gabapentinoids, antidepressants, or NMDA antagonists
* Mixed pain states typically require a combination of therapeutic approaches

**Bottom Line:** Categorizing pain by both pattern (background, intermittent, breakthrough) and mechanism (nociceptive, neuropathic, mixed) provides crucial information for guiding appropriate treatment selection, as different pain types respond optimally to different analgesic strategies.

## 9.1. PATIENT-CONTROLLED ANALGESIA (PCA)

### 9.1.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Analgesic mechanisms of action [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* **Part B:** Clinical implementation of analgesic techniques [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Part C:** Evidence-based selection of analgesia [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]

**Exam Weighting:** High (Core clinical application)



### 9.1.2. PCA OVERVIEW AND VARIABLES

**PCA Overview** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Definition*: Any technique of pain management allowing patients to self-administer analgesia on demand [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Definition* (supplementary): One of the most significant advances in the treatment of postoperative pain ``
* Most common drugs in US:
	* **MORPHINE** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* **HYDROMORPHONE** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* **FENTANYL** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Drug selection considerations:
	* **HYDROMORPHONE**: Recommended alternative in renal failure [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* **FENTANYL**: Better choice in renal failure (no active metabolites) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* ⚠️ **MEPERIDINE** NOT recommended for IV PCA due to potentially toxic metabolite (normeperidine) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Evidence for efficacy** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]] 
	* Intravenous opioid PCA provides better analgesia than conventional parenteral opioid regimens
	* Opioid administration by IV PCA leads to higher opioid consumption, a higher incidence of pruritus, but no difference in other opioid-related adverse effects, or hospital stay compared with traditional methods of intermittent parenteral opioid administration
	* Patient satisfaction with intravenous PCA is higher when compared with conventional regimens
	* In settings where there are high nurse to patient ratios, there may be no difference in the effectiveness of PCA and conventional parenteral opioid regimens

**PCA Variables** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Five key variables for all PCA modes:
	1. Bolus dose
	2. Incremental (demand) dose
	3. Lockout interval
	4. Background infusion rate
	5. 1and 4-hour limits

**Mode of Administration** ``
* Different modes can be employed:
	* Patient-controlled, on-demand bolus administration
	* Continuous background infusion and patient-controlled bolus administration
* Routes of administration: Intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous, epidural

### 9.1.3. STANDARD PCA PROTOCOL

* *Typical regimen (healthy adult)*:
	* Incremental dose: 1-2 mg **MORPHINE**
	* Lockout interval: 8-10 minutes
* *Background infusion*:
	* 💡 Clinical Pearl: Not recommended in opioid-naive patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Reserve for:
		* Patients with chronic pain (malignant/nonmalignant) who are opioid tolerant
		* Patients with persistent pain who failed trial of incremental PCA dosing
	* 🏥 Clinical Practice: Consult pain management expert for opioid-tolerant patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* **Evidence on background infusions** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]] 
		* The addition of a background infusion to intravenous PCA morphine in adults increases the incidence of respiratory depression and does not improve pain relief or sleep, or reduce the number of PCA demands

| Drug | Concentration | Demand (PCA) Dose Range | Lockout Interval | Maximum in 4 h |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Morphine | 1 mg/mL | 0.5–2 mg | 5–10 minutes | 30 mg [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]] |
| Hydromorphone | 0.2 mg/mL | 0.1–0.4 mg | 10 min | 6 mg [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]] |
| Fentanyl | 10 mcg/mL | 5–20 mcg | 4–10 min | 300 mcg [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]] |

### 9.1.4. SPECIAL POPULATIONS

* *Elderly patients*: Decrease PCA dose
* *Higher-risk patients*: Consider avoiding standard PCA dosing with >2 risk factors

**Relative Risk Factors for PCA Use** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Pulmonary disease
* Obstructive sleep apnea
* Renal or hepatic dysfunction
* Congestive heart failure
* Closed head injury
* Altered mental status
* Lactating mothers

**Opioid-Related Side Effects and Management** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Nausea and vomiting*:
	* Management: Combinations of:
		* Dopamine antagonists
		* Serotonin antagonists
		* Neurokinin 1 (NK1) receptor antagonists
		* Glucocorticoids
		* Anticholinergics
		* Antihistamines
		* Nonpharmacologic prophylaxis
	* Prevention: Perioperative **DEXMEDETOMIDINE** (α2-agonist)
		* Provides analgesia and opioid-sparing effects
		* Reduces incidence of postoperative nausea/vomiting
	* **Evidence-based approaches** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]] 
		* Droperidol, metoclopramide, ondansetron, tropisetron, dolasetron, dexamethasone, cyclizine, granisetron, supplemental crystalloid infusions, palonosetron and mirtazapine are effective in the prevention of postoperative nausea and vomiting
		* PC6 acupoint stimulation by multiple techniques reduces postoperative nausea and vomiting
		* Neurokinin-1 receptor antagonists aprepitant and fosaprepitant are effective in the prevention of postoperative nausea and vomiting
		* Paracetamol given preoperatively and intraoperatively reduces postoperative nausea and vomiting; this effect is associated with improved analgesia, not reduced opioid requirements
* *Pruritus management*:
	* **DIPHENHYDRAMINE**
	* **HYDROXYZINE**
	* Low-dose opioid antagonist (e.g., **NALOXONE**)
	* Mixed agonist-antagonist (e.g., **NALBUPHINE**)
	* **Evidence-based approaches** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]] 
		* Naloxone, naltrexone, droperidol, nalbuphine and ondansetron are effective treatments for opioid-induced pruritus
* *Sedation and respiratory depression*:
	* Consider changing opioid
	* 💡 Clinical Pearl: Utilize multimodal analgesia for opioid-sparing effect:
		* Regional anesthesia (epidural/peripheral nerve blockade)
		* **NSAIDs**
		* **ACETAMINOPHEN**
		* NMDA receptor antagonists
		* α2-δ subunit calcium channel ligands
	* **Evidence for opioid-related adverse effects** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]] 
		* The incidence of clinically meaningful adverse effects (nausea, vomiting) of opioids is dose-related
		* Assessment of sedation is a more reliable way of detecting early opioid-induced ventilatory impairment than a decreased respiratory rate
		* Opioid-induced ventilatory impairment occurs in particular in the first 24 h after surgery and important risk factors are cardiac and pulmonary disease, obstructive sleep apnea and use of higher opioid doses
		* Continuous pulse oximetry in patients receiving opioids postoperatively increases detection rate of desaturation, but continuous capnography is superior in identifying episodes of opioid-induced ventilatory impairment

### 9.1.5. PCA EQUIPMENT AND SAFETY FEATURES

**Components** ``
1. Pump with accuracy of at least ±5% of programmed dose
2. Remote demand button connected to the pump and activated by the patient
3. Antisiphon and backflow valve

**Safety features** ``
1. Lockout interval prevents excessive dosing
2. Tamper-resistant features
3. Accidental triggering prevention (requires two successive presses within 1 second)
4. Alarms for malfunction, occlusion, and disconnection
5. Memory capabilities for retaining programming during syringe changes
6. 1-hour and 4-hour dose limits

**Problems and safety considerations** ``
1. Requires patient cooperation and understanding
2. Availability of trained staff to program and monitor
3. Patient may awaken in severe pain if no doses administered during sleep
4. ⚠️ Technical errors can be fatal due to potential for opiate overdose
5. **Evidence on safety issues** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]] 
	* The safety of PCA use can be significantly improved by hospital-wide safety initiatives (equipment, guidelines, education, monitoring)
	* The adoption of "smart pump" technologies in PCA design can improve documentation of patient care, reduce programming errors and improve safety
	* Operator-error, in particular programming error, remains a common safety problem with PCA use often leading to patient harm
	* Costs from PCA errors can be considerable; the most common high cost errors arise from staff communication error and operator error

**Bottom Line:** Patient-controlled analgesia provides effective pain management by allowing patients to self-administer preset doses of analgesics, but requires careful consideration of patient factors, appropriate dosing parameters, and monitoring for side effects, which can be managed with adjunctive treatments.

### 9.2.3. EPIDURAL ANALGESIA

**Importance and Efficacy** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Critical component of multimodal perioperative pain management
* Meta-analysis: Superior to systemic opioids
* 🔑 HY: Improves patient outcomes
* **Evidence for efficacy** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]] 
	* For all types of surgery, epidural analgesia provides better postoperative pain relief compared with parenteral (including PCA) opioid administration; except epidural analgesia using a hydrophilic opioid only
	* Thoracic epidural analgesia for open abdominal aortic surgery reduces pain intensity, time to tracheal extubation, time spent in the intensive care unit, rate of acute respiratory failure, myocardial infarction and gastrointestinal bleeding when compared with intravenous opioids
	* High thoracic epidural analgesia used for coronary artery bypass graft surgery reduces postoperative pain, risk of dysrhythmias, pulmonary complications and time to extubation when compared with intravenous opioid analgesia
	* Thoracic epidural analgesia for thoracotomy reduces the risk of chronic postsurgical pain
	* Thoracic epidural analgesia improves bowel recovery after abdominal surgery (including colorectal surgery)
	* Epidural analgesia is not associated with increased risk of anastomotic leakage after bowel surgery

**Factors Determining Efficacy** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
1. Appropriate catheter placement relative to incision (congruency)
2. Choice of analgesic drugs
3. Rates of infusion
4. Duration of epidural analgesia
5. Type of pain assessment (rest vs. dynamic)

**Catheter Placement** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Ideal placement*: Congruent with surgical incision
* *Thoracic epidural recommendations*:
	* Thoracic procedures
	* Upper abdominal procedures
	* Benefits:
		* Improved coronary artery blood flow
		* Reduced pulmonary complications
		* Reduced duration of postoperative ileus

	![image.png](ACUTE_ACUTE_ACUTE_ACUTE_ACUTE_image_1.png_3ffee2.png_a1c910.png_34eb1c.png_f14e44.png_52a18a.png)

**Duration** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Recommended duration: At least 2-4 days
* Infusions <24 hours: No clear cardiovascular advantages

**Epidural Opioids** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Advantages*: Analgesia without significant sympatholytic effect or motor blockade
* *Mechanism of action*:
	1. Spinal mechanism (diffusion into spinal fluid) determined by meningeal permeability
	2. Supraspinal mechanism following systemic absorption
* *Meningeal permeability*:
	* *Intermediate lipophilicity opioids*: Easy movement between aqueous and lipid regions
		* Examples: **HYDROMORPHONE**, **ALFENTANIL**, **MEPERIDINE**
	* *Bioavailability comparison*: **MORPHINE** > **ALFENTANIL**, **FENTANYL**, **SUFENTANIL**
	* *Opioid-tolerant patients*: **SUFENTANIL** recommended for patients taking >250 mg/d oral morphine (high intrinsic activity)

**Pharmacokinetics by Lipid Solubility** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Hydrophilic opioids*:
	* Onset: Slow
	* Duration: Long
	* Mechanism: Primarily spinal
* *Lipophilic opioids*:
	* Onset: Quick
	* Duration: Short
	* Mechanism: Primarily supraspinal (rapid systemic uptake)
	* 📋 MNEMONIC: "Lipo-Quick" (Lipophilic = Quick onset, quick offset)

**Mode of Administration Effects** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **FENTANYL** bolus: Segmental analgesic effect
* **FENTANYL** epidural infusion: Nonsegmental (systemic) effect
* *Exception*: Thoracic epidural **FENTANYL** may have significant spinal mechanisms

**Local Anesthetic + Opioid Combinations** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* 🔑 HY: Believed to have synergistic effect
* *Unique benefit of local anesthetics*: Block stress response by blocking afferent input to spinal cord
* *Common combinations*:
	* **BUPIVACAINE** + **FENTANYL** (most common)
	* **BUPIVACAINE** + **MORPHINE** (reasonable option)
	* **BUPIVACAINE** + **HYDROMORPHONE** (combines advantages of hydrophilic opioid with excellent meningeal permeability)
* **Evidence for combination therapy** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]] 
	* The quality of epidural analgesia with local anaesthetics is improved with the addition of opioids
	* Epidural morphine provides similar analgesia to epidural fentanyl when combined with local anaesthetic, although the incidence of nausea is greater with morphine

**Clinical Practice Note** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* 🏥 Clinical Practice: Authors' preference is catheter placement congruent with surgical incision + local anesthetic without opioid
* Beneficial for patients who may not tolerate opioid side effects (e.g., pruritus)

**Epidural Adjuvants** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **CLONIDINE**:
	* Enhances analgesia
	* Typical concentration: 2 μg/mL
	* Infusion rate: 5-20 μg/h
	* ⚠️ Limiting side effects: Hypotension, bradycardia, sedation
	* Example combination: **ROPIVACAINE** 0.2% + **FENTANYL** 5 μg/mL + **CLONIDINE** 2 μg/mL at 3-7 mL/h (reported no significant sedation in TKA)

**Safety Consideration** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* ⚠️ Not recommended to administer opioids by multiple simultaneous routes (risk of additive respiratory depression)
* Do not combine systemic opioid (e.g., PCA) with neuraxial opioid (e.g., epidural morphine)
* **Safety evidence** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]] 
	* The incidence of permanent neurological damage in association with epidural analgesia is extremely low, especially in the obstetric population, but increases with various comorbidities and risk factors; the incidence is higher where there have been delays in diagnosing an epidural haematoma or abscess
	* Immediate decompression of an epidural haematoma (within 8 hours of the onset of neurological signs) increases the likelihood of partial or good neurological recovery
	* Epidural abscesses present mainly with neurological deficits and back pain; they are best diagnosed with early MRI and best treated with empiric antibiotics (until abscess culture) and immediate surgical decompression when neurological deficits are present
	* Chlorhexidine‐impregnated dressings of epidural catheters in comparison to placeboor povidone‐iodine‐impregnated dressings reduce the incidence of catheter colonisation

### 9.2.4. INTRATHECAL ANALGESIA

**Overview** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Widely accepted for treatment of both acute and chronic pain
* Common opioid analgesics used:
	* **MORPHINE**
	* **HYDROMORPHONE**
	* **MEPERIDINE**
	* **METHADONE**
	* **FENTANYL**
	* **SUFENTANIL**

**Pharmacokinetics by Lipid Solubility** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Hydrophilic Opioids (e.g., MORPHINE)** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Mechanism*: Penetrate spinal cord and bind to specific pre/post-synaptic receptors in dorsal horn
* *Dural crossing*: Slow
* *Binding to epidural fat*: Poor
* *Plasma entry*: Slow
* *Clinical characteristics*:
	* Onset: Slow
	* Duration: Long
	* Analgesia breadth: Broad band
	* ⚠️ Delayed respiratory depression more common (rostral spread)
* **Evidence for efficacy** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]] 
	* Intrathecal morphine and intrathecal fentanyl prolong spinal local anaesthetic block, with fentanyl being associated with fewer adverse effects
	* Intrathecal morphine produces better postoperative analgesia than intrathecal fentanyl or sufentanil after Caesarean section
	* Intrathecal morphine doses of 300 mcg or more increase the risk of respiratory depression

**Lipophilic Opioids (e.g., FENTANYL)** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Mechanism*: Bind to nonspecific receptors in white matter
* *Dural crossing*: Rapid
* *Sequestration into epidural fat*: Quick
* *Systemic circulation entry*: Swift
* *Clinical characteristics*:
	* Onset: Rapid
	* Duration: Short
	* Analgesia breadth: Narrow band
	* Delayed respiratory depression: Less problematic

**Side Effects of Intrathecal Opioids** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Nausea and vomiting
* Urinary retention
* Pruritus
* Respiratory depression (more with hydrophilic opioids)
* **Evidence on side effects** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]] 
	* The incidence of opioid-induced ventilatory impairment, pruritus and postoperative nausea and vomiting is higher with intrathecal morphine compared with intravenous PCA opioids
	* Pruritus with intrathecal opioids is dose-dependent and can be effectively prevented and treated with 5HT3 antagonists in non-obstetric patients, but only treated but not prevented in obstetric patients
	* Pruritus with intrathecal opioids cannot be treated with methylnaltrexone

### 9.2.5. INTRATHECAL ADJUVANTS

**Alpha-2 Agonists** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Mechanism*: Alter pain transmission by binding to pre/post-synaptic receptors in dorsal horn
* *Benefits of intrathecal CLONIDINE*:
	* Synergistic with spinal local anesthetics
	* Prolongs sensory and motor blockade
	* Less urinary retention than intrathecal morphine
	* No respiratory depression or pruritus
* ⚠️ **Important risk**: Doses >150 mcg increase hypotension, bradycardia, and nausea
* **Evidence for efficacy** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]] 
	* The addition of intrathecal magnesium to opioids and/or local anaesthetics results in slightly longer analgesia in non-obstetric patients
	* The addition of intrathecal clonidine to intrathecal morphine results in slightly longer analgesia and reduced opioid requirements

**Other Adjuvants** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* NSAIDs: Anecdotal reports suggest safety and efficacy (further research needed)
* *Investigational agents* (further research required):
	* Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors
	* NMDA receptor antagonists
	* Adenosine
	* Benzodiazepines

### 9.2.6. PROCEDURAL GUIDELINES FOR INTRATHECAL ANALGESIA

**Labor Analgesia** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **SUFENTANIL**: `Dose: 2.5-5 μg`

**Cesarean Section** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **MORPHINE**: `Dose: 100 μg`
* **CLONIDINE** add-on option: `Dose: 60 μg`
	* 💡 Clinical Pearl: Synergistic, increases duration of spinal analgesia
	* ⚠️ Warning: Increases intraoperative sedation and hypotension

**Outpatient Knee Arthroscopy** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **FENTANYL**: `Dose: 10-25 μg`
	* Improves intraoperative analgesia without prolonging postoperative motor blockade
	* 🏥 Clinical Practice: Authors prefer motor-sparing nerve blocks with elastomeric pump for 48-72h postoperative analgesia

**Total Knee Arthroplasty (TKA)** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Traditional recommendation: **MORPHINE** 100 μg for inpatients
* 🏥 Clinical Practice: Authors avoid intrathecal morphine, preferring targeted motor-sparing peripheral nerve blockade + elastomeric pump with local anesthetic (48-72h analgesia)

**Total Hip Arthroplasty (THA)** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Traditional recommendation: **MORPHINE** 100 μg
* 🏥 Clinical Practice: Authors avoid intrathecal opioids, preferring opioid-free postoperative analgesia via:
	* Fascia iliaca block
	* Pericapsular nerve group block (PENG)
	* Quadratus lumborum block

**Thoracotomy and Major Abdominal Surgery** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Historical doses: **MORPHINE** up to 500 μg
* ⚠️ Current recommendation: Avoid doses >300 μg due to increased side effects:
	* Nausea and vomiting
	* Urinary retention
	* Pruritus
	* Respiratory depression
* 🏥 Clinical Practice: Authors suggest alternative techniques:
	* Thoracic epidural
	* Paravertebral block
	* Erector spinae plane block

**Equivalence Note** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* 50-100 μg intrathecal **HYDROMORPHONE** ≈ 100-200 μg intrathecal **MORPHINE**

**Other Intrathecal Adjuvant Dosing** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **CLONIDINE**: `Dose: 15-45 μg`
	* Improves quality of spinal blockade in outpatient surgery
	* ⚠️ Side effects increase significantly at doses >150 μg
* **EPINEPHRINE**: `Dose: 0.1-0.6 mg`
	* Not recommended for outpatient surgery
	* Dose-related increase in time for:
		1. Return of motor function
		2. Return of micturition

### 9.2.7. EPIDURAL CATHETER DOSING GUIDELINES BY REGION

**Lumbar Catheter Placement (T12-caudal dermatomes)** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Example procedures*: Total knee arthroplasty, lower extremity bypass surgery
* *Congruent catheter*:
	* **BUPIVACAINE** 0.05-0.1% or **ROPIVACAINE** 0.1-0.2% with/without opioid:
		* **FENTANYL** 2-5 μg/mL or
		* **MORPHINE** 0.1 mg/mL or
		* **HYDROMORPHONE** 0.02 mg/mL
* *Incongruent catheter*:
	* Consider bupivacaine/ropivacaine with hydrophilic opioid
	* ⚠️ Not ideal!

**Low Thoracic Catheter Placement (T8-T12 dermatomes)** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Example procedures*: Exploratory laparotomy, xiphopubic incision
* *Congruent catheter*:
	* **BUPIVACAINE** 0.05-0.1% or **ROPIVACAINE** 0.1-0.2% with/without opioid:
		* **FENTANYL** 2-5 μg/mL or
		* **MORPHINE** 0.1 mg/mL or
		* **HYDROMORPHONE** 0.02 mg/mL
* *Incongruent catheter*:
	* Consider bupivacaine/ropivacaine with opioid
	* ⚠️ Not ideal!
	* **FENTANYL** 2-5 μg/mL is not ideal opioid
	* Better choices: **MORPHINE** 0.1 mg/mL or **HYDROMORPHONE** 0.02 mg/mL

**Mid-to-High Thoracic Catheter Placement (T4-T8 dermatomes)** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Example procedures*: Thoracotomy, sternotomy
* *Congruent catheter*:
	* **BUPIVACAINE** 0.05-0.1% or **ROPIVACAINE** 0.1-0.2% with/without opioid:
		* **FENTANYL** 2-5 μg/mL or
		* **MORPHINE** 0.1 mg/mL or
		* **HYDROMORPHONE** 0.02 mg/mL

**Additional Guidelines** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Rate of infusion: 2-10 mL/h
* ⚠️ Max **BUPIVACAINE** dose: Do not exceed 400 mg/24h
* **CLONIDINE** option: 1-2 μg/mL
	* ⚠️ Warning: Hypotension, bradycardia, and sedation common at doses >14 μg/h
* 🔑 HY: Local anesthetic efficacy diminished if catheter placement not congruent with surgical incision
* 💡 Clinical Pearl: Hydrophilic opioids provide broad band of analgesia
	* **MORPHINE** is gold standard
	* **HYDROMORPHONE** may cause less pruritus

⚠️ CRITICAL SAFETY WARNING: Use of multiple routes of opioid administration (e.g., PCA plus epidural) is NOT recommended! Risk of respiratory depression is increased.

**Bottom Line:** Neuraxial analgesia provides superior pain relief when properly implemented, with consideration of pharmacokinetic properties based on lipid solubility, catheter placement congruent to the surgical site, appropriate medication selection, and avoidance of simultaneous administration of opioids by multiple routes.

### 9.3.2. NERVE BLOCK NEEDLES

**Components** ``
* Steel construction with short blunt bevels to minimize nerve trauma ``
* Transparent hubs for earlier recognition of intravascular placement ``
* Port for injecting local anesthetic solution ``
* Connection to nerve stimulator via insulated cable ``
* Available in different sizes, with 22G optimal for most blocks ``
* Different lengths based on depth of nerve/plexus ``:
	* Interscalene block: 25-50 mm
	* Axillary block: 35-50 mm
	* Psoas compartment block: 80-120 mm
	* Femoral nerve block: 50 mm
	* Sciatic nerve block: 80-150 mm

**Mechanism of action** ``
* Blunt tip pushes nerve ahead rather than piercing it ``
* Provides better tactile feedback through tissue layers ``
* Nerve location verified by electrical stimulation with characteristic muscle response ``
* Optimal placement shown by maximum stimulation at minimal current (0.2-0.4 mA) ``
* Currents <0.2 mA may indicate intraneural needle placement (avoid) ``
* Muscle twitching should decrease after injecting small volume (2 mL) of local anesthetic ``
* Persistent twitching or patient pain may indicate intraneural placement ``

**Needle types** ``
* **Insulated needles**:
	* Teflon-coated with exposed tips
	* Current passes only through tip
	* Allows more precise localization with smaller current
	* Slightly larger diameter than noninsulated needles
* **Noninsulated needles**:
	* Current passes through both tip and shaft
	* Maximum current density at tip due to lower resistance
	* Risk of nerve stimulation via shaft (may result in ineffective block)

**Safety considerations** ``
* Immobile needle technique recommended for major blocks ``
* Continuous nerve blocks possible with catheter insertion ``
* Stimulating catheters available for precise placement ``
* Essential to avoid intraneural injections ``
* Injection pressure monitoring important (avoid >20 psi) ``
* Specialized devices like NerveGuard prevent high-pressure injection ``
* **Evidence for ultrasound guidance** ``
	* Blocks performed using ultrasound guidance are more likely to be successful, faster to perform, with faster onset and longer duration compared with localisation using a peripheral nerve stimulator
	* Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity is reduced by the use of ultrasound guidance for regional anaesthesia
	* Ultrasound guidance reduces the risk of vascular puncture during the performance of regional blocks

![Nerve Block Needle](ACUTE_ACUTE_ACUTE_ACUTE_pdf_PAIN.pdf_p36_img1_388c69.jpeg_8c1294.jpeg_5fa368.jpeg_ef2c0f.jpeg) ``

### 9.3.3. NERVE STIMULATORS

**Components** ``
* Device case with on/off switch and current amplitude dial ``
* Two leads: one to ECG skin electrode, one to locating needle ``
* Clear polarity indication (negative lead to needle) ``

**Mechanism of action** ``
* Delivers small constant current (0.25-0.5 mA) to stimulate motor fibers ``
* Negative (cathode) lead causes depolarization and action potentials ``
* Frequency set at 1-2 Hz for comfortable yet frequent feedback ``
* Short stimulus duration (50-100 ms) generates painless motor contraction ``
* Battery operation for improved patient safety ``

**Ideal characteristics** ``
* Constant current output despite resistance changes ``
* Clear digital display to 0.1 mA ``
* Variable output control with linear output ``
* Clearly marked polarity ``
* Short pulse width, pulse of 1-2 Hz ``
* Battery indicator ``
* High-quality clips with low resistance ``

**Problems and safety considerations** ``
* High currents may stimulate fibers even when needle tip is distant from nerve ``
* Ground electrode should have good contact with clear, dry skin ``
* Avoid placing ground over superficial nerves or allowing current through myocardium ``
* Connection/disconnection indicators ensure awareness of stimulus delivery ``
* Never use neuromuscular blockade monitors for nerve blocks (excessive current) ``
* Not a substitute for knowledge of neuroanatomy ``

![Nerve Stimulator](ACUTE_ACUTE_ACUTE_ACUTE_pdf_PAIN.pdf_p43_img1_db4ce0.jpeg_4a6bad.jpeg_e468b8.jpeg_0e7ecc.jpeg) ``

### 9.3.4. ULTRASOUND GUIDANCE IN REGIONAL ANESTHESIA

**Key principles** ``
* Now standard practice in regional anesthesia ``
* Associated with higher success rates and fewer complications ``
* Allows direct visualization of nerves and surrounding structures ``
* Enables real-time visualization of needle advancement and local anesthetic spread ``

**Equipment considerations** ``
* Specially designed echogenic needles enhance ultrasound visibility ``
* Frequency range 10-14 MHz optimal for most nerve blocks ``
* Linear array transducers preferred (parallel sound beam emission) ``
* Curved arrays useful for deeper structures (require lower frequency) ``
* Smaller footprint probes useful for pediatric patients and very superficial blocks ``

**Technical aspects** ``
* True nerve echogenicity best captured with sound beam perpendicular to nerve axis ``
* Continuous blocks possible with catheter insertion under ultrasound guidance ``
* Portable ultrasound units available for increased flexibility ``

![Ultrasound-Guided Block Set](ACUTE_ACUTE_ACUTE_ACUTE_pdf_PAIN.pdf_p50_img1_be09b7.jpeg_13c5f5.jpeg_ea95d5.jpeg_2bf917.jpeg) ``

**Bottom Line:** Regional anesthesia equipment has evolved to include specialized needles (insulated and noninsulated), nerve stimulators with precise current delivery capabilities, and ultrasound guidance technology. Each of these components contributes to safer, more accurate nerve blocks by allowing precise needle placement with minimal nerve trauma and optimal local anesthetic distribution.

### 9.4.2. ELASTOMERIC PUMPS

**Components** ``
* Small balloon-like pump filled with local anesthetic (100-600 mL capacity) ``
* Specially designed catheters (7-30 cm lengths, various gauges) ``
* Bacterial filter and flow restrictor ``
* Multiple-orifice catheters for wider distribution ``

**Mechanism of action** ``
* Balloon deflates slowly, delivering predetermined amount of local anesthetic ``
* Flow rates of 2-14 mL/h available ``
* Some models include on-demand bolus facility (5 mL with 60-minute lockout) ``
* Some designs allow simultaneous infusion at two surgical sites ``
* Silver-coated antimicrobial dressing provided ``

**Clinical applications** ``
* Continuous regional anesthesia for postoperative pain management ``
* Allows ambulatory use for outpatient procedures ``
* Facilitates earlier discharge with ongoing pain control ``

**Problems and safety considerations** ``
* Local anesthetic absorption into balloon ``
* Variable infusion rate profile (typically higher initially, decreasing over time) ``
* Temperature sensitivity affecting flow rate ``
* Positioning relative to insertion site important ``

### 9.4.3. SYRINGE PUMPS

**Characteristics** ``
* Programmable pumps for variable infusion rates and bolus administration ``
* Pulsatile continuous delivery with ±2-5% accuracy ``
* Can accept different syringe sizes ``
* Battery and/or mains power sources ``

**Problems and safety considerations** ``
* Ensure unidirectional flow with antisiphon valves ``
* Syringe must be securely clamped to pump ``
* Position pump at or below patient level (not >100 cm above) ``
* 'Back-off' function prevents bolus after obstruction clearance ``
* Antireflux valves needed for secondary lines ``
* Smart infusion pumps may include dose/rate limits and drug interaction alerts ``
* Require two distinct actions to change rate or give bolus ``

![Syringe Pump](ACUTE_ACUTE_ACUTE_ACUTE_pdf_PAIN.pdf_p6_img1_1309a4.jpeg_11e7c7.jpeg_9e4b60.jpeg_a31ec5.jpeg) ``

### 9.4.4. VOLUMETRIC PUMPS

**Characteristics** ``
* Designed for use with specific giving set tubing ``
* More suitable when total volume accuracy is more important than precise flow rate ``
* Accuracy within ±5-10% ``
* Sensitive to giving set tubing internal diameter ``
* Various mechanisms: peristaltic, cassette, reservoir systems ``
* Battery and/or mains power sources ``

### 9.4.5. TARGET-CONTROLLED INFUSION PUMPS

**Characteristics** ``
* Advanced software technology using patient parameters (age, weight) ``
* Input desired drug plasma concentration rather than infusion rate ``
* Software estimates plasma and effect-site (brain) concentrations ``
* Primarily used for propofol and remifentanil infusions ``
* Allows precise titration based on pharmacokinetic models ``

### 9.4.6. PENTHROX INHALER SYSTEM

**Overview** ``
* Delivers methoxyflurane vapor in subanaesthetic concentrations for analgesia ``
* Discontinued for routine anesthesia due to nephrotoxicity at high doses ``
* Safe and effective at controlled subanaesthetic doses for trauma pain ``
* Rapid onset (4-5 minutes) for short-term pain relief ``
* Minimal cardiovascular and respiratory effects ``

**Components** ``
* Cylindrical tube with mouthpiece ``
* Polypropylene wick (accommodates 3 mL methoxyflurane) ``
* Inlet chamber for methoxyflurane and fresh air addition ``
* Activated charcoal chamber to absorb exhaled methoxyflurane ``

**Mechanism of action** ``
* Wick gradually vaporizes methoxyflurane ``
* Patient inhales and exhales through mouthpiece ``
* 3 mL provides 25-30 minutes of analgesia with continuous use ``
* Covering inlet chamber with finger provides higher concentration for stronger analgesia ``
* Activated charcoal captures exhaled drug to reduce pollution ``

**Safety considerations** ``
* Maximum daily dose: 6 mL (two vials) ``
* Maximum weekly dose: 15 mL (five vials) ``
* No nephrotoxicity risk at these subanaesthetic doses ``

![Penthrox Inhaler](ACUTE_ACUTE_ACUTE_ACUTE_pdf_PAIN.pdf_p13_img1_325a21.jpeg_480d0c.jpeg_8b1215.jpeg_2d4dfd.jpeg) ``

**Bottom Line:** Various specialized delivery systems are available for analgesia administration, including elastomeric pumps for continuous local anesthetic infusion, programmable syringe and volumetric pumps for precise medication delivery, target-controlled infusion pumps for pharmacokinetic-based administration, and the Penthrox inhaler for self-administered methoxyflurane analgesia. Each system has unique features, applications, and safety considerations that must be understood for appropriate use.

## 10.1. CHRONIC PAIN DEFINITIONS

### 10.1.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Pathophysiology of pain and opioid dependence [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* **Part B:** Clinical management of opioid-dependent patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Part C:** Systems approach to opioid dependence [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]

**Exam Weighting:** High (Increasingly prevalent clinical scenario)



**Chronic pain classifications** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* *Traditional definition*: "Pain without apparent biologic value that has persisted beyond normal tissue healing time (3 months)" (International Association for the Study of Pain)
* *Alternative definition*: "Pain of a duration or intensity that adversely affects the function or well-being of the patient" (American Society of Anesthesiologists)
* **Chronic primary pain**: Persists/recurs >3 months in one or more anatomical regions with:
	* Significant emotional distress or functional disability
	* Interference with activities of daily life and social roles
	* Cannot be better accounted for by another chronic pain condition
* **Chronic secondary pain syndromes**: Linked to underlying diseases, including:
	* Chronic cancer-related pain
	* Chronic postsurgical or post-traumatic pain
	* Chronic neuropathic pain
	* Chronic headache and orofacial pain
	* Chronic visceral pain
	* Chronic musculoskeletal pain

**Common chronic pain characteristics** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Tight musculature
* Limited range of motion
* Lack of energy
* Sleep disturbance
* Irritability
* Social withdrawal
* Associated psychiatric diagnoses (hypochondriasis, psychosis)
* Often requires treatment with multiple drug classes:
	* Anxiolytics
	* Antidepressants
	* Anticonvulsants
	* Antiarrhythmics
	* Skeletal muscle relaxants
	* Opioids

**Bottom Line:** Chronic pain is defined by persistence beyond the expected healing period, significant functional impact, and often requires multimodal management, with classifications distinguishing between primary pain and secondary pain syndromes associated with specific underlying conditions.

## 10.2. OPIOID CRISIS OVERVIEW

### 10.2.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Historical development of opioid crisis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Part C:** Public health implications and systems approaches [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#5. Practice-Based Learning and Improvement|ⓘ]]

**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Important contextual knowledge)



**Historical development of opioid crisis** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Late 1990s: Dramatic increase in chronic opioid prescriptions in USA
* Contributing factors:
	* More aggressive treatment of cancer pain in patients surviving longer
	* Medical community willingness to treat nonmalignant pain (e.g., osteoarthritis) with opioids

**Goal vs. Reality of chronic opioid therapy** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* Goal: Improve pain, function, and quality of life
* Reality: No corresponding reductions in disability rates or improvements in health status
* Many patients abandoned therapy due to:
	* Unacceptable side effects
	* Concerns about adverse hormonal effects
	* Immune modulation from long-term exposure

**The three waves of the opioid crisis** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* **Wave 1 (1990s)**: Excessive prescribing of Oxycontin™ due to deceptive marketing claiming reduced addiction potential
* **Wave 2 (2010)**: Drug traffickers marketing heroin to individuals addicted to prescription opioids
* **Wave 3 (2014)**: Illicit manufacturers contaminating counterfeit pharmaceutical pills with fentanyl
* COVID-19 pandemic exacerbated these issues

**Mortality impact** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* 600,000 individuals died from opioid overdose in US/Canada since 1999
* CDC data: 75,673 overdose deaths from opioids (12-month period ending April 2021) ↑ from 56,064 the prior year

**Economic impact** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]] 
* Long term economic consequences from the progression of acute to chronic postsurgical and post-traumatic pain can be significant
* Strategies to optimize acute and subacute pain management (including involvement of transitional pain services) may reduce the economic burden of chronic pain and inappropriate prescription opioid use
* The early pattern of prescription opioid use after surgery may increase the risk of chronic use with significant direct and indirect economic costs
* Prescription drug monitoring may reduce the economic burden through its impact on inappropriate opioid prescribing

**Bottom Line:** The opioid crisis has evolved through three distinct waves, beginning with increased prescription opioid use, followed by heroin trafficking targeting prescription opioid users, and most recently involving illicitly manufactured fentanyl, resulting in hundreds of thousands of deaths and necessitating comprehensive public health responses.

## 10.3. KEY TERMINOLOGY

### 10.3.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Definitions related to opioid dependence [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* **Part B:** Clinical implications of opioid tolerance [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Part C:** Management approaches based on terminology [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#5. Practice-Based Learning and Improvement|ⓘ]]

**Exam Weighting:** High (Essential knowledge)



**Opioid tolerance** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *FDA definition*: Use of oral morphine equivalent ≥60 mg/day for ≥7 days
* Clinical impact: Potentially complicated hospital course with:
	* Increased hospital length of stay (LOS)
	* High readmission rate
* **Multimodal approach highly recommended** for opioid-tolerant patients

**Physical dependence** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* *Definition*: Physiologic state of adaptation to a specific psychoactive substance
* Characterized by withdrawal syndrome during abstinence
* Relieved by re-administration of the substance
* **Opioid withdrawal symptoms**: Increased sympathetic and parasympathetic response
	* Hypertension
	* Tachycardia
	* Diaphoresis
	* Abdominal cramping
	* Diarrhea

**Tolerance** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.2 Core Anaesthetic Drugs|ⓘ]]
* *Definition*: State requiring increased dosage to produce desired effect (rightward shift of dose-response curve)
* Escalating doses may also be due to:
	* Underlying disease progression
	* Development of opioid-induced hyperalgesia (OIH)

**Types of tolerance** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.3 Drugs for Management of Monitored Parameters/Major Systems of Anaesthetic Interest|ⓘ]]
* **Innate tolerance**: Genetically predetermined drug sensitivity
* **Acquired tolerance**:
	* *Pharmacokinetic tolerance*: Diminished drug effects from distribution/metabolism changes (CYP450 enzyme induction → accelerated metabolism)
	* *Learned tolerance*: Compensatory behavior masking intoxication
	* *Pharmacodynamic tolerance*: Neuroadaptive changes after chronic opioid exposure
		* Receptor desensitization from downregulation
		* Receptor internalization
		* Uncoupling of opioid receptors from G proteins

**Cross-tolerance characteristics** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.3 Drugs for Management of Monitored Parameters/Major Systems of Anaesthetic Interest|ⓘ]]
* Opioids exhibit cross-tolerance to each other
* Degree varies widely and is often incomplete
* Clinical application: Opioid rotation restores analgesic sensitivity in highly tolerant patients
* Due to incomplete cross-tolerance, analgesia is restored with new opioid at >50% below predicted equianalgesic dose

**Differential tolerance** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.3 Drugs for Management of Monitored Parameters/Major Systems of Anaesthetic Interest|ⓘ]]
* Different targets of opioid drugs develop tolerance at different speeds/degrees
* Tolerance develops fastest to analgesic effects
* Tolerance for respiratory depression and GI effects significantly delayed
* ⚠️ **Clinical implication**: Opioid-tolerant patients at significant risk for respiratory depression postoperatively when pain is treated with opioids

💡 **Clinical Pearl**: Physical dependence and tolerance do NOT imply addiction [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.3 Drugs for Management of Monitored Parameters/Major Systems of Anaesthetic Interest|ⓘ]]

**Addiction** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.3 Drugs for Management of Monitored Parameters/Major Systems of Anaesthetic Interest|ⓘ]]
* *American Society of Addiction Medicine definition*: "A treatable, chronic medical disease involving complex interactions among brain circuits, genetics, the environment, and an individual's life experiences"
* Characterized by compulsive substance use/behaviors despite harmful consequences

**Bottom Line:** Understanding the distinctions between tolerance (requiring increased doses for effect), physical dependence (withdrawal upon cessation), and addiction (compulsive use despite harm) is critical for appropriate management of opioid-dependent patients, with particular awareness that tolerance develops at different rates for analgesia versus respiratory depression.

* Customizable to institution
* Resources can be shared to reduce expenses

**Evidence for effectiveness** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]] 
* Implementation of transitional pain services may help manage the complex issues of prolonged postoperative opioid use and chronic postsurgical pain
* The recruitment of patients 'at-risk' for persistent pain and/or excessive opioid use into a post-discharge treatment service for early review can improve outcomes
* Strategies to optimize acute and subacute pain management (including involvement of transitional pain services) may reduce the economic burden of chronic pain and inappropriate prescription opioid use

🏥 **Clinical Practice**: TPS implementation has been shown to significantly reduce overall opioid use following orthopedic surgery and demonstrate a positive economic impact [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

⚠️ **Key concept**: Patients receiving opioids preoperatively are at increased risk for adverse outcomes during the perioperative period [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Transitional Pain Services bridge the gap between acute and chronic pain management for complex patients through multidisciplinary, multimodal approaches. They can be implemented in various models from high-intensity to low-intensity, with evidence suggesting positive economic impacts and reduced opioid use even with less resource-intensive implementations.

## 10.5. PERIOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT

### 10.5.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part B:** Clinical perioperative management [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Part C:** Systems-based approaches to perioperative care [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]

**Exam Weighting:** High (Critical perioperative management skill)



### 10.5.2. PREOPERATIVE IDENTIFICATION AND STRATIFICATION

* **Prescription Drug Monitoring Program (PDMP)** should be queried preoperatively [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* In California: Controlled Substance Utilization Review and Evaluation System (CURES)
	* Patient Activity Report (PAR) provides: prescribed drug, dose, dispensing date, patient name, prescribing physician, and pharmacy
* **O-NET Classification System** categorizes patients by preoperative opioid use [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* *Opioid naive*: No opioids in previous 90 days
	* *Opioid exposed*: History of <60 mg/d oral morphine equivalent in previous 90 days
	* *Opioid tolerant*: >60 mg/d oral morphine equivalent in 7 days prior to surgery
		* ⚠️ Higher risk for complicated hospital course with delayed wound healing, increased surgical reintervention, prolonged hospital stays, higher readmission rates, greater costs, and increased mortality
* **Risk Stratification** based on comorbidities and surgery type [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Low-risk
	* Moderate-risk
	* High-risk
	* 🔑 HY: Identifying and managing modifiable risk factors improves surgical outcomes

### 10.5.3. PERIOPERATIVE PLANNING

* **Responsibility for patient identification**: Surgical team, preoperative evaluation staff, and anesthesia team [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
	* Ideally: Formulate perioperative pain management plan before surgery
	* When available: Evaluation by Transitional Pain Service (TPS)
	* Reality: Often identified just before surgery requiring innovative approaches
* **Baseline Opioid Management** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Continue patient's normal opioid dose on day of surgery
	* If patient forgets dose: Administer equivalent dose preoperatively
	* 💡 Clinical Pearl: Opioid-dependent patients have 30-100% greater opioid requirements than opioid-naive patients
	* Continue baseline opioid regimen during hospital admission

### 10.5.4. SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS FOR SPECIFIC OPIOIDS

* **Transdermal Fentanyl Patches** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Usually maintained during surgery to serve as baseline opioid
	* For major surgery or risk of blood loss/sepsis: Discontinue patch and initiate IV fentanyl infusion
* **Methadone** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Continue baseline dose throughout perioperative period
	* Available in both oral and IV forms in US
	* ⚠️ Patients receiving >200 mg/day: Risk of prolonged QT interval and Torsades de Points
		* Obtain baseline ECG for comparison
* **Buprenorphine** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Continue for postoperative pain control
	* Supplement with morphine, hydromorphone, or fentanyl as needed
* **Medications to Avoid** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Full antagonists (naloxone, naltrexone)
	* Partial agonists-antagonists (nalbuphine, pentazocine, butorphanol)
	* ⚠️ These will precipitate withdrawal symptoms

### 10.5.5. MULTIMODAL ANALGESIA FOR OPIOID-TOLERANT PATIENTS

* **Recommended Multimodal Components** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* **Gabapentinoids**:
		* Bind to α-2δ subunit of voltage-gated P/Q-type calcium channels in dorsal horn
		* Inhibit pain transmission and central sensitization by modulating excitatory neurotransmitter release
		* Pregabalin may be preferred over gabapentin due to superior bioavailability and better CNS diffusion
	* **NSAIDs/COX-2 Inhibitors** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
		* Proven efficacy for postoperative pain
		* Significant opioid-sparing effect with reduction in opioid-related side effects
		* **CELECOXIB**: 400 mg orally 2 hours prior to surgery
	* **ACETAMINOPHEN** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
		* Effective adjuvant with opioid-sparing effect
		* Multiple mechanisms: COX inhibition, serotonergic pathway modulation, TRPV1/NMDA interaction, endocannabinoid system effects
		* IV administration: Earlier/higher peak plasma levels than oral route
		* Dosing: 1,000 mg IV q6h (for patients >50 kg)
		* 💡 Clinical Pearl: Combination of NSAID + acetaminophen produces superior analgesia than either drug alone
	* **DEXAMETHASONE** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
		* Beneficial due to decreased neurotransmitter release and decreased production of NMDA receptor antagonist kynurenic acid
		* Dosing: 0.11-0.2 mg/kg IV preoperatively
	* **KETAMINE (NMDA Antagonist)** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
		* Effective at reversing morphine tolerance and restoring analgesic effectiveness
		* Intraoperative dosing: 0.5 mg/kg bolus → 10 mcg/kg/min infusion
		* May continue low-dose for up to 48 hours postoperatively
	* **α2-Adrenoreceptor Agonists** (dexmedetomidine, clonidine) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
		* Moderate analgesic and opioid-sparing effects
		* Sedating and anxiolytic
		* Decrease surgical stress response
		* Mitigate postoperative shivering, nausea, vomiting, and agitation
		* Attenuate opioid withdrawal symptoms
		* Reduce postoperative opioid requirements and pain
	* **IV LIDOCAINE** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
		* Useful for abdominal surgery or when regional anesthesia is contraindicated
		* Analgesic, anti-inflammatory, and anti-hyperalgesic properties
		* Mechanism: Inhibition of voltage-dependent sodium channels, NMDA receptors, and GPCRs
		* Indirectly blocks NMDA receptor through PKC inhibition
	* **IV MAGNESIUM** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
		* NMDA receptor antagonist
		* Suppresses neuropathic pain
		* Potentiates morphine analgesia
		* Attenuates morphine tolerance
		* Abolishes hyperalgesia and central sensitization development
		* 🏥 Clinical Practice: Combination with ketamine may enhance analgesia through "superadditive effect" (bind to different NMDA receptor sites)

### 10.5.6. INTRAOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT

* **Opioid Administration** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Prudent use of **FENTANYL**, **MORPHINE**, or **HYDROMORPHONE**
	* Must provide: effective intraoperative anesthesia, postoperative analgesia, and prevent opioid withdrawal
	* Administer: Baseline opioid requirement + intraoperative requirements
	* Doses may need 30-50% increase compared to opioid-naive patients
* **Opioid Rotation Strategy** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* New opioid binds different receptor subtype and metabolizes differently
	* Dose of new opioid should be <50% of calculated equianalgesic dose (incomplete cross-tolerance)
	* May administer for several days postoperatively
	* Transition to appropriate oral regimen for discharge by pain specialist
* **Clinical Monitoring for Optimal Dosing** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Monitor: Heart rate, pupil size, respiratory rate
	* Technique: Reverse neuromuscular blockade toward anesthesia end
	* Allow spontaneous breathing while still anesthetized
	* If respiratory rate >20/min and pupils dilated: Additional opioid needed
	* Target: Respiratory rate 12-14/min and moderately miotic pupil

### 10.5.7. POSTOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT

* **Patient-Controlled Analgesia (PCA)** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Initiate PCA with both basal and bolus doses
	* **Basal Infusion Calculation**:
		* Should equal patient's hourly preoperative oral opioid dose
		* Prevents withdrawal symptoms
		* Example: 90 mg oral morphine/day = 30 mg IV morphine/day = 1.25 mg/h basal rate
	* **Bolus Dose Calculation**:
		* Initially set to ½ of hourly dose (e.g., 0.6 mg in above example)
		* Lockout interval: Start at 10 minutes
	* Program hourly or 4-hour dose limits for safety
	* ⚠️ Constant monitoring for oversedation and respiratory depression required
	* 💡 Clinical Pearl: Patients with transdermal fentanyl patches don't require basal infusions
* **Same-Day Surgery Patients** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Initial IV opioids in recovery
	* Quickly transition to oral regimen: baseline requirement + short-acting opioid for breakthrough pain
* **Regional Anesthesia** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Highly recommended component
	* Options: Peripheral nerve blockade (single or continuous) or epidural analgesia
	* ⚠️ Avoid neuraxial opioids in this population
	* Deliver baseline and breakthrough opioids systemically
	* Avoid administering opioids by multiple routes simultaneously

### 10.5.8. TRANSITIONAL PAIN SERVICE INTERVENTIONS

* **Pharmacologic Interventions** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Opioid-weaning assistance before/after surgery
	* Perioperative multimodal opioid-sparing analgesia
* **Procedural Interventions** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Neuromodulation (peripheral nerve, transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation)
	* Peripheral nerve blockade/cryoneurolysis (ambulatory continuous perineural blocks)
	* Acupuncture
* **Nonpharmacologic Interventions** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Pain-coping skills applications
	* Electronic health applications
	* Sensory therapy (music, visual, distraction, relaxation techniques)
	* Psychological interventions (cognitive-behavioral therapy, biofeedback, counseling)

### 10.5.9. PERIOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT PROTOCOL

1. **Categorize** preoperative opioid use (naive, exposed, tolerant) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
2. **Risk stratify** using O-NET classification [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

	![image.png](ACUTE_ACUTE_ACUTE_ACUTE_ACUTE_image_2.png_aa8f8a.png_c21b98.png_0f76c3.png_1c5572.png_3285f3.png)

3. **Optimize management** according to O-NET criteria [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Consult perioperative pain specialist pre-surgery for high-risk patients
	* Consider preoperative opioid taper to lowest effective dose
	* Optimize psychosocial comorbidities
	* Continue buprenorphine/methadone for MOUD patients
4. **Implement pain management strategies** for moderate/high-risk patients [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Multimodal analgesia with regional anesthesia when applicable
5. **Consider opioid-free management** possibility [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Shared decision between patient and physician
	* Tailored to surgery type and specific regional technique
6. **Manage postoperative pain** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Optimize nonopioid analgesics
	* Use lowest effective opioid dose
	* Avoid dose escalation
	* Use procedure-specific, opioid-sparing regional anesthesia
	* Consider continuous nerve block when indicated
7. **Plan discharge opioid management** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]:
	* Limit discharge prescription to amount appropriate for expected pain
	* Consider continuing continuous nerve block infusion as outpatient

🔑 HY: Understanding the differential opioid tolerance phenomenon in opioid-tolerant patients is critical - while they have increased opioid requirements, they may paradoxically be at increased risk for respiratory depression when larger doses are administered [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Perioperative management of opioid-dependent patients requires systematic approaches including preoperative identification and stratification, continuation of baseline opioids, multimodal analgesia with multiple adjuncts, and specialized management by transitional pain services when available, all while recognizing the paradoxical risk of respiratory depression despite increased analgesic requirements.

## 10.6. PATIENTS WITH OPIOID USE DISORDER (OUD)

### 10.6.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part B:** Management of patients with OUD [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Part C:** Systems approaches to OUD patients [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#6. Systems-Based Practice|ⓘ]]

**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Important specialized knowledge)



**Medication Treatment of OUD (MOUD) - Overview** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* Patients receiving MOUD require **special consideration for perioperative pain management**
* FDA-approved medications for OUD treatment include:
	* **METHADONE**
	* **BUPRENORPHINE**
	* **NALTREXONE**
* ⚠️ **General principle**: Discontinuation of methadone or buprenorphine prior to surgery is NOT recommended

**METHADONE Management** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Perioperative approach*: Continue maintenance dose throughout perioperative period
* *Dosing options*:
	* Standard: Once daily dosing
	* Alternative: Divide into three doses at 8-hour intervals for better analgesia
* *Serious side effects*:
	* QT interval prolongation
	* Cardiac arrhythmias (e.g., torsades de points)
	* Respiratory depression (related to long half-life)

**BUPRENORPHINE Characteristics and Management** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *FDA approvals*: Management of acute pain, OUD, and chronic pain
* *Pharmacology*:
	* Partial mu receptor agonist
	* Kappa receptor antagonist
	* Nociceptin opioid receptor (ORL-1) agonist
* *Key advantage*: Effective analgesic with ceiling effect on respiratory depression → potentially safer than methadone
* *Available formulations*:
	* Parenteral
	* Transdermal
	* Sublingual
	* Buccal films
	* Subcutaneous
	* Subdermal implants
* *Perioperative approach*: Should NOT be routinely discontinued prior to surgery
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl**: Consultation with acute pain management expert is highly recommended for opioid-tolerant patients

**NALTREXONE Characteristics and Management** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* *Classification*: Semisynthetic opioid antagonist (derived from oxymorphone)
* *Chemical modification*: Substitution of n-methyl with methylcyclopropyl group
* *Clinical use*: For highly motivated alcoholand opioid-dependent patients seeking abstinence
* *Available formulations*:
	* Oral: 50 mg once-daily
	* Depot: 380 mg once-monthly (Vivitrol™) - embedded in biodegradable biosphere matrix
* *Perioperative management*:
	* Oral formulation: Hold for 3 days prior to surgery
	* Depot formulation: Ideally schedule surgery at least 4 weeks after last dose
* ⚠️ **Important risk**: Patients may experience opioid receptor upregulation due to chronic opioid antagonism
	* This places them at increased risk for opioid-related side effects, particularly respiratory depression

🔑 **HY**: Management of OUD patients requires collaboration between anesthesia, pain management specialists, and addiction medicine [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Patients with opioid use disorder being treated with medications such as methadone, buprenorphine, or naltrexone require specialized perioperative management, with the general principle that medication for opioid use disorder (particularly methadone and buprenorphine) should not be discontinued before surgery, while naltrexone may need to be held depending on formulation.

## 11.1. NEW STANDARDS FOR SAFER CONNECTIONS (NRFIT)

### 11.1.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Safety in anesthetic equipment [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.5 Biostatistics/Clinical Trials|ⓘ]]
* **Part B:** Regional anesthesia safety [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]
* **Part C:** Systems-based safety approaches [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#5. Practice-Based Learning and Improvement|ⓘ]]

**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Important safety concept)



* **Purpose**: Prevent wrong-route drug administration errors ``
* **Background**: Luer connections enable cross-connection of devices intended for different clinical applications, allowing potentially fatal wrong-route administration ``
* **Development**: International Organization for Standardization (ISO) developed new standard (ISO 80369-6:2016) for non-Luer devices used in neuraxial/regional blocks ``
* **NRFit characteristics**:
   * 20% smaller than Luer connectors
   * Unique design features preventing cross-connections
   * Functionally equivalent to conventional Luer connections
   * No adapters to Luer connectors manufactured
   * Yellow coloration for easy identification
* **Implementation**: Used for all neuraxial (intrathecal, epidural) equipment including needles, syringes, and other devices ``
* **Safety benefit**: Makes connections to different devices incompatible, minimizing risk of cross-connection and wrong route administration ``

![NRFit Devices](ACUTE_ACUTE_ACUTE_ACUTE_pdf_PAIN.pdf_p19_img1_109da9.jpeg_5b6b15.jpeg_05cdf9.jpeg_379b7a.jpeg) ``

**Bottom Line:** NRFit represents a significant safety advancement by introducing standardized, incompatible connections for neuraxial and regional anesthesia equipment, thus preventing potentially fatal wrong-route medication errors that were previously possible with universal Luer connections.

## 11.2. EPIDURAL EQUIPMENT

### 11.2.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Important technical knowledge)



### 11.2.2. EPIDURAL NEEDLES

**Components** ``
* **Tuohy needle** most common in UK ``
* 10 cm length with 8 cm shaft (with 1-cm markings); 15-cm version for obese patients ``
* Thin-walled to allow catheter insertion ``
* Includes stylet introducer to prevent tissue core occlusion ``
* Huber point bevel - slightly oblique at 20 degrees with blunt leading edge ``
* Optional wings at hub ``
* Common gauges: 16G or 18G ``

**Mechanism of action** ``
* Markings enable measurement of epidural space depth ``
* Bevel shape allows directional control of catheter (cephalad/caudad) ``
* Blunt bevel minimizes accidental dural puncture risk ``
* Pediatric version: 19G, 5-cm long with 0.5-cm markings ``
* Combined spinal-epidural technique possible using long spinal needle through Tuohy needle ``
* Echogenic versions available for ultrasound-guided placement ``

**Problems and safety considerations** ``
* If withdrawing catheter, needle must be withdrawn simultaneously to prevent catheter transection ``
* High risk of post-dural puncture headache with accidental dural puncture due to large bore ``
* NRFit design prevents wrong-route drug errors ``

![Tuohy Needle](ACUTE_ACUTE_ACUTE_ACUTE_pdf_PAIN.pdf_p20_img1_e97f53.jpeg_7c2d73.jpeg_81db1f.jpeg_ea3720.jpeg) ``

### 11.2.3. EPIDURAL CATHETERS

**Components** ``
* 90-cm transparent, malleable tube (nylon or Teflon, biologically inert) ``
* 16G external diameter ~1mm, internal diameter 0.55mm ``
* Distal end has two or three side ports with closed, rounded tip (reduces risk of vascular/dural puncture) ``
* Some designs have open end ``
* Marked at 5-cm intervals with additional 1-cm markings between 5-15cm ``
* Proximal end connects to filter ``
* Some incorporate coil reinforcement to prevent kinking ``
* Some are radio-opaque (more rigid, used for chronic pain) ``

**Mechanism of action** ``
* Designed to pass easily through matched gauge epidural needles ``
* Markings allow precise placement of desired length (usually 3-5cm) in epidural space ``
* Epidural fixing device prevents displacement ``

**Problems and safety considerations** ``
* Test patency before insertion ``
* Can puncture epidural vessel or dura during insertion or later ``
* Never withdraw through Tuohy needle once threaded beyond bevel (risk of transection) ``
* Direction of catheter advancement unpredictable ``
* Inspect for breakage upon removal ``
* Excessive advancement can cause knotting ``

![Epidural Catheter and Filter](ACUTE_ACUTE_ACUTE_ACUTE_pdf_PAIN.pdf_p27_img1_bcbb90.jpeg_d8732f.jpeg_60cdf3.jpeg_64f75b.jpeg) ``

### 11.2.4. FILTERS AND LOSS OF RESISTANCE DEVICES

**Filter characteristics** ``
* 0.22-μm hydrophilic mesh ``
* Functions as bacterial, viral, and foreign body (e.g., glass) filter ``
* Priming volume approximately 0.7 mL ``
* Recommended change every 24 hours for long-term use ``

**Loss of Resistance Device** ``
* Special low-resistance plunger syringe ``
* Used to identify epidural space by loss of resistance to air or saline ``
* Available in plastic or glass versions ``

**Bottom Line:** Epidural anesthesia equipment comprises specialized components including Tuohy needles with blunt, directional bevels; multiorifice catheters with clear markings; bacterial filters; and special loss-of-resistance syringes, all designed to facilitate safe and effective epidural anesthesia while minimizing complications.

## 11.3. SPINAL EQUIPMENT

### 11.3.1. SYLLABUS CORRELATION

* **Part A:** Equipment for spinal anesthesia [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#1.4 Nervous System, Musculoskeletal|ⓘ]]
* **Part B:** Spinal anesthesia techniques [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#S2 Pain Medicine|ⓘ]]

**Exam Weighting:** Medium (Important technical knowledge)



### 11.3.2. SPINAL NEEDLES

**Components** ``
* Length varies from 5-15 cm (10 cm most common) ``
* Transparent hub for quick CSF identification ``
* Includes stylet to prevent tissue core occlusion and strengthen shaft ``
* Available in sizes 18G to 29G (32G described but not widely used) ``
* 25G and smaller used with introducer (usually 18G or 19G needle) ``
* Two main bevel designs:
   * Cutting/traumatic: Yale and Quincke needles
   * Noncutting/atraumatic with side hole: Whitacre and Sprotte needles

**Mechanism of action** ``
* Larger needles (22G) more rigid, easier to direct with better feedback ``
* CSF emerges more slowly from smaller needles (may require gentle aspiration) ``
* Continuous spinal anesthesia possible with specialized 27G microcatheter through Sprotte needle ``
* Maximum intrathecal catheter length should be <50mm to avoid knotting ``

![Spinal Needles](ACUTE_ACUTE_ACUTE_ACUTE_pdf_PAIN.pdf_p30_img1_ebeac3.jpeg_f4dea9.jpeg_4d3951.jpeg_daa236.jpeg) ``

### 11.3.3. NEEDLE DESIGNS AND IMPACT ON COMPLICATIONS

**Dural headache considerations** ``
* NRFit system prevents wrong-route drug errors ``
* Incidence directly proportional to:
   * Needle gauge (30% with 20G vs. 1% with 26G)
   * Number of dural punctures
* Incidence inversely proportional to patient age ``
* Whitacre and Sprotte atraumatic needles separate rather than cut dural fibers:
   * Higher chance of sealing after removal
   * Reduced dural headache incidence
* Traumatic bevels (Yale/Quincke) cut dural fibers, producing ragged tear allowing CSF leakage ``
* Higher risk during pregnancy/labor, day-surgery, and in patients with previous dural headache ``
* **Evidence of differential risk by needle type** ``
	* Lidocaine intrathecal is more likely to cause transient neurologic symptoms than bupivacaine, prilocaine and procaine

**Spinal microcatheter issues** ``
* Difficult to advance ``
* Risk of nerve trauma ``
* Cauda equina syndrome associated with neurotoxicity from anesthetic solutions rather than microcatheter itself ``

![Needle Bevel Comparison](ACUTE_ACUTE_ACUTE_ACUTE_pdf_PAIN.pdf_p34_img1_b95061.jpeg_3374ac.jpeg_8b62a3.jpeg_9334c5.jpeg) ``

**Bottom Line:** Spinal needles come in various designs with the critical distinction between cutting tips (Yale, Quincke) and pencil-point/atraumatic tips (Whitacre, Sprotte), with the latter significantly reducing post-dural puncture headache risk by separating rather than cutting dural fibers. Smaller gauge needles also reduce headache risk, though they may be more challenging to use.
