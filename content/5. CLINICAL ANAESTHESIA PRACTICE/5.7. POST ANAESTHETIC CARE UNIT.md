## 1.1. POSTANESTHESIA RECOVERY








**Each patient recovering from an anesthetic has circumstances that require an individualized problem-oriented approach.**

* Postanesthesia recovery must continue to adapt to meet the needs of the changing perioperative landscape, advances in technology, and changing surgical techniques
* Must respond to improved evidence-based research
* Dissemination of anesthesia services beyond the perisurgical arena has brought changes and greater demands on recovery units

### 1.1.2. STANDARDS FOR POSTANESTHESIA CARE

* Standards for Postanesthesia Care were first approved by the ASA House of Delegates from the Standards and Practice Parameters Committee for Postanesthesia Care on October 12, 1988
* Five standards were last amended on October 23, 2019
* These standards are used to determine:
  * Who needs managed recovery
  * The types and levels of recovery
  * Who is responsible for patient care
  * How the patient should be monitored prior to discharge from the PACU

#### 1.1.2.1. ASA PRACTICE GUIDELINES FOR POSTANESTHETIC CARE

* **Updated Practice Guidelines** were published in 2013, updating the 2002 guidelines [ASA:1]
* **Purpose**: To improve postanesthetic care outcomes for patients who have just had anesthesia or sedation and analgesia care by evaluating available evidence and providing recommendations for patient assessment, monitoring, and management [ASA:1]
* **Goals**: Reducing postoperative adverse events, providing uniform assessment of recovery, improving postanesthetic quality of life, and streamlining postoperative care and discharge criteria [ASA:1]
* **Application scope**: Patients of all ages who have received general anesthesia, regional anesthesia, or moderate or deep sedation [ASA:1]
* **Exclusions**: Guidelines do not apply to patients receiving infiltration local anesthesia without sedation, minimal sedation (anxiolysis), or patients receiving intensive care [ASA:1]
* **Medical supervision**: General medical supervision and coordination of patient care in the PACU should be the responsibility of an anesthesiologist [ASA:1]

### 1.1.3. VALUE AND ECONOMICS OF POSTANESTHESIA CARE UNIT

* **Quality of postanesthesia care** is composed of many variables:
  * Tracking of complications
  * Time per patient spent in recovery
  * Overall clinical outcomes
  * Patient satisfaction
* **Value** of postanesthesia care is a measure of the quality of care provided compared with the amount of resources spent per patient outcome
* The PACU helps to use resources efficiently by having:
  * Trained staff who routinely care for postsurgical patients
  * Staff capable of recognizing/preventing complications
  * Physicians instituting appropriate and timely therapies

#### 1.1.3.1. COST COMPONENTS

* **Actual PACU cost** incorporates:
  * Staffing costs (largest direct cost)
  * Space costs
  * Disposables
  * Hardware (resource utilization)
* **Factors affecting staffing costs**:
  * Mix of nursing staff
  * Experience of nurses
  * Staffing ratios
  * Complexity and duration of PACU stay
* **Equipment factors**:
  * Level of monitoring provided affects capital expenditure
  * Disposable items account for operating expenditures
  * Patient acuity determines needs for equipment (ventilators, monitors, IV pumps, warming devices, PCA pumps)
* **Physician coverage**:
  * Dedicated vs. on-demand coverage affects response time, efficiency, costs, and outcomes

#### 1.1.3.2. COST EFFICIENCY CONSIDERATIONS

* Cost comparisons between institutions are difficult due to:
  * Varying charges and cost factors across institutions and regions
  * Different regulatory requirements and standards of care
  * Varying medical-legal climates and institutional requirements
* Patient factors affecting cost include:
  * Levels of patient comorbidities
  * Procedure complexity
  * Surgeon and anesthetic technique
  * Patient perception and expectations
* **PACU medical directors are challenged to**:
  * Optimize clinical results while minimizing expenditures
  * Guarantee safe care
  * Minimize cost
  * Fulfill regulatory and institutional requirements
* **Cost-effectiveness strategies**:
  * Eliminate useless or routine testing
  * Avoid unjustifiable therapy
  * Prevent inappropriate PACU admissions
  * Recognize when more expensive therapy may generate real savings by decreasing additional therapies
* **Communication importance**:
  * Perhaps the least expensive tool in medicine
  * Most universally proven to be involved in human error events

💡 **Enhanced Recovery After Anesthesia (ERAS) protocols** might create an opportunity to shorten the length of stay in the PACU

* Requires PACU providers to be aware of these protocols and manage patients accordingly
* May reduce:
  * Transportation delays
  * Persistence of pain or nausea
  * Waiting for space
  * Surgeon discharge delays

**Bottom Line:** The PACU represents a critical cost center and care transition point that requires balancing clinical quality with resource utilization. Communication with the intraoperative team, appropriate staffing, and evidence-based protocols are key to optimal outcomes.

### 1.1.4. LEVELS OF POSTOPERATIVE/POSTANESTHESIA CARE

* With continued demand to increase overall health care efficiency, caution must be taken to provide the most appropriate care for each patient
* As anesthesia services expand beyond the OR, selecting the correct type of recovery is essential

#### 1.1.4.1. DETERMINING FACTORS FOR LEVEL OF CARE

* **Factors determining level of postoperative care**:
  * Degree of underlying illness
  * Comorbidities
  * Duration of anesthesia
  * Type of anesthesia and surgery
  * These factors assess the risk of postoperative complications
* **Benefits of appropriate care level assignment**:
  * Less invasive surgeries with shorter anesthetics facilitate high levels of arousal and minimal cardiovascular/respiratory depression at the end of surgery
  * Using less intensive postanesthesia settings for selected patients can reduce costs
  * Alert patients are more satisfied when spared unnecessary assessments
  * Earlier reunion with family in low-intensity settings is desirable (especially important in pediatric population)

#### 1.1.4.2. TYPES OF RECOVERY SETTINGS

* **Separation models**:
  * Separate PACUs for inpatients, ambulatory, or non-operating room anesthesia (NORA) patients
  * **Phase I recovery**: Reserved for more intense recovery requiring more one-on-one care
  * **Phase II recovery**: Less intensive, appropriate for patients after less invasive procedures requiring less attention
* **Unified PACU settings**:
  * If separation is not possible, providing appropriate level of monitoring and coverage based on degree of postoperative impairment
  * Care equal to full-intensity PACU must always be available given incidence of complications
* **Complication considerations**:
  * Major complications occur in 3-17% of inpatient surgery procedures requiring treatment after PACU admission
  * Most patients spend 1 hour or less in the PACU
  * Staff must remain prepared with continued education and simulations

**Bottom Line:** The level of post-anesthesia care must be individualized based on patient factors, procedure complexity, and anesthetic technique. Phase I provides intensive care while Phase II offers less intensive monitoring, but safety standards must always be maintained regardless of setting.

### 1.1.5. POSTANESTHETIC TRIAGE

* **Triage principles**:
  * Patients must be carefully evaluated to determine appropriate level of care
  * Triage based on clinical condition, length/type of procedure and anesthetic, and potential for complications
  * Alternatives to PACU care must be used in a nondiscriminatory fashion
  * Arbitrary criteria (age, ASA classification, ambulatory vs. inpatient, insurance) should not determine recovery care level
* **Standard of care considerations**:
  * An individual patient undergoing a specific procedure or anesthetic should receive the same appropriate level of postoperative care regardless of setting (hospital OR, ambulatory center, endoscopy room, invasive radiology suite, outpatient office)
  * Per ASA Standards of Post Anesthesia Care, Standard I, an anesthesiologist familiar with the patient can determine which level of care is required
  * When in doubt about safety in a lower intensity setting, the patient should be admitted to a higher level of care

#### 1.1.5.1. APPROPRIATE CANDIDATES FOR DIFFERENT CARE PATHWAYS

* **Candidates for less intensive monitoring**:
  * Patients after superficial procedures using local infiltration, minor blocks, or sedation
  * Healthy patients undergoing more extensive procedures (hernia repairs, arthroscopic procedures, minor orthopedic procedures) under local, plexus, or peripheral nerve blockade
* **Fast-track facilitators**:
  * Continuous peripheral nerve catheters have shortened PACU time and eliminated many hospital admissions
  * Innovative anesthetic techniques
  * Advanced surgical techniques
  * Bispectral index monitoring
* **Direct ICU admission considerations**:
  * For more intensive procedures and higher acuity patients
  * Can reduce demands on the PACU and decrease number of hand-offs
  * Requires proper postoperative reporting and communication pathways
  * These ICUs must be trained and prepared to receive immediate postoperative patients and meet PACU standards

**Bottom Line:** Postanesthetic triage should be based on individual patient needs and clinical factors rather than arbitrary criteria. Fast-tracking and appropriate care level assignment can improve efficiency without compromising safety when protocols are properly implemented.

### 1.1.6. SAFETY IN THE POSTANESTHESIA CARE UNIT

* Every PACU should have medical oversight in the form of a Medical Director
* The PACU Medical Director must ensure the PACU environment is as safe as possible for both patients and staff

#### 1.1.6.1. SAFETY MEASURES AND CONSIDERATIONS

* **Staffing safety measures**:
  * Maintain staffing and training to ensure appropriate coverage and skill mix
  * Incidence of adverse events correlates with nursing workload and staff availability
  * Ideally, all staff should have PACU certification
  * Staffing ratios should never fall below acceptable standards
  * Less skilled or training staff must be appropriately supervised
  * Sufficient certified personnel must always be available to handle worst-case scenarios
* **Patient rights protection**:
  * PACU staff protects patients who are temporarily incompetent
  * Preserves patients' rights to observance of advanced directives
  * Maintains informed consent for additional procedures
  * Optimizes each patient's privacy and dignity
  * Minimizes psychological impact of unpleasant or frightening events
* **Infection control measures**:
  * Handwashing
  * Sterility procedures
  * Infection control protocols should be strictly enforced
  * Access to the PACU should be strictly controlled
* **Safety for professionals**:
  * Air handling should guarantee personnel are not exposed to unacceptable levels of trace anesthetic gases
  * Staff vaccinations (hepatitis B, influenza, and others required by institution)
  * Policies for radiation safety, infection control, disposal of sharps, universal precautions
  * Personal protective equipment (PPE) availability
  * Sufficient help for lifting and positioning patients
  * Precise documentation and clear delineation of responsibility

**Bottom Line:** PACU safety requires a multifaceted approach focusing on staffing adequacy, proper supervision, patient rights protection, infection control, and staff safety measures. The Medical Director must ensure all safety protocols are implemented and maintained.

### 1.1.7. ADMISSION TO THE POSTANESTHESIA CARE UNIT

* **Basic monitoring requirements**:
  * Every patient admitted to a PACU should have vital signs, airway patency, peripheral oxygen saturation, ventilatory rate/character, and level of pain recorded and periodically monitored
  * Assessment with periodic recording every 5 minutes for the first 15 minutes and every 15 minutes thereafter is a minimum
  * Document temperature, level of consciousness, mental status, neuromuscular function, hydration status, degree of nausea on admission/discharge
* **Monitoring devices**:
  * Every patient should be continuously monitored with a pulse oximeter and at least a single-lead ECG
  * Extra leads (particularly precordial V3–6) are appropriate if left ventricular ischemia is likely
  * Capnography is necessary for patients receiving mechanical ventilation or those at risk for compromised ventilatory function
  * Transduction and recorded output from invasive monitors must be accomplished
  * Diagnostic testing should be ordered only for specific indications or as part of a designed recovery protocol

#### 1.1.7.1. ASA GUIDELINES FOR PATIENT ASSESSMENT AND MONITORING

* **Evidence-based monitoring recommendations** [ASA:2]:
  * Assessment and monitoring of respiratory function during recovery is associated with early detection of hypoxemia (Category A2-B evidence)
  * Neuromuscular blockade monitoring is effective in detecting neuromuscular dysfunction (Category B2-B evidence)
* **Recommended periodic assessments** during emergence and recovery [ASA:2]:
  * **Respiratory function**: Airway patency, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation with particular attention to oxygenation and ventilation
  * **Cardiovascular function**: Routine pulse and blood pressure monitoring with electrocardiographic monitors immediately available
  * **Neuromuscular function**: Required for patients who received nondepolarizing neuromuscular blocking agents or who have medical conditions associated with neuromuscular dysfunction
  * **Mental status**: Periodic assessment using available scoring systems
  * **Temperature**: Routine assessment to detect complications and reduce adverse outcomes
  * **Pain**: Periodic assessment and monitoring
  * **Nausea and vomiting**: Routine periodic assessment
  * **Hydration status**: Assessment in PACU with appropriate management; certain procedures involving significant blood or fluid loss may require additional fluid management
  * **Urine output and voiding**: Case-by-case basis for selected patients or procedures
  * **Drainage and bleeding**: Routine assessment and monitoring

💡 **Expert and membership consensus** indicates that these monitoring practices detect complications, reduce adverse outcomes, and should be performed during emergence and recovery [ASA:2]

#### 1.1.7.2. HANDOFF AND TRANSFER OF CARE

* **Anesthesiologist responsibilities**:
  * Should manage the patient in the PACU until a PACU nurse secures admission vital signs, attaches appropriate monitors, and care is transferred with a complete report
  * A succinct but thorough report must include sufficient information to allow rapid evaluation and intervention for postoperative complications
  * Use a standardized format printed on the PACU record (similar to OR timeout)
* **Essential handoff components**:
  * Patient identification
  * Procedure performed
  * Anesthetic type
  * Continuing therapies
  * Documentation of time and amount of all neuromuscular relaxants, respiratory depressant medications, and reversal agents
  * Outlined orders, specific therapeutic end points
  * Contact information for the responsible anesthesiologist

⚠️ The anesthesiologist should never transfer responsibility until the patient's airway status, ventilation, and hemodynamics are appropriate for the caregivers receiving the patient

* Function of indwelling cannulae, intravenous catheters, monitors, and medication infusions should be verified before completing transfer of care
* Leaving a patient with someone unfamiliar or incapable of handling the acuity may constitute abandonment of care

**Bottom Line:** PACU admission requires thorough monitoring, comprehensive handoff communication, and ensuring the receiving care team is capable of managing the patient's needs. A standardized handoff process and verification of all devices and medications are essential for safe care transition.

### 1.1.8. POSTOPERATIVE PAIN MANAGEMENT

* **Pain assessment requirements**:
  * Relief of surgical pain with minimal side effects is a major goal during PACU care and a top priority for patients
  * Periodically assess and document level of pain throughout recovery
  * The Joint Commission mandates use of a numerical pain scale or visual scale with periodic recording and acceptable score for discharge
* **Importance of adequate analgesia**:
  * Inadequate postoperative analgesia is a major source of preoperative fear and ultimate dissatisfaction
  * Analgesia reduces sympathetic nervous system response, avoiding hypertension, tachycardia, and dysrhythmias
  * In hypovolemic patients, sympathetic nervous system activity may mask relative hypovolemia

⚠️ Administration of analgesics can precipitate hypotension in an apparently stable patient, especially if direct or histamine-induced vasodilation occurs

* Assess a tachycardic patient with low or normal blood pressure who complains of pain carefully before giving analgesics that might precipitate hypotension

#### 1.1.8.1. ASA GUIDELINES FOR PAIN MANAGEMENT

* **Evidence for pain assessment** [ASA:3]:
  * Literature is insufficient regarding whether routine assessment and monitoring of pain is associated with fewer postoperative complications
  * Expert consultants and ASA members agree that routine assessment and monitoring of pain detects complications, reduces adverse outcomes, and should be done during emergence and recovery [ASA:3]
* **Recommended practice**: Pain should be periodically assessed during emergence and recovery [ASA:3]

💡 **Clinical integration**: The ASA guidelines support the existing institutional practices but emphasize that pain assessment should be routine and systematic rather than optional [ASA:3]

#### 1.1.8.2. PAIN ASSESSMENT CHALLENGES

* **Assessment difficulties**:
  * Severity of pain varies among surgical procedures and anesthetic techniques
  * Staff members may be relatively ineffective at quantifying level of discomfort
  * Patients may be impaired in communication abilities or afraid to express needs
  * Inexperienced nurses overestimate pain, whereas more experienced nurses tend to underestimate
  * Numeric pain scales yield more reliable results but require patient communication
  * Wide divergence can exist between cognitive perception and sympathetic nervous system response
* **Best assessment approaches**:
  * Patient's perception is the best measure of analgesia
  * Heart rate, respiratory rate and depth, sweating, nausea, and vomiting may indicate pain
  * Absence or presence of physiologic signs is not reliable as a measure of pain

#### 1.1.8.3. MULTIMODAL PAIN MANAGEMENT

* **Patient subgroups requiring special consideration**:
  * Careful identification of patient subgroups
  * Assessment of individual analgesic requirements
  * Implementation of planned, multimodal approach for seamless pain control
* **Opioid use disorder (OUD) patients**:
  * Patients on maintenance plans of methadone or opioid agonist/antagonist (buprenorphine/naloxone) present pain control challenges
  * Knowing if patients have received daily treatment doses helps manage post-procedure acute pain
  * Recommended to continue preoperative dose of maintenance plan
  * Higher doses of opioids may be expected even with maintenance treatment
  * Opioids should be continuous or scheduled rather than as-needed
  * Undertreatment predisposes to decreased responsiveness to opioid therapy
  * Aggressive multimodal plan recommended including:
	* NSAIDs
	* Acetaminophen
	* Gabapentinoids
	* Alpha 2 agonists
	* Regional anesthesia
* **Multimodal approaches**:
  * Regional and local anesthetic techniques
  * Acetaminophen
  * NSAIDs
  * Neuroleptics
  * Alpha agonists
  * Sufficient analgesia is the end point, even if large doses of opioids necessary in tolerant patients
* **Opioid considerations**:
  * Short-acting opioids useful to expedite discharge and minimize nausea in ambulatory settings
  * Duration of analgesia can be a problem
  * During IV titration, assess for incremental respiratory or cardiovascular depression
  * Oral and transdermal analgesics have limited PACU role but help transition ambulatory patients
  * Rectal analgesics sometimes useful in small children
* **Additional analgesic modalities**:
  * Titration of IV opioids in PACU important for smooth transition to IV PCA
  * Epidural or intrathecal opioids yield prolonged analgesia in selected patients
  * Side effects of neuraxial opioids include nausea, pruritus, and delayed ventilatory depression
  * Addition of local anesthetic or clonidine enhances analgesia and decreases side effects
  * Epidural analgesia effective after thoracic or abdominal procedures
  * Continuous flow catheters with pressure delivery systems of local anesthetics used intrawound
  * Regional blocks reduce pain, control sympathetic activity, and often improve ventilation

**Bottom Line:** Effective postoperative pain management requires thorough assessment, recognition of individual patient needs, and a multimodal approach. Special consideration should be given to patients with opioid use disorder, and analgesic plans should extend beyond the PACU stay.

### 1.1.10. DISCHARGE CRITERIA

* **Basic requirements before discharge**:
  * Patient should be sufficiently oriented to assess their physical condition and able to summon assistance
  * Airway reflexes and motor function must be adequate to maintain patency and prevent aspiration
  * Ventilation and oxygenation should be acceptable, with sufficient reserve for minor deterioration
  * Blood pressure, heart rate, and indices of peripheral perfusion should be relatively constant for at least 15 minutes and appropriately near baseline
  * Achieving normal body temperature is not an absolute requirement, but shivering should be resolved
  * Acceptable analgesia must be achieved and nausea/vomiting appropriately controlled
* **Medication-specific considerations**:
  * Patients should be observed for at least 15 minutes after the last IV opioid or sedative to assess peak effects and side effects
  * If regional anesthetics have been administered, longer observation may be appropriate to assess effectiveness and rule out local toxicity
  * Monitor oxygen saturation for 15 minutes after discontinuation of supplemental oxygen
* **Complication surveillance**:
  * Rule out likely complications of surgery (bleeding, vascular compromise, pneumothorax)
  * Rule out complications of underlying conditions (hypertension, myocardial ischemia, hyperglycemia, bronchospasm)
  * Document brief neurologic assessment to ensure patient is at baseline
  * Review results of diagnostic tests
* **Scoring systems**:
  * Modified Aldrete Score or Postanesthesia Discharge Scoring System are two commonly used systems
  * Attempt to simplify and standardize patient discharge criteria
  * Fixed PACU discharge criteria must be used with caution due to tremendous variability among patients
  * Scoring systems are useful for assessment but cannot replace individual evaluation
  * Ideally, each patient should be evaluated for discharge by an anesthesiologist using a consistent set of criteria
  * Consider severity of underlying disease, anesthetic and recovery course, and level of care at destination
* **Post-discharge planning**:
  * A plan for continued management of likely postdischarge symptoms must be made prior to discharge (pain, nausea, headache, dizziness, drowsiness, and fatigue)
  * Using postanesthesia discharge criteria reduces discharge delays for inpatients versus physician examination only

#### 1.1.10.1. ASA GUIDELINES FOR DISCHARGE PROTOCOL

* **Urination requirement** [ASA:4]:
  * Literature is insufficient to evaluate benefits of requiring patients to urinate before discharge
  * Expert consultants and ASA members disagree that such requirement reduces adverse outcomes or increases patient satisfaction
  * Requirement increases length of recovery stay
  * **Recommendation**: Routine requirement for urination before discharge should not be part of discharge protocol and may only be necessary for selected patients [ASA:4]
* **Clear fluid requirement** [ASA:4]:
  * Literature findings are equivocal regarding whether requiring patients to drink clear fluids before discharge is associated with frequency of vomiting or time to discharge (Category A2-E evidence)
  * Expert consultants and ASA members disagree that drinking clear fluids before discharge reduces adverse outcomes or increases patient satisfaction
  * Requirement increases length of recovery stay
  * **Recommendation**: Requirement of drinking clear fluids should not be part of discharge protocol and may only be necessary for selected patients, determined case-by-case (e.g., diabetic patients) [ASA:4]
* **Responsible individual accompaniment** [ASA:4]:
  * Literature is insufficient regarding whether decrease in postdischarge complications or adverse outcomes is associated with requirement for responsible individual accompaniment
  * Expert consultants and ASA members agree this requirement reduces adverse outcomes, increases patient comfort and satisfaction, and should be mandatory
  * **Recommendation**: As part of recovery room discharge protocol, all patients should be required to have a responsible individual accompany them home [ASA:4]
* **Mandatory minimum stay** [ASA:4]:
  * Literature is insufficient to evaluate effects of mandatory minimum stay in recovery
  * Expert consultants disagree and ASA members are equivocal regarding whether minimum stay improves patient comfort and satisfaction or should be required
  * **Task Force consensus**: Mandatory minimum stay is not necessary and length of stay should be determined case-by-case
  * **Recommendation**: Patients should be observed until they are no longer at increased risk for cardiorespiratory depression; mandatory minimum stay should not be required; discharge criteria should be designed to minimize risk of CNS or cardiorespiratory depression after discharge [ASA:4]

💡 **Clinical integration**: ASA guidelines challenge traditional discharge requirements like urination and clear fluid intake, emphasizing evidence-based individualized care rather than blanket policies [ASA:4]

**Bottom Line:** Discharge from the PACU requires meeting specific physiologic criteria, ensuring adequate recovery from medications, and having a plan for continued management of symptoms. Standardized scoring systems help guide decision-making but must be supplemented with individualized assessment.

### 1.1.11. POSTOPERATIVE EVALUATION

* The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) have instituted compliance policies for participants in Medicare and Medicaid programs
* Policy for postanesthesia follow-up requirements:
  * Written follow-up performed by an individual qualified to administer anesthesia
  * Must be completed no later than 48 hours post procedure
  * Timeframe starts as soon as the patient arrives in recovery area or ICU
  * Evaluation should be performed only after patient has sufficiently recovered and is able to participate/answer questions
* **Required elements of postanesthesia evaluation**:
  * Respiratory function (respiratory rate, airway patency, oxygen saturation)
  * Cardiovascular function (pulse rate and blood pressure)
  * Mental status
  * Temperature
  * Pain
  * Nausea and vomiting
  * Postoperative hydration

**Bottom Line:** A formal postanesthesia evaluation must be completed within 48 hours by qualified personnel, documenting specific physiologic parameters and symptoms to comply with CMS requirements and ensure appropriate recovery progress.

## 1.2. CARDIOVASCULAR COMPLICATIONS








* This section focuses on events unique to recovering patients rather than reviewing all possible cardiovascular events in the PACU
* In the PACU, some reflexes previously blunted by general anesthetics, sedatives, and opioids return toward baseline, revealing unexpected cardiovascular compromise
* Most common types of patients encountering troubles are:
  * Patients with coronary artery disease
  * Patients with congestive heart failure
* Patients in the PACU may not complain of angina due to residual anesthetics and pain medications
* First sign of myocardial ischemia may be hypotension
* Sedation techniques that cause hypotension (e.g., dexmedetomidine) may cloud the clinical picture

### 1.2.2. MYOCARDIAL ISCHEMIA

* **Most common sign of myocardial ischemia is tachycardia**
* Tachycardia is very often a reaction to, not the cause of, myocardial ischemia
* Not all tachycardia heralds myocardial ischemia, but in high-risk patients, new-onset tachycardia not caused by pain should be taken seriously
* **ECG findings**:
  * May show classic ST-T wave elevation or depression depending on lead placement and area of ischemia
  * Lack of ST-T wave elevation does not rule out coronary artery disease
  * Transmural myocardial infarctions outside the PACU show no ECG diagnostic changes 10-30% of the time

⚠️ **Clinicians must be especially suspicious of a series of hemodynamic changes in a person at risk for coronary artery disease**

* **Intervention strategies**:
  * Early intervention with nitrates, opioids, beta-blockers, and anticoagulants may be life-saving
  * Cardiology should be involved for timely access to cardiac catheterization laboratory or angiolytic drug therapy
  * Special considerations for patients with stents:
	* Increasing use of bare metal and drug-eluting stents (DES)
	* Recognition that patients who have stopped antiplatelet therapy and are in postoperative hypercoagulable states can quickly occlude stents
	* Proactive intervention requires quick recognition and response
* **Multidisciplinary approach**:
  * Immediate involvement and communication with the surgical service
  * Decisions regarding anticoagulation and lytic therapy should be made among several services in consultation

### 1.2.3. CONGESTIVE HEART FAILURE

* Congestive heart failure is epidemic in the aging population
* Outpatient cardiology services have an expanding armamentarium of treatments:
  * New inotropic/vasodilator therapy
  * Devices
  * Interventions that allow patients to compensate for CHF
* **Assessment considerations**:
  * Helpful to know ejection fraction, activities of daily living, exercise tolerance, and other risk indices
  * Ejection fraction is only an estimate of fractional shortening of myocardial fibrils
  * Some patients may be stable with a large, dilated heart contracting at a 15% ejection fraction
  * These patients are compensated but have little reserve
* **PACU risks**:
  * Bleeding, volume shifts, and respiratory compromise could quickly cause decompensation
  * No absolute numbers for fluid restriction but precaution should be taken with fluid challenges
  * Use of transesophageal and transthoracic echocardiography may be valuable
  * Echocardiogram allows rapid viewing of:
	* Myocardial contractility
	* Regional wall motion
	* Volume status
	* Valvular dysfunction

### 1.2.4. SPECIALIZED PACU FOR CARDIAC PATIENTS

* With evolving cardiac treatments and less invasive techniques, specialized PACU care can be as safe and more cost-effective than ICU admission
* Natural extension is to establish specialized PACUs functioning as step-down or short-term ICUs
* **Evidence from off-pump CABG study**:
  * 85 patients were extubated 12 ± 2 minutes after chest closure
  * Patients monitored in special PACU section before discharge to cardiac floors or ICU
  * Only 4/85 patients failed PACU stay and required ICU (3 bradycardia cases, 1 myocardial infarction)
  * Two patients later returned to ICU from cardiac ward (atrial fibrillation, myocardial infarction)
  * Cost for PACU stay was $5,140 less than for an ICU-admitted patient
* **Extended application**:
  * High-risk vascular and thoracic surgery patients have shown they can be adequately cared for in prepared PACU
  * Hospitals can improve patient throughput by allocating more resources to expanded PACU care
* **Expanding PACU role**:
  * Anesthesiology services in increased demand throughout hospitals
  * PACUs may need to prepare to care for patients from:
	* Invasive cardiology suites (ablation techniques)
	* Automated implantable defibrillator placement
	* Percutaneous valve replacements
	* Hybrid and percutaneous coronary revascularization procedures

### 1.2.5. CARDIAC IMPLANTABLE ELECTRICAL DEVICES

* Cardiac pacemakers and cardioverter-defibrillators are commonplace in postanesthetic patients
* **Management requirements**:
  * Knowledge of device type and how it was managed during anesthesia is important
  * Continuous monitoring of cardiac rate and rhythm per ASA standard monitors throughout immediate post-procedure period
  * Devices that were reprogrammed should be restored to preoperative ambulatory settings before discharge from monitored setting
  * Backup pacing and cardioversion/defibrillation equipment should be immediately available
* **Device interrogation indications**:
  * Emergency surgery performed without appropriate pre-op device interrogation
  * Significant electromagnetic interference close to the device
  * Suspicion that antitachycardia therapy might have been disabled rather than suspended
  * Antitachycardia therapy was observed
  * Concern of electronic device malfunction
* Cardiac devices not interrogated during immediate postoperative period should be interrogated within 30 days after the procedure

**Bottom Line:** Cardiovascular complications in PACU require vigilant monitoring and prompt recognition. Special attention should be given to patients with coronary disease or heart failure. Specialized PACU care for cardiac patients can be cost-effective and appropriate with proper staffing and protocols. Management of cardiac implantable electrical devices requires specific knowledge and precautions.

## 1.3. POSTOPERATIVE PULMONARY DYSFUNCTION










* Mechanical, hemodynamic, and pharmacologic factors related to surgery and anesthesia impair ventilation, oxygenation, and airway maintenance
* **Risk factors for postoperative ventilatory events**:
  * Heavy smoking
  * Obesity
  * Sleep apnea
  * Severe asthma
  * COPD
* Preoperative pulmonary function testing has limited predictive value for postoperative complications, perhaps with the exception of postoperative bronchospasm in smokers

### 1.3.2. INADEQUATE POSTOPERATIVE VENTILATION

* In PACU patients, mild respiratory acidemia due to decreased minute ventilation is expected
* Elevated PaCO2 does not necessarily indicate inadequate postoperative ventilation
* **Inadequate ventilation should be suspected when**:
  * Respiratory acidemia occurs coincident with tachypnea, anxiety, dyspnea, labored ventilation, or increased sympathetic nervous system activity
  * Hypercarbia reduces the arterial pH below 7.30
  * PaCO2 progressively increases with a progressive decrease in arterial pH

#### 1.3.2.1. INADEQUATE RESPIRATORY DRIVE

* During early recovery, residual effects of intravenous and inhalation anesthetics blunt ventilatory responses to both hypercarbia and hypoxemia
* Sedatives augment depression from opioids or anesthetics
* **Hypoventilation evolution**:
  * Can evolve insidiously during transfer and admission to PACU
  * Peak depressant effect of an intravenous opioid given just before transfer occurs in the PACU
  * Depression of medullary centers can blunt signs of acidemia or hypoxemia
  * Patients might communicate lucidly and even complain of pain while experiencing significant opioid-induced hypoventilation
* **Higher risk patients**:
  * Patients with abnormal CO2/pH responses from morbid obesity, chronic airway obstruction, or sleep apnea
  * Children with active or recent upper respiratory infection (more prone to breath-holding, severe cough, and arterial desaturations)
* **Management approaches**:
  * Balance between acceptable level of ventilatory depression and tolerable level of pain/agitation
  * For excessive hypoventilation from opioids: forced arousal and careful titration of naloxone (20-40 μg at a time)
  * Flumazenil (0.1 mg titrated to effect up to 1.5 mg) for benzodiazepine reversal, usually not needed
* **Special considerations**:
  * Abrupt diminution of noxious stimulus (tracheal extubation, nerve block) may promote hypoventilation
  * Intracranial hemorrhage or edema can present with hypoventilation
  * Bilateral carotid body injury after endarterectomy can ablate peripheral hypoxic drive
  * Chronic respiratory acidemia from COPD alters CNS sensitivity to pH, making hypoxic drive dominant

#### 1.3.2.2. INCREASED AIRWAY RESISTANCE

* High resistance to gas flow increases work of breathing and CO2 production
* If inspiratory muscles cannot generate sufficient pressure gradients, alveolar ventilation fails to match CO2 production, leading to progressive respiratory acidemia
* **Upper airway resistance causes**:
  * Pharyngeal obstruction (posterior tongue displacement, soft tissue collapse)
  * Laryngeal obstruction (laryngospasm, laryngeal edema)
  * Large airway obstruction (extrinsic compression from hematoma, tumor, tracheal stenosis)
  * Contributing factors: weakness from residual neuromuscular relaxation, myasthenia gravis, myasthenic syndromes
* **Simple airway maneuvers**:
  * Improving level of consciousness
  * Lateral positioning
  * Chin lift
  * Mandible elevation
  * Placement of oropharyngeal or nasopharyngeal airway
  * Nasopharyngeal airway may be better tolerated with functional gag reflexes
  * Acute extrinsic upper airway compression must be relieved
* **Laryngospasm management**:
  * Can be triggered by stimulation of pharynx or vocal cords by secretions, blood, foreign matter, or extubation
  * Higher risk in patients who smoke, have irritable airways, copious secretions, or after upper airway surgery
  * Management:
	* Gentle positive pressure (10-20 mmHg continuous) in oropharynx by mask with 100% O2
	* Prolonged laryngospasm: small dose of succinylcholine (0.1 mg/kg) or propofol
	* ⚠️ Intubating dosage of succinylcholine should generally not be used
	* As little as 5-10 mg of succinylcholine can break laryngospasm
  * Severe laryngeal obstruction can occur from acute hypocalcemia after parathyroid excision
* **Soft-tissue edema considerations**:
  * Worsens airway obstruction, especially in children and adults after neck procedures
  * Nebulized vasoconstrictors like epinephrine help somewhat
  * Steroids have little effect acutely
  * Patients with C1 esterase inhibitor deficiency can develop severe angioneurotic edema
* **Small airway resistance factors**:
  * Reduction of cross-sectional area increases resistance (varies inversely with fourth power of radius during turbulent flow)
  * Triggers: pharyngeal/tracheal stimulation, histamine release, decreased radial traction
  * Risk assessment: preoperative spirometry evidence of increased airway resistance predicts risk
  * Highest risk: smokers and patients with bronchospastic conditions
* **Treatment approaches**:
  * Eliminate laryngeal or airway stimulation
  * Pre-existing regimen of inhalers often effective
  * Options for bronchospasm:
	* Levalbuterol or metaproterenol nebulized in oxygen (minimal tachycardia)
	* Nebulized racemic epinephrine (side effects: tachycardia, flushing)
	* Isoproterenol nebulization
	* Intramuscular or sublingual terbutaline
	* Steroids (little acute improvement but may prevent recurrence)
	* Anticholinergics (atropine or ipratropium) for resistant bronchospasm
	* Intravenous epinephrine infusion for life-threatening cases

#### 1.3.2.3. DECREASED COMPLIANCE

* Reduced pulmonary compliance increases elastic work of breathing
* In extreme cases leads to progressive respiratory muscle fatigue, hypoventilation, and respiratory acidemia
* **Factors affecting compliance**:
  * **Lung parenchymal changes**:
	* Reduction of FRC (small airway closure and distal lung collapse)
	* Pulmonary edema (increases lung weight/inertia, elevates surface tension)
	* Pulmonary contusion or hemorrhage
	* Restrictive lung diseases
	* Skeletal abnormalities
	* Intrathoracic lesions, hemothorax, pneumothorax, cardiomegaly
  * **Body habitus factors**:
	* Obesity (especially when adipose tissue compresses thoracic cage or increases intra-abdominal pressure)
  * **Extrathoracic factors**:
	* Tight muscles of chest or abdominal dressings
	* Gas in stomach or bowel
	* Post-laparoscopic retained CO2 (may impair diaphragm movement)
	* Intra-abdominal tumor, hemorrhage, ascites, bowel obstruction, pregnancy
* **Treatment approaches**:
  * Semi-sitting (semi-Fowler) position reduces work of breathing
  * Incentive spirometry and chest physiotherapy help restore lung volume
  * Positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) or continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP)
  * ⚠️ In COPD patients with highly compliant lungs, positive airway pressure might force rib cage and diaphragms toward excursion limits, accentuating inspiratory effort

#### 1.3.2.4. NEUROMUSCULAR AND SKELETAL PROBLEMS

* Postoperative airway obstruction and hypoventilation are accentuated by incomplete reversal of neuromuscular relaxation
* Residual paralysis compromises:
  * Airway patency
  * Ability to overcome airway resistance
  * Airway protection
  * Ability to clear secretions
* **Risk factors and considerations**:
  * Shorter-acting relaxants decrease incidence but don't eliminate the problem
  * Marginal reversal can be more dangerous than near-total paralysis due to subtle presentation
  * Somnolent patient with mild stridor and shallow ventilation might be overlooked
  * PACU staff should be aware of patients who received nondepolarizing muscle relaxants without reversal
* **Reversal considerations**:
  * Safety of techniques designed to avoid reversal not substantiated
  * Reversal of nondepolarizing relaxants is recommended
  * Sugammadex (γ-cyclodextrins) can avoid side effects of anticholinesterases and anticholinergics
* **Special neuromuscular conditions**:
  * Myasthenia gravis, Eaton–Lambert syndrome, periodic paralysis, muscular dystrophies
  * Medications that potentiate relaxation: antibiotics, furosemide, propranolol, phenytoin
  * Hypocalcemia or hypermagnesemia
* **Diaphragmatic and chest wall factors**:
  * Diaphragmatic contraction may be compromised, forcing reliance on intercostal muscles
  * Impairment of phrenic nerve function from interscalene block, trauma, or thoracic/neck operations
  * Adequate ventilation maintained with one diaphragm, marginal with intercostal muscles alone
  * Thoracic spinal or epidural blockade interferes with intercostal muscle function
  * Other factors: abnormal motor neuron function, flail chest, severe kyphosis or scoliosis
* **Assessment tests**:
  * Ability to sustain head elevation in supine position
  * Forced vital capacity of 10-12 mL/kg
  * Inspiratory pressure more negative than –25 cm H2O
  * Train-of-four assessment
  * ⚠️ None of these reliably predicts recovery of airway protective reflexes
* **Support options**:
  * Noninvasive ventilation (CPAP or BiPAP) may prevent intubation/reintubation
  * Units other than ICUs can often manage these patients
* **Clinical mimics**:
  * Voluntary limitation of chest expansion to avoid pain (splinting)
  * Small tidal volumes due to restriction generating dyspnea despite adequate minute ventilation
  * Spontaneous hyperventilation compensating for metabolic acidemia

#### 1.3.2.5. INCREASED DEAD SPACE

* Ventilation of unperfused dead space or poorly perfused alveoli with high V̇/Q̇ ratios is less effective in removing CO2
* Expansion of dead space volume or reduction of tidal volume increases V̇D/V̇T and CO2 rebreathing
* Patients with high V̇D/V̇T at greater risk for postoperative ventilatory failure
* **Causes of acute dead space increase**:
  * Excessive tubing volume or valve reversal in breathing circuits
  * PEEP or CPAP (elevates physiologic dead space, especially with high pulmonary compliance)
  * Pulmonary embolization (air, thrombus, cellular debris)
  * Decreased cardiac output (increases V̇D/V̇T by decreasing perfusion to well-ventilated, nondependent lung)
  * ARDS (destroys pulmonary microvasculature)

#### 1.3.2.6. INCREASED CARBON DIOXIDE PRODUCTION

* CO2 production varies directly with metabolic rate, body temperature, and substrate availability
* During anesthesia, CO2 production falls to approximately 60% of normal 2-3 mL/kg/min
* During recovery, metabolic rate and CO2 production can increase by 40%
* **Factors increasing CO2 production**:
  * Shivering
  * High work of breathing
  * Infection
  * Sympathetic nervous system activity
  * Rapid carbohydrate metabolism during intravenous hyperalimentation
  * Malignant hyperthermia (generates CO2 production many times greater than normal)
* Even mild increases of CO2 production can precipitate respiratory acidemia with complications of low compliance, airway resistance, or neuromuscular paralysis
* **Management approaches**:
  * Adjusting hyperalimentation
  * Improving work of breathing
  * Reducing shivering
  * Treating hyperthermia

### 1.3.3. INADEQUATE POSTOPERATIVE OXYGENATION

* **Assessment approaches**:
  * **PaO2**: Best indicator of pulmonary oxygen transfer from alveolar gas to pulmonary capillary blood
  * **Pulse oximetry**: Yields less information on alveolar-arterial gradients; not helpful for hemoglobin dissociation curve shifts or carboxyhemoglobin
  * **Metabolic assessment**: Evaluation of metabolic acidemia or mixed-venous oxygen content yields insight into peripheral oxygen delivery and utilization
* **Important considerations**:
  * Adequate arterial oxygenation does not guarantee adequate tissue oxygenation
  * Sepsis, hypotension, anemia, or hemoglobin dissociation abnormalities can cause tissue ischemia despite adequate arterial oxygenation
* **PaO2 targets**:
  * Acceptable lower limit varies with individual patient characteristics
  * PaO2 below 65-70 mmHg causes significant hemoglobin desaturation
  * Maintaining PaO2 between 80-100 mmHg (saturation 93-97%) ensures adequate oxygen availability
  * Little benefit from PaO2 above 110 mmHg (hemoglobin fully saturated, negligible additional dissolved oxygen)
  * During mechanical ventilation, PaO2 above 80 mmHg with 0.4 FIO2 and 5 cm H2O CPAP usually predicts sustained adequate oxygenation after extubation

#### 1.3.3.1. DISTRIBUTION OF VENTILATION

* Loss of dependent lung volume commonly causes V̇/Q̇ mismatching (shunt effect) and hypoxemia
* Reduction in FRC decreases radial traction on small airways, leading to collapse and distal atelectasis that can worsen for 36 hours after surgery
* **Risk factors and situations**:
  * Obese patients sustain large decreases in FRC during surgery
  * Older patients normally exhibit some airway closure at end expiration
  * COPD patients have more severe closure exacerbated by small FRC reductions
  * Surgical positioning factors:
	* Retraction, packing, manipulation, or peritoneal insufflation during upper abdominal surgery
	* Prone, lithotomy, or Trendelenburg positions (especially in obese patients)
  * Right upper lobe collapse secondary to partial right main stem intubation
  * One-lung anesthesia issues (weight of unsupported mediastinal contents, pressure from abdominal contents)
  * Acute pulmonary edema (hydrodynamic forces, increased capillary permeability)
  * Small airway occlusion from compression, retained secretions, or aspiration
  * Pneumothorax or hemothorax
* **Treatment approaches**:
  * Semi-sitting or reverse Trendelenburg position to reduce abdominal pressure on diaphragms
  * Analgesia to reduce pain with ventilation and maintain FRC
  * Deep ventilation, cough, chest physiotherapy, and incentive spirometry
  * CPAP (5-7 cm H2O) or bilevel CPAP by face mask
  * Tracheal intubation for severe hypoxemia or CPAP/bilevel mask intolerance
  * PEEP (5-10 cm H2O) typically improves PaO2 without risking hypotension, increased intracranial pressure, or barotrauma

💡 Tracheal intubation eliminates normal expiratory resistance and physiologic PEEP (2-5 cm H2O). Generally, 5 cm H2O CPAP is prudent for intubated postoperative patients

#### 1.3.3.2. DISTRIBUTION OF PERFUSION

* Poor distribution of pulmonary blood flow interferes with V̇/Q̇ matching and oxygenation
* Flow distribution primarily determined by hydrodynamic factors affected by:
  * Gravity
  * Airway pressure
  * Lung volume
  * Cardiac dynamics
* Flow distribution is modulated by hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV)
* **Factors affecting distribution**:
  * Position (placing poorly ventilated lung in dependent position can reduce PaO2)
  * Residual inhalation anesthetics, vasodilators, and sympathomimetics affect vascular tone and HPV
  * Liver cirrhosis patients exhibit poor V̇/Q̇ matching from small arteriovenous shunts
  * Circulating endotoxin impairs HPV in septic patients
* **Intervention options**:
  * Avoid placing atelectatic or diseased lung in dependent position
  * Consider tradeoffs of placing poorly ventilated parenchyma in non-dependent position
  * Caution placing diseased lung in "up" position as it may promote drainage of purulent material into unaffected lung
  * Consider avoiding vasodilatory medications to improve HPV (but benefits of these medications usually outweigh HPV impairment)

#### 1.3.3.3. INADEQUATE ALVEOLAR PAO2

* Postoperative hypoxemia can be caused by global reduction of PAO2, usually from inadequate ventilation and marked increase in PACO2
* Hypoventilation must be severe to cause hypoxemia based on the alveolar gas equation
* **Causes and considerations**:
  * Apnea, airway obstruction, soft-tissue edema, or laryngospasm lead to rapid depletion of alveolar oxygen
  * Rate of PAO2 decline varies with age, body habitus, underlying illness, and initial PAO2
  * Severe opioid or residual anesthetic depression
  * Partial airway obstruction typically doesn't reduce PAO2 with supplemental oxygen
  * Supplemental oxygen safeguards against hypoxemia from hypoventilation or airway obstruction
  * ⚠️ Supplemental oxygen eliminates pulse oximetry's ability to monitor hypoventilation
* **Other considerations**:
  * Rapid outpouring of nitrous oxide can displace alveolar gas and lower PAO2 (diffusion hypoxia)
  * Volume displacement of oxygen could occur during severe hypercarbia in patients breathing room air

#### 1.3.3.4. REDUCED MIXED VENOUS PO2

* Mixed-venous PO2 (Pv̄O2) is affected by arterial oxygen content, cardiac output, distribution of peripheral blood flow, and tissue oxygen extraction
* If arterial oxygen content decreases or tissue extraction increases, Pv̄O2 falls
* **Clinical significance**:
  * Lower Pv̄O2 in shunted blood or low V̇/Q̇ units causes greater reduction of PaO2
  * Blood with low Pv̄O2 extracts larger volumes of oxygen from alveolar gas
  * Very low Pv̄O2 increases risk of resorption atelectasis in poorly ventilated alveoli
* **Common causes in PACU**:
  * Shivering, infection, and hypermetabolism (increase peripheral oxygen extraction)
  * Low cardiac output and hypotension (decrease tissue oxygen delivery)
  * Supplemental oxygen reduces impact of low Pv̄O2 on alveolar extraction and arterial oxygenation

### 1.3.4. OBSTRUCTIVE SLEEP APNEA

* **Definition and prevalence**:
  * Syndrome with periods of partial or complete upper airway obstruction
  * Interrupts sleep patterns causing daytime hypersomnolence, decreased concentration, irritability
  * In children: increased aggression and distractible behavior
  * May cause episodic oxygen desaturation, hypercarbia, and cardiac dysfunction
  * Estimated prevalence:
	* 9% of women and 24% of men show disordered breathing while asleep
	* 2% of women and 4% of men show overt OSA symptoms
	* Recent urban study shows 7.5% 5-year incidence of moderate-to-severe sleep disordered breathing
	* Incidence influenced by age, sex, BMI, waist-hip ratio, and serum cholesterol
* **ASA classification system**:
  * Mild OSA: AHI 5-14 events/hr
  * Moderate OSA: AHI 15-29 events/hr
  * Severe OSA: AHI >30 events/hr
* **Perioperative management**:
  * Must start preoperatively with well-planned anesthetic
  * Consider type, location, and recovery of surgery
  * Postoperative management concerns:
	* Analgesia (regional with minimal sedation preferable to opioids)
	* Oxygenation (supplemental oxygen immediately postoperatively)
	* Patient positioning
	* Monitoring
  * CPAP or noninvasive positive pressure ventilation should be continued in PACU for regular users
  * High-flow nasal cannula oxygenation (HFNCO) may be beneficial when other modalities unavailable
	* Delivers flows up to 60 L/min
	* Improves respiratory distress and reduces intubation need
	* More comfortably provides higher FIO2, adjustable FIO2, and heated/humidified air
* **Positioning and monitoring**:
  * Position to minimize airway obstruction (limited by type of surgery)
  * Adult OSA patients show improved AHI scores in lateral, prone, and sitting positions vs. supine
  * Pulse oximetry recommended until oxygen saturation remains above 90% on room air while sleeping
  * Telemetry for monitoring pulse oximetry, ECG, or ventilation can reduce adverse events

### 1.3.5. ANEMIA

* Preoperative hematocrit and intraoperative hemorrhage determine a patient's red cell mass and oxygen-carrying capacity after surgery
* Reduction of hematocrit by dilution has less impact
* **Transfusion considerations**:
  * Hematocrit at which oxygen delivery becomes insufficient varies with cardiac reserve, oxygen consumption, hemoglobin dissociation, PaO2, and blood flow distribution
  * Critical DO2 requires at least 3-3.5 g/dL hemoglobin concentration in patients with normal myocardial function who are euvolemic
  * Each patient has a minimum hematocrit below which tissues use inefficient anaerobic metabolism (generating lactic acidemia)
  * Patients with vascular disease at increased risk of vital organ ischemia as hematocrit falls
* **Transfusion guidelines**:
  * ASA, Society of Thoracic Surgeons, and Society of Cardiovascular Anesthesiologists guidelines:
  * Stable, non-bleeding, euvolemic patients may tolerate hemoglobin of 6.0 g/dL
  * Transfusion may benefit patients with hemoglobin between 6-8 g/dL
  * Transfusion rarely useful above 10 g/dL

### 1.3.6. SUPPLEMENTAL OXYGEN

* **Incidence of hypoxemia**:
  * High in postoperative patients
  * In PACU patients on room air, patients with hemoglobin saturations below 90%:
	* 30% of patients younger than 1 year
	* 20% aged 1-3 years
	* 14% aged 3-14 years
	* 7.8% of adults
	* Many fall below 85%
* **Assessment challenges**:
  * Clinical observation and cognitive function do not accurately screen for hypoxemia
  * Monitoring with pulse oximetry is essential throughout PACU admission
  * Cannot predict which patients will become hypoxemic or when
* **High-risk patients**:
  * Lung disease or obesity
  * Recovering from thoracic or upper abdominal procedures
  * Preoperative hypoxemia
  * Children, especially with respiratory infections or chronic adenotonsillar hypertrophy
  * Patients after regional anesthesia
* **Administration guidelines**:
  * Should be administered to patients at high risk of hypoxemia or with low SpO2
  * Some recommend supplemental oxygen for all patients during initial recovery and transport
  * Does not address underlying causes of hypoxemia
  * Does not guarantee hypoxemia will not occur
  * Likely to mask hypoventilation
  * Routine humidification of little benefit unless intubation bypasses natural humidification
  * Oxygen apparatus can increase risk of corneal abrasion during emergence

#### 1.3.6.1. ASA GUIDELINES FOR SUPPLEMENTAL OXYGEN ADMINISTRATION

* **Evidence base** [ASA:5]:
  * One RCT corroborates findings that administration of supplemental oxygen during patient transportation or in recovery room reduces incidence of hypoxemia (Category A3-B evidence)
* **Expert opinion** [ASA:5]:
  * Expert consultants and ASA members are equivocal regarding whether administration of supplemental oxygen during patient transportation or in the PACU should be routine
* **Recommendation** [ASA:5]:
  * Administering supplemental oxygen during transportation or in the recovery room should be done for **patients at risk of hypoxemia**
  * Emphasizes selective rather than universal application based on individual patient risk assessment

💡 **Clinical integration**: ASA guidelines support targeted oxygen therapy for high-risk patients rather than routine universal administration, aligning with evidence-based individualized care [ASA:5]

### 1.3.7. PERIOPERATIVE ASPIRATION

* During anesthesia, depression of airway reflexes places patients at risk for intraoperative or postoperative pulmonary aspiration
* Pulmonary morbidity varies with type and volume of aspirate
* **Types of aspiration**:
  * **Clear oral secretions**:
	* Common and usually insignificant
	* Immediate sequelae: cough, mild tracheal irritation, transient laryngospasm
	* Large volumes predispose to infection, small airway obstruction, or pulmonary edema
  * **Blood**:
	* Secondary to trauma, epistaxis, or airway surgery
	* Marked changes on chest radiograph out of proportion with clinical signs
	* "Sterile" blood causes minor airway obstruction but is rapidly cleared
	* Massive aspiration or clots obstructs airways, interferes with oxygenation
	* Risk of secondary infection, especially with tissue or purulent matter
  * **Food, objects, teeth, dental appliances**:
	* Causes persistent cough, diffuse bronchospasm, airway obstruction, distal atelectasis, pneumonia
	* Complications often localized and treated with antibiotics and supportive care
	* Secondary thermal, chemical, or traumatic airway injury possible
	* Complete upper airway or tracheal obstruction is life-threatening
  * **Acidic gastric contents**:
	* During vomiting or regurgitation
	* Causes chemical pneumonitis with diffuse bronchospasm, hypoxemia, and atelectasis
	* Morbidity increases with volume and acidity
	* Food particles worsen pneumonitis, especially vegetable matter
	* Severe cases progress to ARDS with high-permeability pulmonary edema
	* Can lead to V̇/Q̇ mismatching, reduced compliance, increased pulmonary vascular resistance
* **Risk in PACU**:
  * Incidence relatively low but risk still significant
  * Frequency of postoperative vomiting remains high
  * Protective airway reflexes suppressed by:
	* Depressant medications (inhalation anesthetics, sedatives, opiates)
	* Persisting effects of laryngeal nerve blocks or topical local anesthetics
	* Residual neuromuscular paralysis
  * Patients can maintain airway patency and ventilation, pass head lift test, have T4/T1 ratio >0.7, but still have impaired reflexes
  * T4/T1 ratio should exceed 0.9 before reflexes are completely competent
  * Hypotension, hypoxemia, or acidemia cause both emesis and obtundation
* **Prevention strategies**:
  * Vigilance against aspiration is important
  * Trendelenburg position might promote regurgitation but aids airway clearance if vomiting occurs
  * Head elevation in unconscious patients should be avoided (creates gravitational gradient from pharynx to lung)
  * High-risk patients should not have trachea extubated until airway reflexes are restored
  * Even awake, command-following patients may have depressed gag reflex
  * Opioids and sedatives may compromise airway protection
  * Monitor upper airway for secretions or vomitus
  * Avoid cuff deflation until extubation (rigid tube impairs protective reflexes)
  * Proper extubation technique:
	* Suction pharynx
	* Extubate at end inspiration with positive airway pressure
	* Observe after extubation as reflexes might be temporarily impaired
* **Management of suspected aspiration**:
  * Immediate lateral head positioning (assuming intact cervical spine) and airway suction
  * If airway reflexes are compromised, tracheal intubation often appropriate
  * Suction trachea through tube before positive pressure ventilation to avoid disseminating material
  * Instillation of saline or alkalotic solutions not recommended
  * Checking pharyngeal aspirate pH more accurate but of little practical value
  * Suspected aspiration requires 24-48 hours of monitoring
  * Ambulatory patients with small likelihood of aspiration can have outpatient follow-up if no issues within 4-6 hours
  * Provide explicit instructions to contact medical facility if symptoms develop
  * High likelihood of aspiration warrants hospital admission
  * Chest radiograph findings may appear within 24 hours
* **Treatment for aspiration complications**:
  * Supplemental oxygen and CPAP for hypoxemia, increased airway resistance, or pulmonary edema
  * Mechanical ventilation if necessary
  * Steroids yield no improvement and may increase infection risk
  * Prophylactic antibiotics not recommended (promotes resistant organisms)
  * Use broad-spectrum antibiotics if bacterial infection apparent (coverage for gram-negative rods and anaerobes)
  * Treat pulmonary edema as ARDS (avoid diuretics unless hypervolemia exists)

**Bottom Line:** Postoperative pulmonary dysfunction requires systematic assessment and management. Understanding the underlying pathophysiology of ventilation and oxygenation problems guides appropriate interventions. Special attention should be given to high-risk populations like OSA patients. Prevention and prompt management of aspiration are critical for avoiding serious pulmonary complications.

## 1.4. POSTOPERATIVE RENAL COMPLICATIONS










### 1.4.2. ABILITY TO VOID

* The ability to void should be assessed in postoperative patients
* Opioids and autonomic side effects of regional anesthesia interfere with sphincter relaxation and promote urine retention
* **Risk considerations**:
  * Urinary retention is common after urologic, inguinal, and genital surgery
  * Retention frequently delays discharge
  * Observation after these surgeries needed to determine if inability to urinate is a possible surgical complication
* **Assessment approaches**:
  * Neither patient nor staff can accurately estimate bladder volume through sensation or palpation
  * Ultrasonic bladder scan helps assess volume before discharge
  * Avoids archaic practice of routine "straight catheterization"
* **Discharge considerations**:
  * Reasonable to discharge select ambulatory patients and inpatients before they void
  * For inpatients transferred prior to voiding, urination should be monitored to avoid complications
  * If retention persists beyond prescribed interval (10-12 hours after discharge), patient should contact a health care facility
  * High return rates after urologic procedures are related to urinary retention

### 1.4.3. RENAL TUBULAR FUNCTION

* Analysis of urine yields information about postoperative renal tubular function
* Urine color assessment:
  * Not useful for assessing concentrating ability
  * Assists recognition of hematuria, hemoglobinuria, or pyuria
* **Tubular function markers**:
  * **Urine osmolarity**: More reliable index than specific gravity (reflects number of particles in solution)
	* Osmolarity >450 mOsm/L indicates intact tubular concentrating ability
  * **Electrolyte concentrations**: Urine sodium far below or potassium above serum indicates viability
  * **pH changes**: Acidification or alkalinization of urine shows function
  * Values close to serum may indicate poor function or acute tubular necrosis
* **Anesthetic agent effects**:
  * Inorganic fluoride from certain inhalation anesthetics can cause transient reduction of concentrating ability
  * Higher fluoride levels cause renal tubular necrosis
  * Sevoflurane interaction with dry CO2 absorbents generates compound A, releasing fluoride
  * May cause transient impairment of protein retention and concentrating ability but typically doesn't seriously affect renal function

### 1.4.4. OLIGURIA

* **Definition and significance**:
  * Oliguria (≤0.5 mL/kg/hr) occurs frequently during recovery
  * Usually reflects appropriate renal response to hypovolemia
  * Stress response of surgery increases antidiuretic hormone (ADH), leading to decreased output
  * May indicate abnormal renal function
* **Assessment approach**:
  * Acceptable degree and duration varies with baseline renal status, surgical procedure, and anticipated course
  * For patients without catheters, assess interval since last void and bladder volume
  * Check indwelling catheters for kinking, obstruction by blood clots or debris, and positioning
  * Aggressively evaluate if intraoperative events could jeopardize renal function (aortic cross-clamping, severe hypotension, possible ureteral ligature, massive transfusion)
  * Ensure systemic blood pressure is adequate for renal perfusion
* **Management steps**:
  * Send urine for electrolyte and osmolarity determinations
  * Give 300-500 mL IV crystalloid bolus to assess hypovolemia response (if no intraoperative diuretics given)
  * If no improvement, consider larger bolus or diagnostic trial of furosemide (5 mg IV)
  * Furosemide increases output if oliguria reflects tubular resorption
  * Chronic diuretic therapy patients might require a diuretic to maintain output
* **Persistent oliguria considerations**:
  * Despite hydration, adequate pressure, and furosemide challenge suggests:
	* Acute tubular necrosis
	* Ureteral obstruction
	* Renal artery or vein occlusion
	* Inappropriate ADH secretion
  * Further diagnostics: cystoscopy, intravenous pyelography, angiography, radionuclide scanning
  * Osmotic or loop diuretics may attenuate renal damage
  * Low-dose dopamine or dobutamine not proven to improve function
  * Fenoldopam may reduce acute kidney injury risk in high-risk cardiac surgical patients
  * Nephrology consultation prudent

### 1.4.5. POLYURIA

* **Clinical significance**:
  * Relying on high postoperative output to gauge intravascular volume or renal viability can be misleading
  * Profuse output often reflects generous intraoperative fluid administration
  * Osmotic diuresis from hyperglycemia and glycosuria is common, especially with glucose-containing crystalloids
  * May reflect intraoperative diuretic administration
* **Concerning polyuria**:
  * Sustained polyuria (4-5 mL/kg/hr) can indicate abnormal water clearance regulation or high-output renal failure
  * Especially concerning if output compromises intravascular volume and blood pressure
* **Diabetes insipidus**:
  * Occurs secondary to:
	* Intracranial surgery
	* Pituitary ablation
	* Head trauma
	* Increased intracranial pressure
  * Diagnostic markers:
	* Urine specific gravity ≤1.005
	* Urine osmolality <200 mOsm/kg

**Bottom Line:** Postoperative renal function assessment includes monitoring voiding ability, urine output, and tubular function. Oliguria requires systematic evaluation to distinguish between hypovolemia and renal dysfunction. Both oliguria and polyuria can signal important underlying conditions requiring specific interventions.

## 1.5. METABOLIC COMPLICATIONS










### 1.5.2. POSTOPERATIVE ACID–BASE DISORDERS

* Categorization of postoperative acid–base abnormalities into primary and compensatory disorders is difficult
* Rapidly changing pathophysiology often generates multiple primary disorders

#### 1.5.2.1. RESPIRATORY ACIDEMIA

* **Causes and characteristics**:
  * Frequently encountered in PACU patients
  * Caused by anesthetics, opioids, and sedatives depressing CNS sensitivity to pH and PaCO2
  * In awake, spontaneously breathing patients with adequate analgesia, typically mild (PaCO2 45-50 mmHg, pH 7.36-7.32)
  * More profound in deeply sedated patients
  * Residual neuromuscular paralysis, increased airway resistance, or decreased compliance may prevent adequate ventilation
  * Worsened by increased CO2 production (fever, shivering, hyperalimentation)
  * Limited compensation in recovery period (kidneys require hours to generate compensatory metabolic alkalosis)
* **Symptoms and signs**:
  * Agitation, confusion, and tachypnea
  * Sympathetic response: hypertension, tachycardia, and dysrhythmias
  * CNS depression often produces less intense sympathetic activity
  * In patients with head injury, intracranial tumors, or cerebral edema, increases cerebral blood flow and intracranial pressure
  * At very low pH, catecholamines cannot interact with adrenergic receptors, causing precipitous heart rate and blood pressure decrease
* **Treatment approaches**:
  * Correct imbalance between CO2 production and alveolar ventilation
  * Improve ventilatory drive by judicious reversal of opioids or benzodiazepines
  * Ensure patient doesn't have increased airway resistance or residual neuromuscular blockade
  * If spontaneous ventilation cannot maintain CO2 excretion, provide tracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation
  * Consider reducing CO2 production by controlling fever or shivering

#### 1.5.2.2. METABOLIC ACIDEMIA

* **Causes and evaluation**:
  * Ketoacidosis in diabetic patients (elevated glucose, detectable ketones in blood/urine)
  * Renal failure or renal tubular acidosis (typically present preoperatively)
  * Large volume saline infusions (mild hyperchloremic acidemia; avoidable with lactated Ringer)
  * Toxic ingestion (rare: aspirin, methanol)
  * Most commonly represents lactic acidemia from insufficient oxygen delivery/utilization
* **Common causes of peripheral hypoperfusion**:
  * Low cardiac output (hypovolemia, cardiac failure, dysrhythmia)
  * Peripheral vasodilation (sepsis, catecholamine depletion, sympathectomy)
  * Arteriolar constriction (hypothermia, pressor administration)
  * Other causes of lactic acidemia:
	* Hypoxemia
	* Severe anemia
	* Impaired hemoglobin dissociation
	* CO poisoning
	* Inability to use oxygen in mitochondria (cyanide or arsenic poisoning)
* **Compensation and symptoms**:
  * Spontaneously breathing patients increase ventilation, quickly generating respiratory alkalosis
  * General anesthetics and analgesics can suppress this ventilatory response
  * Sympathetic response often milder than with respiratory acidemia (hydrogen and bicarbonate ions cross blood-brain barrier with more difficulty than CO2)
* **Treatment approach**:
  * Resolve condition causing metabolic acid accumulation
  * For ketoacidosis: intravenous potassium, insulin, and glucose
  * For lactic acidosis: improve cardiac output, systemic blood pressure, rewarming
  * For mild acidemia with improved underlying conditions: allow renal excretion of hydrogen ions
  * For severe/progressive acidemia: consider intravenous bicarbonate or calcium gluconate

#### 1.5.2.3. RESPIRATORY ALKALEMIA

* **Causes**:
  * Pain or anxiety during emergence causing hyperventilation
  * Excessive mechanical ventilation
  * Pathologic "central" hyperventilation:
	* Sepsis
	* Cerebrovascular accident
	* Paradoxic CNS acidosis (imbalance of bicarbonate concentration across the blood-brain barrier)
* **Clinical manifestations**:
  * Confusion, dizziness, atrial dysrhythmias, abnormal cardiac conduction
  * Decreased cerebral blood flow (hypoperfusion risk, stroke risk with cerebrovascular disease)
  * Reduced serum ionized calcium with muscle fasciculation or hypocalcemic tetany if severe
  * Very high pH depresses cardiovascular, CNS, and catecholamine receptor functions
  * Limited metabolic compensation (time constants for bicarbonate excretion are large)
* **Treatment**:
  * Reduce alveolar ventilation, usually with analgesics and sedatives
  * Rebreathing of CO2 has limited PACU application

#### 1.5.2.4. METABOLIC ALKALEMIA

* **Causes**:
  * Rare unless pre-existing condition:
	* Vomiting
	* Gastric suctioning
	* Dehydration
	* Alkaline ingestion
	* Potassium-wasting diuretics
  * Excessive intraoperative bicarbonate administration
  * Alkalemia from metabolism of lactate or citrate usually doesn't appear within first 24 hours
* **Physiology and treatment**:
  * Respiratory compensation through CO2 retention is rapid but limited (hypoventilation eventually causes hypoxemia)
  * Treatment: hydration and correction of hypochloremia and hypokalemia allow kidney to excrete excess bicarbonate

### 1.5.3. GLUCOSE DISORDERS AND CONTROL

* Adequate glucose control recommended to reduce morbidity in postsurgical patients
* Benefits in diabetic and nondiabetic patients:
  * Reduced complications
  * Shorter hospital stays
  * Improved outcomes

⚠️ **Potential risks**:

* Hypoglycemia and coma risk should not be discounted
* Insulin therapy must be based on serum glucose levels
* Requires careful, timely monitoring to avoid hypoglycemia
* Clear, concise handoff of information when patient care is transferred
* **Monitoring considerations**:
  * Urine glucose measurements should be reserved to assess osmotic diuresis and estimate renal transport thresholds
  * Many diabetic patients present with insulin pumps
  * Important to know if devices were continued through perioperative period or when to reinstitute therapy

#### 1.5.3.1. HYPERGLYCEMIA

* **Causes and management**:
  * Glucose infusions and stress responses commonly elevate levels after surgery
  * For most patients during anesthesia, glucose should not be included in maintenance IV solutions
  * Moderate postoperative hyperglycemia (150-250 mg/dL) in nondiabetic patients:
	* Usually resolves spontaneously
	* Has little adverse effect
  * Higher glucose levels:
	* Cause glycosuria with osmotic diuresis
	* Interfere with serum electrolyte determinations
	* Can increase serum osmolality to point of cerebral disequilibrium and hyperosmolar coma
  * Type I diabetic patients risk ketoacidosis
  * Management requires potassium replacement and serial blood glucose determinations

#### 1.5.3.2. HYPOGLYCEMIA

* **Causes**:
  * Endogenous insulin secretion
  * Excessive or inadvertent insulin administration
* **Management considerations**:
  * Serious postoperative hypoglycemia is rare
  * Easily treated with IV 50% dextrose followed by glucose infusion
  * Either sedation or excessive sympathetic activity can mask symptoms
  * Diabetic patients and those receiving intraoperative insulin must have serum glucose measured
  * Extreme care with documenting and reporting insulin use is paramount

### 1.5.4. ELECTROLYTE DISORDERS

#### 1.5.4.1. HYPONATREMIA

* **Causes**:
  * Free water infusion during surgery
  * Absorption of sodium-free irrigating solution during:
	* Transurethral prostatic resection
	* Hysteroscopy
  * Accumulation of serum glycine or ammonia metabolite may exacerbate symptoms
  * Free water retention from:
	* Inappropriate ADH secretion
	* Prolonged labor induction with oxytocin
	* Respiratory uptake of nebulized droplets
  * Excessive isotonic saline theoretically leads to hypertonic urine excretion and iatrogenic hyponatremia
* **Clinical manifestations**:
  * Moderate hyponatremia: agitation, disorientation, visual disturbances, nausea
  * Severe hyponatremia: unconsciousness, impaired airway reflexes, CNS irritability progressing to grand mal seizures
* **Treatment**:
  * IV normal saline
  * IV furosemide to promote free water excretion
  * Hypertonic saline for severe cases (with caution not to increase sodium by >0.5 mEq/hr to avoid CNS lesions or pulmonary edema)
  * Measure serum sodium and osmolarity every 2-4 hours during hypertonic saline therapy

#### 1.5.4.2. HYPOKALEMIA

* **Significance and causes**:
  * Often inconsequential but can cause serious dysrhythmias, especially with digoxin
  * Underlying deficits from:
	* Chronic diuretic therapy
	* Nasogastric suctioning
	* Vomiting
  * Acute causes:
	* Urinary and hemorrhagic losses
	* Dilution
	* Insulin therapy
  * Worsens during respiratory alkalemia
  * Exacerbated by:
	* Excess sympathetic activity
	* Calcium infusion
	* β-mimetic medications
* **Treatment considerations**:
  * Adding potassium to peripheral IV fluids often restores concentration
  * Concentrated solutions through central catheter may be necessary
  * Deceptive to think 10-30 mEq will correct deficits
  * Potassium primarily intracellular; plasma deficit indicates far greater intracellular deficit
  * Intracellular-to-extracellular ratio important; rapid changes can contribute to dysrhythmias

#### 1.5.4.3. HYPERKALEMIA

* **Causes and diagnosis**:
  * Spurious hyperkalemia possibilities:
	* Hemolyzed specimen
	* Sampling near IV catheter containing potassium/banked blood
  * True hyperkalemia from:
	* Excessive potassium infusion
	* Renal failure
	* Malignant hyperthermia
  * Acute acidemia exacerbates hyperkalemia
* **Treatment**:
  * IV insulin and glucose acutely lowers potassium
  * IV calcium counters myocardial effects
  * Hemodialysis may be necessary for high levels or symptomatic patients

#### 1.5.4.4. CALCIUM AND MAGNESIUM

* **Calcium disorders**:
  * Hypocalcemia causes:
	* Underlying parathyroid disease
	* Massive fluid replacement
  * Symptomatic hypocalcemia rarely occurs in PACU
  * Upper airway obstruction possible after parathyroid excision
  * Acute alkalemia reduces ionized fraction causing:
	* Myocardial conduction/contractility abnormalities
	* Decreased vascular tone
	* Tetany
  * Transfusion with chelating agents (citrate) rarely causes symptoms
  * Treatment: calcium chloride or calcium gluconate improves cardiovascular dynamics
* **Magnesium considerations**:
  * Key role in:
	* Neuromuscular function recovery
	* Cardiac rhythm and conduction maintenance
  * Hypermagnesemia rare (efficient renal excretion)
  * Obstetric patients receiving magnesium for tocolysis or hypertension may present with:
	* Hyporeflexia
	* Prolonged AV conduction or heart block (higher levels)
  * Treatment: IV calcium and diuretics

**Bottom Line:** Postoperative metabolic complications require systematic diagnosis and targeted treatment. Acid-base disorders commonly occur during recovery and may be multifactorial. Glucose control benefits outcomes but requires vigilance to prevent hypoglycemia. Electrolyte disorders, especially involving sodium and potassium, need prompt correction to prevent serious complications.

## 1.6. MISCELLANEOUS COMPLICATIONS








### 1.6.2. INCIDENTAL TRAUMA

* Each patient admitted to PACU should be carefully evaluated for traumatic complications
* If a complication is discovered:
  * Document carefully
  * Notify physicians responsible for extended care
  * Consult with specialists
  * Ensure appropriate follow-up

### 1.6.3. OCULAR INJURIES AND VISUAL CHANGES

* **Corneal abrasion**:
  * Common intraoperative eye injury
  * Incidence in non-ophthalmic patients: 0.034-0.17% (higher with prone or lateral positioning)
  * PACU causes:
	* Patients rubbing eyes
	* Rigid oxygen mask riding up on eye
	* Rubbing with pulse oximeter probe
	* Eye make-up being rubbed into eyes
  * Symptoms: tearing, decreased visual acuity, pain, and photophobia
  * Diagnosis aided by fluorescein staining
  * Usually heals spontaneously within 72 hours without scarring
  * Severe injury can cause cataract formation and impair vision
  * No standardized treatment, but options include:
	* Artificial tears
	* Topical antibiotics
	* Topical analgesics
	* Eye closure
  * Daily follow-up recommended until healing verified
  * Ophthalmology referral if not improved in 48 hours
* **Visual acuity impairment**:
  * Often impaired after anesthesia
  * Causes:
	* Autonomic medication side effects impair accommodation
	* Residual ocular lubricants cloud vision
	* Retinal perfusion impairment from ocular compression
	* Ischemic optic atrophy (can occur without external compression)
  * Increased risk of blindness:
	* Long procedures in prone position
	* Vascular disease
	* Pre-existing hypertension
	* Diabetes
	* Sickle cell anemia
  * Some patients suffer permanent deficits in acuity requiring refractive adjustment
  * Anesthesiologists should check acuity when assessing high-risk patients

### 1.6.4. HEARING IMPAIRMENT

* **Characteristics**:
  * Relatively common after anesthesia and surgery
  * Often subclinical
  * Sometimes manifests as decreased auditory acuity, tinnitus, or roaring
  * High incidence after dural puncture for spinal anesthesia (8-16%)
	* Varies with needle size, type, and patient age
	* Can be unilateral or bilateral
	* Usually resolves spontaneously
  * Also occurs after general anesthesia (cardiac and non-cardiac)
  * Possible causes:
	* Disruption of round window
	* Tympanic membrane rupture
	* Eustachian tube inflammation
	* Otitis secondary to endotracheal intubation

### 1.6.5. ORAL, PHARYNGEAL, AND LARYNGEAL INJURIES

* **Soft tissue trauma**:
  * Causes: laryngoscope blades, surgical instruments, rigid oral airways, dentition
  * Lip, tongue, or gum abrasions treated with ice pack and analgesia
  * Penetrating injuries may require topical antibiotics
  * Traumatic intubation can cause hematoma or edema leading to partial upper airway obstruction
  * Nebulized racemic epinephrine often improves stridor more quickly than steroids
  * Dental damage from airway manipulations or forceful clenching
  * Document tooth or dental appliance damage, obtain dental consultation, observe for aspiration signs
* **Sore throat and hoarseness**:
  * Occurs in 20-50% of patients after intubation
  * Factors: trauma during laryngoscopy, oropharyngeal suctioning, intubation duration, tube type
  * Mucosal irritation presents as unquenchable dryness
  * Local anesthetic ointments may cause additional irritation
  * Topical viscous lidocaine helps with nasogastric tube irritation but may increase aspiration risk
  * In children: severity of postextubation laryngeal edema or tracheitis varies with:
	* Age
	* Intubation duration
	* Trauma or tube movement
	* Treatment: cool mist therapy, possibly nebulized racemic epinephrine and dexamethasone
* **Other injuries**:
  * Hypoglossal, lingual, or recurrent laryngeal nerve damage
  * Vocal cord evulsion
  * Desquamation of laryngeal or tracheal mucosa
  * Edema or ulceration
  * Tracheal perforation
  * Non-intubation causes of sore throat:
	* Laryngeal mask airways
	* Oral airways
	* Suctioning trauma
	* Drying from non-humidified gases
  * Neck and jaw soreness common after face mask anesthetics

### 1.6.6. NERVE INJURIES

* **Positioning injuries**:
  * Improper positioning during anesthesia can cause serious long-term complications
  * Spinal cord injury from intubation positioning or neuraxial anesthetic hematoma
  * Peripheral nerve compression or stretch injuries can cause permanent sensory/motor deficits
  * Any bruising or skin breakdown should prompt evaluation for nerve damage
  * Many postoperative neuropathies have no identifiable cause
* **Ulnar neuropathy**:
  * May be related to subtle positioning, pre-existing impairment, or nerve ischemia sensitivity
  * Every complaint of nonsurgical pain, numbness, or weakness requires careful evaluation
  * For neuropathic weakness, electromyography may determine lesion location and reversibility
  * Sensory neuropathies rarely last longer than 5 days; refer to neurology if exceeds this time or progresses
* **Post-dural puncture headache**:
  * May first occur in PACU, but most appear within 24-48 hours
  * More frequent after difficult subarachnoid anesthetics or inadvertent dural puncture
  * Subarachnoid air bubbles may contribute
  * PACU treatment: supportive with hydration, analgesics, and positioning
  * Severe cases may require early epidural blood patch
  * Nerve injury from regional anesthesia rare but does occur
  * In one study: 6.3% of 4,767 patients experienced paresthesia during spinal placement, but only 0.126% had persisting symptoms
  * PACU symptoms (usually transient): pain, focal numbness, residual paresthesia, dysesthesia
  * Management: analgesia, reassurance, documentation, follow-up for evolving deficits
* **Neurologic irritation after spinal anesthesia**:
  * Some patients exhibit lower extremity discomfort, buttock pain, and lumbar/sacral irritation
  * More common in obese patients, after lithotomy position, and with 5% lidocaine
  * Symptoms are transient and treated supportively
  * Rarely, chemical meningitis from contaminated or pH-inappropriate injectate (presents with headache and meningeal signs)

### 1.6.7. SOFT-TISSUE AND JOINT INJURIES

* **Pressure-related injuries**:
  * Improperly padded pressure points can develop ischemia and necrosis
  * Higher risk with lateral or prone positioning
  * Potential complications:
	* Localized alopecia from scalp pressure
	* Entrapment of ears, breasts, genitalia, or skin folds causing inflammation/necrosis
	* Regional ischemia from major arterial compression (rare)
	* Burns from cautery equipment, preparatory solutions, or adhesives
	* Extravasation causing sloughing, localized chemical neuropathy, or compartment syndromes
	* Excessive joint/muscle extension leading to backache, joint pain, stiffness, or instability
  * After regional anesthesia, extremities must be properly secured and padded

### 1.6.8. SKELETAL MUSCLE PAIN

* **Contributing factors**:
  * Prolonged immobility or unusual muscle stretch during positioning
  * Succinylcholine-related myalgia (reported in 5-83% of patients)
  * Pathogenesis unclear but also occurs with other relaxants and in patients without relaxants
  * Delayed-onset muscle fatigue can appear days after surgery and resolves spontaneously

### 1.6.9. HYPOTHERMIA AND SHIVERING

* **Incidence and patterns**:
  * Intraoperative temperature maintenance is a goal, but patients still exhibit postoperative hypothermia
  * Heat loss similar during general or regional anesthesia
  * Rewarming slower after regional anesthesia (residual vasodilation and paralysis impede heat generation/retention)
  * Higher risk groups:
	* Cachectic patients
	* Traumatized patients
	* Burned patients
	* Infants (low body mass-to-surface area ratio)
* **Complications**:
  * Complicates and prolongs PACU care
  * Average PACU stay increased by 40-90 minutes for hypothermic patients
  * Increases sympathetic activity (elevated epinephrine and norepinephrine)
  * Elevates peripheral vascular resistance and decreases venous capacitance
  * Increases myocardial ischemia and dysrhythmia risk
  * Interferes with pulse oximetry and intra-arterial pressure monitoring reliability
  * Compromises marginal tissue grafts and promotes tissue hypoxia/acidemia
  * Impairs oxygen unloading due to higher hemoglobin affinity
  * Contributes to coagulopathy through:
	* Platelet sequestration
	* Decreased platelet function
	* Reduced clotting factor function
  * Causes moderate hyperglycemia
  * Compromises cellular immune responses
  * Increases infection rates
  * Prolongs drug effects:
	* Decreases MAC of inhalation anesthetics (5-7% per 1°C cooling)
	* Accentuates residual sedation
	* May increase duration of neuromuscular relaxants and sedatives
  * Cardiac effects by temperature:
	* Moderate hypothermia (28-32°C): dysrhythmias
	* Severe hypothermia (≤28°C): interferes with rhythm generation and conduction
	* ECG changes: PR, QRS, or QT intervals lengthen, J waves appear
	* Spontaneous ventricular fibrillation below 28°C
* **Shivering physiology**:
  * During emergence, hypothalamic regulation generates shivering to increase heat production
  * Complications of shivering:
	* Incidental trauma risk
	* Disrupts medical devices
	* Interferes with ECG and pulse oximetry
	* Increases oxygen consumption and CO2 production up to 200%
	* Associated increases in minute ventilation and cardiac output may precipitate ventilatory failure or myocardial ischemia
  * Accentuated by tremors related to inhalation anesthetic emergence
  * Includes clonic and tonic components from decreased cortical influence on spinal reflexes
* **Management approaches**:
  * Restoration of normothermia is an important PACU goal
  * Provide supplemental oxygen
  * Forced-air warming devices most useful
  * Warm intravenous fluids and blood
  * For most patients, shivering is uncomfortable but self-limited
  * Many medications suggested to suppress shivering, but meperidine most efficacious
  * Fentanyl useful when meperidine contraindicated
  * Withholding relaxant reversal in ventilated, sedated patients attenuates shivering but increases rewarming time
  * Transfer appropriate when temperature near normal (>96°F) and shivering resolved
* **Quality measures**:
  * Surgical Care Improvement Project (CMS, TJC) measure: maintaining temperatures above 36°C
  * PACU has 15-minute period from admission to achieve measured temperature ≥36°C
  * Normothermia reduces wound infections, improving outcomes and shortening stay

#### 1.6.9.1. ASA GUIDELINES FOR TEMPERATURE MANAGEMENT

* **Evidence for active warming** [ASA:6]:
  * Active patient warming is associated with normalizing patient temperature (Category A2-B evidence)
  * Forced-air warming device normalizes patient temperature and reduces shivering (Category A1-B evidence)
  * One new RCT corroborates findings for normalization of patient temperature (Category A3-B evidence) but equivocal for reduction of shivering (Category A3-E evidence)
* **Expert consensus** [ASA:6]:
  * Expert consultants and ASA members agree that perioperative maintenance of normothermia and use of forced-air warming reduce shivering and improve patient comfort and satisfaction
* **Meperidine for shivering** [ASA:6]:
  * Meperidine is effective in reducing patient shivering during emergence and recovery when compared with placebo or other opioid agonists or agonist-antagonists (Category A1-B evidence)
  * One new RCT corroborates efficacy of meperidine in reducing shivering when compared with nonopioid pharmacologic agents (Category A3-B evidence)
  * Expert consultants and ASA members agree that meperidine is more effective in treatment of patient shivering than other opioid agonists or agonist-antagonists
* **ASA Recommendations** [ASA:6]:
  * **Normothermia should be a goal during emergence and recovery**
  * **When available, forced air warming systems should be used for treating hypothermia**
  * **Meperidine should be used for treatment of patients shivering during emergence and recovery when clinically indicated**
  * **Hypothermia, a common cause of shivering, should be treated by rewarming**
  * **Practitioners may consider other opioid agonists or agonist-antagonists when meperidine is contraindicated or not available**

💡 **Clinical integration**: ASA guidelines strongly support aggressive temperature management and provide clear hierarchy for shivering treatment, emphasizing rewarming as primary intervention with meperidine as preferred pharmacologic option [ASA:6]

### 1.6.10. HYPERTHERMIA

* **Incidence and causes**:
  * Relatively uncommon in PACU
  * Occasional short-lived hyperthermia from:
	* Close draping
	* Aggressive intraoperative heat preservation
  * Postoperative fever possible from:
	* Pre-existing infection (sinusitis, respiratory/urinary tract)
	* Infection exacerbated by surgery (infected tonsils/appendix, abscess drainage, urinary tract manipulation)
	* Drug or transfusion reaction
	* Muscarinic blocking agents (atropine) interfering with cooling
	* Hypermetabolic states (thyroid storm)
	* Malignant hyperthermia (usually identified by other signs first)
* **Management**:
  * Ambient cooling
  * Chest physiotherapy
  * Incentive spirometry
  * Antipyretics
  * Withhold offending medications or blood products if reaction suspected
  * Notify responsible physician for post-discharge evaluation

### 1.6.11. PERSISTENT SEDATION

* **Definition and assessment**:
  * ~90% of patients regain consciousness within 15 minutes of PACU admission
  * Unconsciousness beyond this period considered prolonged
  * Even highly susceptible patients should respond to stimuli within 30-45 minutes
  * Assessment approach:
	* Research preoperative responsiveness (uncover intoxication, pre-existing dysfunction)
	* Note timing and amount of preoperative/intraoperative sedatives
	* Review unusual intraoperative events
	* Assess ventilation (bradypnea pathognomonic for opioid effect)
	* Include tactile stimulus (light skin pinch elicits greater arousal than verbal stimulation)
	* Note limited diagnostic value of pupillary response
* **Common causes**:
  * **Inhalation anesthetics**:
	* Especially after long procedures, in obese patients, or with high concentrations until surgery end
	* Less likely with low-solubility agents (sevoflurane, desflurane)
  * **Premedications**:
	* Diphenhydramine, hydroxyzine, promethazine, droperidol, lorazepam, midazolam, meclizine, scopolamine
  * **Intraoperative medications**:
	* Dose-related sedation from opioids or sedatives
	* Opioids uniquely cause bradypnea - if respiratory rate <14-16, opioids are affecting consciousness
* **Reversal options**:
  * **For opioids**:
	* Low-dose naloxone (0.04-mg increments every 2 minutes, up to 0.2 mg)
	* With careful titration, can reverse respiratory depression and sedation without dangerous analgesia reversal
	* If related to opioids, ventilatory rate and arousal increase with ≤0.2 mg IV naloxone
  * **For benzodiazepines**:
	* Flumazenil (0.2 mg IV per minute up to 1.0 mg)
	* Differentiates sedation from midazolam and diazepam
	* Duration of action is short
	* Risk of seizures in chronic benzodiazepine users
  * **Other approaches**:
	* Neither naloxone nor flumazenil for routine postoperative care
	* Reserve pharmacologic reversal for specific indications
	* IV physostigmine (0.5-1 mg) counteracts but doesn't reverse sedation from inhalation anesthetics, other sedatives, and anticholinergics
* **Special considerations**:
  * Dexmedetomidine can cause persistent sedation
	* Patients typically easy to arouse and follow commands
	* Less respiratory depression unless combined with other depressants
	* Safe PACU discharge possible if destination appropriate for sedation level
  * Profound residual paralysis could mimic unconsciousness
	* Occurs with overdosage, omitted reversal, unrecognized neuromuscular disease, phase II blockade
	* Ruled out by purposeful motion, spontaneous ventilation, or reflex movement
	* Low-energy peripheral nerve stimulation helps diagnose
  * Other potential causes:
	* CNS depression from local anesthetic toxicity or inadvertent subarachnoid injection
	* Exhausted children, especially after disrupted sleep patterns
	* Hypothermia below 33°C (increases depressant medication effects)
	* Core temperatures below 30°C can cause fixed pupillary dilation, areflexia, and coma
	* Hypoglycemia or hyperglycemic hyperosmolar coma (check serum glucose)
	* Hyposmolar states (<260 mOsm/L) such as acute hyponatremia (Na <125 mEq/L)
	* CO2 narcosis (PaCO2 >80-100 mmHg)
	* Carbon monoxide poisoning (check carboxyhemoglobin)
	* Feigned unresponsiveness or hysterical reaction (diagnosis of exclusion)
* **Further evaluation**:
  * If diagnosis remains unclear, consult neurology
  * Consider possibility of:
	* Subclinical seizures (delirium tremens, underlying seizure disorder)
	* Cerebral anoxia from hypoperfusion or hypoxemia
	* Unrecognized head trauma, intracerebral hemorrhage, increased intracranial pressure
	* Slow awakening after long intracranial procedures
	* Cerebral thromboembolism (higher risk with jugular/subclavian cannulation)
	* Patients with atrial fibrillation, carotid bruits, or hypercoagulable states
	* Paradoxic air or fat embolism through right-to-left intracardiac shunt
	* Postoperative stroke (2.2-5.2% after cardiac/vascular/neck surgery; 0.03-0.08% in fourth decade, rising to 3-4% by eighth decade)

### 1.6.12. ALTERED MENTAL STATUS

* Recovering patients sometimes exhibit inappropriate mental reactions ranging from lethargy and confusion to physical combativeness and extreme disorientation

#### 1.6.12.1. EMERGENCE REACTIONS

* **Consequences of stormy emergence**:
  * Disturbance to staff and other patients
  * Increased risk of incidental trauma (contusion, fracture, corneal abrasion, sprains)
  * Jeopardy to suture lines, orthopedic fixations, vascular grafts, drains, tubes, catheters
  * High sympathetic tone, tachycardia, hypertension
  * Risk of injury to staff protecting combative patients
* **Patterns and predictors**:
  * Initial post-consciousness period: inability to process sensory input
  * Most exhibit somnolence, slight disorientation, sluggish reactions that rapidly clear
  * Some experience emotional swings (weeping, escalating resistance)
  * Difficult to predict which patients will have adverse reactions
  * Emergence delirium prevalent in children and young adults
  * Not clearly related to specific anesthesia types
  * Anxiety is consistent predictive factor
  * Young children: heightened anxiety from parental separation
  * Very young children may react to improved hearing after myringotomies
  * Patients with reduced mental capacity, psychiatric disorders, brain dysfunction, or hostile preoperative interactions manifest those problems postoperatively
  * Communication barriers (intubation, oral fixation) generate frustration/fear
  * Ethnic, cultural, and psychological characteristics play roles
  * Language barriers or new hearing impairment exacerbate reactions
  * Higher incidence after emotionally significant procedures
  * Intraoperative recall can generate severe panic and anxiety
* **Contributing factors**:
  * **Substance-related**:
	* Alcohol, opioid, cocaine, or illicit drug abuse (intoxication or withdrawal)
	* Scopolamine (premedication or antiemetic) causing disorientation, paranoia, combativeness
	* Ketamine (dysphoria and hallucination, though acute reactions rare)
	* Etomidate (possible restlessness)
  * **Pain-related**:
	* Amplifies agitation, confusion, and aggressive behavior
	* Helpful to ensure adequate analgesia early in PACU course
	* Discomfort sources: urinary urgency, gastric distention, tight dressings, phlebotomy, poor positioning
	* Endotracheal/nasogastric tubes and urinary catheters cause discomfort
	* Unusual pain sources: corneal abrasion, entrapment, infiltrated catheters, small devices beneath patient
	* Nausea, dizziness, and pruritus are distressing
	* Positioning discomfort, especially for patients with reflux, pulmonary congestion, obesity
	* Resistance to physical restraint
  * **Residual paralysis**:
	* Elicits agitation or uncoordinated motions mimicking disorientation/combativeness
	* Diagnosed by weakness observation or peculiar voluntary motion
	* Patients can pass head lift and train-of-four monitoring but still perceive impaired swallowing, vision, strength
  * **Respiratory dysfunction**:
	* Moderate hypoxemia presents with clouded mentation, disorientation, agitation like pain
	* Respiratory acidemia causes profound agitation (less if ventilatory center depressed)
	* Hypercarbia more likely causes lethargy or somnolence
	* Limited inspiration (chest dressings, gastric distention, splinting) causes air hunger dissatisfaction
	* Similar sensation during mechanical ventilation with low volumes (mediated by lung stretch receptors)
	* Forceful cough/secretion clearance inability causes distress
	* High work of breathing problematic
	* Interstitial pulmonary edema causes air hunger before airway flooding
  * **Metabolic abnormalities**:
	* Lactic acidemia: anxiety and mild disorientation
	* Acute hyponatremia: clouded sensorium
	* Hypoglycemia: initial agitation then diminished responsiveness
	* Seizure activity may mimic agitation/combativeness
	* Higher seizure risk: epilepsy, head trauma, chronic alcohol/cocaine abuse
	* Cerebral hypoperfusion: disorientation, agitation, combativeness
	* May require increasing mean arterial pressure for adequate cerebral perfusion
* **Management approaches**:
  * Few interventions prevent combative reactions
  * Supportive treatment as most reactions disappear within 10-15 minutes
  * Verbal reassurance that surgery is completed and patient is doing well
  * Use patient's name frequently with reassurance
  * Stress time and location
  * Allow patients to choose their position when practical
  * Provide adequate analgesia
  * Selective parenteral sedation for fear/anxiety
  * Important to identify whether reaction is to pain or anxiety
	* Benzodiazepines and barbiturates ineffective for pain
	* Opioids poor for sedation
  * Avoid sedatives/analgesics if altered status might reflect physiologic abnormality
  * Use restraints only if patient's or staff's safety is jeopardized

#### 1.6.12.2. DELIRIUM AND COGNITIVE DECLINE

* **Incidence and patterns**:
  * High percentage of elderly patients (5-50%) experience some degree of:
	* Postoperative confusion
	* Delirium
	* Cognitive decline
  * Manifestations:
	* Fluctuating consciousness and orientation
	* Deterioration of memory and mental functions
	* Impaired acquisition of new information
  * Two subtypes:
	* Hypoactive predominates
	* Smaller percentage hyperactive
  * Possible causes:
	* Exacerbation of central cholinergic insufficiency by narcotics, sedatives, anticholinergics
	* Stress of surgery
	* Fever, pain, emesis
	* Sleep deprivation
	* Loss of routine
  * Predictive factors:
	* Pre-existing dementia, cognitive abnormalities, organic brain syndrome
	* Hearing and visual impairment
	* Physical infirmity (high ASA status, lack of stress response)
  * Also occurs in younger patients (15% greater than control)
	* More frequently resolves within 3 months
	* May relate to inactivity during recuperation
  * Often appears on first to third postoperative day, but may begin in PACU
* **Age-related considerations**:
  * Slower cognitive recovery in elderly
  * Older patients often skilled at concealing declining capabilities
  * Careful preoperative assessment helps identify deficits affecting postoperative status
  * Postoperative lethargy, clouded sensorium, or delirium may reflect acute physiologic change:
	* Hyperosmolarity from hyperglycemia or hypernatremia
	* Hyponatremia
	* Cerebral fluid shifts (dialysis patients, rapid dehydration correction)
	* Anticholinergic delirium (atropine premedication, chronic meperidine)
	* Psychogenic drugs, long-acting sedatives, unrecognized intoxication
  * Life-threatening conditions presenting as confusion or reduced consciousness:
	* Seizures
	* Hypoxemia
	* Hypoglycemia
	* Hypotension
	* Acidemia
	* Cerebrovascular accident
* **Prevention and management**:
  * No anesthetic technique known to prevent delirium
  * Avoid factors that increase risk:
	* Benzodiazepines
	* Anticholinergics
	* Meperidine
  * Protocol-based approach starting with surgical planning
  * Consistent implementation through PACU recovery
  * Key components:
	* Effective pain management
	* Proper hydration
	* Removing unnecessary catheters
	* Restoring cognitive stimulation (return eyeglasses and hearing aids)
	* Reorientation and frequent human interaction

### 1.6.13. POSTOPERATIVE NAUSEA AND VOMITING

* **Significance**:
  * Significant challenge after many anesthetics
  * Considered by many patients the most unpleasant aspect following anesthesia
  * Often patients' greatest fear for subsequent anesthetics
  * Patients frequently more concerned about PONV than pain or other risks
* **Medical risks**:
  * Increased abdominal pressure
  * Increased central venous pressure
  * Aspiration risk
  * Sympathetic responses (increased BP and HR)
  * Parasympathetic responses (bradycardia and hypotension)
  * Significant economic impact:
	* Prolonged PACU stays
	* Unanticipated admissions
* **Risk factors**:
  * Timing variations:
	* Often experience nausea/emesis after PACU discharge
	* May coincide with increased oral intake or waning antiemetics
  * High-risk surgeries:
	* Eye procedures
	* Peritoneal or intestinal irritation
	* ENT procedures (especially middle ear manipulation)
	* Dental and cosmetic procedures
  * High-risk patient groups:
	* Previous history of PONV or motion sickness
	* Menstruating females
	* Children over age two
	* Obesity
	* Nonsmokers
  * Perioperative factors:
	* No PO intake (starvation, dehydration)
	* Autonomic imbalance
	* Pain
	* Anesthetic effects on chemotactic center
* **Anesthetic considerations**:
  * Lower incidence with regional vs. general anesthesia
  * Reduced incidence with decreased opioid use
  * Nonopioid analgesics reduce frequency while providing adequate pain control
  * Induction agent differences:
	* Propofol and barbiturates associated with reduced incidence
	* Higher rates with etomidate and ketamine
  * Total intravenous anesthetic (TIVA) with propofol greatly reduces PONV vs. inhalation technique
  * Little significant difference among inhalation agents
	* Sevoflurane and desflurane might produce slightly higher rates
  * Unclear impact of anticholinergic reversal agents
* **Prevention strategies**:
  * Preoperative meclizine 25 mg for motion sickness-prone patients
  * 5-HT3 receptor antagonists (ondansetron) prior to emergence
  * Dexamethasone (effective with other prophylactic agents)
  * Hydration (effective, easy, cost-effective)
  * Droperidol (usage decreased after 2001 FDA warning and 2013 manufacturing issues)
	* FDA clarified in 2008 that black box warning only applies to doses ≥2.5 mg
	* Standard prophylaxis dose of 0.625 mg not addressed in original warning
  * Similar 2011 FDA warning for ondansetron regarding QT prolongation
	* No routine ECG monitoring recommendation
	* Caution with known prolonged QT syndrome patients
* **Rescue therapy**:
  * Use different drug class than prophylactic agent
  * 5-HT3 antagonists useful only after 6 hours from initial dose
  * Options:
	* Promethazine
	* Dimenhydrinate
	* Droperidol
	* Metoclopramide
	* Intramuscular ephedrine
	* Isopropyl alcohol swab aromatherapy
  * Caution with nonselective antihistamines in patients where sedation is problematic (children, OSA)
  * Alternative approaches:
	* Acupuncture
	* Acupressure
	* TENS therapy
	* Limited by provider proficiency, patient acceptance, comparative efficacy
* **Serious causes requiring investigation**:
  * Hypotension
  * Hypoxia
  * Hypoglycemia
  * Increased intracranial pressure
  * Gastric bleeding

#### 1.6.13.1. ASA GUIDELINES FOR PONV PROPHYLAXIS AND TREATMENT

* **Assessment requirements** [ASA:7]:
  * Literature is insufficient regarding whether routine periodic assessment of nausea and vomiting is associated with fewer postoperative complications
  * Expert consultants are equivocal, but ASA members agree that routine assessment and monitoring of nausea and vomiting detects complications and reduces adverse outcomes
  * Both consultants and ASA members agree that routine assessment and monitoring should be done during emergence and recovery
  * **Recommendation**: Periodic assessment of nausea and vomiting should be performed routinely during emergence and recovery [ASA:7]

##### 1.6.13.1.1. PROPHYLACTIC AGENTS - EVIDENCE-BASED EFFICACY

* **Antihistamines** [ASA:8]:
  * One new RCT comparing promethazine with placebo corroborates findings of reduced nausea and vomiting (Category A3-B evidence)
* **5-HT3 antiemetics** [ASA:8]:
  * Meta-analysis of new double-blind RCTs corroborate findings that 5-HT3 antiemetics compared with placebo are effective in prophylaxis of postoperative nausea and vomiting and reduced use of rescue antiemetics (Category A1-B evidence)
  * **Specific agents with proven efficacy**:
	* **Dolasetron**: Reduced vomiting (Category A1-B evidence)
	* **Granisetron**: Reduced vomiting (Category A1-B evidence)
	* **Ondansetron**: Reduced vomiting and rescue antiemetics (Category A1-B evidence)
	* **Tropisetron**: Reduced vomiting and rescue antiemetics (Category A1-B evidence)
	* **Ramosetron**: Effective in prophylaxis of postoperative nausea, vomiting, and use of rescue antiemetics (Category A2-B evidence)
  * **Palonosetron**: New RCTs are equivocal regarding effect (Category A2-E evidence)
* **Tranquilizers/Neuroleptics** [ASA:8]:
  * **Droperidol**: Meta-analysis of new double-blind RCTs corroborate that droperidol effectively reduces postoperative nausea, vomiting, and use of rescue antiemetics (Category A1-B evidence)
  * **Haloperidol**: New double-blind RCTs indicate effectiveness in reduction of postoperative nausea, vomiting, and rescue antiemetic use (Category A2-B evidence)
  * **Dixrazine**: One new RCT indicates effectiveness in prophylaxis of postoperative nausea (Category A3-B evidence), with equivocal findings for vomiting, headache, dizziness, and anxiety (Category A3-E evidence)
* **Metoclopramide** [ASA:8]:
  * Meta-analysis of new double-blind RCTs comparing metoclopramide (10 mg) with placebo controls report no statistically significant differences in nausea and vomiting in immediate postoperative period (Category A1-E evidence)
  * Indicates efficacy in reduction of vomiting during first 24-hour postoperative period (Category A1-B evidence)
* **Scopolamine** [ASA:8]:
  * New double-blind RCTs comparing transdermal scopolamine with placebo patch corroborate findings of reduced nausea and vomiting (Category A3-B evidence)
  * No differences reported in dizziness, drowsiness, fatigue, blurred vision, or dry mouth (Category A3-E evidence)
* **Dexamethasone** [ASA:8]:
  * Meta-analyses of new double-blind RCTs comparing dexamethasone with placebo controls corroborate findings that this antiemetic is effective in prophylaxis of postoperative vomiting and reduced use of rescue antiemetics, and for prophylaxis of nausea when higher doses are administered (Category A1-B evidence)

##### 1.6.13.1.2. COMBINATION THERAPY

* **Multiple prophylactic agents** [ASA:9]:
  * New RCTs comparing two antiemetic drugs with single antiemetic drug controls corroborate findings that antiemetic combinations are effective in prophylaxis of postoperative nausea and vomiting (Category A2-B evidence)
  * No differences in headache, dizziness, drowsiness, anxiety, or akathisia/restlessness reported (Category A2-E evidence)
  * Expert consultants and ASA members are equivocal regarding whether multiple pharmacologic agents should be used for prophylaxis of nausea and vomiting
* **Multiple treatment agents** [ASA:9]:
  * Literature continues to be insufficient to evaluate impact of multiple pharmacologic agents compared with single agents for treatment of nausea and vomiting
  * Expert consultants and ASA members are equivocal regarding whether multiple agents should be used for postoperative treatment of nausea and vomiting

##### 1.6.13.1.3. TREATMENT OF ESTABLISHED PONV

* **Treatment efficacy** [ASA:10]:
  * Ondansetron is effective for treating vomiting during recovery (Category A1-B evidence)
  * Dolasetron and tropisetron were effective (Category A2-B evidence)
  * Expert consultants and ASA members agree that pharmacologic treatment of nausea and vomiting improves patient comfort and satisfaction, reduces time to discharge, and should be done

##### 1.6.13.1.4. ASA RECOMMENDATIONS FOR PONV MANAGEMENT

* **Expert consensus** [ASA:11]:
  * Expert consultants and ASA members agree that pharmacologic prophylaxis of nausea and vomiting improves patient comfort and satisfaction, reduces time to discharge, and should be done selectively
* **Formal recommendations** [ASA:11]:
  * **Antiemetic agents should be used for prevention and treatment of nausea and vomiting when indicated**
  * **Multiple antiemetic agents may be used for prevention or treatment of nausea and vomiting when indicated**

💡 **Clinical integration**: ASA guidelines provide robust evidence for multiple effective prophylactic agents and support multimodal approaches while emphasizing selective rather than universal prophylaxis based on individual risk assessment [ASA:7-11]

**Bottom Line:** Miscellaneous PACU complications require vigilant assessment and targeted management. Ocular, hearing, oral, and nerve injuries must be promptly identified and treated. Temperature management is critical, with hypothermia significantly impacting outcomes. Persistent sedation requires systematic evaluation and appropriate interventions. Emergence reactions, delirium, and PONV require multimodal prevention and management strategies tailored to individual patient risk factors.

## 1.7. ANTAGONISM OF ANESTHETIC AGENTS








### 1.7.2. ASA GUIDELINES FOR ANTAGONISM OF ANESTHETIC AGENTS

#### 1.7.2.1. ANTAGONISM OF BENZODIAZEPINES [ASA:12]

* **Evidence base**:
  * One new RCT corroborates findings regarding efficacy of flumazenil to antagonize (reduced time to emergence) the residual effects of benzodiazepines after general anesthesia (Category A3-B evidence) when compared with placebo
  * Flumazenil reduces time to emergence after sedation (Category A1-B evidence)
  * Equivocal findings for selected complications (nausea, blood pressure variations, agitation/restlessness, dizziness, and resedation/drowsiness) after use of flumazenil after sedation (Category A1-E evidence)
* **Expert opinion**:
  * Expert consultants and ASA members disagree that routine use of flumazenil reduces adverse outcomes or improves patient comfort and satisfaction
* **ASA Recommendations**:
  * **Specific antagonists should be available whenever benzodiazepines are administered**
  * **Flumazenil should not be used routinely, but may be administered to antagonize respiratory depression and sedation in selected patients**
  * **After pharmacologic antagonism, patients should be observed long enough to ensure that cardiorespiratory depression does not recur**

#### 1.7.2.2. ANTAGONISM OF OPIOIDS [ASA:12]

* **Evidence base**:
  * Naloxone reduces time to emergence and recovery of spontaneous respiration after general anesthesia (Category A3-B evidence)
* **Expert opinion**:
  * Expert consultants and ASA members disagree that routine use of naloxone reduces adverse outcomes or improves patient comfort and satisfaction
* **ASA Recommendations**:
  * **Specific antagonists should be available whenever opioids are administered**
  * **Opioid antagonists (e.g., naloxone) should not be used routinely but may be administered to antagonize respiratory depression in selected patients**
  * **After pharmacologic antagonism, patients should be observed long enough to ensure that cardiorespiratory depression does not recur**
  * **Task Force reminder**: Acute antagonism of the effects of opioids may result in pain, hypertension, tachycardia, or pulmonary edema

#### 1.7.2.3. REVERSAL OF NEUROMUSCULAR BLOCKADE [ASA:12]

* **Evidence base**:
  * One new RCT corroborates findings regarding efficacy of edrophonium to antagonize effects of neuromuscular blocking agents (e.g., rocuronium, cisatracurium, rapacuronium) when compared with spontaneous recovery (Category A3-B evidence)
  * Neostigmine is effective for antagonism of residual neuromuscular blockade (Category A1-B evidence)
  * Increased frequency of postoperative emetic episodes with use of neostigmine (Category A1-H evidence)
* **Expert opinion**:
  * Expert consultants and ASA members are equivocal regarding whether anesthetic regimens designed to avoid need for antagonism of neuromuscular blockade reduce adverse outcomes or improve patient comfort and satisfaction
* **ASA Recommendation**:
  * **Specific antagonists should be administered for reversal of residual neuromuscular blockade when indicated**

### 1.7.3. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS OF ASA ANTAGONIST GUIDELINES

#### 1.7.3.1. FLUMAZENIL USE CONSIDERATIONS

* **Indications for selective use**:
  * Significant respiratory depression from benzodiazepines
  * Prolonged sedation affecting PACU discharge
  * Need to differentiate benzodiazepine effects from other causes of altered mental status
* **Contraindications and cautions**:
  * Risk of seizures in chronic benzodiazepine users
  * Short duration of action may require repeated dosing
  * Not recommended for routine reversal

#### 1.7.3.2. NALOXONE USE CONSIDERATIONS

* **Indications for selective use**:
  * Significant respiratory depression from opioids (respiratory rate <8-10/min)
  * Severe sedation with inadequate respiratory drive
  * Suspected opioid overdose
* **Dosing strategy**:
  * Small incremental doses (0.04-0.1 mg IV) to avoid precipitating withdrawal or severe pain
  * Titrate to adequate respiratory function rather than complete reversal
* **Complications to monitor**:
  * Acute pain exacerbation
  * Hypertension and tachycardia
  * Potential pulmonary edema in susceptible patients

#### 1.7.3.3. NEUROMUSCULAR REVERSAL STRATEGY

* **Evidence-based approach**:
  * Routine reversal recommended for all patients receiving nondepolarizing neuromuscular blocking agents
  * Train-of-four monitoring helps guide reversal decisions
  * Consider newer agents like sugammadex when available
* **Monitoring considerations**:
  * Observe for adequate recovery of neuromuscular function
  * Assess airway protective reflexes
  * Monitor for postoperative nausea associated with neostigmine use

💡 **Clinical integration**: ASA guidelines emphasize selective rather than routine use of antagonists, availability of reversal agents whenever indicated drugs are used, and adequate monitoring after reversal to prevent recurarization or re-sedation [ASA:12]

**Bottom Line:** Antagonist use should be selective and evidence-based rather than routine. All antagonists should be immediately available when their corresponding agents are used. Careful monitoring after reversal is essential to prevent recurrent depression, and clinicians must be aware of potential complications from acute antagonism.
