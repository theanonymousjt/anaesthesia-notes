# 1. DRUG PASSAGE ACROSS CELL MEMBRANE

## 1.1. MEMBRANE STRUCTURE

**Cell membranes consist of a phospholipid bilayer containing embedded proteins that regulate the movement of substances in and out of cells.**

* **General Structure:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Phospholipid bilayer approximately 10 nm thick
  * Hydrophilic heads on outside, lipophilic chains facing inward
  * Creates "sandwich effect" with hydrophilic layers surrounding central hydrophobic layer
  * Membrane proteins span or attach to the bilayer
  * Acts as "fluid mosaic" - phosphoglycerides and glycoproteins not fixed in position
* **Embedded Proteins Include:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Ion channels
  * Receptors
  * Intermediate messengers (G-proteins)
  * Enzymes
* **Specialized Membrane Areas:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Neuromuscular junction - fixed array of postsynaptic receptors opposite motor nerve ending
  * Blood-brain barrier - tight junctions between endothelial cells limiting passage of polar molecules
  * Intestinal mucosa and renal tubules - tight junctions preventing lateral movement of glycoproteins
* **Membrane Modifications:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Capillary Endothelial Cells:** Contain fenestrae (regions where outer and inner membranes fuse) allowing rapid fluid passage
  * **Renal Glomerular Endothelium:** Gaps/clefts between cells permit passage of larger molecules during filtration
  * **Tight Junctions:** Help maintain specialized glycoproteins at their site of action

**Bottom Line:** The phospholipid bilayer structure of cell membranes creates a selective barrier that regulates drug movement. Specialized membrane structures and embedded proteins modify drug transport in different tissues, affecting drug distribution and action.

## 1.2. METHODS OF CROSSING THE CELL MEMBRANE

### 1.2.1. PASSIVE DIFFUSION

* **Definition:** Movement of molecules down concentration gradient without energy expenditure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Key Features:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Most common method for drug crossing cell membrane
  * Rate depends on concentration gradient and lipid solubility
  * Weak acids/bases exist in ionized or unionized forms depending on pH
  * Only unionized form is lipid-soluble and diffuses easily
* **Specialized Ion Channels:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Allow intermittent passive movement of selected ions
  * When open, permit rapid ion flux for milliseconds
  * Suitable for propagating action potentials in nerves and muscles
  * Example: ACh receptor (pentameric structure forming ion channel)
  * Selectivity for ion types varies (e.g., ACh receptor selective for small cations)
* **Pharmacological Modification:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Drugs may alter channel permeability
  * Local anesthetics bind to internal surface of Na+ channels preventing activation
  * Non-depolarizing muscle relaxants inhibit ACh binding to receptor sites

**Bottom Line:** Passive diffusion is the primary mechanism for drugs crossing cell membranes, with rate determined by concentration gradient and lipid solubility. Specialized ion channels allow rapid, selective ion movement that can be pharmacologically modified.

### 1.2.2. FACILITATED DIFFUSION

* **Definition:** Process where molecules combine with membrane-bound carrier proteins to cross membrane [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Key Features:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Still follows concentration gradient but faster than simple diffusion
  * No energy required
  * Specific for particular molecules
  * Important for polar molecules that diffuse poorly alone
* **Transport Proteins:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Solute Carrier (SLC) Family 2:** Responsible for facilitated glucose diffusion
  * **SLC Family 6:** Transport of neurotransmitters
	* SLC6A3: Dopamine
	* SLC6A4: Serotonin (target for SSRIs)
	* SLC6A5: Noradrenaline

**Bottom Line:** Facilitated diffusion accelerates transport of specific molecules down concentration gradients via carrier proteins, allowing polar compounds like glucose and neurotransmitters to cross membranes more efficiently.

### 1.2.3. ACTIVE TRANSPORT

* **Definition:** Energy-requiring process moving molecules against concentration gradient [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Energy Supply Types:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Primary Active Transport:** Energy supplied directly to ion pump
  * **Secondary Active Transport:** Energy from coupling to ionic gradient maintained by primary transport
* **Common Locations:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Gut mucosa
  * Liver
  * Renal tubules
  * Blood-brain barrier
* **Transport Examples:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Na+/K+ ATPase (Primary):** Uses ATP hydrolysis energy to transport Na+ out and K+ into cell
	* Example of an antiport (substances moved in opposite directions)
  * **Na+/Amino Acid Symport (Secondary):** Amino acids cross membrane when Na+ bound to carrier moves down concentration gradient
	* Example of a symport (substances moved in same direction)
* **ABC Transport Proteins:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * ATP-binding cassette family responsible for transporting nutrients in and toxins out
  * **P-glycoprotein (PGP):** Found in gut mucosa and blood-brain barrier
	* Many drugs are substrates for PGP and cannot penetrate blood-brain barrier
	* Example: Dabigatran (PGP substrate)
	  * PGP inhibitors (amiodarone, verapamil) increase dabigatran bioavailability
	  * PGP inducers (rifampicin) reduce dabigatran bioavailability
* **Clinical Significance:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Drug interactions often occur through PGP inhibition/induction
  * PGP inhibitors/inducers commonly also affect CYP3A4
  * Drugs that are substrates for both PGP and CYP3A4 have strong interaction potential

**Bottom Line:** Active transport moves drugs against concentration gradients using energy, allowing cells to concentrate or exclude specific molecules. Transport proteins like PGP significantly affect drug distribution, particularly in the gut and blood-brain barrier, and are important sites of drug interactions.

### 1.2.4. PINOCYTOSIS
* **Definition:** Process where cell membrane invaginates around target molecule, moving it into cell [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Key Features:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Used primarily for large molecules unable to cross membrane by other mechanisms
  * Molecule may be released into cell or remain in created vacuole
  * Vacuole may undergo reverse process on opposite side of cell

**Bottom Line:** Pinocytosis allows large molecular weight drugs to enter cells by engulfment in membrane vacuoles, though this is a less common route for most therapeutic agents.

## 1.3. FACTORS INFLUENCING RATE OF DIFFUSION

### 1.3.1. MOLECULAR SIZE
* **Physical Principle:** Rate of passive diffusion inversely proportional to square root of molecular size (Graham's law) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Clinical Application:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Small molecules diffuse more readily than large ones
  * Anesthetic agents have relatively small molecular weights enabling rapid diffusion through lipid membranes

**Bottom Line:** Smaller drug molecules generally penetrate cell membranes more quickly, which is advantageous for anesthetics where rapid onset is often desired.

### 1.3.2. CONCENTRATION GRADIENT
* **Physical Principle:** Fick's law states rate of transfer across membrane is proportional to concentration gradient [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Clinical Applications:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Increasing plasma concentration of unbound drug accelerates transfer across membranes
  * Explains Bowman's principle for neuromuscular blockers:
	* Less potent drugs require higher doses → greater concentration gradient
	* Faster onset of action due to steeper gradient

**Bottom Line:** Concentration gradient is a primary driver of diffusion rate. Less potent drugs often have faster onset because their higher doses create steeper concentration gradients between plasma and effect site.

### 1.3.3. IONIZATION
* **Basic Principle:** Only uncharged (unionized) drug fraction crosses lipid cell membrane [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation:** Determines degree of ionization based on drug pKa and solution pH [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * pH = pKa + log([proton acceptor]/[proton donor])
  * For acids: pH = pKa + log([X-]/[XH])
  * For bases: pH = pKa + log([X]/[XH+])
* **Ionization Patterns:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Weak Acids:** More unionized at pH below pKa; more ionized at pH above pKa
  * **Weak Bases:** More ionized at pH below pKa; more unionized at pH above pKa
* **Clinical Examples:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Bupivacaine:** Weak base with pKa 8.1, 83% ionized at physiological pH
  * **Aspirin:** Acid with pKa 3.0, almost wholly ionized at physiological pH but unionized in stomach

**Bottom Line:** Drug ionization, determined by pKa and environmental pH, significantly affects membrane penetration. This principle explains why acidic drugs absorb well in the stomach and why blood pH changes alter drug distribution.

### 1.3.4. LIPID SOLUBILITY
* **Basic Principle:** Lipid solubility reflects ability to pass through cell membrane (independent of pKa) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Complex Interactions:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * High lipid solubility alone doesn't guarantee rapid onset
  * Example: Alfentanil vs. Fentanyl
	* Alfentanil less lipid-soluble but faster onset due to:
	  * Lower potency → greater concentration gradient
	  * Lower pKa (6.5 vs. 8.4) → greater unionized fraction at physiological pH
* **Clinical Applications:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Transdermal Administration:** High lipid solubility (fentanyl) enables effective transfer across skin
  * **Intrathecal Administration:** More lipid-soluble drugs (diamorphine) fix to local lipid tissues quickly, while less lipid-soluble drugs (morphine) remain in CSF longer with increased risk of cranial spread

**Bottom Line:** Lipid solubility affects membrane penetration rate, but must be considered alongside other factors like concentration gradient and ionization state. These principles guide route selection and explain differences in drug behavior within the body.

### 1.3.5. PROTEIN BINDING
* **Basic Principle:** Only unbound drug fraction crosses cell membrane [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Clinical Significance:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Most important for highly protein-bound drugs (>90%)
  * Small binding changes produce large effects on unbound drug concentration
  * Generally leads to increased metabolism rate with minimal clinical impact
  * Exception: Drugs with nearly saturated metabolic pathways (e.g., phenytoin)
* **Binding Proteins:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Albumin:** Binds neutral/acidic drugs (e.g., barbiturates)
	* Two important binding sites: warfarin and diazepam sites
  * **α1-Acid Glycoprotein:** Binds basic drugs (e.g., morphine)
  * **Other Globulins:** Bind specific substances (β1 globulin binds iron, α2 globulin binds copper)
* **Pathological Effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Inflammation alters protein proportions
  * Albumin decreases in acute infective/inflammatory processes
  * Severe hypoalbuminemia increases unbound drug fraction → exaggerated pharmacological effects

**Bottom Line:** Protein binding restricts drug availability for membrane transport. While changes in binding usually have minimal clinical impact due to compensatory metabolism, severe protein depletion or drugs with narrow therapeutic windows can produce significant clinical effects that require dose adjustment.

# 2. PHARMACOKINETICS

### 2.1.1. BASIC CONCEPTS
**Pharmacokinetics studies how drugs move through the body by measuring drug concentrations in plasma and at effect sites.**

* **Definition:** The study of drug movement throughout the body and the mathematical description of this process [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Primary Focus:** Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion (ADME) of drugs and their metabolites [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Clinical Relevance:** Understanding PK helps predict:
  * Drug onset time [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Peak effect timing [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Duration of action [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Appropriate dosing regimens [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Effects of organ dysfunction on drug disposition [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Pharmacokinetics provides the scientific foundation for predicting drug behavior in the body, enabling rational drug selection and dosing in clinical practice.

## 2.2. DRUG ABSORPTION AND ROUTES OF ADMINISTRATION

#### 2.2.1.1. DRUG TRANSPORT ACROSS MEMBRANES
* **Membrane Structure:** Lipophilic core between two hydrophilic layers [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Transport Mechanisms:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Lipophilic drugs:** Passive diffusion across membrane
  * **Water-soluble drugs:** Require hydrophilic protein channels
  * 💡 **Important Principle:** Blood-to-organ transport often limited by blood flow, not lipid solubility [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

#### 2.2.1.2. BLOOD-BRAIN BARRIER (BBB)
* **Structure:** Limited hydrophilic channels [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Drug Entry Methods:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Hydrophilic drugs: Require specific protein transporters
  * Transport mechanisms: Active transport or facilitated diffusion
  * Lipophilic drugs: Cross freely, may also use transporters
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl:** BBB explains why some drugs (like antibiotics) have poor CNS penetration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

#### 2.2.1.3. BIOAVAILABILITY
* **Definition:** Percentage of drug reaching systemic circulation unchanged [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Clinical Significance:** Determines effective dose required for different administration routes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Measurement:** Area under concentration-time curve ratio (AUCoral/AUCiv) × 100 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

#### 2.2.1.4. ADMINISTRATION ROUTES
##### 2.2.1.4.1. INTRAVENOUS (IV) ADMINISTRATION
* **Bioavailability:** 100%, immediate delivery [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Risks:** Plasma concentration overshoot → side effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Therapeutic Index:** Ratio of toxic concentration (50% population) to therapeutic concentration (50% population) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Bypass Effect:** Circumvents absorption barriers [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* ⚠️ **Important Exception:** Pulmonary endothelium may delay systemic circulation of: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **FENTANYL** (highly distributed) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **PROPOFOL** (metabolized) [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

##### 2.2.1.4.2. ORAL ADMINISTRATION
* **Anesthesia Use:** Limited due to variable bioavailability [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Variable Absorption Factors:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Gastric emptying timing (affected by pH, enzymes, motility, food, other drugs)
  * Drug ionization state:
	* Acidic stomach → better absorption of acidic drugs
	* Alkaline intestine → better absorption of basic drugs
	* Most absorption occurs in small intestine (larger surface area)
  * First-pass metabolism reduces bioavailability [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

##### 2.2.1.4.3. ALTERNATIVE ROUTES
* **Sublingual/Buccal:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Best for: Lipophilic drugs
  * Advantage: Bypasses first-pass metabolism
  * Result: Direct entry to systemic circulation
  * Examples: Glyceryl trinitrate spray for angina, sublingual nifedipine for hypertension
* **Rectal:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Advantage: Partial first-pass bypass
  * Common use: Alternative for pediatrics
  * Limitations: Erratic absorption and irritation
  * Applications: Local effects (steroids for inflammatory bowel disease) or systemic effects (diclofenac suppositories)

##### 2.2.1.4.4. INJECTABLE ROUTES
* **Intramuscular (IM):** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Properties: Rapid, reliable absorption (high blood flow)
  * Best for: Aqueous drugs like **NEUROMUSCULAR BLOCKERS**
  * Bioavailability: Approaches 1 (100%)
  * Preferred sites: Well-perfused muscles (deltoid, quadriceps, gluteus)
  * ⚠️ **Warning:** Avoid in hypoperfusion states - may result in delayed absorption followed by sudden toxic levels when perfusion restored
* **Subcutaneous (SubQ):** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Properties: Variable absorption (blood flow dependent)
  * Examples: **HEPARIN**, **INSULIN**
  * Uses: Drugs well-absorbed from subcutaneous tissues
  * Special applications: Depot preparations (anti-psychotics, contraceptives)
  * Co-formulations: Insulin with zinc/protamine creates slow absorption profile

##### 2.2.1.4.5. TRANSCUTANEOUS
* **Limitations:** Only small, lipid-soluble drugs [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Examples:** **CLONIDINE**, **NITROGLYCERIN**, **SCOPOLAMINE**, **FENTANYL**, **EMLA** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Clinical Application:** Drug patches better for maintenance therapy due to slow onset [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Factors Favoring Absorption:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * High lipid solubility
  * Good regional blood supply (thorax/abdomen preferred over limbs)

##### 2.2.1.4.6. NEURAL ROUTES
* **Intrathecal:** Direct delivery, bypasses absorption barriers [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Epidural/Perineural:** Requires absorption through tissue barriers [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* 🔑 **HY:** Neural routes enable targeted drug delivery with minimal systemic effects [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

##### 2.2.1.4.7. INHALATIONAL
* **Advantages:** Large surface area, high blood flow [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Research Status:** Ongoing for new applications [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Local vs. Systemic Action:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Local: Bronchodilators (may have unwanted systemic effects if absorbed)
  * Systemic: Volatile anesthetics (rapid increase in concentration due to large surface area)
  * Specialized: Inhaled nitric oxide (absorbed but short half-life prevents systemic effects)

**Bottom Line:** Route of administration significantly impacts onset time, bioavailability, and systemic effects. IV administration provides 100% bioavailability but risks higher peak concentrations, while other routes offer specific advantages for particular clinical scenarios.

## 2.3. DISTRIBUTION

#### 2.3.1.1. TIME COURSE OF DISTRIBUTION
* **Complexity:** Best described using computer models and simulation [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Key Principle:** Cardiac output distribution determines speed of drug exposure to organs [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

#### 2.3.1.2. DISTRIBUTION SEQUENCE
* **1st Phase:** Highly perfused core organs (brain, lungs, heart, kidneys) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Receive highest relative distribution of cardiac output (75%)
  * First to reach equilibrium with plasma concentrations
* **2nd Phase:** Less well-perfused muscles (19% of cardiac output) and liver [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Final Phase:** Poorly perfused splanchnic vasculature, adipose tissue (6% of cardiac output), bone (0% of cardiac output) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

#### 2.3.1.3. DETERMINANTS OF DISTRIBUTION
* **Initial Delivery:** Dependent on relative blood flow to the organ [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Rate of Equilibration:** Dependent on ratio of blood flow to tissue content [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

#### 2.3.1.4. INITIAL BOLUS DYNAMICS
* **Central Compartment Mixing:** First few minutes after IV bolus, drug mixes within: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Venous blood volume of the arm
  * Great vessels volume
  * Heart
  * Lung
  * Upper aorta
  * First-pass lung uptake
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Many of these volumes are fixed but the lungs are variable [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

#### 2.3.1.5. DISTRIBUTION DETERMINANTS
* **Key Factors:** Blood flow:tissue content ratio and tissue solubility (lipophilicity) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Result:** Drug diffusion into peripheral compartments following pattern: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Vessel-rich group (VRG) > muscle > fat > vessel-poor group (VPG)
* **Effect:** Rapid drop in plasma concentration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

#### 2.3.1.6. FIRST-PASS UPTAKE
* **Mechanism:** Highly fat-soluble drugs may be avidly taken up during first passage through lungs [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Result:** Reduced arterial blood concentration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Effect:** Apparent increase in central compartment size [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **High First-Pass Drugs** (>65% of initial dose): [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **LIDOCAINE**
  * **PROPOFOL**
  * **MEPERIDINE**
  * **FENTANYL**
  * **SUFENTANIL**
  * **ALFENTANIL**

#### 2.3.1.7. TISSUE DISTRIBUTION PATTERNS
* **VRG Organs** (brain, heart, kidneys, liver): [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Receive disproportionate amount of drug in first minutes
* **Fat Distribution** (for highly lipid-soluble drugs): [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Capacity to hold drug greatly exceeds capacity of highly perfused tissues
  * Redistribution from VRG to fat accounts for substantial offset of drug effect after bolus of IV anesthetics/fat-soluble opioids (e.g., **FENTANYL**)
* **Metabolism Effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * *Rapid Metabolism Drugs*: Contribute significantly to rapid initial concentration drop
	* **PROPOFOL** (rapid hepatic clearance)
	* **REMIFENTANIL** (metabolized in blood)

#### 2.3.1.8. EQUILIBRATION TIMING
* **Critical Point:** For each tissue, there is a point when apparent tissue concentration equals plasma concentration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Full Mixing Time:** May take hours/days for drug to fully mix with all bodily tissues (some tissues have very low perfusion) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

#### 2.3.1.9. PLASMA CONCENTRATION PHASES
* **Initial Rapid Drop:** Due to drug uptake by well-perfused tissues [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Redistribution Phase:** Slower decrease as drug continues being taken up by less perfused tissues [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
  * When VRG (brain) concentration > plasma concentration, drug returns to plasma and redistributes to other tissues
  * Process continues for each organ bed; eventually adipose tissue contains majority of lipophilic drug not eliminated
* **Terminal Phase:** Even slower, log-linear decrease until drug is completely eliminated [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

#### 2.3.1.10. DISTRIBUTION VS. REDISTRIBUTION EFFECTS
* **Distribution Effect:** Contributes to rapid emergence by removing drug from plasma for many minutes after bolus [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Redistribution Effect:** Delays emergence after prolonged infusions as drug returns from tissue reservoirs to plasma for many hours [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* 🔑 **HY:** Distribution explains why many anesthetic drugs have short clinical duration after single dose despite long elimination half-life [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

#### 2.3.1.11. DISTRIBUTION TO SPECIAL POPULATIONS
* **Blood-Brain Barrier:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Functional barrier between circulation and CNS
  * Active transport and facilitated diffusion predominate
  * Only lipid-soluble, low molecular weight drugs cross by simple diffusion
  * Explains why glycopyrrolate (quaternary charged nitrogen) has minimal central effects vs. atropine (tertiary amine)
  * Contains protective enzymes (monoamine oxidase) and ABC transport proteins
  * Physical disruption may release neurotransmitters explaining circulatory disturbance in head injury
  * Inflammation increases permeability (explains increased penicillin penetration in meningitis)
* **Fetal Distribution:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Placental membrane separates maternal and fetal circulation
  * Crosses more readily by lipid-soluble than polar molecules
  * Rate determined by placental blood flow and concentration gradient
  * Fetal pH typically lower (may affect drug ionization)
  * Fetal protein binding affects drug transfer
  * Safety considerations:
	* Avoid drugs during pregnancy when possible
	* Potential teratogenic effects may not be apparent for years
	* Risk-benefit assessment needed (e.g., epilepsy treatment, anticoagulation)
  * Specific drugs at birth:
	* Bupivacaine: Crosses less readily than lidocaine (higher pKa, more ionized)
	* ⚠️ Ion trapping may occur with fetal acidosis
	* Pethidine: Lipid-soluble, significant placental crossing
	* Thiopental: Rapid placental crossing (detectable in umbilical vein within 30 seconds)
	* Muscle relaxants: Generally minimal placental transfer due to polarity

**Bottom Line:** Drug distribution follows a predictable sequence from highly perfused organs to less perfused tissues, with initial clinical effect termination often due to redistribution rather than elimination. Understanding this principle explains why single doses of lipophilic drugs have brief effects despite long elimination half-lives.

### 2.3.2. VOLUME OF DISTRIBUTION

**The apparent volume into which a drug has distributed to account for observed plasma concentration.**

* **Basic Concept:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Theoretical volume relating dose administered to plasma concentration
  * Calculated as: Vd = Dose/Concentration
* **Multi-Compartment Model:** Most drugs more precisely described using 3 compartments: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Central compartment:** Blood and ultra-rapidly equilibrating tissues (lungs)
  * **Rapidly equilibrating compartment:** Organs and muscles
  * **Slowly equilibrating compartment:** Fat and skin
* **Volume of Distribution at Steady State (Vdss):** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * *Definition:* Algebraic sum of compartment volumes
  * **Small Vdss Drugs:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
	* *Properties:* High aqueous solubility, remain largely within IV space
	* *Example:* **VECURONIUM** (200 mL/kg in adult men, 160 mL/kg in adult women)
	* *Distribution:* Mostly in body water with little distribution into fat
  * **Large Vdss Drugs** (exceeds total body water - 600 mL/kg in adult males): [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
	* *Properties:* Mostly lipophilic (applies to most anesthesia drugs)
	* *Examples:*
	  * **FENTANYL**: 350 L in adults
	  * **PROPOFOL**: 5000 L
* **Clinical Implications:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * *Recovery Prediction:* Complex distribution process makes half-lives poor predictors for emergence times
  * *Better Predictors:* Computer models using context-sensitive half-time or decrement time
  * 💡 **Clinical Pearl:** The offset of drug effect depends more on redistribution than elimination for most anesthetic drugs

**Bottom Line:** Volume of distribution is a theoretical construct that relates dose to plasma concentration, varies widely among drugs, and helps explain why highly lipid-soluble anesthetics distribute extensively beyond plasma and have complex recovery profiles.

### 2.3.3. PROTEIN BINDING

**Drugs in blood exist either free or bound to plasma proteins and lipids, with only free drug able to cross membranes.**

* **Binding Mechanisms:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Most acidic drugs bind to albumin
  * Basic drugs bind to alpha1-acid glycoprotein (AAG)
  * Competition: Drugs may compete for binding sites
	* Example: Sulfonamides may displace unconjugated bilirubin from albumin → increased risk of bilirubin encephalopathy in neonates
* **Effect on Drug Distribution:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * *Pharmacological Principle:* Only free/unbound fraction can cross cell membranes
  * *Receptor Effect:* Free fraction determines concentration at receptor
  * *Distribution Dynamics:*
	* Free concentration equilibrates between organs and tissues
	* Free/bound equilibration in blood is instantaneous
	* As unbound molecules diffuse into tissue, they're instantly replaced by previously bound molecules
* **Binding Patterns:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * *Lipid Solubility Correlation:* Extent of protein binding parallels lipid solubility
  * *Hydrophobic Drugs:* More likely to bind to proteins in plasma and lipids in fat
  * *Molecule Movement:*
	* Lipophilic molecules: Readily transfer between blood and organs
	* Charged molecules: Pass in small quantities into most organs except CNS
	* Blood-brain barrier: Limits permeation by ionized drugs due to pericapillary glial cells and endothelial cell tight junctions
* **Factors Affecting Plasma Protein Concentration:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * *Clinical Significance:* Most important for highly protein-bound drugs (>90%)
  * **Decreased Albumin Production:**
	* Kidney disease
	* Liver disease
	* Chronic congestive heart failure
	* Malignancies
  * **Increased Albumin Loss:**
	* Major burns (>20% body surface area)
  * **Increased AAG Concentration:**
	* Trauma (including surgery)
	* Infection
	* Myocardial infarction
	* Chronic pain
  * **Reduced AAG Concentration:**
	* Pregnancy
  * ⚠️ **Exception:** **PROPOFOL** (administered with its own binding molecules in lipid emulsion) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Clinical Effects of Altered Protein Binding:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * *Distribution Change:* If plasma proteins diminish → relative solubility in blood decreases → increasing tissue uptake
  * *Free Fraction Change:* Inverse proportion with protein concentration change
	* Example: If free fraction is 2% normally, with 50% decrease in plasma protein, free fraction becomes 4% (100% increase)
  * *Pharmacological Effect:* Theoretically increased, but actual effect may be minimal
	* Reason: Unbound fraction equilibrates throughout body including receptor sites
	* Free drug concentration driving receptor effects may change very little

**Bottom Line:** Protein binding impacts drug distribution by determining the free fraction available to cross membranes and interact with receptors. While altered protein binding can change the free drug fraction, the clinical significance is limited for most drugs due to rapid re-equilibration throughout the body.

## 2.4. DRUG ELIMINATION

**All processes removing drug from body, including distribution, metabolism, and excretion.**

* **Basic Concepts:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * *Definition:* All processes removing drug from body
  * *Elimination Clearance:* Theoretical volume of blood from which drug is completely/irreversibly removed in unit time
  * *Measurement:* Calculated with PK models of blood concentration versus time data

**Bottom Line:** Drug elimination encompasses all processes that remove drug from the body, with clearance quantifying the rate of this removal as a volume of blood completely cleared of drug per unit time.

## 2.5. DRUG METABOLISM

**Chemical transformation of drugs, primarily in the liver, to facilitate elimination.**

#### 2.5.1.1. VARIABILITY FACTORS
* **Genetic Variations:** Genes controlling biotransformation enzymes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Most drugs: Rate of metabolism is unimodal
  * Polymorphisms: Rate of metabolism is multimodal (subpopulations with different rates)
* **Chronologic Variations:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Fetal/neonatal periods: Subnormal CYP enzyme levels
  * Age 1 year: Normal levels

#### 2.5.1.2. BIOTRANSFORMATION PATHWAYS
* **Hydrophilic Drugs:** Likely excreted unchanged in urine/stool [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Lipophilic Drugs:** Require modification into more hydrophilic, excretable compounds through Phase I and/or II reactions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

#### 2.5.1.3. PHASE I REACTIONS
* **Function:** Transform drugs into more polar compounds [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Mechanisms:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Hydrolysis:** Insertion of water molecule → forms unstable intermediate that splits apart
	* Examples: Amides (**LIDOCAINE**), esters (**SUCCINYLCHOLINE**)
  * **Oxidation:** Remove electrons from molecule
	* Enzymatically mediated, inserting hydroxyl group (OH)
	* Includes: Dealkylation, deamination, oxidation of nitrogen-containing groups, desulfuration, dehalogenation, dehydrogenation
  * **Reduction:** Add electrons to molecule
* **Key Enzymes:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **CYP450:** Large family of membrane-bound proteins containing heme cofactor
  * **CYP3A4:** Most important enzyme (40-45% of all CYP-mediated drug metabolism)
  * **Enzyme Locations:**
	* High concentrations: Set of hepatocytes, membranes of upper intestinal enterocytes
	* Lower concentrations: Lungs, kidneys, skin
  * **Non-CYP450 Enzymes:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
	* Monoamine oxidase: Metabolizes catecholamines
	* Alcohol dehydrogenase: Metabolizes ethanol to acetaldehyde
	* Esterases: Found in cytoplasm of liver, muscle, metabolize esters
	* Angiotensin-converting enzyme: Found in lung, breaks down bradykinin

#### 2.5.1.4. CYP450 SYSTEM PROPERTIES
* **Protective Features:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Broad substrate specificity
  * Ability to adapt to different substances through isoenzyme induction
* **Interaction Mechanism:** Biotransformations inhibited if different substrates compete for drug-binding site on same CYP member [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Effect depends on relative affinities for the enzyme

#### 2.5.1.5. PHASE II REACTIONS
* **Function:** Conjugate endogenous compounds at new polar functional group to increase hydrophilicity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Requirement:** Most drugs need polar chemical group (from Phase I) first [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Exception: Some drugs (e.g., **MORPHINE**) already have polar group and enter Phase II directly
* **Key Enzymes:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Glucuronosyltransferases
  * Glutathione-S-transferases
  * N-acetyl-transferases
  * Sulfotransferases
* **Clinical Importance:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Phase II reactions generally less affected in liver failure than Phase I
  * Drugs with predominantly Phase II metabolism (e.g., lorazepam) less affected by liver disease

#### 2.5.1.6. METABOLISM SITES
* Hepatic microsomal enzymes in hepatic Set [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Plasma (Hofmann elimination, ester hydrolysis) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Lungs, kidneys, GIT, placenta (tissue esterases) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* 🔑 **HY:** Extrahepatic metabolism is significant for many anesthetic drugs [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Drug metabolism involves chemical transformation processes (primarily Phase I and II reactions) that convert lipophilic drugs to more water-soluble compounds for excretion. The CYP450 enzyme system plays a crucial role, with significant individual variability based on genetics, age, and enzyme induction/inhibition status.

### 2.5.2. HEPATIC DRUG CLEARANCE

**Hepatic clearance is determined by blood flow and extraction capability, with drugs categorized by extraction ratio.**

* **Basic Mechanism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * *Determinants:* Function of hepatic blood flow and ability of liver to extract drug from blood
  * *Extraction Ability:* Depends on activity of drug-metabolizing enzymes and capacity for hepatobiliary excretion (intrinsic clearance - Cli)
	  * Michaelis constant = concentration of substrate at which the enzyme works at 50% of its maximum rate (high Michaelis constant = high metabolic capacity)
* **Key Equations:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Extraction Ratio (ER)** = (Ca-Cv)/Ca
	* Ca: Mixed hepatic arterial-portal venous drug concentration
	* Cv: Mixed hepatic venous drug concentration
  * **Total Clearance (ClH)** = Q × ER
	* Q: Liver blood flow
  * **Relationship of total hepatic drug clearance to ER and Cli**: ClH = Q × (Cli/(Q+Cli))
* **Clearance Principles:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **High Intrinsic Clearance Drugs**: Total hepatic clearance approaches hepatic blood flow
	* When Cli >> Q
	* Minimal effect from doubling intrinsic clearance
	* Hepatic elimination clearance directly proportional to hepatic blood flow
  * **Low Intrinsic Clearance Drugs**: Hepatic clearance nearly equivalent to intrinsic clearance
	* When Cli is very small (Q + Cli ≈ Q)
	* Nearly proportional increase in extraction ratio when doubling intrinsic clearance

  * **High ER Drugs**: Decreased CO → decreased hepatic drug clearance
  * **Low ER Drugs**: Minimal change in clearance with CO changes

* **Extraction Ratio Classification:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Group 1 - High Extraction:**
	* Rapid hepatocyte uptake and high metabolic capacity
	* Examples: Propofol, lidocaine
	* Free drug rapidly removed from plasma
	* Bound drug released to maintain equilibrium
	* Metabolism rate independent of protein binding but highly dependent on liver blood flow
  * **Group 2 - Low Extraction, High Protein Binding:**
	* Low metabolic capacity, high protein binding (>90%)
	* Examples: Phenytoin, diazepam
	* Limited by metabolic capacity, not blood flow
	* Changes in protein binding may significantly affect free drug levels if metabolic capacity saturated
	* Drugs with narrow therapeutic index require careful monitoring
  * **Group 3 - Low Extraction, Low Protein Binding:**
	* Metabolism affected by neither hepatic blood flow nor protein binding changes

### 2.5.3. RENAL DRUG CLEARANCE

**The kidneys eliminate unchanged hydrophilic drugs and metabolites through filtration, secretion, and reabsorption processes.**

* **Primary Functions:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Excrete unchanged hydrophilic drugs
  * Excrete hepatically derived metabolites (after Phase I and II)
* **Clearance Mechanisms:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Passive Glomerular Filtration:**
	* Inefficient alone (protein binding decreases drug clearance below GFR)
	* Enhanced by proximal renal tubular cell active transporters
	  * Properties: Saturable but allow rate of renal drug clearance to approach renal blood flow
	* Small, non-protein-bound, water-soluble drugs filtered into glomerular ultrafiltrate
	* Only free drug in filtered fraction removed; no change in extent of protein binding
  * **Active Secretion at Proximal Tubules:**
	* Energy-requiring processes against concentration gradients
	* Separate carrier systems for acidic and basic drugs (capacity-limited)
	* Can be inhibited (e.g., probenecid blocks penicillin secretion)
  * **Inhibitory Factors:**
	* Passive tubular reabsorption of lipophilic drugs
	* Active, carrier-mediated tubular reabsorption of hydrophilic drugs (preference for nonionized form)
  * **Diffusion at Distal Tubules:**
	* Passive diffusion down concentration gradient
	* pH-dependent excretion:
	  * Acidic drugs: Better excreted in alkaline urine (ionized form trapped)
	  * Basic drugs: Better excreted in acidic urine (ionized form trapped)
* **Clinical Considerations:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Renal drug clearance depends on renal function even for drugs eliminated primarily by tubular secretion
  * Autoregulation of renal blood flow maintains relatively constant renal elimination clearance across wide range of cardiac outputs
	* Exception: Low urine output states eventually allow increased reabsorption from distal tubules

# 3. PHARMACOKINETIC MODELS





### 3.1.1. MODEL TYPES
#### 3.1.1.1. PHYSIOLOGIC VS. COMPARTMENT MODELS
* **Initial Physiologic/Perfusion Model:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Vessel-rich group (75% of CO; large blood flow/vol of tissue; 10% of BW): Brain, lung, kidney, subset of muscle
  * Lean tissue group (balanced blood flow/vol of tissue): Muscle, splanchnic bed (liver)
  * Vessel-poor group (limited blood flow/vol of tissue): Adipose tissue, rest of splanchnic bed, bone, ligament
* **Model Evolution:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Perfusion-based PK models require measurement of drug concentrations in many tissues (impractical)
  * Body thus compartmentalized for simplicity
  * *Important Concept*: Compartments are theoretical entities, not equivalent to tissue groups

**Bottom Line:** Pharmacokinetic models simplify the complex processes of drug movement through the body, with compartmental models being more practical for clinical use than physiologic models, although they represent theoretical rather than actual physiological compartments.

### 3.1.2. PHARMACOKINETIC CONCEPTS

#### 3.1.2.1. FIRST-ORDER KINETICS
* **Definition:** Constant fraction of drug removed during finite time period, regardless of drug amount/concentration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Mathematical Representation:** Fraction = rate constant of process (k; units: min⁻¹ or h⁻¹) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Example: 10% of drug eliminated/min → rate constant 0.1 min⁻¹ → T₁/₂ of 6.93 minutes
  * t_1/2 = \frac{\ln 2}{k} = \frac{0.693}{k}
* **Key Principle:** Rate of change proportional to concentration present [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * High concentration → more drug removed than when concentration is low
* **Comparison with Zero-Order Kinetics:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * First-order: Constant proportion removed per unit time, concentration falls exponentially, half-life constant
  * Zero-order: Constant amount removed per unit time, concentration falls linearly, half-life progressively shortens
  * Zero-order occurs when enzymes are saturated (e.g., alcohol, phenytoin)

#### 3.1.2.2. VOLUME OF DISTRIBUTION
* **Governing Factors:** Overall capacity of tissues vs. capacity of blood for that drug [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Tissue Capacity:** Function of total mass of tissue into which drug distributes and average affinity for drug [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Definition:** "Apparent" volume representing hypothetical compartments explaining drug concentration in reference compartment [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* $V_d = \frac{amount of drug administered}{initial drug plasma concentration}$
* **Relationship Principle:** Extensively distributed drugs → lower concentration relative to amount present → larger volume of distribution [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Important Properties:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * No relationship to actual volume of any tissue/group
  * May be as small as plasma volume or larger than total body volume
  * Generally, lipophilic drugs have larger Vd than hydrophilic drugs
  * Doesn't indicate actual tissue concentration in specific organs
  * Can inform loading dose to achieve target plasma concentration
	* $Loading Dose = V_d × Target Concentration \quad (11-8)$
  * Categories based on size: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
	* <40 mL/kg: Confined to plasma
	* 40-200 mL/kg: Mainly in extracellular fluid
	* 200-600 mL/kg: Distributed in total body water
	* > 1000 mL/kg: Highly lipophilic or protein-bound

#### 3.1.2.3. TOTAL DRUG (ELIMINATION) CLEARANCE
* **Definition:** Theoretical volume of blood from which drug is completely/irreversibly removed in unit time (mL/min/kg or mL/min/m²) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Relationship:** Rapid removal from plasma → faster concentration fall → smaller area under concentration vs. time curve → greater clearance [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* $Cl = \frac{dose of drug administered}{area under the concentration versus time curve} \quad (11-9)$
* **Formula:** Cl = ke × Vd (elimination rate constant × volume of distribution) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Relationship to Elimination:** Rate of elimination = clearance × plasma concentration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

#### 3.1.2.4. ELIMINATION HALF-LIFE
* **Definition:** Time during which amount of drug in body decreases by 50% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Complexity:** Influenced by distribution and elimination (not just metabolism/elimination) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Example: **THIOPENTAL** elimination T₁/₂ in elderly is prolonged due to increased Vd, not decreased metabolism
* t_1/2\beta = \frac{\ln 2}{k_\beta} = 0.693 × \frac{V_d}{Cl_E}
* **Limited Utility:** Does not indicate time to terminate effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Termination often due to redistribution from brain to blood and other tissues
  * Effects of most anesthetics wane long before one elimination t₁/₂ completes
* 🔑 **HY:** Elimination half-life is a poor predictor of clinical duration for most anesthetic drugs [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

#### 3.1.2.5. DISEASE EFFECTS ON PK PARAMETERS
* **Hepatic Disease:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Increases elimination T₁/₂ of drugs metabolized/excreted by liver
  * Increases Vd (ascites and altered protein binding)
* **Steady State Principle:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Drug concentration at steady state inversely proportional to elimination clearance
  * If clearance reduced and repeated bolus/infusion given → drug accumulation

#### 3.1.2.6. NONLINEAR PK
* **Standard Models:** Assume first-order distribution and elimination [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Nonlinear Patterns:** Some drugs show dose-dependent kinetics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Example: When drug-eliminating systems (enzymes/carrier proteins) saturate
  * Example drug: **PHENYTOIN**

**Bottom Line:** First-order kinetics, volume of distribution, clearance, and half-life are fundamental PK concepts that describe drug behavior mathematically. Elimination half-life, while commonly cited, is a poor predictor of clinical duration for anesthetic drugs due to the significant impact of redistribution on drug effect termination.

### 3.1.3. COMPARTMENTAL PK MODELS

#### 3.1.3.1. ONE-COMPARTMENT MODEL
* **Assumptions:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Body is single homogeneous compartment
  * Distribution is instantaneous (no concentration gradients)
  * Concentration dependent on elimination
  * Clearance is product of elimination rate constant and Vd
* **Mathematical Representation:** Simple monoexponential function [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* $C(t) = C_0 × e^{-k_e × t}$
* t_1/2\beta = \frac{\ln 2}{k_e} = 0.693 × \frac{V_d}{Cl_E}
* **Relationships:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Greater clearance → shorter elimination half-life
  * Larger volume of distribution → longer elimination half-life
  * Elimination half-life depends on clearance and volume of distribution
* **Infusion Dynamics:** Concentration rises to steady state after ~5 half-lives [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

#### 3.1.3.2. TWO-COMPARTMENT MODEL
* **Central (Plasma) Compartment Dynamics:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Two discrete phases of decline
  * First phase after injection: Rapid decrease in concentration ("distribution phase")
	* Approaches zero after several distribution t₁/₂
  * Second phase: Slower decline of concentration ("elimination phase")
* **Mathematical Representation:** Plasma concentration equals sum of two exponential terms [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* $C_p(t) = Ae^{-\alpha t} + Be^{-\beta t}$
* **Analysis Method:** Graphic analysis of plasma concentration versus time curve [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Elimination phase line extrapolated back to time zero
  * Hybrid rate constants (α and β) equal to slopes of two lines
  * Used to calculate distribution and elimination half-lives

#### 3.1.3.3. THREE-COMPARTMENT MODEL
* **Sequence After IV Injection:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Rapid distribution phase (to more rapidly equilibrating compartment)
  * Slower distribution phase
  * Elimination phase
* **Mathematical Representation:** Sum of 3 exponential terms [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * $C_p(t) = Ae^{-\alpha t} + Be^{-\beta t} + Ge^{-\gamma t}$
* **Better Characterization:** Most anesthetics best described by this model [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

**Bottom Line:** Compartmental PK models represent the body as connected theoretical compartments that explain observed drug concentration-time profiles. One-compartment models describe simple drugs with rapid distribution, while twoand three-compartment models better characterize most anesthetics, capturing the distinct distribution and elimination phases observed clinically.

### 3.1.4. NONCOMPARTMENTAL (STOCHASTIC) PK MODELS

**Statistical models that use area under the curve rather than theoretical compartments.**

* **Advantages:** No need for complex mathematical modeling techniques [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Basic Assumptions:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * All elimination clearance occurs directly from plasma
  * Distribution and elimination are linear and first-order processes
  * Pharmacokinetics do not vary over data collection time (time invariant)
* **Unique Parameter:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Mean Residence Time (MRT)**: Average time drug molecule spends in body before elimination
  * *Limitation*: Fails to capture contribution of extensive distribution vs limited elimination
  * MRT = AUMC/AUC (area under moment curve divided by area under curve)

**Bottom Line:** Noncompartmental modeling offers a simpler alternative to compartmental models by focusing on statistical analysis of concentration-time data rather than defining theoretical compartments, but has limitations in distinguishing distribution from elimination processes.

### 3.1.6. MATHEMATICAL CONCEPTS

**Mathematical principles that form the foundation of pharmacokinetic modeling.**

#### 3.1.6.1. THE EXPONENTIAL FUNCTION
* **Basic Definition:** A function that defines a unique value for dependent variable (y) given a value for independent variable (x): [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * y = f(x)
  * For pharmacokinetics: C = f(t) where C is concentration and t is time
* **Drug Concentration Pattern:** After a single dose, plasma concentration falls with time at a rate proportional to concentration itself [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **General Form of Exponential Function:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * y = An^ax
  * n is the base, x is the exponent, A and a are constants
  * Natural number e (2.716…) chosen as base for mathematical convenience
	* Only function that integrates and differentiates to itself
* **Negative vs. Positive Exponentials:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Negative: Rate decreases as x increases (used for drug elimination)
  * Positive: Rate increases as x increases (e.g., bacterial growth, compound interest)
* **Single-Compartment Model Equation:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * C = C₀e^(-kt)
  * C₀: Concentration at time t=0
  * k: Rate constant for elimination
  * Describes "wash-out curve" for drug
* **Time Constant and Half-Life:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Time constant (τ) = 1/k
  * Process is 63% complete after one time constant
  * Half-life (t₁/₂) = 0.693τ = 0.693/k
  * Process virtually complete after 5 half-lives
  * Time constant is longer than half-life (e is greater than 2)
* **Wash-In Curves:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Also described by negative exponentials
  * Rate approaches maximum value decreases with time
  * Describes constant rate infusion concentration increase

**Bottom Line:** Exponential functions are fundamental to pharmacokinetic modeling, describing both the decrease in drug concentration after bolus administration (wash-out curve) and the approach to steady-state during infusion (wash-in curve). The mathematics of these functions allows prediction of drug behavior over time.

#### 3.1.6.2. LOGARITHMS
* **Basic Definition:** Logarithm is the exponent to which a base must be raised to yield a particular number [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * x = 10^y
  * y = log x (log to base 10)
  * Examples: log 1000 = 3, log 5 = 0.699, log 1 = 0
* **Natural Logarithms:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Logarithms to base e written as ln x
  * Essential for working with exponential functions
  * Example: ln 2 = 0.693 (important value relating time constant to half-life)
* **Application in Pharmacokinetics:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Converting exponential equation to logarithmic form:
	* C = C₀e^(-kt) becomes ln C = ln C₀ - kt
  * Creates straight line when ln C plotted against time
	* Slope = -k
	* Intercept on ln C axis = ln C₀
* **Logarithmic Operations:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Multiplication: log(w·z) = log w + log z
  * Division: log(w/z) = log w - log z
  * Converting between log bases: ln C = 2.302 log C and log C = 0.434 ln C

**Bottom Line:** Logarithmic transformations convert exponential drug concentration-time profiles into straight lines, facilitating the calculation of key pharmacokinetic parameters like elimination rate constants and half-lives from experimental data.

#### 3.1.6.3. CALCULUS: DIFFERENTIATION
* **Rate of Plasma Concentration Change:** The tangent to the concentration-time curve [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Mathematically expressed as differential equation: dC/dt
  * For a single-compartment model: dC/dt = -kC
  * Describes first-order relationship (rate depends on C raised to power 1)
* **First-Order vs. Zero-Order Kinetics:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * First-order: dC/dt = -kC (rate dependent on concentration)
  * Zero-order: dC/dt = -k (rate constant regardless of concentration)
  * Zero-order occurs when elimination processes are saturated
  * Linear decline in concentration vs. time (not exponential)
* **Differentiation of Exponential Function:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * d(C₀e^(-kt))/dt = -kC₀e^(-kt) = -kC
  * Exponential function differentiates to itself (multiplied by derivative of exponent)

**Bottom Line:** Differential calculus describes how rapidly drug concentration is changing at any point in time. First-order kinetics (where rate depends on concentration) apply to most drugs, while zero-order kinetics (constant elimination rate) occur when metabolic pathways become saturated.

#### 3.1.6.4. CALCULUS: INTEGRATION
* **Basic Concept:** Calculates area under the curve of a function [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * In pharmacokinetics: Area between time zero and infinity
  * Integration is the reverse of differentiation
* **Integral of dC/dt = -kC:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Gives original equation C = C₀e^(-kt)
  * Requires boundary condition that at infinite time, plasma concentration = 0
* **Area Under Curve Applications:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Clearance = Dose/AUC
  * Mean Residence Time (MRT) = AUMC/AUC (where AUMC is area under first moment curve)
  * Volume of distribution at steady state (Vss) = Cl × MRT

**Bottom Line:** Integral calculus determines the area under the concentration-time curve, which is essential for calculating pharmacokinetic parameters like clearance, mean residence time, and volume of distribution through non-compartmental analysis.

### 3.1.7. MEASUREMENT OF PHARMACOKINETIC PARAMETERS

**Methods for determining key PK parameters from experimental concentration-time data.**

#### 3.1.7.1. VOLUME OF DISTRIBUTION
* **Definition:** Apparent volume into which drug disperses to produce observed plasma concentration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Does not correspond to any physiological volume
  * Can be much larger than total body water
  * Units: liters (can be indexed to body weight as L/kg)
  * Vd = dose/plasma concentration = X/C₀
* **Influencing Factors:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Molecular size
  * Lipid solubility
  * Charge characteristics
  * Tissue binding (especially intracellular sequestration)
  * Pathology (hepatic/renal disease can increase Vd)
* **Clinical Examples:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Propofol: Highly lipid-soluble with large Vd (~250 L)
  * Vecuronium/atracurium: Charged molecules with smaller Vd (19 L and 11 L)
  * Chloroquine: Extremely high Vd (>10,000 L) due to intracellular sequestration
* **Multiple Volume Measurements in Multi-compartment Models:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Central Compartment Volume (Vinitial):** Initial volume after distribution
	* Estimated from rapid distribution phase
	* Vinitial = X/A (where X is dose, A is y-intercept)
	* Influenced by protein binding (higher binding = larger central volume)
	* Example: Propofol (98% protein bound) has central volume of ~16 L vs. plasma volume of ~3 L
  * **Total Volume of Distribution Estimates:**
	* **Vextrap:** Based on extrapolated terminal elimination phase
	  * Limitation: Overestimates total volume, especially when distribution phase significant
	* **Varea:** Relates clearance to terminal elimination constant
	  * Varea = Clearance/β = X/(AUC·β)
	  * Better estimate but still overestimates true volume
	* **Vss:** Based on non-compartmental model
	  * Vss = (dose/AUC)·(AUMC/AUC) = dose·AUMC/AUC²
	  * Independent of elimination, usually most accurate
	  * Relationship: Vextrap > Varea > Vss

**Bottom Line:** Volume of distribution is a theoretical construct relating dose to plasma concentration that varies widely among drugs based on their physicochemical properties. For multi-compartment models, different volume measurements (initial, area, steady-state) provide complementary information about drug distribution patterns.

#### 3.1.7.2. CLEARANCE
* **Definition:** Volume of plasma completely cleared of drug per unit time [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Units: mL/min
  * For single-compartment model: Cl = k·Vd (rate constant × volume of distribution)
* **Clearance in Multi-compartment Models:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Elimination Clearance:** Product of k₁₀ and V₁ (central compartment volume)
	* Example: Propofol with V₁ ≈ 16 L and k₁₀ ≈ 0.12 has clearance of ~2 L/min
	* Example: Remifentanil with V₁ ≈ 5.1 L and k₁₀ ≈ 0.5 has clearance of ~2.5 L/min
  
  * **Non-compartmental Calculation:** Cl = Dose/AUC
  * **Inter-compartmental Clearance:** Describes drug transfer between compartments
	* C₁₂ and C₁₃ define transfer between compartments 1-2 and 1-3
	* Higher values indicate more rapid distribution/redistribution

**Bottom Line:** Clearance quantifies the body's ability to remove drug from the circulation and can be calculated from elimination rate constant and volume of distribution, or non-compartmentally from dose and area under the concentration-time curve. Both systemic elimination clearance and inter-compartmental clearance impact drug concentration profiles.

#### 3.1.7.3. NON-LINEAR KINETICS
* **Standard Models:** Based on first-order kinetics with rate of elimination proportional to concentration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Saturation Kinetics:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Occurs when metabolic enzymes become saturated
  * Rate of drug elimination becomes constant (zero-order kinetics)
  * Plasma concentration declines linearly rather than exponentially
  * Also known as capacity-limited metabolism
* **Clinical Examples:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Ethanol: Metabolized at constant rate due to limited alcohol dehydrogenase cofactor
  * Phenytoin: Metabolism becomes saturated within therapeutic range
  * Thiopental: Shows zero-order kinetics during prolonged infusion (status epilepticus)
* **Clinical Implications:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Small dose increases may cause large increases in plasma concentration
  * No steady-state possible if intake exceeds elimination capacity
  * Plasma concentration rises continuously until toxicity or death
  * Monitoring plasma levels essential for drugs with narrow therapeutic windows

![image.png](BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_pdf_extracted_pages_48-93.pdf_p17_img1_bb2ea8.png)

**Bottom Line:** Non-linear pharmacokinetics occurs when elimination processes become saturated, causing a shift from first-order to zero-order kinetics. This results in a linear decline in plasma concentration and can lead to disproportionate increases in drug levels with small dose increases, potentially causing toxicity.

### 4.1.2. RISE TO STEADY-STATE CONCENTRATION

#### 4.1.2.1. ONE-COMPARTMENTAL MODEL
* **Pattern:** Monoexponential rise/decline in concentration vs. time [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Accumulation Pattern:** After 1 t₁/₂, concentration reaches 50% of eventual Css; after 2 t₁/₂, reaches 75% [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Clinical Guideline:** Concentration reaches 90% of CSS after 3.3 half-lives (usually sufficient for clinical purposes) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* C_p(t) = C_ss[1 - e^{-kt}] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* C_p(n) = C_ss[1 - (1/2)^n] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

#### 4.1.2.2. MULTI-COMPARTMENTAL MODELS
* **Limitation:** Monoexponential equation insufficient for highly distributing drugs (e.g., **PROPOFOL**) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Requirement:** Multi-exponential models with respective exponential terms [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* C_p(t) = C_ss \left[ \frac{A}{A+B+G}(1-e^{-\alpha t}) + \frac{B}{A+B+G}(1-e^{-\beta t}) + \frac{G}{A+B+G}(1-e^{-\gamma t}) \right] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Coefficient Relationships:** For most lipophilic anesthetics/opioids: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * A typically one order of magnitude greater than B
  * B typically one order of magnitude greater than G
  * Distribution-phase kinetics have much greater influence on time to reach CSS than elimination-phase kinetics
* **Example:** **PROPOFOL** (elimination t₁/₂ 6h) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * One-compartment model prediction: 6h to achieve 50% of eventual Css
  * Multi-compartmental model reality: 30min to reach 50% of Css, 4h to reach 75%

**Bottom Line:** Achieving steady-state plasma concentrations of anesthetics follows mathematical principles, but most IV anesthetic agents require multi-compartmental models to accurately predict concentration rise. The distribution phase has greater influence than elimination on reaching steady state.

### 4.1.3. MANUAL BOLUS AND INFUSION DOSING SCHEMES

#### 4.1.3.1. STEADY-STATE MAINTENANCE
* **Principle:** Stable steady-state plasma concentration (Cp,ss) maintained by infusion rate (I) proportional to elimination from body (ClE) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Time Factor:** If using infusion alone, would take 3.3 t₁/₂ to reach 90% of target concentration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* I = C_p,ss × Cl_E [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

#### 4.1.3.2. BOLUS ADMINISTRATION
* **Purpose:** Decrease time to target plasma concentration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Calculation Problem:** Using steady-state elimination clearance (ClE) and volume of distribution (VdSS) for loading dose calculation causes excessive dosing [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Bolus = C_p,SS × V_d,SS [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Example: **PROPOFOL** calculation using Vd,SS (262 L) and ClE (1.7 L/min) for 5 μg/mL target: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
	* Formula result: 1,300 mg (18 mg/kg) loading and 120 μg/kg/min infusion
	* Clinically used doses: 1-2 mg/kg
  * *Reason for Discrepancy*: Initial bolus doesn't instantly mix/equilibrate with entire eventual distribution volume [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

#### 4.1.3.3. ACCURATE BOLUS DESIGN
* **Key Principle:** Choose bolus based on small, initial volume of distribution (Vc) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]

#### 4.1.3.4. BET SCHEME
* **Definition:** Manual dosing scheme with series of decreasing rate infusions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Components:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * B - loading bolus dose
  * E - infusion compensating for elimination
  * T - continuously decreasing infusion compensating for drug transfer to peripheral tissue

### 4.1.4. CONTEXT-SENSITIVE DECREMENT TIME

#### 4.1.4.1. RECOVERY CONCEPT
* **Definition:** When effect site concentration decreases below threshold for pharmacologic effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Limitation of Half-Life:** For multicompartmental kinetics, elimination half-life always overestimates recovery time [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

#### 4.1.4.2. CONTEXT-SENSITIVE HALF-TIME
![image.png](BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_image_9.png_13c294.png_7ac8b6.png_854d5a.png)

* **Definition:** Time required for plasma drug concentration to decrease by 50% after infusion of particular length [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Calculation Method:** Simulate TCI of specific length (the context), then observe time for plasma concentration to reach half of target [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Termination Dynamics:** Upon stopping at steady state: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Net drug distribution begins toward central from peripheral compartments + elimination
  * Net drug distribution flux into central compartment balances elimination flux → constant elimination rate

#### 4.1.4.3. LIMITATIONS
* Describes only a specific simulation event [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Cannot be extrapolated to lesser/greater concentration decrements [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Cannot be directly calculated [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* If other decrease percentages required (25% or 75%), new simulations needed [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Not at effect site (requires context-sensitive effect site decrement time) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* 🔑 **HY:** Context-sensitive half-time is more clinically relevant than elimination half-life for predicting recovery [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
* **Context-Sensitive Half-Times for Different Drugs:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Remifentanil: 5 mins (after 8h infusion) - context-insensitive
  * Propofol: 40 mins
  * Fentanyl: 250 mins

![image.png](BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_image_10.png_4b684b.png_d15f86.png_97c9b8.png)

**Bottom Line:** Context-sensitive half-time more accurately predicts recovery from anesthetics than elimination half-life by accounting for the effects of infusion duration on drug redistribution from tissues to plasma, which explains why drugs with similar elimination half-lives can have dramatically different recovery profiles.

### 4.1.5. CONTEXT-SENSITIVE TIME TO RECOVERY

#### 4.1.5.1. HALF-TIME VS. RECOVERY TIME
* **Elimination Half-Life:** Time for half of drug remaining in body after pseudoequilibration to be eliminated [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Context-Sensitive Half-Time:** Time for 50% concentration decrement after stopping drug administration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Common Misunderstanding:** Both often mistaken for time to recovery from drug effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

![image.png](BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_image_11.png_6aa3bb.png_0df2d2.png_148227.png)

#### 4.1.5.2. RECOVERY THRESHOLD CONCEPT
* **Improved Metric:** Context-sensitive time to recovery [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Definition:** Time to reach particular threshold concentration after terminating infusion, resulting in dissipation of drug effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl:** More accurately predicts awakening time than half-lives [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Context-sensitive time to recovery is the most clinically relevant parameter for predicting emergence from anesthesia, as it accounts for the specific concentration decrement needed for clinical recovery rather than an arbitrary 50% reduction in concentration.

### 4.1.6. SOFT PHARMACOLOGY IN ANESTHESIOLOGY

#### 4.1.6.1. DESIGN STRATEGY
* **Approach:** Create compounds rapidly metabolized by plasma/tissue enzymes ("soft drugs") [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Purpose:** Reduce tissue uptake of drug to avoid affecting plasma concentration decline rate [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Examples:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **REMIFENTANIL**: Context-insensitive (relatively) decrement times
  * **REMIMAZOLAM**: Benzodiazepine with esterase metabolism
* 🔑 **HY:** Soft drugs have more predictable recovery profiles regardless of infusion duration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

![image.png](BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_image_12.png_dcd360.png_781849.png_97afbf.png)

**Bottom Line:** "Soft drugs" like remifentanil are designed to undergo rapid metabolism by ubiquitous enzymes, resulting in context-insensitive recovery profiles that maintain predictable emergence times regardless of infusion duration—a significant advantage for anesthetic practice.

### 4.1.7. TARGET CONTROLLED INFUSIONS (TCI)

#### 4.1.7.1. BASIC CONCEPT
* **Components:** Computer + PK models + Infusion pumps [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Function:** Enter desired target plasma concentration → computer-driven BET scheme [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

![image.png](BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_image_13.png_c0e4d8.png_d8ce8f.png_bfd4d4.png)

#### 4.1.7.2. SUCCESS FACTORS
* Depends on how well programmed drug PK/PD parameters match particular patient [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

#### 4.1.7.3. MODEL PRINCIPLES
![image.png](BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_image_14.png_bf5a8b.png_2403b7.png_79b520.png)

* Initial dose: Product of central compartment V1 and target concentration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* Subsequent doses: Maintain target concentration by balancing: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Drug elimination from central compartment
  * Drug distributed from central to peripheral compartment
  * Drug returning to central from peripheral compartment
* Software tracks estimated drug in each compartment over time [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* Applies rate constants for intercompartmental drug transfer from PK model [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* Can target effect site concentrations [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

#### 4.1.7.4. CLINICAL BEHAVIOR
![image.png](BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_image_15.png_7a491f.png_19a453.png_7ba9c0.png)

* TCIs tend to overshoot for 10-20 minutes when increasing target concentration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* *Reason*: Ignores complexity of intravascular mixing, overestimates central compartment volume, transfer rate to fast peripheral tissue, and peripheral compartment size [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* *Adjustment Principle*: Incremental target adjustments result in stable new concentrations if not too frequent [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Divergence low (~1%) with stable targets
  * Increases to ~20% with frequent adjustments (every 12 minutes)
  * Error may exist (±30%) between targeted and actual concentration, but remains relatively stable over time

#### 4.1.7.5. EFFICACY EVIDENCE
* Mixed results: Some studies show better control and more predictable emergence, others show no advantage [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Target-controlled infusions use computer algorithms to achieve and maintain specific plasma or effect-site concentrations by dynamically adjusting infusion rates based on pharmacokinetic models. While TCI systems may overestimate central compartment size initially, they generally maintain stable concentrations with gradual target adjustments.

### 4.1.8. TIME TO MAXIMUM EFFECT COMPARTMENT CONCENTRATION (TMAX)

#### 4.1.8.1. DELAY MECHANISM
* Lag between plasma and effect-site concentration results from: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Transfer of drug between plasma compartment (Vc) and effect compartment (Ve)
  * Time required for cellular response
* *Modeling Approach*: Simultaneous modeling of plasma concentration vs. time data and measured drug effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Estimates drug transfer rate constant (ke0) between plasma and effect site
  * Makes ke0 model-specific (cannot be compared across models)

**Bottom Line:** The time to maximum effect-site concentration (Tmax) accounts for the delay between plasma concentration and observed drug effect, which is critical for understanding the onset of IV anesthetics. This parameter is model-specific and cannot be directly compared between different model systems.

### 4.1.9. VOLUME OF DISTRIBUTION AT PEAK EFFECT (VDPE)

#### 4.1.9.1. CONCEPT
* **Definition:** Time-dependent volume at time of peak effect (Tmax) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Application:** Proper bolus = target effect site concentration × VDPE + amount lost to elimination in time to Tmax [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **TCI Use:** May give bolus until Tmax then resume infusion in "stop loss" manner [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Example:** **PROPOFOL** using Schnider model in 70-kg man: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Tmax: 1.6 minutes
  * VDPE: 16.62 L
  * Elimination loss: 23.8% of dose over 1.6 minutes
  * Proper bolus for 5 μg/mL target: 109 mg
  * *Delivery Method*: Computer-controlled pump delivers dose rapidly, then begins targeted infusion at t = 1.6 minutes

**Bottom Line:** Volume of distribution at peak effect (VDPE) is a time-dependent parameter that helps calculate the optimal bolus dose to achieve a desired effect-site concentration, accounting for both distribution and elimination during the onset phase.

### 4.1.10. FRONT-END PHARMACOKINETICS

#### 4.1.10.1. CONCEPT AND LIMITATIONS
* **Definition:** Intravascular mixing, pulmonary uptake, and recirculation events in first few minutes during/after IV administration [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Standard Model Limitation:** Classic PK-PD models assume instantaneous/complete mixing in intravascular space [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Disregards intercompartmental clearance, cardiac output, distribution
  * Results in overestimation of central distribution volume (Vc)
* **Current Status:** TCI doesn't incorporate front-end kinetics into models for calculating infusion rates [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Front-end kinetics explains why calculated doses sometimes produce greater effects than expected [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].

### 4.1.11. CLOSED-LOOP INFUSIONS

#### 4.1.11.1. CONTROLLER MECHANISMS

* 🔑 **HY:** Understanding response surfaces helps clinicians find combinations that minimize side effects while maintaining efficacy [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Response surface models quantify drug-drug interactions in anesthesia, enabling selection of optimal drug combinations that maximize therapeutic effect while minimizing side effects. These models are particularly valuable for balanced anesthesia techniques that combine hypnotics and opioids.

## 4.2. APPLIED PHARMACOKINETIC MODELS

### 4.2.1. IDEAL DRUGS FOR INTRAVENOUS USE IN ANESTHESIA
* **Definition:** Total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) refers to providing anesthesia solely through IV infusions [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Ideal Drug Properties:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Predictable plasma concentrations according to a known PK model
  * Predictable link between PK and PD effects
  * No active metabolites
  * Rapid onset of action
  * Rapid offset of action
  * Stable in plastic syringe
* **Delivery Improvements:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Pumps with drug-specific algorithms for most favorable kinetic profiles
  * Target-controlled infusions (TCI) to maintain constant plasma/effect-site concentration
  * Common combinations: propofol + remifentanil (separate pumps for independent control)
  * Alternative approach: propofol + alfentanil mixture (single pump for shorter cases)

**Bottom Line:** Ideal IV anesthetic drugs should have predictable PK/PD profiles, rapid onset/offset, and no active metabolites. Modern TIVA delivery uses computerized pumps with drug-specific algorithms, with propofol and remifentanil being the most common combination due to their favorable characteristics.

### 4.2.2. PROPOFOL
* **Chemistry:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Sterically hindered phenol (hydroxyl group shielded by isopropyl groups)
  * Insoluble in water, highly lipid-soluble → formulated as lipid emulsion
  * Weak acid (ionization only at pH >10)
  * High lipid solubility → risk of accumulation in fatty tissues after long infusions
  * No active metabolites
* **Pharmacokinetics:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Extensively protein-bound (98% to albumin)
  * Very rapid CNS uptake after injection (high lipophilicity)
  * Distribution half-life: 1-2 minutes
  * Redistribution from CNS causes offset after bolus
  * Rapid elimination by hepatic and extrahepatic metabolism contributes to rapid concentration decrease
  * Terminal elimination half-life: 5-12 hours (minimal role in clinical offset after bolus)
* **Pharmacodynamics:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Effect-site (CNS) concentration lags behind plasma concentration
  * Time-to-peak effect: 1.7-3.9 minutes (model-dependent)
  * Effect compartment/plasma relationships:
	* Induction phase: Brain concentration < plasma concentration
	* Elimination phase: Brain concentration > plasma concentration
* **Clinical Application:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Can be delivered using three-compartment model + effect compartment
  * Effect compartment has no volume but uses rate constant keo for equilibration
  * Half-life for equilibration (t1/2keo) accounts for plasma-brain lag

**Bottom Line:** Propofol's rapid clinical onset and offset are due to its high lipid solubility, allowing quick CNS penetration followed by redistribution. Its pharmacokinetics are best described by a three-compartment model with an effect compartment to account for the plasma-brain equilibration delay.

### 4.2.3. REMIFENTANIL
* **Chemistry:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Fentanyl derivative with pure μ-opioid agonist properties
  * Contains ester linkage rapidly broken down by plasma/tissue esterases (especially muscle)
  * Metabolites have minimal pharmacological activity
* **Pharmacokinetics:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Rapid metabolism by non-specific esterases
  * Affected by age (muscle bulk decreases with age → slower metabolism)
  * Rapid offset, minimally affected by infusion duration
  * Context-sensitive half-time remains relatively constant (3-8 minutes)
  * Minimal drug accumulation even with prolonged infusions
* **Clinical Considerations:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Advantage: Can maintain analgesia during surgery with rapid offset when no longer needed
  * Disadvantage: Analgesic effect wears off rapidly → potential for immediate post-operative pain
  * Hyperalgesia risk if discontinued abruptly without alternative analgesia
  * Post-operative pain management strategies:
	* Regional anesthesia technique
	* Longer-acting opioid before end of surgery

**Bottom Line:** Remifentanil's unique pharmacokinetic profile provides rapid offset regardless of infusion duration due to ester metabolism by tissue esterases. This makes it ideal for intraoperative analgesia, but requires proactive management of post-operative pain due to its rapid cessation of effect.

### 4.2.4. TOTAL INTRAVENOUS ANESTHESIA
* **Definition:** Induction and maintenance of anesthesia using IV agents and oxygen/air mix [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Delivery Options:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Manual infusion
  * Target-controlled infusion (plasma or effect-site targeting)
  * Historically used with nitrous oxide, but less common now (environmental concerns)
* **Monitoring Limitations:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * No "point-of-delivery" concentration measurement (unlike end-tidal monitoring for volatile agents)
  * TCI displays calculated value based on:
	* Software model
	* Patient parameters (weight, height, age, sex)

**Bottom Line:** Total intravenous anesthesia provides a complete anesthetic technique without volatile agents, but lacks the direct feedback of end-tidal concentration monitoring, making calculated model-based concentration estimates the primary monitoring tool.

### 4.2.5. MANUAL TOTAL INTRAVENOUS ANESTHESIA: THE BRISTOL MODEL
* **Definition:** Simple manual infusion scheme for propofol targeting ~3 μg/ml blood concentration [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Components:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Temazepam premedication
  * Fentanyl 3 μg/kg
  * Propofol bolus 1 mg/kg
  * Variable-rate infusion:
	* 10 mg/kg/hr for 10 minutes
	* 8 mg/kg/hr for 10 minutes
	* 6 mg/kg/hr thereafter
  * Nitrous oxide (historical component)
  * Mechanical ventilation
* **Clinical Adaptation:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * If higher plasma concentrations needed: adjust 10-8-6 algorithm proportionally
  * Alternative: Add remifentanil infusion
* **Comparison with TCI:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Studies show TCI provides:
	* Lower total propofol dose
	* Improved hemodynamic stability
	* More rapid awakening

![image.png](BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_pdf_extracted_pages_48-93.pdf_p25_img1_60efb0.png)

**Bottom Line:** The Bristol model ("10-8-6") provides a simple manual approach to propofol infusion with predictable pharmacokinetics, though TCI systems offer advantages in drug dose optimization, hemodynamic stability, and emergence time.

### 4.2.6. TARGET-CONTROLLED INFUSION IN PRACTICE
* **Basic Principles:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Microprocessor-controlled pump with programmed PK model
  * Can target plasma or effect-site concentration
  * Calculates variable-rate infusion to achieve target
  * Common models:
	* Adults: Marsh and Schnider (propofol), Minto (remifentanil)
	* Pediatrics: Paedfusor (propofol)
* **Model Differences:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Targeting Options:**
	* Plasma targeting: Delivers bolus to reach plasma target, then maintains
	* Effect-site targeting: Administers bolus then pauses as plasma concentration falls to meet rising effect-site concentration at target
  * **Key Parameter Differences:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
	* Marsh: V1 proportional to weight (0.228 L/kg)
	* Schnider: V1 fixed (4.27 L)
	* Model impacts:
	  * Initial bolus size
	  * Response to weight changes
	  * Maintenance infusion rates
* **Clinical Implementation:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **Equipment Requirements:** TCI pumps, appropriate giving sets, depth of anesthesia monitors
  * **Target Ranges:**
	* Induction: Higher target for bolus effect
	* Maintenance: Typically 2.5-4 μg/ml for propofol, 3-6 ng/ml for remifentanil
	* Synergistic combinations possible (see isobologram)
* **Propofol-Remifentanil Strategy:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Synergistic combination allows dose reduction of each component
  * Strategies:
	* Long operations: Lower propofol + higher remifentanil (fixed, short context-sensitive half-time)
	* Optimal synergy: Combination near most concave part of isobologram

  ![image.png](BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_pdf_extracted_pages_48-93.pdf_p31_img1_e68bb6.png)

* **Clinical Caveats:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Actual blood level may differ from target by ±25% due to PK variations
  * Target must be adjusted to effect, similar to volatile anesthetic titration
  * Some pumps display "decrement time" (predicted time to awakening threshold)
  * Actual awakening may take longer due to drug interactions

**Bottom Line:** TCI systems simplify IV anesthetic delivery through automated calculation of infusion rates based on pharmacokinetic models. Different models (Marsh, Schnider) handle volume calculations differently, affecting dosing. Target concentrations must still be adjusted based on clinical effect, as actual blood levels may vary from displayed targets.

### 4.2.7. SAFETY OF TIVA AND TCI IN CLINICAL PRACTICE
* **Setup Complexity:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * More complicated than inhalational anesthesia
  * Additional equipment checking needed
  * Programming errors possible (wrong drug/concentration)
  * Lack of "key index" system (as used for volatile agents)
* **Monitoring Limitations:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * No real-time measurement of drug delivery (unlike exhaled gas analysis)
  * Breathing circuit failures immediately detected with volatiles but not with TIVA
  * Depth of anesthesia monitoring recommended when using muscle relaxants with TIVA
* **Clinical Advantages:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Single agent for induction/maintenance prevents "gap" in anesthesia depth
  * Maintenance independent of ventilation

**Bottom Line:** TIVA setup is more complex than volatile anesthesia and lacks direct verification of drug delivery, making depth of anesthesia monitoring and careful equipment setup critical for safety. Benefits include seamless transition from induction to maintenance and independence from ventilation systems.

### 4.2.8. CONTEXT-SENSITIVE HALF-TIME (EXPANDED)
* **Definition Revisited:** Time for plasma concentration to decrease by 50% after infusion of specific duration [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Clinical Relevance:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Terminal elimination half-life has little clinical relevance
  * CSHT varies with infusion duration
  * "Context" refers to duration of infusion
  * Maximum CSHT occurs at steady state
* **Drug-Specific Profiles:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Remifentanil: Consistently short (5-8 minutes) regardless of infusion duration
  * Propofol: Moderately increases with duration (maximum ~25 minutes)
  * Fentanyl: Dramatically increases with duration (up to 300 minutes)
  * Key factor: Ratio of distribution clearance to elimination clearance
	* Remifentanil: Ratio <1 (elimination dominates)
	* Fentanyl: Ratio very high (redistribution dominates)

![image.png](BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_pdf_extracted_pages_48-93.pdf_p32_img1_f5c3cc.png)

* **Clinical Application Nuances:** [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Half-times are not constant (subsequent half-times longer than initial)
  * Wakening may require greater than 50% concentration reduction
  * Awakening time may exceed CSHT during long, stimulating surgery
  * TCI pumps display "decrement time" rather than CSHT

![image.png](BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_pdf_extracted_pages_48-93.pdf_p33_img1_8c47a2.png)

**Bottom Line:** Context-sensitive half-time varies significantly between drugs and with infusion duration. Remifentanil's consistently short CSHT makes it ideal for long cases, while fentanyl's dramatically increasing CSHT with duration can significantly extend recovery time after prolonged administration.

# 5. PHARMACODYNAMICS





### 5.1.1. DRUG ACTION MECHANISMS

**Drugs exert their effects through various mechanisms ranging from non-specific physicochemical actions to highly specific receptor-mediated effects.**

* **Actions Dependent on Chemical Properties:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Antacids:** Neutralize gastric acid through simple chemical reaction
  * **Chelating Agents:** Reduce concentration of metallic ions
	* Dicobalt edetate chelates cyanide ions (treatment for cyanide poisoning)
	* Sugammadex (γ-cyclodextrin) selectively chelates rocuronium
* **Enzyme Actions:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Most drugs interacting with enzymes are inhibitors
  * Effects: Increased substrate concentration and decreased product concentration
  * Types:
	* Competitive inhibition (edrophonium for anticholinesterase)
	* Non-competitive inhibition
	* Irreversible inhibition (aspirin for cyclo-oxygenase, omeprazole for Na+/H+ ATPase)
  * Example: ACE inhibitors prevent conversion of angiotensin I to II and bradykinin to inactive fragments
* **Ion Channel Actions:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Target voltage-gated ion channels in excitable tissues
  * Examples:
	* Local anesthetics: Block sodium channels in nerve membrane
	* Anticonvulsants: Block similar channels in brain
	* Calcium channel blockers: Act on vascular smooth muscle channels
	* Anti-arrhythmics: Block myocardial channels
* **Receptor Interactions:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Most specific drug actions mediated through receptors
  * Discussed in detail in subsequent sections

**Bottom Line:** Drugs can act through various mechanisms ranging from simple chemical interactions to complex receptor-mediated processes. Understanding these mechanisms helps explain both therapeutic effects and side effects of medications.

### 5.1.2. DRUG-RECEPTOR INTERACTIONS

#### 5.1.2.1. BASIC PRINCIPLES
* **Binding Process:** Usually reversible, follows law of mass action [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Concentration Effect:** Higher free drug or unoccupied receptor concentration → greater tendency to form drug-receptor complex [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* $[drug] + [receptor] \leftrightarrow [drug-receptor~complex]$ [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

#### 5.1.2.2. DOSE-RESPONSE RELATIONSHIP
* **Typical Shape:** Sigmoidal [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

  ![image.png](BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_image_23.png_dcf58c.png_ed68e3.png_791eb7.png)

  * *Left Side*: Flat until drug concentration reaches threshold (minimal amount needed for measurable response) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * *Right Side*: Flat at maximum physiological response (beyond which body cannot respond) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Effect = E_0 = E_max \frac{C^\gamma}{C_50^\gamma + C^\gamma} [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Effect = E_0 + (E_max - E_0) \frac{C^\gamma}{C_50^\gamma + C^\gamma} [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Probability = P_0 + (P_max - P_0) \frac{C^\gamma}{C_50^\gamma + C^\gamma} [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Key Parameters:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **E₀**: Baseline effect in absence of drug
  * **C**: Drug concentration
  * **C₅₀**: Concentration associated with half-maximal effect (related to drug potency - highly potent drugs have low C₅₀)
  * **γ**: Steepness of concentration vs. response relationship (Hill coefficient)
	* γ < 1: Very gradual increase in effect with increasing concentration
	* γ > 4: Once effect observed, small concentration increases produce large effect increases
  * **Emax**: Maximum change from baseline or maximum physiological measurement (related to intrinsic efficacy - highly efficacious drugs have large Emax)

#### 5.1.2.3. RECEPTOR OCCUPANCY VS. EFFECT
* **Critical Concept:** Percentage of receptors occupied ≠ percentage of maximal effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Reason:** Most receptor systems have more receptors than required for maximum effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Advantages:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Extra unoccupied receptors promote faster formation of drug-receptor complexes
  * Near-maximal effects possible at very low concentrations (efficient)
  * Large margin of safety (many receptors must be antagonized before reducing effect)
* **Examples:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Neuromuscular junction: Only 20-25% of nicotinic cholinergic receptors need acetylcholine binding for complete muscle contraction
  * 75% of receptors must be blocked by nondepolarizing antagonist for significant drop in muscle strength
* 🔑 **HY:** This principle explains why neuromuscular blockers may have little effect until reaching critical threshold [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

#### 5.1.2.4. RECEPTOR ACTIVATION MECHANISMS
* Two primary schemes for agonist binding to change cellular function: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Receptor-linked membrane ion channels** (ionophores)
  * **Guanine nucleotide-binding proteins** (G-proteins)

**Bottom Line:** Drug-receptor interactions follow mathematical principles of binding and activation, with sigmoidal dose-response relationships reflecting threshold, linear, and maximal effect phases. Importantly, receptor occupancy is not linearly related to effect, with many systems showing "receptor reserve" where maximal effects occur without full receptor occupancy.

### 5.1.3. DESENSITIZATION AND DOWNREGULATION OF RECEPTORS

#### 5.1.3.1. RECEPTOR DYNAMICS
* **Basic Principle:** Receptors are dynamic cellular components [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Desensitization:** Prolonged agonist exposure → subsequent doses produce lower maximal effects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

#### 5.1.3.2. MECHANISM
* **GPCR Pathway:** Sustained cytosolic second messenger levels → [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Phosphorylation by G-protein receptor kinases
  * Arrestin-mediated blockade of coupling site needed for active G-protein complex
  * Inactivates GPCR (increased internalization, decreased replenishing)
  * Downregulation (decrease in total receptor number)
* **Reversibility:** Signals producing downregulation are reversed during constant receptor inactivity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

#### 5.1.3.3. CLINICAL EXAMPLES
* **Pheochromocytoma:** Excess catecholamines → decreased β-adrenergic receptors in cell membrane (homeostatic mechanism) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Asthma:** Prolonged β-agonist inhaler use → tachyphylaxis (downregulation of β-adrenergic receptors) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **LMN Injury:** Increased nicotinic acetylcholine receptors in NMJ → exaggerated response to **SUCCINYLCHOLINE** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Understanding receptor regulation explains why drug effects may change with prolonged use or in disease states [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Receptors undergo adaptive regulation with prolonged exposure to agonists, resulting in decreased responsiveness through desensitization (reduced receptor function) and downregulation (reduced receptor number). These mechanisms explain tolerance development with chronic drug use and altered responses in certain pathological conditions.

### 5.1.4. AGONISTS, PARTIAL AGONISTS AND ANTAGONISTS

#### 5.1.4.1. AGONISTS
* **Definition:** Drugs binding to receptors producing an effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Binding Mechanism:** Usually via ionic, hydrogen, and van der Waals interactions (reversible) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Rarely via covalent bonds (irreversible)
* **Receptor State:** Binary (either unbound or bound) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Magnitude of effect reflects total number of bound receptors
* **Potency Comparison:** Different agonists may produce same maximal effect (Emax) at different concentrations [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Agonists differing in potency but binding to same receptor have parallel concentration-response curves

#### 5.1.4.2. PARTIAL AGONISTS
* **Definition:** Drugs that bind to receptor and activate it, but less than a full agonist [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Effect Limitation:** Even at supramaximal doses, cannot cause full drug effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Dual Activity:** May also have antagonist activity (termed agonist-antagonist) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Interaction Effect:** When administered with full agonist, decreases the full agonist's effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

#### 5.1.4.3. ANTAGONISTS
* **Definition:** Drugs binding to receptor without activating it (usually reversible) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Competitive Antagonism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Increasing antagonist concentrations progressively inhibit agonist response
  * Causes rightward displacement of agonist dose-response relationship
  * Can be overcome by high agonist concentrations (competition)
* **Non-Competitive Antagonism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Occurs when antagonist binds irreversibly to receptor site or to different site with allosteric effect
  * Causes rightward shift of dose-response relationship AND decreased max efficacy
  * Reversed only by synthesis of new receptor molecules
* 🔑 **HY:** Understanding antagonist type helps predict whether increasing agonist dose can overcome the blockade [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

#### 5.1.4.4. INVERSE AGONISTS
* **Definition:** Bind at same site as agonist (likely compete) but produce opposite effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Mechanism:** "Turn off" constitutive receptor activity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Drug-receptor interactions include full agonists (produce maximal effects), partial agonists (limited efficacy), antagonists (block receptor without activating), and inverse agonists (reverse constitutive activity). Understanding these interactions is crucial for predicting drug behavior, especially when multiple agents compete for the same receptors.

### 5.1.5. DOSE-RESPONSE RELATIONSHIPS

#### 5.1.5.1. BASIC PATTERN
* **Relationship:** Curvilinear between dose and response intensity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Low dose: Little pharmacologic effect
  * Beyond threshold: Small dose increases cause large effect changes
  * High dose: Near-maximal response means little effect change

#### 5.1.5.2. KEY METRICS FROM DOSE-RESPONSE CURVE
* **Potency (ED₅₀):** Dose required to produce effect in 50% of subjects [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Curve Slope** (between 20-80% of maximal effect): Reflects receptor affinity for drug [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Efficacy:** Maximum effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Population Variability:** Multiple curves from different subjects show variability in potency, efficacy, and affinity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Dose-response relationships characterize how increasing drug doses affect response magnitude, with key parameters including potency (ED50), efficacy (maximum effect), and slope (rate of effect change). Individual variability in these parameters underlies differences in patient responses to the same drug doses.

### 5.1.6. CONCENTRATION-RESPONSE RELATIONSHIPS

#### 5.1.6.1. CONCENTRATION DEFINITION
* **Ideal Measurement:** Concentration at site of action [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Practical Substitute:** Concentration in blood [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

#### 5.1.6.2. INFUSION DYNAMICS
* **Constant Rate Infusion:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Plasma concentration initially increases rapidly, asymptotically approaches steady-state after ~5 elimination half-lives
  * Effect initially increases slowly, then rapidly, then reaches steady state
* **Discontinuation:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Time lag between plasma concentration and pharmacologic response changes (temporal disequilibrium)
* **Disequilibrium Factors:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Target organ perfusion
  * Tissue:blood partition coefficient
  * Rate of diffusion from blood to cellular action site
  * Rate/affinity of drug-receptor binding
  * Time for processes initiated by interaction to produce cellular function changes

#### 5.1.6.3. STEADY-STATE RELATIONSHIP
* **Key Principle:** Plasma concentration has unvarying relationship with pharmacologic effect only under steady-state conditions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Measurement:** CPss₅₀ (steady-state plasma concentration producing 50% of maximal response) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Concentration-response relationships describe the correlation between drug concentration and effect, which is most reliable under steady-state conditions. Time lags between plasma concentration changes and observed effects reflect various physiological processes including distribution to the effect site.

### 5.1.7. COMBINED PK-PD MODELS

#### 5.1.7.1. EFFECT COMPARTMENT CONCEPT
![image.png](BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_BASIC_PHARMACOLOGY_image_25.png_28afd3.png_29f80e.png_fbc41f.png)

* **Approach:** Add hypothetical "effect compartment" (biophase) to standard compartmental PK model [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Assumption:** Effect and central compartment transfer is first-order [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Model Type:** "Virtual" - not actually receiving/returning drug [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Key Parameter:** T₁/₂ke₀ (time for effect site concentration to reach 50% of plasma concentration when plasma concentration is held constant) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Shorter T₁/₂ke₀ means more rapid equilibration and faster effect after initiation
  * Decline in effect site concentration still more dependent on body PK than biophase-plasma equilibration speed
* T_1/2k_{c0} = \frac{\ln 2}{k_c0} = \frac{0.693}{k_c0} [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* 🔑 **HY:** T₁/₂ke₀ explains why some drugs have rapid onset despite similar distribution volumes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Combined PK-PD models incorporate an "effect compartment" to account for the delay between plasma concentration and drug effect. The rate constant ke0 characterizes this delay and explains differences in onset time between drugs, even those with similar distribution volumes.

### 5.1.8. THERAPEUTIC THRESHOLDS AND THERAPEUTIC WINDOW

* **Primary Factors:** Effect site concentration profile (PK/PD) AND minimum concentration for discernible drug effect (therapeutic threshold) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Threshold Dependence:** Magnitude of desired effect or intensity of stimulus treated [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Example: Superficial procedures (less painful) vs. orthopedic procedures (more painful) - different **FENTANYL** doses for analgesia

Understanding this concept is essential for balancing efficacy and safety in drug dosing.

### 5.1.9. RECEPTOR TYPES AND SIGNAL TRANSDUCTION

**Receptors can be classified into three main types based on their mechanism of action: ion channel receptors, receptors using intermediate messengers, and those regulating gene transcription.**

#### 5.1.9.1. ALTERED ION PERMEABILITY
* **Basic Concept:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Part of membrane-spanning protein complex forming potential channel
  * Ligand binding causes conformational change opening channel
  * Allows ion passage down concentration/electrical gradients
  * Term "ionotropic" used for these receptors

##### 5.1.9.1.1. THE PENTAMERIC FAMILY
* **Structure:** Five membrane-spanning subunits [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Nicotinic ACh Receptor:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Contains one β, one ε, one δ, and two α subunits
  * Two ACh molecules bind to α subunits
  * Results in rapid Na+ flux through ion channel
  * Selective for small cations but not specific for Na+
* **GABAA Receptor:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Typically 2α, 2β, 1γ subunits
  * Binding site for benzodiazepines at α-γ interface
  * Also has binding sites for propofol, etomidate, volatiles (β subunits)
  * Chloride-selective ion channel formation leads to membrane hyperpolarization
* **5-HT3 Receptor:** Only serotonin receptor acting through ion channel opening [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

##### 5.1.9.1.2. IONOTROPIC GLUTAMATE
* **Structure:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * NMDA receptor has two subunits: one pore-forming (NR1) and one regulatory (NR2, binds co-activator glycine)
  * In vivo receptors likely dimerize forming four-subunit complex
  * Each NR1 subunit has three membrane-spanning helices separated by re-entrant pore-forming loop
* **Properties:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Equally permeable to Na+ and K+
  * Highly permeable to Ca2+
* **Drug Actions:** Ketamine, xenon, nitrous oxide are non-competitive antagonists [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

##### 5.1.9.1.3. IONOTROPIC PURINERGIC RECEPTORS
* **Members:** PX1 and PX2 families [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Structure:** Two membrane-spanning helices, no pore-forming loops [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Properties:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Form cationic channels equally permeable to Na+ and K+
  * Also permeable to Ca2+
* **Activation:** By ATP [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Function:** Involved in mechanosensation and pain [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Distinction:** Different from G-protein coupled purinergic receptors (selective for adenosine or ATP) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

#### 5.1.9.2. PRODUCTION OF INTERMEDIATE MESSENGERS
**Membrane-bound systems that transduce external signals into intracellular responses via secondary messengers.**

##### 5.1.9.2.1. G-PROTEIN COUPLED RECEPTORS AND G-PROTEINS
* **GPCR Structure:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Membrane-bound proteins with serpentine structure
  * Seven helical regions traversing membrane
* **G-protein Structure:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Heterotrimeric proteins (α, β, γ subunits)
  * Associated with inner leaflet of cell membrane
  * Universal transducers converting extracellular stimulus to intracellular change
* **Activation Mechanism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Ligand binding causes conformational change
  * Increases GPCR-G-protein coupling probability
  * GTP replaces GDP on α subunit
  * α-GTP dissociates from βγ dimer
  * α-GTP activates/inhibits effector protein (enzyme or ion channel)
  * α subunit acts as GTPase, eventually regenerating inactive α-GDP
* **Signal Amplification:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Single activated GPCR can stimulate multiple G-proteins
  * Each G-protein can activate several intermediate messengers
  * G-protein:GPCR ratio ≈ 100:1 (enables amplification)
* **G-protein Types:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Gs:** α subunit activates adenylyl cyclase
  * **Gi:** α subunit inhibits adenylyl cyclase
  * **Gq:** α subunit activates phospholipase C
* **Regulation Mechanism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Phosphorylation at intracellular carboxyl-terminal
  * β-arrestin binding signals receptor removal from membrane
  * Agonist binding may increase phosphorylation (self-regulation)
  * Explains tachyphylaxis with β-adrenergic agonists
* **Second Messenger Pathways:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **cAMP:** Formed by adenylyl cyclase (Gs-activated)
	* Stimulates protein kinase A (two regulatory, two catalytic units)
	* cAMP binds to regulatory unit, revealing active catalytic unit
	* May cause protein synthesis, gene activation, ion permeability changes
	* Broken down by phosphodiesterases (PDE)
	* PDE III most important in heart (target for milrinone)
  * **Phospholipase C:** Activated by Gq
	* Breaks down membrane lipid PIP2 to IP3 and DAG
	* IP3 causes calcium release from endoplasmic reticulum
	* DAG activates protein kinase C
	* Increased calcium acts as trigger for many cellular events
  * **Receptor Examples:**
	* β-adrenergic effects mediated via Gs
	* Opioid effects via Gi
	* α1-adrenoceptors, muscarinic types 1,3,5, angiotensin II type 1 via Gq

##### 5.1.9.2.2. MEMBRANE GUANYLYL CYCLASE
* **Mechanism:** Some hormones (atrial natriuretic peptide) bind to receptors with intrinsic guanylyl cyclase activity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Effect:** Increases cGMP levels, acting as secondary messenger via phosphorylation of intracellular enzymes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Nitric Oxide Mechanism:** Increases cGMP by stimulating cytosolic (not membrane-bound) guanylyl cyclase [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

##### 5.1.9.2.3. MEMBRANE TYROSINE KINASE
* **Applications:** Insulin, growth factors act through this system [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Insulin Receptor Structure:** Two α and two β subunits, latter spanning cell membrane [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Activation Mechanism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Ligand binds to α subunits
  * Intracellular tyrosine residues on β subunits phosphorylated
  * Activates tyrosine kinase activity
  * Catalyzes phosphorylation of other protein targets
  * Generates multiple insulin effects
* **Effects Include:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Intracellular metabolic changes
  * Insertion of glucose transport protein into cell membrane
  * Gene transcription actions

#### 5.1.9.3. REGULATION OF GENE TRANSCRIPTION
**Intracellular receptors that alter expression of DNA and RNA, indirectly changing protein production.**

##### 5.1.9.3.1. ADRENOSTEROID HORMONES
* **Receptor Types:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Mineralocorticoid receptor (MR)
  * Glucocorticoid receptor (GR)
* **Distribution:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * GR: Widespread in cells, including liver
  * MR: Restricted to epithelial tissue (renal collecting tubules, colon)
  * Epithelial cells contain both MR and GR receptors
* **Selective Activation:** MR activation occurs due to 11-β hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase, which converts cortisol to cortisone (inactive at GR receptor) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

##### 5.1.9.3.2. OTHER NUCLEAR RECEPTORS
* **Example:** Pioglitazone (antidiabetic) is agonist at peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Function:** Controls protein transcription associated with increased insulin sensitivity in adipose tissue [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Receptors function through three main mechanisms: altered ion permeability (for rapid responses), production of intermediate messengers (for signal amplification), and regulation of gene transcription (for longer-term effects). These diverse mechanisms explain the varied time courses and effects observed with different drug classes.

### 5.1.10. DRUG-RECEPTOR BINDING DYNAMICS

**Mathematical description of how drugs bind to and interact with receptors.**

* **Basic Reaction:** L + R ↔ LR [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * L = ligand, R = receptor, LR = ligand-receptor complex
* **Forward Reaction Rate:** V1 = k1 · [L] · [R] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * k1 = rate constant for forward reaction
* **Reverse Reaction Rate:** V2 = k2 · [LR] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * k2 = rate constant for reverse reaction
* **Equilibrium State:** V1 = V2 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Equilibrium Dissociation Constant:** KD = [L] · [R]/[LR] = k2/k1 [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Reciprocal (KA) is equilibrium association constant
  * KD units: mmol·L⁻¹; KA units: L·mmol⁻¹
* **Receptor Conformations:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Active and inactive forms in equilibrium
  * Without agonist: equilibrium favors inactive form
  * Antagonists: bind equally to both forms, don't alter equilibrium
  * Agonists: push equilibrium toward active conformation
  * Active conformation triggers molecular events resulting in observed effect

#### 5.1.10.1. TYPES OF DRUG-RECEPTOR INTERACTION
* **Key Properties:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Affinity:** How avidly drug binds to receptor (like key fitting lock)
	* Determined by KD or KA
  * **Intrinsic Activity (IA):** Magnitude of effect once bound
	* Value between 0 and 1 (or -1 to 0 for inverse agonists)
* **Drug Categories Based on Properties:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Agonist:** Significant affinity and full intrinsic activity (IA = 1)
  * **Antagonist:** Significant affinity but no intrinsic activity (IA = 0)
  * **Partial Agonist:** Significant affinity but fractional intrinsic activity (0 < IA < 1)
  * **Inverse Agonist:** Can be full or partial with negative intrinsic activity (-1 ≤ IA < 0)

#### 5.1.10.2. RECEPTOR AGONISM
**Full Agonists** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Generate maximal response from receptor
* High affinity and high intrinsic activity
* Potency determined by KD (lower KD = higher potency)
* For many drugs, ED50 corresponds to KD

**Partial Agonists** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Intrinsic activity less than 1
* Occupy receptors but produce submaximal effect compared to full agonist
* Cannot achieve maximal effect even with full receptor occupancy
* Example: Buprenorphine at μ-opioid receptor
* Dual effects:
  * Alone: Act as agonists
  * With full agonist:
	* Additive at low full agonist doses
	* Competitive antagonism at high full agonist doses
* Never push equilibrium entirely toward active conformation

**Inverse Agonists** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Bind and exert effect opposite to endogenous agonist
* Mechanism: Related to constitutive receptor activity
  * Some receptors show low activity even without ligand
  * Inverse agonists reduce active conformation probability
  * Appear to have opposite effect to agonist
* Difference from competitive antagonist:
  * Inverse agonist: Shifts equilibrium toward inactive receptors
  * Competitive antagonist: Binds equally to active/inactive forms, prevents agonist binding
* Example: Ketanserin (inverse agonist at 5HT2c receptors)

#### 5.1.10.3. RECEPTOR ANTAGONISM
**General Properties** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Exhibit affinity but no intrinsic activity
* Binding may be reversible or irreversible

**Reversible Antagonism** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Competitive Antagonists:**
  * Compete with agonist for same binding site
  * Effect can be overcome by increasing agonist concentration
  * Log[dose] vs. response curve shifted right along x-axis
  * Shift extent known as dose ratio
  * pA2 value: negative logarithm of antagonist concentration producing dose ratio of 2
  * Examples:
	* Non-depolarizing muscle relaxants competing with acetylcholine
	* β-blockers competing with noradrenaline
  * Bowman principle: Weaker antagonists have more rapid onset due to higher doses creating faster receptor occupancy
* **Non-competitive Antagonists:**
  * Don't bind to same site as agonist
  * Don't alter agonist binding
  * Prevent receptor activation through conformational distortion
  * Cannot be overcome by increasing agonist concentration
  * Example: Ketamine as NMDA receptor antagonist
  * Modern classification: Often grouped with negative allosteric modulators

**Allosteric Modulation** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Drugs binding to sites distant from agonist receptor site
* Alter agonist binding characteristics
* **Negative Allosteric Modulators:** Reduce agonist activity
* **Positive Allosteric Modulators:** Enhance agonist activity without own effects
* Example: Benzodiazepines as positive allosteric modulators of GABA at GABAA receptor

**Irreversible Antagonism** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* Bind irreversibly to same site as agonist or distant site
* Increasing agonist concentration cannot overcome blockade
* Examples:
  * Phenoxybenzamine: Irreversibly binds to and antagonizes catecholamines at α-adrenoceptors
  * Clopidogrel: Prodrug with active metabolite binding irreversibly to Gi-protein coupled ADP PY2Y12 receptors

**Bottom Line:** The interaction between drugs and receptors follows mathematical principles determining affinity and efficacy. Understanding the different types of interactions (agonism, antagonism, allosteric modulation) is essential for predicting drug effects and interactions in clinical settings.

### 5.1.11. SPARE RECEPTORS

**The neuromuscular junction contains an excess of acetylcholine receptors beyond what is needed for maximal response.**

* **Basic Concept:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Only fraction of NMJ receptors need occupation for maximal effect
  * Small quantity of ACh produces maximal response
  * "Spare receptors" provide protection against transmission failure
* **Demonstration Method:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Small dose of irreversible inhibitor → portion of receptors unavailable
  * Log[dose] vs. response curve shifts right (higher fraction of remaining receptors needed)
  * Further irreversible antagonist → additional right-shift
  * When >75% receptors occupied by irreversible antagonist:
	* Maximum response cannot occur regardless of ACh dose
	* Curve shape changes (reduced maximum response and slope)

**Bottom Line:** Many receptor systems contain "spare receptors" that provide a safety margin, allowing maximal responses even when a significant portion of receptors are unavailable. This explains the need for substantial receptor blockade before clinical effects become apparent with some drugs.

### 5.1.12. TACHYPHYLAXIS, DESENSITIZATION AND TOLERANCE

**Repeated drug administration may lead to changes in pharmacological response over time.**

#### 5.1.12.1. TACHYPHYLAXIS
* **Definition:** Rapid decrease in response to repeated doses over short time period [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Common Mechanism:** Depletion of transmitter stores before resynthesis can occur [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Example:** Diminishing response to repeated ephedrine doses due to noradrenaline depletion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

#### 5.1.12.2. DESENSITIZATION
* **Definition:** Chronic loss of response over longer period [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Mechanisms:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Structural change in receptor morphology
  * Absolute loss of receptor numbers
* **Usage Note:** Often used synonymously with tachyphylaxis [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Example:** Loss of β-adrenergic receptors from myocardial cell surface with continued adrenaline/dobutamine exposure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

#### 5.1.12.3. TOLERANCE
* **Definition:** Phenomenon where larger doses required to produce same pharmacological effect [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Example:** Chronic opioid use or abuse [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Mechanism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Altered CNS sensitivity to opioids
  * May involve reduction in receptor density or affinity
* **Other Example:** Nitrate tolerance with continuous infusion [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Due to depletion of vascular smooth muscle sulfhydryl groups
  * Managed with "drug holiday" (few hours overnight) allowing replenishment

**Bottom Line:** Repeated drug exposure can lead to diminished responses through multiple mechanisms, including neurotransmitter depletion (tachyphylaxis), receptor changes (desensitization), and altered cellular sensitivity (tolerance). Understanding these phenomena helps explain the need for dose adjustments and dosing strategies for chronic medications.

# 6. DRUG INTERACTIONS





### 6.1.1. PHARMACEUTICAL (PHYSICOCHEMICAL) INTERACTIONS

**Chemical or physical incompatibilities between pharmaceutical preparations.**

#### 6.1.1.1. IN-VITRO INTERACTIONS
* **Mechanism:** Drug-drug interactions (DDIs) due to physicochemical properties [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Clinical Effect:** Can alter bioavailability and produce toxic byproducts [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].
* **Examples:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Insoluble salt precipitation when acidic drugs (**THIOPENTAL**) and basic drugs (opioids, muscle relaxants) mix in IV tubing with insufficient flow rate
  * Commercial local anesthetic solutions with epinephrine have lower pH than fresh mixtures due to antioxidant stabilizers (sodium bisulfite/pyrosulfite), making them seem less efficacious
  * Compound A formation (by degradation of strong base in CO2 absorbent) - FDA recommends sevoflurane exposure not exceed 2 MAC-hours with FGF 1-2 L/min
  * CO formation from desflurane/isoflurane degradation with CO2 absorbent → led to Amsorb development (CaOH lime only, no Na/KOH)
  * Insulin denatured in dextrose solutions
  * Glyceryl trinitrate absorbed by polyvinyl chloride (requires special polyethylene sets)
* ⚠️ **Warning:** Always check compatibility before mixing drugs in the same IV line [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].

#### 6.1.1.2. IN-VIVO INTERACTIONS
* **Application:** Used to develop NMBA antagonists (e.g., **SUGAMMADEX**) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Mechanism:** Physical chelation or binding of drug molecules [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Pharmaceutical interactions occur due to physicochemical properties of drugs, either in vitro (mixing incompatibilities in IV lines) or in vivo (physical binding of drugs). These interactions can be harmful (precipitation of thiopental with acidic drugs) or therapeutically exploited (sugammadex binding with rocuronium).

### 6.1.2. PHARMACOKINETIC INTERACTIONS

**Alterations in absorption, distribution, metabolism, or excretion of one drug by another.**

#### 6.1.2.1. BASIC CONCEPT
* **Definition:** Alteration of ADME by other drugs [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

#### 6.1.2.2. ABSORPTION (UPTAKE) INTERACTIONS
* **GI Absorption:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * **RANITIDINE**: Alters gastric pH
  * **METOCLOPRAMIDE**: Increases gastric emptying speed
  * Both affect oral drug absorption
  * Other mechanisms: Direct binding (charcoal adsorbs drugs in stomach)
  * P-glycoprotein effects: Intestinal transport protein affecting bioavailability
	* Inducers (rifampicin, phenytoin) reduce drug bioavailability
	* Inhibitors (amiodarone, verapamil) increase bioavailability
* **Local Absorption:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Vasoconstrictors: Decrease local blood flow and systemic uptake (e.g., in LA use)
  * Benefits: Prolonged duration of action, decreased systemic toxicity risk
* **Pulmonary Absorption:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * Vasoactive agents: Alter V/Q ratio → changed pulmonary uptake of volatile anesthetics

#### 6.1.2.3. DISTRIBUTION INTERACTIONS
* **Mechanism:** DDIs change blood exposure to tissue for drug uptake/clearance or amount available for tissue uptake [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Cardiac Output Effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Decreased cardiac output (β-blockers): Reduced blood flow carrying absorbed drug to site of action
  * Affects timing of drug onset (e.g., suxamethonium fasciculation onset dependent on cardiac output)
  * May disproportionately reduce hepatic blood flow, slowing elimination
* **Protein Binding:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]]
  * For most IV anesthetics, protein binding changes have minimal clinical impact
  * Exceptions requiring dose adjustment: Drugs that are extensively protein-bound, have high hepatic extraction ratio, and low therapeutic index
  * *Limiting Factors:* [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
	* Unoccupied binding sites vastly outnumber anesthetic drug molecules in clinical practice
	* Even with significant displacement, liver can metabolize sudden influx, restoring free drug concentration
  * Important primarily for highly protein-bound drugs when enzyme systems near saturation (e.g., phenytoin)
  * Example: Amiodarone inhibiting S-warfarin metabolism by CYP2C9 (often mistaken for protein binding effect)
* **Chelation:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Therapeutic applications:
	* Sodium calcium edetate chelates lead (treatment for lead poisoning)
	* Dicobalt edetate chelates cyanide (treatment for cyanide poisoning)

* **Hepatic Blood Flow:** Drugs altering flow proportionately alter metabolism of drugs with flow-limited clearance [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Enzyme Effects:** Drugs that inhibit or induce CYP enzymes change metabolism rates [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].
* 🔑 **HY:** Recognizing common enzyme inhibitors/inducers helps prevent clinically significant drug interactions [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].

  * Used therapeutically in aspirin overdose (fluid diuresis plus bicarbonate)

**Bottom Line:** Pharmacokinetic interactions alter absorption, distribution, metabolism, or excretion processes. Metabolism interactions through enzyme induction or inhibition are particularly important in anesthesia practice, with common examples including opioid-CYP3A4 inhibitor interactions that can prolong opioid effects.

### 6.1.3. PHARMACODYNAMIC INTERACTIONS

**Alterations in the pharmacological effect of one drug by another at the site of action.**

#### 6.1.3.1. INTERACTION TYPES
* **Direct:** At receptor level (e.g., opioid antagonist at receptor) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Examples: Flumazenil reversing benzodiazepines, naloxone reversing opioids
  * Clinical use: Treating drug overdose
* **Indirect:** Through related mechanisms (e.g., cholinesterase inhibitor in NMBA reversal) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
  * Example: Neostigmine inhibits acetylcholinesterase, increasing acetylcholine to compete with NDMRs at receptors
* **Clinical Application:** Allows for synergism (e.g., hypnotics and opioids) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* 💡 **Clinical Pearl:** Synergistic interactions allow reduced doses of individual drugs [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Important Interaction Example:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Phenylephrine ineffectiveness in patients taking α1-adrenergic blockers for BPH
  * Affected drugs: Alfuzosin, doxazosin, indoramin, prazosin, tamsulosin, terazosin

#### 6.1.3.2. SEROTONIN SYNDROME
* **Symptoms:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * CNS: Confusion, hyperactivity, memory problems
  * Physical: Muscle twitching, fever, shivering, excessive sweating
* **Triggers:** MAOIs, SSRIs, SNRIs [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * High-risk combinations:
	* Meperidine + MAOIs
	* Methylene blue + phenylpiperidine opioids (**FENTANYL**, **METHADONE**, **MEPERIDINE**, **TRAMADOL**)
* **Management Considerations:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * Many SSRIs have long elimination half-lives (>4 weeks' cessation needed)
  * Risk of withholding these drugs (worsening depression/neuropathic pain) often outweighs benefits
  * When adequate washout impossible and methylene blue needed: Don't reinstate serotonergic drug for 24 hours after last methylene blue dose
* **Treatment:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]
  * **CYPROHEPTADINE**: Only PO formulation available
  * IV **CHLORPROMAZINE**: Selective serotonin antagonist
  * Supportive care
* ⚠️ **Warning:** This is a potentially life-threatening condition requiring immediate recognition and treatment [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].

#### 6.1.3.3. INTERACTION PATTERNS
* **Summation:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

  * Each drug has independent action in absence of the other
  * Example: Midazolam and propofol at induction (reduces propofol needed)
* **Potentiation:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

  * Example: Probenecid reduces renal excretion of penicillin (enhances effect without antibiotic activity)
* **Synergism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Combined action greater than expected from purely additive effect
  * Often due to similar effects through different mechanisms
  * Example: Propofol and remifentanil in anesthesia maintenance

### 6.1.4. SUMMATION, POTENTIATION, AND SYNERGISM

**Patterns of combined drug effects that can be intentionally used or avoided in anesthetic practice.**

* **Milrinone + Adrenaline:** Positive inotropic synergism via different mechanisms (PDE inhibition and β-adrenergic stimulation) both increasing cAMP [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Ramipril + β-blockers:** Additive blood pressure lowering through different systems (renin-angiotensin and adrenoceptors) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Diuretics + Digoxin:** Indirect interaction where diuretic-induced hypokalemia may cause digoxin toxicity [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[MMed_Anaesthesiology_Part_B_Syllabus#C2 Sedation and General Anaesthesia|ⓘ]].
* **Less-than-additive Effects:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Some combinations produce less effect than predicted
  * Example: ACE inhibitor + angiotensin II receptor antagonist
  * Clinical utility: May help minimize adverse reactions while maintaining efficacy

**Bottom Line:** Patterns of drug interactions include summation (additive effects), potentiation (enhancement without independent action), and synergism (greater-than-additive effects). Understanding these patterns helps clinicians optimize therapeutic regimens while minimizing adverse effects.

### 6.1.5. ISOBOLOGRAM CONCEPT

**Graphical method for analyzing drug interactions and determining whether combinations are additive, synergistic, or antagonistic.**

* **Definition:** Graph describing combined effect of two different drugs [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Basic Setup:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Two drugs A and B producing required effect at concentrations a and b mmol·L⁻¹ respectively
  * Plot fractional concentrations of each on xand y-axes
  * Construct curve showing concentration combinations producing target effect
* **Interpretation:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Straight Line:** Pure additive effect (half concentrations of each combine to produce original target effect)
  * **Concave Curve:** Synergism (less than proportional concentrations needed)
  * **Convex Curve:** Antagonistic interaction (more than proportional concentrations needed)

# 7. ISOMERISM





### 7.1.1. STRUCTURAL ISOMERISM

**Molecules with identical chemical formulae but different structural arrangements of atoms.**

* **Static Structural Isomers:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Same formula, different structure
  * Includes positional, chain, or functional group isomers
  * Example: Isoflurane/enflurane
  * Clinical effects may range from identical to markedly different
  * Examples with different actions:
	* Isoprenaline (β-adrenoceptor activity) vs. methoxamine (α-adrenoceptor activity)
	* Prednisolone (glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid) vs. aldosterone (predominantly mineralocorticoid)
	* Dihydrocodeine vs. dobutamine (coincidentally identical formulae with very different effects)
* **Dynamic Structural Isomers:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Tautomerism:** Dynamic interchange between two forms
  * Environment-triggered changes (pH, physical conditions)
  * Examples:
	* Midazolam: Ionized at pH 4, forms lipid-soluble seven-membered unionized ring at pH 7.4 (increases BBB passage)
	* Keto-enol transformation in morphine and thiopental

**Bottom Line:** Structural isomers have the same chemical formula but different atomic arrangements, resulting in potential differences in pharmacological effects. Dynamic isomerism like tautomerism can result in environment-dependent changes to drug properties that may affect clinical use.

### 7.1.2. STEREOISOMERISM

**Molecules with the same chemical formula and bond structure but different three-dimensional configurations.**

#### 7.1.2.1. GEOMETRIC ISOMERISM
* **Definition:** Exists when dissimilar groups are attached to atoms linked by double bond or ring structure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Conformations:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Cis-:** Groups on same side of plane (double bond or ring)
  * **Trans-:** Groups on opposite sides
* **Examples:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Bis-benzylisoquinolinium muscle relaxants (mivacurium)
  * Cisatracurium: Cis-cis configuration (one of ten stereoisomers of atracurium)
  * Mivacurium: Contains three geometric isomers - trans-trans (58%), cis-trans (36%), cis-cis (6%)

#### 7.1.2.2. OPTICAL STEREOISOMERS
* **Chiral Center Definition:** Carbon atom or quaternary nitrogen surrounded by four different chemical groups [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Arrangement:** Bonds point to vertices of tetrahedron creating mirror image conformations that cannot be superimposed [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

##### 7.1.2.2.1. SINGLE CHIRAL CENTRE
* **Classification Systems:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * **Historical:** Direction of polarized light rotation (dextro/laevo, d/l, +/-)
  * **Modern:** Absolute configuration (R/S) based on atomic number arrangement
	* R (rectus): Atomic numbers descend clockwise
	* S (sinister): Atomic numbers descend counterclockwise
* **Clinical Significance:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Receptor-mediated actions highly dependent on conformation
  * Physicochemical properties less affected by isomerism
* **Examples with Single Chiral Center:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * S-warfarin and R-warfarin
  * S-ketamine and R-ketamine
  * S-etomidate and R-etomidate
  * S-isoflurane and R-isoflurane
  * S-halothane and R-halothane
  * S-bupivacaine and R-bupivacaine
  * S-ropivacaine and R-ropivacaine

##### 7.1.2.2.2. DIASTEREOISOMERS
* **Definition:** Multiple stereoisomers with more than one chiral center that are not mirror images [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Mathematical Formula:** With n chiral centers, n² stereoisomers possible [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Exception:** Internal symmetry may reduce actual number of distinct structures [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Example:** Atracurium - 4 chiral centers, 16 theoretical isomers, but only 10 distinct structures due to symmetry [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].

**Bottom Line:** Stereoisomerism significantly affects drug properties, particularly for receptor-mediated actions. Understanding these relationships helps explain differences in potency, efficacy, and side effect profiles between isomers of the same drug.

### 7.1.3. DRUG PREPARATIONS

**Different pharmaceutical preparations of drugs may contain single isomers or mixtures depending on synthesis and therapeutic goals.**

#### 7.1.3.1. DRUGS WITHOUT ISOMERIC FORMS
* No chiral center or structural features giving rise to geometric isomers [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* Examples: [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Sevoflurane
  * Propofol
  * Dopamine

#### 7.1.3.2. RACEMIC MIXTURES
* **Definition:** Equal proportions of enantiomers in drugs with single chiral center [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Examples:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Isoflurane
  * Warfarin
  * Bupivacaine
  * Ketamine
  * Adrenaline
* **Natural vs. Synthetic:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Natural molecules typically exist as single isomers (enzymatic specificity)
  * Artificial synthesis often produces racemic mixtures
  * Purification may result in racemization of natural agents (e.g., atropine)
* **Clinical Significance:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Equal amounts of isomers may have unequal contributions to activity
  * One isomer may cause undesirable toxicity or side effects

#### 7.1.3.3. ENANTIOPURE PREPARATIONS
* **Definition:** Drug containing only single isomer selected for desirable properties [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Examples:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * R-etomidate
  * S-bupivacaine (levobupivacaine)
  * S-ropivacaine
  * S-ketamine
  * S-ketoprofen (dexketoprofen)
  * S-medetomidine (dexmedetomidine)
  * R-noradrenaline

#### 7.1.3.4. MIXTURES OF MULTIPLE ISOMERS
* **Definition:** Drugs with complex carbon structures having multiple isomeric forms [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Content:** May contain all or only some possible isomers depending on synthetic processes [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]].
* **Examples:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Vecuronium
  * Pancuronium
  * Atracurium
  * Ephedrine
  * Morphine
  * Fentanyl
  * Remifentanil

**Bottom Line:** Drug formulations vary in isomeric composition from single enantiomers to racemic mixtures or complex combinations. The selection of specific isomers or mixtures is based on therapeutic advantages, manufacturing considerations, and safety profiles.

### 8.1.3. PHARMACOGENETICS

**Genetic variations affecting individual responses to medications.**

* **Definition:** How genetic variation affects drug response [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]].
* **Clinical Examples:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]] [[mmed_anaes_part_c_syllabus#1. Medical Knowledge|ⓘ]]

## 9.1. BUILDING BLOCKS


**Organic compounds forming the foundation of drugs and physiological structures.**

### 9.1.1. AMINO ACIDS

* **Basic Structure:** Hydrocarbon group with both carboxyl and amine group attached to end carbon (α-carbon) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Types:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * 20 common α-amino acids form protein building blocks (5 are essential)
  * Some amino acids are neurotransmitter precursors (e.g., phenylalanine → tyrosine → catecholamines)
  * Others are direct neurotransmitters (glycine, glutamate)
  * GABA (γ-amino butyric acid): Important inhibitory neurotransmitter with amine and carboxyl groups on opposite ends of butyl backbone

**Bottom Line:** Amino acids are fundamental building blocks for proteins and important neurotransmitters. Understanding their structure helps explain the pharmacology of drugs targeting amino acid pathways and receptors.

### 9.1.2. NUCLEIC ACIDS, NUCLEOSIDES AND NUCLEOTIDES

* **Nucleic Acids:** Purines (adenine, guanine) or pyrimidines (cytosine, uracil, thymine) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Nucleosides:** Nucleic acid + sugar (usually ribose) [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Examples: Adenosine, guanosine
* **Nucleotides:** Nucleosides + phosphate group; building blocks of DNA/RNA [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Pharmacological Significance:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Many anti-cancer drugs are nucleic acid or nucleotide analogs
  * Nucleosides combine with high-energy phosphate groups as enzyme cofactors
  * Important in cellular metabolic processes

**Bottom Line:** Nucleic acids, nucleosides, and nucleotides are essential components of genetic material and metabolic processes. Drugs targeting these structures include important anticancer and antiviral agents.

### 9.1.3. SUGARS

* **Chemical Formula:** (CH2O)n [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Types Based on Carbon Number:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Triose (n=3): Glyceraldehyde
  * Tetrose (n=4)
  * Pentose (n=5): Ribose
  * Hexose (n=6): Glucose
* **Properties:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Naturally occurring carbohydrates
  * Pentoses and hexoses exist in cyclic forms in vivo
  * Glucose metabolized to CO2 and water through oxidative tissue respiration

**Bottom Line:** Sugars provide energy for cellular function and serve as structural components in many biological molecules including nucleic acids. Their chemistry affects how carbohydrate-containing drugs are absorbed and metabolized.

## 9.2. DRUGS AND THEIR STRUCTURES


**Chemical structures of major drug classes used in anesthesia.**

### 9.2.1. CATECHOLAMINES AND DERIVATIVES

* **Origin:** Derived from amino acid tyrosine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Synthesis:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Hydroxylation (addition of -OH)
  * Decarboxylation (removal of -COOH)
* **Structure:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Ethylamine side chain (two carbons + amine group)
  * α-carbon bound to amine group
  * β-carbon linked to catechol ring
* **Activity Determinants:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Size/nature of functional groups on terminal amine and α-carbon determine α/β selectivity
  * Catechol structure (two adjacent -OH groups) required for COMT metabolism
  * Primary amines preferred substrates for MAO (though adrenaline, a secondary amine, is also metabolized)

**Bottom Line:** Catecholamine structure directly relates to receptor selectivity, with slight modifications determining whether a compound acts primarily at α or β receptors. Structural features also determine metabolic pathways and duration of action.

### 9.2.2. BARBITURATES

* **Formation:** Condensation reaction between malonic acid and urea creates barbituric acid [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Properties:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Weak acids with imino groups
  * Thio barbiturates replace keto group (=O) with thio group (=S)
* **Structural Features:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Hydrocarbon groups on 5-carbon determine duration of action
  * Oxybarbiturates undergo less hepatic metabolism than thiobarbiturates

**Bottom Line:** Barbiturate structure determines their pharmacokinetic properties, with specific substitutions affecting metabolism rate and duration of action. Thiobarbiturates (like thiopental) generally have faster hepatic metabolism than oxybarbiturates.

### 9.2.3. BENZODIAZEPINES

* **Core Structure:** Heterocyclic compounds with sixand seven-membered rings; some have five-membered ring [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Solubility:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Carbon-containing ring structures make BDZs poorly water-soluble
  * Special formulations needed (e.g., diazemuls for diazepam)
  * Tautomeric forms can enhance water solubility (e.g., midazolam at acidic pH)
* **Categories:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * 1,4-BDZs: Diazepam, temazepam, lorazepam
  * Heterocyclic BDZs: Midazolam
  * 1,5-BDZs: Clobazam
* **Metabolism:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Some are metabolically related (diazepam → oxazepam and temazepam)

**Bottom Line:** Benzodiazepine structure affects water solubility, with drugs like midazolam utilizing pH-dependent tautomerism to enhance solubility in solution while maintaining lipophilicity at physiological pH. Structural differences also influence metabolism pathways and duration of action.

### 9.2.4. NON-DEPOLARIZING MUSCLE RELAXANTS

* **Bis-benzylisoquinoliniums:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Two isoquinolinium structures linked through carbon chain containing two ester linkages
  * Related to papaverine (smooth muscle relaxant)
  * Distance between quaternary nitrogens: ~1 nm
  * Atracurium: Reverse orientation of ester linkages favors Hofmann degradation
  * Mivacurium: Ester linkage orientation prevents Hofmann degradation
  * Multiple isomers exist with differing activity
* **Aminosteroids:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Complex polycyclic steroid nucleus
  * Relatively inflexible structure (advantage for drug design)
  * Water solubility improved through hydrophilic substitutions
  * Examples: Vecuronium, rocuronium, pancuronium
  * Key structural features:
	* Distance between nitrogen atoms
	* Acetyl groups ester-linked to 3 and 17 positions
	* Bis-quaternary structure (pancuronium) or monoquaternary with protonated Nin 2 position (vecuronium, rocuronium)

**Bottom Line:** Non-depolarizing muscle relaxants divide into two structural families: bis-benzylisoquinoliniums and aminosteroids. The structure directly impacts metabolism pathways, with ester orientation in bis-benzylisoquinoliniums determining susceptibility to Hofmann degradation, and quaternary nitrogens creating permanent charges that limit membrane permeability.

### 9.2.5. OPIOIDS

* **Parent Compound:** Morphine with complex ring structure [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Critical Structural Features:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Phenolic hydroxyl (-OH) group at position 3 (essential for activity)
  * Cyclohexanol -OH group at position 6 (not essential for activity)
  * Amine group (necessary for receptor binding)
* **Common Modifications:** [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
  * Diamorphine: Acetylation at positions 3 and 6 (pro-drug with increased lipid solubility)
  * Codeine: Methylation at position 3 (increased lipid solubility but reduced activity)
  * Antagonists: Substitutions at amine group (e.g., naloxone)
  * Synthetic derivatives: Simplified structures (fentanyl and derivatives) with phenolic ring preserved

**Bottom Line:** Opioid structure-activity relationships center on the phenolic hydroxyl group (position 3) and amine group, both essential for μ-receptor binding. Modifications to these groups directly affect potency, lipid solubility, and whether the compound acts as an agonist or antagonist.

## 9.3. MEDICINAL CHEMISTRY MINI-DICTIONARY


**Quick reference for common chemical groups and structures in drugs.**

* **Acetyl:** CH3COO-R group; proton donor (acidic, hydrophilic); present in aspirin, NSAIDs, acetylcholine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Alkane:** Saturated hydrocarbon chains; lipid-soluble though short chains are water-miscible [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Alkanol:** Alkanes with -OH substitutions; -OH increases water solubility [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

* **Amide:** R-CO·NH2 structure; found in chain linking xylidine ring with substituted amine in amide local anesthetics [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Amine:** Nitrogen-containing groups; at pH above pKa become protonated (+) increasing water solubility; quaternary nitrogens permanently charged [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Benzyl:** Unsaturated 6-carbon ring structure; lipid-soluble; common in drugs like etomidate, ketamine [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Carbamyl:** -CONH2 group; found in anticholinesterase inhibitors [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Carboxylic acid:** Acids derived from alkanols; proton donors and water-soluble [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Catechol:** 1,2-hydroxybenzene; both -OH groups required for COMT metabolism [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]
* **Choline:** CH3 N(CH3)3; acetylcholine precursor [[mmed_anaes_part_a_syllabus#2.1 General Pharmacology|ⓘ]]

# 10. SUMMARY OF KEY PRINCIPLES [MULTI-PART]


* **Pharmacokinetics:** Describes drug movement through the body (ADME); distribution often explains early offset of anesthetic effect despite long elimination half-lives.
* **Pharmacodynamics:** Describes drug effects on the body; receptor interactions follow mathematical principles but receptor occupancy doesn't linearly correlate with effect.
* **Clinical Applications:** Context-sensitive half-time and effect-site targeting better predict recovery than elimination half-life; computer models enable precise drug delivery through TCI systems.
* **Drug Interactions:** Occur at pharmaceutical, pharmacokinetic, and pharmacodynamic levels; synergistic interactions between hypnotics and opioids form the foundation of balanced anesthesia.

**Bottom Line:** Understanding basic pharmacology principles is fundamental to anesthesia practice. Key concepts include distinguishing distribution from elimination processes, recognizing context-sensitivity of drug recovery profiles, appreciating effect-site concentration dynamics, and leveraging beneficial drug interactions while avoiding harmful ones.
